Exercise hyperpnoea: Unravelling layers of regulation
Authors: Nasimi A. Guluzade, Gordon S. Mitchell, Daniel A. Keir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHow ventilation (V˙E) matches gas exchange (V˙CO2) while maintaining isocapnia during exercise remains unresolved. We measured V˙E, V˙CO2, arterialized‐venous PCO2 (PāCO2) and hypercapnic ventilatory responsiveness (HCVR = ∆V˙E/∆PCO2) during four steady‐state exercise intensities below the respiratory compensation point (RCP) and used a mathematical model to characterize feedforward contributions to exercise hyperpnoea and test the hypothesis that CO2 chemofeedback is inhibitory. Model assumptions were that the ventilatory response to exercise‐intensity changes (GSYS = ∆V˙E/∆V˙CO2) equals the sum of a feedforward exercise stimulus proportional to V˙CO2 (GEX) modified by the CO2 chemoreflex (HCVR). Using experimental data GSYS was quantified as ∆V˙E/∆V˙CO2 between exercise steps and GEX equalled GSYS minus chemoreflex feedback (i.e. HCVRx∆PāCO2/∆V˙CO2). Twenty healthy young participants completed a maximal cycling test and three submaximal step‐incremental protocols of two 8 min stages below the estimated lactate threshold (θLT; MOD1 and MOD2) and two 12 min stages between θLT and RCP (HVY1 and HVY2). During visits 2 and 3, three rebreathing tests performed at each intensity provided HCVR (average V˙E–end‐tidal PCO2 slope). During visit 4, heated hand vein blood provided PāCO2 at each intensity, and V˙CO2 and V˙E were measured by metabolic cart. With higher steady‐state V˙CO2, one‐way repeated‐measures ANOVA showed that HCVR rose from 2.4 ± 0.9 at MOD1 to 2.8 ± 0.8, 3.2 ± 0.8 and 3.6 ± 1.0 L · min−1 · mmHg−1 at MOD2, HVY1 and HVY2, respectively; ∆PāCO2/∆V˙CO2 decreased (P < 0.001) from 0.2 ± 4.3 at MOD1 to −1.9 ± 5.0, −5.5 ± 4.5 and −8.0 ± 4.6 mmHg · L · min−1; and GEX increased (P < 0.001) from 26.9 ± 15.1 at MOD1 to 34.5 ± 20.3, 53.9 ± 19.1 and 73.4 ± 27.5 L · min−1. Exercise hyperpnoea may reflect the balance of intensity‐dependent feedforward and inhibitory CO2 chemofeedback drive. Key points We aimed to quantify exercise intensity‐dependent changes in CO2 chemoreflexes and interpret their role in exercise hyperpnoea.Ventilatory, gas exchange, arterialized‐venous PCO2 (PāCO2) and hypercapnic ventilatory responses (HCVR) were measured during successive work‐to‐work cycling exercise transitions from low‐to‐moderate to heavy‐intensity work rates.The measured HCVR at each intensity and ∆V˙E/∆V˙CO2 and ∆PāCO2/∆V˙CO2 from one work rate to the next were included in a mathematical model to characterize feedforward contributions to exercise hyperpnoea at four intensities.With increasing steady‐state V˙CO2, ∆V˙E/∆V˙CO2 and HCVR progressively increased, whereas ∆PāCO2/∆V˙CO2 progressively became more negative.Assuming ∆V˙E is the sum of a feedforward gain plus linear CO2 chemofeedback gain responding to changes in V˙CO2 and PaCO2 from rest to exercise, respectively, we estimated that feedforward gain rises with increasing exercise intensities, attaining a drive that exceeds requirements for PCO2 regulation at its resting level.Exercise hyperpnoea, therefore, requires a feedforward exercise stimulus constrained by inhibitory chemofeedback from rest to exercise to minimize hypocapnia .

Introduction

During steady‐state exercise the rate of carbon dioxide production (V˙CO2) can increase more than 15‐fold resting values. Yet up to metabolic rates associated with the respiratory compensation point (RCP) the arterial partial pressure of CO2 (PaCO2) and hydrogen ion concentration ([H+]) remain within relatively narrow limits due to a close matching of minute ventilation (V˙E) with V˙CO2 (Diamond et al., 1977; Ferretti et al., 2022; Guluzade et al., 2025; Ward et al., 1983, 2022; Whipp & Wasserman, 1972). Despite years of research mechanism(s) responsible for this exercise hyperpnoea remain elusive, with evidence from both animal models and humans indicating the necessity of both feedback and feedforward mechanisms (Forster et al., 2012; Mitchell & Babb, 2006).

One prevailing theory describes a feedforward exercise stimulus (either from exercising limbs, an unknown mixed venous chemoreceptor or ‘central command’) as the primary driver of hyperpnoea in humans and other mammals (Dempsey et al., 2014; Eldridge et al., 1981; Mitchell & Babb, 2006; Poon & Greene, 1985). If the feedforward stimulus is not precisely matched to V˙CO2 during exercise, large deviations in PaCO2 are expected. However this does not occur, likely because these deviations trigger feedback responses from CO2 chemoreceptors (peripheral and central medullary) that minimize deviations from a PCO2 ‘set point’ within arterial blood and brainstem interstitium (Bennett & Fordyce, 1988, 1993; Mitchell, 1990; Mitchell & Babb, 2006).

Recently Welch and Mitchell (2024) reintroduced a simplified model of CO2 control during exercise hyperpnoea that describes this concept. The model assumes that the total ventilatory response gain (∆V˙E/∆V˙CO2) to a step change in exercise work rate (quantified as GSYS = ∆V˙E/∆V˙CO2) is equal to a primarily feedforward exercise gain proportional to V˙CO2 (GEX) plus linear CO2 feedback (i.e. ‘feedback’ gain or the hypercapnic ventilatory response (HCVR)), which depends on consequent changes in PaCO2 (HCVR × ∆PaCO2). In this model the predominant feedforward component is an open‐loop PaCO2 regulator that refers to an anticipated or learned neural drive to breathe; V˙E is further modified (up or down) by chemoreceptive feedback to minimize acute deviations (or errors) in PaCO2 from its resting value (i.e. the set point). Assuming additivity: ΔV˙Esystem=ΔV˙Efeedforward+ΔV˙Efeedback (1) ΔV˙Esystem=GEX×ΔV˙CO2+HCVR×ΔPaCO2

The advantage of this equation is that GEX – which reflects the integrated response to all excitatory and inhibitory neural inputs in response to changes in exercise intensity apart from the HCVR – can be estimated during an exercise transition where ∆V˙E, ∆V˙CO2, ∆PaCO2 and HCVR are known. In this model CO2 chemofeedback is treated as a single response (HCVR × ∆PaCO2) that reflects combined peripheral and central chemoreflex inputs. Rearranging eqn (1) gives: (2) GEX=(ΔV˙E/ΔV˙CO2)−HCVR×(ΔPaCO2/ΔV˙CO2)

During transitions from low to higher exercise work rates below the intensity associated with metabolic acidosis and hyperventilation (i.e. the RCP) ∆V˙E/∆V˙CO2 averages ∼22 (Diamond et al., 1977; Ferretti et al., 2022; Phillipson et al., 1981; Ward et al., 2022), and within an individual this value appears fixed across exercise intensities giving the characteristic linear V˙E versus V˙CO2 relationship below the RCP (Whipp, 1987; Whipp et al., 1989). With such precision V˙E–V˙CO2 matching it is assumed that the PaCO2 is regulated at its set point, equivalent to the resting PaCO2 (∼40 mmHg; (Ferretti et al., 2022; Mitchell & Babb, 2006)). In addition a series of recent studies reported that HCVR remains unchanged with increasing exercise intensity (near ∼1.0 L · min−1 · mmHg−1; (Mann et al., 2022; Wright et al., 2024)). Applying fixed values for these variables in eqn (2) (i.e. ∆PaCO2 = 0 mmHg and HCVR = 1.0 L · min−1 · mmHg−1), progressive step exercise‐induced increases in V˙CO2 (i.e. ∆V˙CO2) yield a feedforward gain (i.e. GEX) that is unchanged with exercise intensity (Fig. 1, hypothesis 1).

A hypothetical representation of the feedforward and feedback contribution to exercise hyperpnoea

Three potential hypotheses are depicted demonstrating the relationship of hypercapnic ventilatory response (HCVR) and partial pressure of arterial carbon dioxide (PaCO2) to increasing carbon dioxide production (V˙CO2) during exercise and its contribution to the feedforward exercise gain (GEX) response.

However, perfect PCO2 regulation (or isocapnic hyperpnoea) seldom occurs during even mild or moderate exercise. Recently, we showed that the steady‐state PCO2 estimated from arterialized‐venous (PāCO2) samples does not remain fixed at resting values with increasing exercise intensity below the RCP (Guluzade et al., 2025). Rather during steady‐state exercise it fell slightly from ∼41 mmHg at 20 W to 41, 40, 39 and 37 mmHg at intensities equivalent to ∼65% of the estimated lactate threshold (θLT), 85% θLT, 25% between θLT and RCP and 80% between θLT and RCP, respectively. Similar reductions were reported during treadmill exercise, with PaCO2 falling from 41.5 to 41.4, 38.1 and 34.8 mmHg with 3 min of running at 24%, 40%, 67% and 79% of maximal V˙O2 (Clark et al., 1980). Combining an HCVR that does not change with exercise intensity and including negative ∆PaCO2 values with increasing ∆V˙CO2 in eqn (2), GEX must increase with exercise intensity (Fig. 1, hypothesis 2) implying CO2 feedback provides an inhibitory drive to breathe, similar to other mammalian species (Mitchell, 1990; Mitchell & Babb, 2006).

The extent to which CO2 feedback inhibits the normal feedforward ventilatory response with increasing exercise intensity may be underestimated by hypothesis 2. Although several recent studies report a fixed HCVR with higher work rates (Mann et al., 2022; Wright et al., 2024), in non‐human vertebrate species CO2 feedback exerts a ‘braking’ action on the feedforward ventilatory drive, which prevents steady‐state PaCO2 from falling by more than 10 mmHg (Forster et al., 1983; Mitchell et al., 1984; Pan et al., 1983, 1986).

Previous characterizations of intensity‐dependent changes in the HCVR have mostly relied on transient CO2 breathing computed as baseline‐to‐peak changes in V˙E before versus after two breaths of 10% CO2 divided by the absolute change in end‐tidal PCO2 (i.e. ∆V˙E/∆PETCO2) (Mann et al., 2022). A limitation of this method is the assumption of a fixed PETCO2–PaCO2 gradient with increasing exercise intensity. At higher V˙CO2 up to θLT, changes in breathing pattern typically lead to gradually narrowing underestimations of PaCO2 by PETCO2, followed by a continual overestimation beyond θLT up to the RCP (Whipp et al., 1989). Thus with transient CO2 breathing, ∆PaCO2 (the controlled variable of V˙E and denominator of the HCVR) may be progressively overestimated at higher intensities, masking a rising HCVR versus V˙CO2 relationship. This concern is alleviated by rebreathing methods, where the PETCO2–PaCO2 gradient is minimized and PCO2 of both compartments rises in direct proportion to each other (Carr et al., 2023). Nevertheless an intensity‐dependent rise in HCVR would suggest an excessive feedforward exercise ventilatory response that is constrained by greater CO2 feedback inhibition (Fig. 1, hypothesis 3).

To assess the three alternate hypotheses depicted in Fig. 1 and further explore this alternate model of exercise hyperpnoea our objectives were to (1) use rebreathing to assess HCVR at steady‐state intensities below RCP; (2) quantify intensity‐dependent changes in V˙E, V˙CO2 and PaCO2; and (3) use these data and the mathematical model to characterize feedforward contributions to exercise hyperpnoea. To accomplish these goals we measured ventilation (V˙E), gas exchange (V˙CO2), arterialized‐venous PCO2 and quantified the linear HCVR via rebreathing (HCVR = ∆V˙E/∆PETCO2) during four steady‐state exercise intensities below the RCP. We hypothesized that with increasing steady‐state V˙CO2 (1) HCVR would increase, (2) PāCO2 would decrease and (3) the feedforward gain with respect to PaCO2 would rise supporting the third hypothesis from Fig. 1.

Methods

Ethical approval

All procedures were approved by The University of Western Ontario Ethics Committee for Research on Human Subjects (WREM: 123924). This study was conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki, except for registration in a public database. All participants volunteered and provided written informed consent to participate.

Participants

Twenty participants (10 females; age: 23 ± 4 years; body mass: 71 ± 10 kg; height: 171 ± 10 cm; relative V˙O2peak: 42.1 ± 8.6 mL · kg−1 · min−1) completed the study. All participants were healthy individuals who were non‐smokers and free of cardiovascular or respiratory disease, acute or chronic musculoskeletal injury or any other health issues. Study design

The study involved four visits to the laboratory. Each of the four visits was separated by a minimum of 24 h. The first visit involved resting pulmonary, cardiovascular and metabolic measures and a cycling‐based step‐ramp‐step (SRS) exercise protocol to quantify aerobic fitness and establish the power output ranges associated with the θLT and RCP, and from these the exercise intensity domains of each participant (Iannetta et al., 2020; Keir et al., 2022). The next three visits involved the completion of a submaximal, step‐incremental protocol. In two of the three visits participants rebreathed a mixture of gas (22% O2, 4% CO2, balance N2) at select time points during steady‐state exercise at each intensity. In one of the three visits arterialized‐venous blood was collected with participants breathing room air. Except for the initial visit, visits 2–4 were completed in a randomized order. The volunteers were asked to abstain from caffeine, alcohol, recreational drug use and strenuous exercise for ≥12 h before each visit to minimize the within‐ and between‐day data fluctuations. The visits were completed at a similar time of day within 2 weeks to minimize time‐related physiological changes.

Experimental protocol

Step‐ramp‐step protocol

The first visit consisted of resting cardiovascular and metabolic measurements, pulmonary function tests (spirometry) and the SRS exercise protocol. Heart rate and blood pressure were measured at rest. During this time, resting V˙O2 and V˙CO2 were measured using a metabolic cart (Quark, CPET, COSMED, Rome, Italy). Next the participants completed three trials of forced vital capacity (FVC), slow vital capacity and maximal voluntary ventilation (MVV) tests to assess lung function via the spirometry test feature of the metabolic cart (Koegelenberg et al., 2013; Quanjer et al., 2012). The trial with the highest percent‐predicted value was used to characterize pulmonary function. Finally the SRS exercise protocol was completed on an electromagnetically braked cycle ergometer (Velotron, Quarq, Seattle, WA, USA) at a self‐selected cadence. The protocol consisted of a 4 min 20 W baseline, followed by a 6 min constant‐power output bout of moderate‐intensity exercise (60 W for females and 100 W for males), followed by 4 min at a 20 W baseline before the ramp portion, where the power output was raised at a rate of 25 W per minute until volitional fatigue. Following the termination of the ramp test the participant rested for 20 min and completed a 12 min constant‐power output bout of heavy‐intensity exercise prescribed at ∼50% of the peak power output achieved in the ramp.

Step‐incremental protocol

For visits 2–4, participants performed a 44 min submaximal step‐incremental exercise test at four individual‐specific intensities (identified from analysis of the SRS protocol): two within the moderate‐ (below participants’ θLT) and two within the heavy‐intensity domain (between θLT and RCP). After a 4 min 20 W baseline, participants completed 8 min of constant‐work rate exercise at 50% θLT (i.e. lower moderate, referred to as MOD1), followed by 8 min at 90% θLT (i.e. upper moderate; MOD2), then, 12 min at 30% of the difference between θLT and RCP (∆30, i.e. lower heavy; HVY1) and then 12 min at 80% of the difference between θLT and RCP (∆80, i.e. upper heavy; HVY2). During visit 2, arterialized blood gas measurements were obtained 7 times throughout the step‐incremental protocol, and participants’ gas exchange and ventilatory measures were acquired every 10 s using a mixing chamber‐based metabolic system (COSMED). Visits 3 and 4 involved acute rebreathing (22% O2, 4% CO2, balance N2) gas administration of 6–10 breaths performed 3 times during steady‐state exercise at each intensity (12 total).

Measurements

Participants were equipped with a facemask (V2 Mask, Hans Rudolph Inc., Shawnee, Kansas, USA) for all visits, which during visits 1 and 2 was attached to the metabolic cart to collect respired gas concentrations, flows and volumes. Breath‐by‐breath gas exchange and ventilatory data were obtained during the SRS protocol on visit 1, and mixing chamber‐based measures were acquired during the step‐incremental protocol on visit 2. A heart rate monitor was worn during all visits (Polar H10, Polar Elecro Oy, Kempele, Finland).

Blood gas measures were taken during visit 2 by sampling blood from the dorsal hand vein via a percutaneous Teflon catheter (22‐gauge, Angiocath; Becton Dickinson Canada Inc., Utah, USA) secured to the hand with Tegaderm. Blood was ‘arterialized’ (i.e. venous blood collected under conditions that cause it to closely resemble arterial blood) by placing the hand, palm down in a heated plexiglass box that circulated air heated to 60°C (Department of Chemistry, University of Vermont, Burlington, VT, USA) (Forster et al. 1972). Approximately 1.0 mL of arterialized‐venous blood was sampled at rest and at minutes 4, 11, 17, 25, 29, 39 and 43 of the step‐incremental protocol, totalling ∼7 mL. These time points yielded one sample during the 20 W baseline, a sample at each of the two moderate intensities (MOD1 and MOD2) and two samples during each of the heavy intensities (HVY1.1, HVY1.2, HVY2.1, HVY2.2), respectively. Arterialized blood samples were drawn over three breaths via heparinized syringes following a catheter flush sufficient to eliminate blood in the dead space of the tubing and stopcock (lithium heparin) and immediately analysed, in duplicate, for PCO2, pH and concentrations of lactate, glucose, bicarbonate and other electrolytes, using a blood gas analyser (ABL90Flex, Radiometer, Denmark).

During visits 3 and 4 participants wore a facemask connected in series to a heated pneumotachometer (3810 Series, Hans Rudolph Inc.) and a five‐way rebreathing valve (2400 Series, Hans Rudolph Inc.). With the main port attached to the pneumotachometer the second port on the five‐way valve was open to room air and ports 3 through 5 were connected to 6 L bags filled with premixed hypercapnic gas. This set‐up allowed for transitions between breathing room air and breathing from the rebreathing bag. In the final 5 min of each of the four intensity steps, participants were manually transitioned from breathing room air to a rebreathing bag for ∼6–10 breaths. At each intensity the switch occurred 3 times, each separated by 90 s. During these trials respired gas was sampled continuously at the mouth and analysed for fractional concentrations of O2 and CO2 using mass spectrometry (MATE MGA 1100, MA Tech Services, St Louis, MO, USA). Inspiratory and expiratory pressures were sampled continuously using a heated pneumotach (Heat Controller, Hans Rudolph Inc.). Analog signals were amplified and converted to flow via an amplifier (Pneumotach Amplifier 1, Hans Rudolph Inc.), and inspired and expired volumes for each breath were derived from integration of the flow signal. Respiratory flows and fractional gas concentration data were collected at 60 Hz via a 16‐bit analog‐to‐digital converter (National Instruments, Austin, USA). Custom software aligned gas concentrations and flow signals as measured by the pneumotachometer and executed a peak‐detection programme to determine breath‐by‐breath end‐tidal pressure of CO2 (PETCO2) and O2 (PETO2), tidal volume, breathing frequency and V˙E.

Data analysis

Exercise prescription and steady‐state gas exchange

The θLT and RCP were identified from the ramp portion of the SRS protocol, and the PO and V˙O2 associated with θLT (i.e. moderate‐heavy‐intensity domain boundary) and RCP (i.e. heavy‐severe‐intensity domain boundary) were identified as previously described (Iannetta et al., 2020, 2023). The SRS protocol was favoured over a traditional ramp because it corrects the effect of V˙O2 kinetics on the V˙O2 versus ramp‐PO relationship (Iannetta et al., 2020; Keir, Paterson et al., 2018). The MOD1, MOD2, HVY1 and HVY2 intensities were determined based on these boundaries (Iannetta et al., 2020; Keir et al., 2022). The V˙O2peak was defined as the highest point of a 20 s rolling average, and peak power output (POpeak) was defined as the highest wattage reached by the participant.

Mixing chamber data from visit 2 were used to identify the steady‐state gas exchange (V˙O2 and V˙CO2) and ventilatory (V˙E, mixing chamber‐based partial pressure of mixed expired CO2 (PECO2)) data for each exercise intensity by computing the average of all data within the final 2 min of each stage. Each duplicate blood sample was averaged. The [H+] (in nM) was obtained by computing 10−pH. Physiological dead‐space volume (VD) was calculated for visits 3–4 using blood gas measurements of PāCO2, PECO2 obtained from visit 2, and the Bohr‐Engelh off equation: VDVT=Pa¯CO2−PECO2Pa¯CO2 where V D is dead‐space volume (L), V T is tidal volume (L), PāCO2 is measured partial pressure of arterialized‐venous CO2 (mmHg) and PECO2 is mixed expired CO2 (mmHg). Dead‐space ventilation (V˙D, L · min−1) = V D × breathing frequency (fB, breaths per minute). Alveolar ventilation (V˙A) was computed as V˙A (in L · min−1) = V˙E − V˙D.

For visits 3 and 4, V D, V˙D and V˙A were computed breath‐by‐breath during the steady‐state portions of each step, using the PāCO2 and PECO2 values obtained from visit 2. These were assumed to be similar in visits 3 and 4. For the two heavy‐intensity bouts two PāCO2 values were obtained near the onset of steady state and near the end of the bout (HVY1.1, HVY1.2 and HVY2.1, HVY2.2).

Feedback gain (HCVR)

During the rebreathing condition the PETCO2 versus time relationship was fitted with a linear regression line to compute the rate of rise of carbon dioxide (rrCO2). The equation of the line was used to predict the PETCO2 versus time relationship during rebreathing. Thereafter V˙E was plotted relative to predicted PETCO2 and fitted with a linear regression line. Model parameter estimates for each participant were calculated by linear least‐square regression analysis that found the minimal sum of squared residuals between the linear model and the experimental data (Origin 2024, Northampton, USA). The slope of the line from the V˙E versus PETCO2 relationship from the rebreathing test provided the HCVR (L·min−1·mmHg−1). Data of the first two breaths at the onset of rebreathing were excluded to account for any anticipatory effects and the lung‐carotid circulatory delay (Cunningham et al., 1977; Mihara et al., 2003). Fits were performed thrice during steady‐state exercise of each intensity. With repeated experiments a total of six steady‐state exercise values for each exercise intensity were obtained and averaged (Guluzade et al., 2022). Feedforward gain (GEX)

Feedforward exercise gain with respect to PaCO2 regulation (GEX) was calculated at each intensity using the eqn (2) (Mitchell, 1990): GEX=GSYS−HCVR×ΔPa¯CO2ΔV˙CO2 where GSYS is the system gain (∆V˙E/∆V˙CO2), HCVR is the hypercapnic ventilatory response measured at each steady‐state exercise intensity, PāCO2 is measured arterialized‐venous PCO2 (mmHg). The changes in PāCO2, V˙E and V˙CO2 were calculated as the difference in steady state between successive step changes in power output. Steady‐state V˙E and V˙CO2 were computed as the last 2 min average of each step. For PāCO2, values measured closest to the end of each exercise bout were used. Statistical analysis

Data are presented as means ± SD. A paired Student's t test was used to determine sex differences in physical and baseline characteristics. Exploratory analyses examined potential sex differences in feedback and feedforward responses. A two‐way (sex x intensity) repeated‐measures ANOVA with Bonferroni post hoc test consisting of two groups (males and females) and four intensities (MOD1, MOD2, HVY1 and HVY2) was used to examine between‐group and between‐condition differences in rebreathing and feedforward responses (GEX). Where no significant main effects or interactions with sex were found, data were collapsed across sexes to evaluate between‐condition differences. A one‐way repeated‐measures ANOVA was used to assess the differences in metabolic variables and arterialized blood gas measurements across the different intensities. An intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) and coefficient of variance (CV) were used to assess the between‐day reproducibility of the repeated hypercapnic rebreathing trials on visits 3 and 4. CV was used to assess the variability and reproducibility between days at rest and for all exercise intensities. Statistical significance was accepted at an α level of 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using RStudio 1.4.1717 and SPSS V29.0.2.

Discussion

Our objectives were to explore the role of CO2 feedback during exercise by quantifying the HCVR during steady‐state exercise of increasing intensity within the moderate‐ and heavy‐intensity domains, and using measurements of ventilatory, gas exchange and acid–base responses at these intensities to estimate the feedforward contribution to exercise hyperpnoea (with respect to PaCO2 regulation, i.e. GEX). With increasing steady‐state V˙E and V˙CO2, HCVR increased progressively, and PāCO2 fell from 41 to 37 mmHg. Below we describe model interpretations based on measured intensity‐dependent changes in HCVR as they relate to the regulation of air breathing hyperpnoea. In doing so we provide support for the hypothesis of CO2 chemoreflex inhibition during exercise below the RCP and summarize experimental evidence for and against this concept.

Intensity‐dependent changes in HCVR

A recent series of experiments reported that HCVR increases from rest to unloaded exercise but does not change thereafter with work rates up to RCP (Mann et al., 2022). This fixed HCVR profile was later determined to be independent of fitness status (Wright et al., 2024), expiratory flow limitation (Mann et al., 2022) and menstrual cycle phase (Mann et al., 2024). A major methodological difference of these studies from the rebreathing approach presented herein is the use of transient CO2 breathing. Key assumptions of HCVR testing are that the measured change in PETCO2 is of equal magnitude to the change in arterial PCO2 (i.e. the denominator of HCVR and sensed variable of the chemoreceptors), and that any difference between these values is constant as exercise intensity increases. However the PETCO2–PaCO2 gradient is not fixed during exercise; the same ∆PETCO2 may underestimate ∆PaCO2 below θLT and overestimate ∆PaCO2 between θLT and RCP (Whipp et al., 1989). Unlike transient CO2 breathing methods, rebreathing minimizes the PETCO2–PaCO2 gradient and raises both variables in unison (Carr et al., 2023; Duffin, 2010), thereby providing a more accurate estimate of changes in PCO2 less influenced by exercise intensity below the RCP. Thus, methodological differences could explain our observation that the HCVR increases with intensity.

Alternatively a rebreathing‐derived HCVR likely reflects the combined impact of excitatory inputs from both peripheral and central chemoreceptors; the transient method may emphasize the peripheral chemoreflex due to the relatively slow equilibration between arterial blood and the brain tissues (Carr et al., 2023; Eldridge et al., 1984). Indeed the transient HCVR averaged slightly below 1.0 L · min−1 · mmHg−1 (Mann et al., 2022; Thompson et al., 2024; Wright et al., 2024), ∼1/3–1/4 of the mean values found with our rebreathing approach (2.4–3.6 L · min−1 · mmHg−1). This difference is consistent with the notion that central chemoreflexes dominate the HCVR in normoxic conditions. If both experimental approaches are accurate, intensity‐dependent increases in HCVR found in this study may have resulted from central chemoreflex sensitization without changes in peripheral chemoreflex sensitivity.

Our findings are also at odds with other exercise investigations of rebreathing where the HCVR was reported to be unchanged with increasing exercise intensity (Clark et al., 1980; Erling Asmussen & Nielsen, 1957; Gallagher et al., 1987; Mcclaran et al., 1999; Menitove et al., 1984; Poon, 1989). A notable difference from these studies is that we performed six (vs. 1) rebreathing tests at each exercise intensity (2 visits × 3 rebreathes per visit), with the average of these six tests used to quantify individual HCVR. We previously demonstrated that increasing the number of rebreathing trials at rest reduces signal‐to‐noise and enhances the ability to detect between‐condition differences (Guluzade et al., 2022).

Although the HCVR increased with exercise intensity, how (and where) such changes occur is unknown. Excitatory input from simultaneously stimulated peripheral and central chemoreflexes during HCVR is integrated in brainstem respiratory nuclei, relayed to spinal motor neurons and then transduced into the muscular contraction that drives breathing. Although enhanced carotid body and central chemoreceptor sensitivity to PaCO2 and [H+] may underly observed intensity‐dependent changes in HCVR, the explicit sensitivity of the chemoreceptors could remain unchanged. Altered ventilatory responses to CO2 (i.e. the CO2 chemoreflex) could occur at any step in the integrated chemoreflex response (e.g. within spinal inspiratory motor neurons). For example, short‐term modulation of the exercise ventilatory response (Mitchell & Babb, 2006), expressed as an increase in GSYS (i.e. ∆V˙E/∆V˙CO2) when a hypercapnic load is imposed by 200 mL of external respiratory dead space (with hyperoxia), maintains ∆PaCO2/∆V˙CO2 at normal levels (i.e. in normocapnic exercise; Mitchell. (1990)). Increased system gain with retained PCO2 regulation during acute dead‐space loading indicates an active regulatory process (i.e. short‐term modulation (STM)). If the HCVR is unchanged relative to control conditions, STM must reflect an increase in the feedforward exercise drive to breathe (i.e. GEX as dicated by equation 1) (Mitchell & Babb, 2006; Welch & Mitchell, 2024). However, there is a lack of conclusive evidence differentiating increased GEX versus increased HCVR in determining Gsys under these conditions. The increased exercise ∆V˙E response with imposed hypercapnia can be interpreted as either.

The mechanism of STM in goats requires spinal serotonin receptor activation as ∆PaCO2/∆V˙CO2 increases (∼ +2 mmHg · min · L−1) with systemic (Bach et al., 1993) or thoracic spinal serotonin receptor inhibition, demonstrating a reduced ventilatory response to increased respiratory dead space (Mitchell et al., 2008). Although intrathecal (spinal) serotonin receptor antagonists increase ∆PaCO2/∆V˙CO2, they do so only with a hypercapnic load imposed by increased respiratory dead space, and not during control exercise trials (Mitchell et al., 2008). Thus the actions of serotonin are unique to hypercapnic exercise. Like dead‐space loading rebreathing provides an additional CO2 stimulus during exercise that, through STM, may manifest as an increased exercise ventilatory response, and/or (in the current study) as an increased HCVR. These effects represent an interaction (exercise × chemofeedback) and cannot be apportioned to either factor without additional information (Mitchell, 1990). This multiplicative effect is readily reversed when room air breathing is restored (Mitchell, 1990; Turner et al., 1997). Although less is known about the mechanism of STM in humans, it has been demonstrated to occur in both males and females from the general population (Babb et al., 2010; Wood et al., 2011; Wood, Mitchell et al., 2008), as well as in obese individuals (Wood, Semon et al., 2008) or individuals with obstructive sleep apnoea (Bernhardt et al., 2017).

HCVR inhibits the ventilatory response to exercise

Applying a simplified, additive mathematical model to quantify feedforward contributions to exercise CO2 regulation GEX rose curvilinearly from 27 to 72 L·min−1 of V˙E per L·min−1 V˙CO2. Based on the model these data suggest that intensity‐dependent increases in HCVR provide an inhibitory stimulus to breathing to prevent hypocapnia during moderate‐intensity exercise and minimize substantial hypocapnia in the heavy‐intensity exercise domain, consistent with hypothesis 3 depicted in Fig. 1.

Below the θLT and within the moderate‐intensity domain PāCO2 remained constant, averaging 41 mmHg at 20 W, MOD1 (∼53 W) and MOD2 (∼86 W) as did [Lac−] and [H+]. However compared to the average ∆PāCO2/∆V˙CO2 for the transition from 20 W to MOD1 (0.18 mmHg.L.min−1) from MOD1 to MOD2 the ∆PāCO2/∆V˙CO2 fell to −1.9 mmHg.L.min−1. In those same conditions HCVR rose from 2.4 in MOD1 L·min−1·mmHg−1 to 2.8 L·min−1·mmHg−1 in MOD2, which resulted in a 22% increase in computed feedforward gain (i.e. GEX). Albeit slight this shift from no change to a negative change in ∆PāCO2/∆V˙CO2 suggests that feedforward stimuli (i.e. GEX) drive V˙E beyond metabolic demand, necessitating feedback inhibition via HCVR to maintain PCO2 near the set point of lower exercise intensities (Welch & Mitchell, 2024).

Within the heavy‐intensity domain hypocapnia became more apparent with PāCO2 falling from 40.6 mmHg (MOD2) to 39.0 mmHg in HVY1 (∆PāCO2/∆V˙CO2 = −5.5 mmHg.L.min−1) and further to 36.9 in HVY2 (∆PāCO2/∆V˙CO2 = −7.7 mmHg.L.min−1). The HCVR continued to increase during steady‐state exercise above θLT but below RCP, rising to 3.2 and 3.6 L·min−1·mmHg−1. Therefore the computed GEX increased two‐ and threefold from MOD1 values. From a standpoint of feedback inhibition these intensity‐dependent increases in HCVR are not trivial. For example had HCVR remained fixed at 2.4 L·min−1·mmHg−1 instead of increasing, our calculations (rearranging eqn (1): ∆PāCO2 = [(∆V˙E/∆V˙CO2) + GEX] × ∆V˙CO2/HCVR) indicate that PāCO2 would have dropped to ∼32 mmHg at HVY2, a tension associated with alkalosis and its sequalae, including reduced cerebral blood flow (Ainslie & Duffin, 2009; Hellstrom et al., 1996; Subudhi et al., 2011), impaired O2 delivery (Chin et al., 2007; Hayashi et al., 1999; LeBlanc et al., 2002) and altered metabolic function (Keir, Pollock et al., 2018; LeBlanc et al., 2002). Important to consider is that HVY1 and HVY2 were performed below the maximal metabolic steady state (as approximated by RCP), below which V˙E versus V˙CO2 is approximately linear (Ferretti et al., 2022; Keir et al., 2024), V˙E/V˙CO2 is stable and unchanged from the mid‐ to upper‐regions of the moderate‐intensity domain (Keir et al., 2024); see also Table 2), [Lac−] and [H+] are elevated but stable (Black et al., 2017; Keir et al., 2024; Wasserman et al., 2011) and exercise may be sustained for 30 min or more (Jones et al., 2008). Furthermore, all data were collected during verified steady‐state intensities prescribed using the SRS protocol. This design strengthens confidence in the model assumptions and distinguishes our approach from shorter protocols that may not fully achieve steady state at higher intensities.

The respiratory model used here reflects a summation of dynamic feedforward and feedback inputs (Welch & Mitchell, 2024). The feedback arm is mediated by central and peripheral chemoreceptors, whereas the feedforward arm is defined with respect to PaCO2 regulation and may include a combination of inputs from the CNS (likely cortical and subcortical regions) or peripheral inputs from muscle afferent neurons – or any other non‐PCO2‐reflex‐mediated drives to breath (e.g. venous CO2 content or cardiac output). We acknowledge that this model reduces the complexity of respiratory drives and their integration to establish V˙E during exercise. Despite its simplicity the interpretation of an inhibitory role of respiratory chemoreceptors during exercise hyperpnoea does have experimental support. For example such chemoreceptive restraint on exercise V˙E within the intensity range studied here has been demonstrated in non‐human mammals (Dempsey et al., 1985). With carotid body denervation both goats and ponies become more hypocapnic when transitioning from light‐ to heavy‐intensity exercise versus normal conditions (Forster et al., 1983; Mitchell et al., 1984; Pan et al., 1983, 1986). Further when arterial PCO2 during mild or moderate exercise is restored to resting values in exercising goats, breathing increases, demonstrating relief from ongoing chemoreflex inhibition of breathing (Mitchell & Babb, 2006). Therefore feedback inhibition from CO2 chemoreflexes may be necessary to prevent excessive hypocapnia during moderate‐to‐heavy exercise.

Evidence against HCVR as an inhibitory stimulus

Despite consistency between our model‐based interpretation and experimental data, additional evidence supports the idea that CO2 chemoreflexes are excitatory or unimportant. These investigations are summarized below.

If the HCVR facilitates exercise hyperpnoea, diminished chemoreflex sensitivity during exercise should result in hypercapnia. In accordance in asthmatic humans carotid body resection leads to transient hypercapnia during moderate‐intensity exercise (Wasserman et al., 1975). However this hypercapnic response may be unique to asthmatics, irrespective of CO2 feedback (Forster et al., 1993; Wasserman et al., 1975). In individuals with congenital central hypoventilation syndrome (CCHS) end‐tidal PCO2 increases from rest to steady‐state moderate exercise, whereas no change is observed in healthy controls (Shea et al., 1993). Exercise V˙E/V˙CO2 was also lower in CCHS, supporting a faciliatory role of CO2 feedback. However it is not clear that the only pathophysiology associated with CCHS is diminished chemoreception. From another perspective low‐dose dopamine infusion reduced submaximal steady‐state V˙E (by ∼3 L · min−1) and increased end‐tidal PCO2 (by ∼2 mmHg) versus placebo in a mixed group of adults in good health or with heart failure or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) (Collins et al., 2025). Furthermore heightened chemoreceptor sensitivity is reported to correlate with exaggerated (not blunted) V˙E responses to exercise in patients with heart failure (Andreas et al., 1996; Chua et al., 1996). Finally, in healthy humans transient high O2 breathing leads to slight increases in PETCO2 and decreases in V˙E , effects that becomes more pronounced as exercise intensity increases (Guluzade et al., 2025; Jeyaranjan et al., 1987; Masuda et al., 1988; St Croix, Cunningham, Paterson, & Kowalchuk, 1996; Stockley, 1978).

Although these findings are at odds with the hypothesis of CO2 feedback‐mediated inhibition, several factors warrant consideration. First the linear ventilatory response to CO2 rebreathing identifies an HCVR that is a composite of peripheral and central chemoreflexes (Duffin et al., 2000; Guluzade et al., 2023; Keir et al., 2019). All of the aforementioned interventions (with the exception of CCHS) specifically target the peripheral arm of the HCVR. For example although hyperoxia diminishes carotid body CO2 sensitivity (Buckler & Vaughan‐Jones, 1994; Dasso et al., 2000; Duchen & Biscoe, 1992; Fitzgerald & Parks, 1971; Hornbein & Roos, 1963; Vidruk et al., 2001), its effect on HCVR is small (∼13% (Mohan & Duffin, 1997)); in contrast the central chemoreflex dominates the rebreathing response (Duffin, 2010; Huggard et al., 2023). Although hyperoxia, carotid body denervation and dopamine infusion diminish the CO2 feedback response in humans, none would reduce HCVR by more than one third and, thus, may not be effective means to release the braking action of CO2 chemofeedback. Further carotid body input to respiratory centres consists of tonic and reflexive components (Narkiewicz et al., 2016; Sayegh et al., 2022). Ventilatory suppression by these interventions may reflect a loss of tonic peripheral chemoreceptor drive while only modestly impacting the HCVR (Mitchell et al., 2001). Finally although peripheral and central chemoreflexes appear not to interact in most humans during quiet breathing at rest (Clement et al., 1992; Cui et al., 2012; Guluzade et al., 2023; Milloy et al., 2022; St Croix, Cunningham, & Paterson, 1996), whether the carotid body exerts a synergistic effect on central chemoreflex drive during exercise is unknown.

Measurement of HCVR via CO2 rebreathing is an experimentally imposed condition that would never be encountered by healthy humans performing exercise in normal room air breathing. Thus the irrelevance of HCVR in the control of breathing during exercise is an intuitive argument. Moreover if the HCVR is unimportant for exercise hyperpnoea, interventions and conditions that alter its function should not impact PCO2 regulation during exercise. Although asthmatic humans who have undergone carotid body resection exhibit transient hypercapnia, with steady‐state moderate‐intensity exercise V˙E/V˙CO2 proportionality is retained, minimizing significant deviations in PaCO2 from rest (Honda et al., 1979; Lugliani et al., 1971; Wasserman et al., 1975). Such preservation of ventilatory control suggests no substantial role of HCVR in mediating exercise hyperpnoea.

Feedforward contributions to the regulation of PaCO2 during exercise

In the model, GEX represents a composite of central neurogenic (cortical and subcortical) and peripheral neurogenic (muscle afferents and other) factors driving ventilatory responses (Welch & Mitchell, 2024). These factors encompass feedforward signals with respect to the regulation of arterial PCO2, such as descending locomotor‐driven stimuli from the brain and ascending signals from peripheral sources, notably groups III and IV muscle afferents, which relay mechanical and metabolic information from locomotor and respiratory muscles to the respiratory control centres (Amann & Dempsey, 2008; Amann et al., 2010; Dempsey et al., 2014; Eldridge et al., 1981; Iannetta et al., 2024; Kaufman & Forster, 1996; Waldrop I et al., 1986). The increase in GEX with exercise intensity may reflect the heightened activation of these neurogenic inputs. Specifically with the absence of any mixed venous or pulmonary CO2 sensors, group IV afferents that are sensitive to metabolic by‐products, like [Lac−] and [H+], may become increasingly active, amplifying the ventilatory drive that contributes to GEX (Kaufman & Forster, 1996). Applying the additive respiratory control model presented herein such intensity‐dependent increases in GEX would be in excess of gas exchange requirements, necessitating a progressively sensitized HCVR to provide inhibitory ventilatory restraint to ensure that V˙E is matched to the rising metabolic demands and blood gas homoeostasis is maintained.

Evidence from exercising animal models demonstrates that serotoninergic modulation can intensify the ventilatory response to a CO2 stimulus at the spinal cord level, potentially contributing to a reversible increase in HCVR during rebreathing that is not apparent during normal, normocapnic exercise. This interaction could appear as an augmented HCVR – not through amplification of CO2 chemoreceptor sensitivity but rather through a mechanism operative within the thoracic or cervical spinal cord (i.e. STM; Mitchell et al., 2008) that is far from sites of traditional chemoreception. Based on reports that multiple treatments that increase resting ventilation via distinct mechanisms that either raise or decrease resting PaCO2 also increase the exercise ventilatory response (i.e., STM; (Mitchell & Babb, 2006; Mitchell et al., 1984; Schaefer & Mitchell, 1989), it was previously argued that STM reflects an increase in GEX versus HCVR per se, even though it occurs at a shared common pathway: the respiratory motor neurons (Mitchell & Babb, 2006). Importantly if the elevated HCVR we observed reflects STM rather than a measurable change in CO2 chemosensitivity, the magnitude of the GEX increase would be substantially lower than our calculated values. A smaller GEX would align our findings more closely with one or more of the alternative hypotheses regarding feedforward drive. The curvilinear V˙E/V˙CO2 relationship during steady‐state exercise observed here, which differs from the linear relationship reported by Welch and Mitchell (2024), may be influenced by STM mechanisms that become prominent at the higher work rates examined in this study.

Short‐term modulation: beyond additive thinking

Our computation and interpretation of the GEX term rely on an assumption of additivity between the feedforward and feedback systems without interaction. When steady‐state PaCO2 is perturbed, for example by increasing V˙CO2 with exercise and raising PaCO2 with increased respiratory dead space, neither a fixed feedforward gain nor the CO2 chemofeedback gain was sufficient to account for the full magnitude of exercise hyperpnea (i.e. ∆V˙E). With dead space the measured change in PaCO2 was significantly smaller than that predicted by the conventional additive model (Mitchell et al., 1984; Wood, Mitchell et al., 2008). This discrepancy demonstrated that the combined ventilatory response exceeded the simple sum of the individual feedforward and feedback contributions. It was hypothesized that a multiplicative interaction between the primary feedforward stimulus and CO2 feedback must have been present to regulate PaCO2 about a new set point. This multiplicative interaction (i.e. STM of the exercise ventilatory response) (Babb et al., 2010; Mitchell & Babb, 2006; Wood et al., 2010, 2011) is postulated to arise from amplification of the input–output relationship of respiratory alpha motor neurons in response to descending brainstem respiratory drive (Mitchell & Babb, 2006; Mitchell et al., 2008). Thus the exercise ventilatory response may be ‘layered’, reflecting an imperfect feedforward exercise ventilatory response, attenuated by classical CO2 chemoreceptor feedback, and further refined by STM, regulating the ‘final common pathway’ to respiratory muscles during exercise. The concept of STM cannot be attributed to exercise or CO2 chemoreception per se as it is an interaction between them and manifests in both the slope of the V˙E versus V˙CO2 relationship in exercise (the exercise ventilatory response) and the ventilatory response to changes in PaCO2 (i.e. HCVR). These principles, particularly STM, have been developed most extensively in animal models of exercise hyperpnoea (Mitchell & Babb, 2006), with validation in humans (Babb et al., 2010).

Limitations

We relied on arterialized‐venous samples to reflect arterial blood gas and metabolite changes. Although heated vein measures closely approximate arterial blood chemistry (except for PO2) (Forster et al., 1972), minor deviations may occur. Studies comparing arterial and heated‐hand arterialized‐venous blood during rest and exercise demonstrate that PCO2 differences are not significant (Forster et al., 1972), suggesting that arterialized‐venous sampling remains a valid surrogate for PaCO2 (Brooks et al., 1989). Given the 44 min of continuous exercise with increasing intensity, it is possible that core temperature rose. Significant changes in core temperature are known to impact blood gas measurements (Shaffrath & Adams, 1984; Shipp et al., 2004); however throughout the exercise protocol a cooling fan was directed towards the rear of all participants to maintain comfort and body temperature across intensities. Furthermore in a comparable treadmill‐based protocol changes in rectal temperature did not exceed 1°C during successive bouts of exercise at 24%, 40%, 67% and 79% of maximal V˙O2 (Clark et al., 1980).

The respiratory model does not account for any plasticity of the control system that may occur, such as long‐term facilitation of the exercise ventilatory response (Martin & Mitchell, 1993; Mitchell & Babb, 2006), that could change the ventilatory response for prolonged periods (Welch & Mitchell, 2024). However within‐day variability in HCVR was strong to excellent, indicating that repeated measurements during a single visit were highly consistent and unaffected by factors such as time elapsed or potential influences from prior tests. Similarly between‐day variability was also strong to excellent, demonstrating that HCVR remained stable across separate testing sessions, thereby confirming the reproducibility of the measure and suggesting minimal day‐to‐day fluctuations due to physiological or environmental variations. This reliability was consistent across all exercise intensities.

Conclusion

With work‐to‐work transitions of increasing steady‐state V˙CO2, ∆V˙E/∆V˙CO2 and HCVR rose progressively, and ∆PāCO2/∆V˙CO2 became increasingly negative. Incorporating our experimental data and a mathematical model of respiratory control with a predominant feedforward regulator that is modified (up or down) by linear CO2 feedback we suggest that the feedforward (GEX) drive of the hyperpnoeic response increases with exercise intensity up to RCP. The intensity‐dependent increases in both estimated GEX and measured HCVR may be caused by STM‐mediated amplification in the feedforward response and feedback gains, respectively. However confidence in the magnitude of these gain changes requires verification beyond a simple additive model where interactions between ∆V˙CO2 and ∆PaCO2 can amplify the feedforward (GEX), chemofeedback response (HCVR) or both. Collectively we suggest that below RCP, CO2 chemoreceptor feedback plays an inhibitory role, and that intensity‐dependent increases in HCVR are required to minimize hypocapnia and maintain reasonable PaCO2 regulation during air breathing exercise.
