When Resources Define Operability After Pulmonary Endarterectomy
Authors: Cristiano Berardo Cunha, Fabiano Lima Cantarelli, Rafael Alessandro Ferreira Gomes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey Words: anticoagulation, pulmonary hypertension, thrombosis

A recurrent pulmonary embolism after pulmonary endarterectomy is never a simple recurrence. It forces clinicians to ask whether they are facing a new acute thromboembolic event, residual or progressive chronic thromboembolic pulmonary disease, recurrent chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH), or a combination of these processes. In the accompanying case in this issue of JACC: Case Reports, Batool et al1 describe a young patient with previous pulmonary endarterectomy, hyperhomocysteinemia, recurrent pulmonary embolism while receiving rivaroxaban, right ventricular dysfunction, and limited access to invasive hemodynamics, catheter-based therapies, and advanced cardiopulmonary support. The report is important not because it offers a definitive solution, but because it exposes a frequent and underdiscussed problem: in pulmonary vascular disease, the boundary between inoperability and nondeliverability is often drawn by the system around the patient.

The first lesson is diagnostic. The authors appropriately acknowledge that CTEPH could not be confirmed because right heart catheterization was unavailable. Echocardiography suggested elevated right-sided pressures and right ventricular dysfunction, but noninvasive imaging cannot replace invasive hemodynamic confirmation when CTEPH is suspected.2 This distinction matters. Acute recurrent pulmonary embolism, residual chronic obstruction after pulmonary endarterectomy, and CTEPH share symptoms, imaging abnormalities, and right ventricular consequences, yet they drive different therapeutic pathways. In this patient, a new proximal filling defect and subsequent radiographic improvement after thrombolysis support an important acute component, whereas previous endarterectomy, reduced right lung volume, and suspected pulmonary hypertension raise concern for chronic disease. In such scenarios, diagnostic uncertainty should not be disguised; it should structure the therapeutic plan.

From an interventional standpoint, the decision to administer systemic thrombolysis in a patient without overt hemodynamic instability deserves careful framing. Intermediate- to high-risk pulmonary embolism remains a challenging category: right ventricular dysfunction and hypoxemia identify a vulnerable patient, but routine full-dose systemic thrombolysis carries substantial bleeding risk.3 In centers with catheter-directed reperfusion, multidisciplinary pulmonary embolism teams may consider catheter-directed thrombolysis, mechanical thrombectomy, or surgical embolectomy in selected patients who deteriorate, have contraindications to systemic thrombolysis, or require an alternative reperfusion strategy.3 In the present case, these options were simply not available, and systemic thrombolysis became the most feasible reperfusion strategy. This is defensible, but the editorial lesson is broader: limited resources do not negate guideline-based decision-making; they narrow the menu of available rescue options.

The same principle applies to balloon pulmonary angioplasty (BPA) and riociguat. BPA is not a treatment for an acute embolus; it is a staged interventional therapy for inoperable CTEPH, residual pulmonary hypertension after pulmonary endarterectomy, or selected distal chronic thromboembolic lesions.2 Its use requires careful anatomical definition, hemodynamic confirmation, procedural expertise, and the capacity to manage complications. Riociguat likewise has the most robust role in confirmed inoperable or persistent/recurrent CTEPH.2 In this patient, riociguat was started empirically because symptoms, echocardiography, and local constraints suggested residual pulmonary vascular disease. That decision is understandable, but it should trigger a plan for reassessment rather than close the diagnostic loop. Once clinically stabilized, the patient should ideally undergo right heart catheterization, perfusion imaging, and expert review of the pulmonary arterial anatomy whenever such resources can be accessed.

From a surgical perspective, the central issue is the distinction between true inoperability and the inability to safely deliver surgery in a given environment. Severe right ventricular dysfunction increases perioperative risk, but it should not be treated in isolation as a contraindication to pulmonary endarterectomy or reintervention. In expert centers, operability is determined by the accessibility of obstructive material, the relationship between anatomic burden and hemodynamic impairment, comorbidities, and the availability of specialized perioperative rescue strategies.4 Pulmonary endarterectomy is designed to unload the right ventricle; the presence of right ventricular dysfunction should prompt expert evaluation and perioperative planning, not automatic exclusion.

In the present report, the decision to defer repeat surgery was nevertheless clinically reasonable. The team judged that repeat pulmonary endarterectomy was prohibitively risky because the patient might not be weaned from cardiopulmonary bypass without right ventricular assist device support, which was unavailable locally.1 This nuance is crucial. The limitation was not only the patient's ventricle, but the absence of an ecosystem capable of rescuing the right ventricle if surgery failed to restore adequate cardiopulmonary physiology immediately. In complex pulmonary vascular surgery, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) or right ventricular assist device (RVAD) availability is not a luxury; it is part of the safety architecture that allows high-risk decisions to be made responsibly.4

Another surgical nuance is the difference between acute pulmonary embolectomy and repeat pulmonary endarterectomy. When the dominant lesion is an acute proximal embolus, surgical embolectomy may be conceptually and technically different from redo endarterectomy for organized chronic thromboembolic disease. The former can be framed as an acute reperfusion operation, whereas the latter is a specialized pulmonary vascular procedure requiring precise dissection of fibrotic material in a reoperative field. The case report labels the presentation as recurrent pulmonary embolism, yet the surgical discussion centers on repeat endarterectomy. A useful clinical framework first clarifies whether the primary target is acute thrombus, chronic organized obstruction, or both.

In resource-limited settings, a defer-and-optimize strategy may represent the safest bridge when advanced rescue support is not available. At 3 months, this patient showed improvement in oxygen saturation, functional class, right ventricular size, tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion, and estimated right ventricular systolic pressure.1 That trajectory supports the decision not to force a high-risk emergency operation. However, stabilization should open a new window for reassessment. Ongoing therapeutic anticoagulation should be followed, when feasible, by right heart catheterization, perfusion imaging, renewed anatomical review, and referral to a national or regional pulmonary vascular center. In many health systems, international referral is unrealistic, but structured regional networks and remote image review may be more scalable.

Anticoagulation is another important teaching point. Recurrence during rivaroxaban does not automatically prove direct oral anticoagulant failure, particularly when adherence, absorption, drug interactions, and diagnostic classification remain uncertain. Nonetheless, switching to warfarin is reasonable in suspected chronic thromboembolic disease or recurrent thrombosis, especially because vitamin K antagonism allows international normalized ratio monitoring and remains widely used in expert CTEPH practice.2,4 Observational data in chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension have linked direct oral anticoagulants to higher rates of recurrent venous thromboembolism than vitamin K antagonists, with comparable bleeding.5 Hyperhomocysteinemia may have contributed to thrombotic risk, but it should not become a single-cause explanation. Vitamin replacement is appropriate when deficiency is suspected or documented, yet recurrent thrombosis in this setting still warrants broader thrombophilia, anatomical, and hemodynamic reassessment.

Ultimately, the case argues for a resource-sensitive pulmonary vascular algorithm. First, define the physiology: is pulmonary hypertension confirmed or only suspected? Second, define the biology: acute thrombus, chronic organized obstruction, or mixed disease? Third, define the anatomy: proximal surgically accessible disease, distal disease suitable for BPA, or predominantly microvascular disease? Fourth, define local deliverability: which components of care—anticoagulation, systemic thrombolysis, catheter-directed therapy, BPA, pulmonary endarterectomy, ECMO/RVAD, and referral pathways—are actually available and reliable? Only then should the team choose among medical therapy, catheter-based reperfusion, BPA, embolectomy, repeat endarterectomy, or staged referral.

The core message is not that surgery was wrong, nor that thrombolysis, BPA, or riociguat would have been universally superior. The message is that pulmonary vascular decisions are not dictated by anatomy and physiology alone. They are taken at the intersection of disease, expertise, rescue capacity, and access. In that intersection, language matters. A patient may be anatomically operable but locally nondeliverable. Recognizing that distinction is the first step toward safer decisions, better referrals, and more equitable pulmonary vascular care.

Patients with recurrent symptoms after pulmonary endarterectomy should be evaluated along 4 axes: physiology, biology, anatomy, and deliverability (Figure 1). Physiology requires confirmation of pulmonary hypertension with right heart catheterization when available. Biology distinguishes acute embolus, chronic organized obstruction, and mixed disease. Anatomy identifies surgically accessible proximal disease, distal lesions potentially suitable for BPA, or residual microvascular disease. Deliverability defines what the treating system can safely provide, including anticoagulation, thrombolysis, catheter-based therapy, BPA, pulmonary endarterectomy, and ECMO/RVAD rescue. The final strategy should match the disease process and the resource environment.
