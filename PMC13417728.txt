ABSTRACT
AbstractGenetically modified soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 was developed by conventional crossing to combine two single events: DAS‐44406‐6 and FG72. The soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 expresses the AAD‐12, 2mEPSPS, PAT and HPPD W336 proteins to confer herbicide tolerance to 2,4‐D‐, glyphosate‐, glufosinate‐containing and HPPD‐inhibiting herbicide products, respectively. The GMO Panel previously assessed the two single soybean events and did not identify safety concerns. No new data on the single soybean events were identified that could lead to modification of the original conclusions on their safety. The molecular characterisation, comparative analysis (agronomic, phenotypic and compositional characteristics) and the outcome of the toxicological, allergenicity and nutritional assessment indicate that the combination of the single soybean events and of the newly expressed proteins in the soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 does not give rise to food and feed safety or nutritional concerns. The GMO Panel concludes that the DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 stack soybean, as described in this application, is as safe as the non‐GM comparator and non‐GM reference varieties tested, and no post‐market monitoring of food and feed is considered necessary. In the case of release of processed soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 or accidental spillage of viable GM soybean seeds into the environment, this would not raise environmental safety concerns. The post market environmental monitoring plan and reporting intervals are in line with the intended uses of soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72. The GMO Panel concludes that soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 is as safe as its non‐GM comparator and the tested non‐GM reference varieties with respect to potential effects on human and animal health and the environment.

SUMMARY

Following the submission of application EFSA‐Q‐2025‐00294 under Regulation (EC) No 1829/2003 from Syngenta Crop Protection AG (referred to hereafter as ‘the applicant’), the Panel on Genetically Modified Organisms of the European Food Safety Authority (referred to hereafter as ‘GMO Panel’) was asked to deliver a Scientific Opinion on the safety of genetically modified (GM) herbicide tolerant soybean (Glycine max) DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 (Unique Identifier DAS‐444Ø6‐6 × MST‐FGØ72‐2) (generally referred to hereafter as ‘two‐event stack soybean’), according to Regulation (EU) No 503/2013. The scope of application EFSA‐Q‐2025‐00294 is for import, processing and food and feed uses within the European Union (EU) of soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 and does not include cultivation in the EU. The soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 was produced by crossing to combine two single soybean events: DAS‐44406‐6, expressing 2mEPSPS, AAD‐12, and PAT proteins to confer tolerance to glyphosate‐, 2,4‐D‐, and glufosinate‐containing herbicide products, respectively; FG72 expressing 2mEPSPS and HPPD W336 proteins, to confer tolerance to glyphosate‐containing and HPPD‐inhibiting herbicide products, respectively.

The GMO Panel evaluated the DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 stack soybean with reference to the scope and appropriate principles described in its applicable guidelines for the risk assessment of GM plants and the post‐market environmental monitoring (PMEM). The GMO Panel considered the information submitted in application EFSA‐Q‐2025‐00294/GMFF‐2025‐34192, additional information provided by the applicant during the risk assessment, the scientific comments submitted by the Member States and the relevant scientific literature. For application EFSA‐Q‐2025‐00294/GMFF‐2025‐34192, previous assessments of the two single events (DAS‐44406‐6 and FG72) provided a basis for the assessment of the soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72. No safety concerns were identified by the GMO Panel in the previous assessments. No safety issue concerning the two single soybean events was identified by the updated bioinformatics analyses, nor reported by the applicant since the publication of the previous GMO Panel scientific opinions. Therefore, the GMO Panel considers that its previous conclusions on the safety of the single soybean events remain valid.

For the soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72, the risk assessment included the molecular characterisation of the inserted DNA and analysis of protein expression. An evaluation of the comparative analysis of agronomic, phenotypic and compositional characteristics was carried out, and the safety of the newly expressed proteins and the whole food and feed were evaluated with respect to potential toxicity, allergenicity and nutritional characteristics. Environmental impacts and PMEM plan were also evaluated. The molecular characterisation data establish that the events DAS‐44406‐6 and FG72 combined in the DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 stack soybean have retained their integrity. Protein expression analysis showed that the levels of the newly expressed proteins are comparable in all tissues, except for 2mEPSPS protein levels that tend to be higher, which can be explained by the combination of events DAS‐44406‐06 and FG72 both producing 2mEPSPS in the two‐event stack soybean.

Considering the selection of test materials, the field trial sites and the associated management practices and the agronomic–phenotypic characterisation as an indicator of the overall field trial quality, the GMO Panel concludes that the field trials are appropriate to support the comparative analysis. The comparative analysis of agronomic and phenotypic characteristics and seeds and forage composition identified no differences between soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 and the non‐GM comparator (referred to hereafter as comparator) that required further assessment except for the changes in seed weight, and for myristic acid (C14:0), palmitoleic acid (C16:1), heptadecanoic acid (C17:0), total isoflavones in seeds and fat in forage. These changes underwent additional evaluation and were found to not raise any environmental impact and safety or nutritional concerns. The molecular characterisation, the comparative analysis and the outcome of the toxicological, allergenicity and nutritional assessment indicate that the combination of the single soybean events and of the newly expressed proteins in the DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 stack soybean does not give rise to food and feed safety and nutritional concerns. The GMO Panel concludes that soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 is as safe as the comparator and the selected commercial non‐GM reference varieties (referred to hereafter as non‐GM reference varieties). Considering the combined events and their potential interactions, the outcome of the comparative analysis and the routes and levels of exposure, the GMO Panel concludes that soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 would not raise safety concerns in the case of release of GM soybean material, including viable seeds, into the environment.

Given the absence of safety concerns for foods and feeds from soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72, the GMO Panel considers that post‐market monitoring of these products is not necessary. The PMEM plan and reporting intervals are in line with the intended uses of the DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 stack.

The literature searches did not identify any relevant publications on soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72; therefore, the GMO Panel does not identify any safety issue pertaining to the intended uses of two‐event stack soybean.

The GMO Panel concludes that soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72, as described in this application, is as safe as the comparator and the selected non‐GM reference varieties with respect to potential effects on human and animal health and the environment.

INTRODUCTION

The scope of the dossier GMFF‐2025‐34192 is for food and feed uses, import and processing of the genetically modified (GM) herbicide tolerant soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 and does not include cultivation in the European Union (EU).

Background and Terms of Reference as provided by the requestor

On 6 May 2025, the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) received from the Competent Authority of Spain dossier GMFF‐2025‐34192 for authorisation of soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 (Unique Identifier DAS‐444Ø6‐6 × MST‐FGØ72‐2), submitted by Syngenta Crop Protection AG (hereafter referred to as ‘the applicant’) according to Regulation (EC) No 1829/2003. 1

EFSA checked the application for compliance with the relevant requirements of Regulation (EC) No 1829/2003 and Regulation (EU) No 503/2013, 2 with the EFSA guidance documents. On 01/09/2025, EFSA declared the application valid and informed EU Member States (MS), the European Commission (EC) and the dossier was published at https://open.efsa.europa.eu/study‐inventory/EFSA‐Q‐2025‐00294.

From validity date, EFSA and the Panel on Genetically Modified Organisms of the European Food Safety Authority (referred to hereafter as ‘GMO Panel’) endeavoured to respect a time limit of 6 months to issue a scientific opinion on dossier GMFF‐2025‐34192. Such time limit was extended whenever EFSA and/or GMO Panel requested supplementary information to the applicant. The EU Member States had 3 months to make their opinion known on dossier GMFF‐2025‐34192.

According to Articles 6 and 18 of Regulation (EC) No 1829/2003, EFSA and its GMO Panel were requested to carry out a scientific risk assessment of soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 in the context of its scope as defined in dossier GMFF‐2025‐34192.

According to Regulation (EC) No 1829/2003, this scientific opinion is to be seen as the report requested under Articles 6(6) and 18(6) of that Regulation. In addition to the present scientific opinion, EFSA was also asked to report on the particulars listed under Articles 6(5) and 18(5) of Regulation (EC) No 1829/2003, but not to give an opinion on them because they pertain to risk management. 3

The GMO Panel conducted its assessment in line with the principles described in Regulation (EU) No 503/2013, the applicable guidelines (i.e. EFSA GMO Panel, 2010a, 2011a, 2011b, 2015a; EFSA Scientific Committee, 2011) and explanatory notes and statements (i.e. EFSA, 2010, 2014, 2017, 2018, 2019a, 2019b; EFSA GMO Panel, 2010b) for the risk assessment of GM plants.

ASSESSMENT 6

Introduction

Application GMFF‐2025‐34192 covers the two‐event stack soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72. The two‐event stack soybean was produced by crossing to combine two single soybean events: DAS‐44406‐6, expressing 2mEPSPS, AAD‐12 and PAT to confer tolerance to glyphosate‐, 2,4‐D‐ and glufosinate‐containing herbicides, respectively, and FG72 expressing 2mEPSPS and HPPD W336 to confer tolerance to glyphosate‐containing and HPPD‐inhibiting herbicides, respectively.

The two single events were assessed previously (see Table 1) and no safety concerns for human and animal health or environmental safety were identified.

Updated information on single events

Since publication of the scientific opinions on the single soybean events by the GMO Panel (see Table 1), no safety issue concerning the two single events has been reported by the applicant.

Updated bioinformatics analyses of the junction regions for soybean events DAS‐44406‐6 and FG72, using up‐to‐date sequence databases and methodology specified in EFSA guidance (EFSA GMO Panel, 2011a), confirmed that no known endogenous genes were disrupted by any of the inserts.

Updated bioinformatics analyses of the amino acid sequence of the newly expressed AAD‐12, 2mEPSPS, PAT and HPPD W336 proteins confirmed previous results indicating no significant similarities to known toxins and allergens. Updated bioinformatics analyses of the newly created open reading frames (ORFs) within the inserts or spanning the junctions between the insert and the flanking regions for events DAS‐44406‐6 and FG72 did not indicate significant similarities to toxins and allergens, which confirmed previous analyses (Table 1).

In order to update the bioinformatics analyses to assess the possibility for horizontal gene transfer (HGT) by homologous recombination (HR), the applicant performed a sequence identity analysis with microbial DNA for soybean events DAS‐44406‐6 and FG72. The likelihood and potential consequences of plant‐to‐bacteria gene transfer are described in Section 3.4.4.2.

Based on the above information, the GMO Panel considers that its previous conclusions on the safety of the single soybean events remain valid.

Systematic literature review

The GMO Panel assessed the applicant's literature searches on soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72, which include a scoping review, according to the guidelines given in EFSA (EFSA, 2010, 2019b).

A systematic review as referred to in Regulation (EU) No 503/2013 has not been provided in support to the risk assessment of application GMFF‐2025‐34192. Based on the outcome of the scoping review, the GMO Panel agrees that there is limited value in undertaking a systematic review for soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 at present.

The GMO Panel considered the overall quality of the performed literature searches acceptable. The literature searches did not identify any relevant publications on soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72; therefore, the GMO Panel does not identify any safety issue pertaining to the intended uses of two‐event stack soybean.

Integrity of the events in the DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 stack

The genetic stability of the inserted DNA over multiple generations in the single soybean events DAS‐44406‐6 and FG72 was demonstrated previously (Table 1 and Section 3.1). The integrity of these events in soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 was demonstrated by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) followed by sequence analysis demonstrating that the sequences of the events (inserts and their flanking regions) in the soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 stack are identical to the sequences already assessed (Table 2 and Section 3.2), thus confirming that the integrity of these events was maintained in the soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72.

Information on the expression of the insert

In order to assess the changes in protein expression levels which may result from potential interactions between the events, protein levels were determined for the DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 stack and the corresponding single events in different parts of the plant.

Protein levels of 2mEPSPS, AAD‐12, PAT and HPPD W336 were analysed by an enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) in material harvested in a field trial across three locations in the United States during the 2023 growing season. Samples analysed included leaves (BBCH 15, BBCH 19, BBCH 77), flower (BBCH 65), forage (BBCH 71‐75), root (BBCH 77) and seed (BBCH 89) from plants not treated with the intended herbicide.

The levels of all the newly expressed proteins in the soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 and the corresponding singles were comparable in all tissues, except for 2mEPSPS protein levels that tend to be higher, which can be explained by the combination of events DAS‐44406‐06 and FG72 both producing 2mEPSPS in the soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 (Table 4). Therefore, there is no indication of an interaction that may affect the levels of the newly expressed proteins in this stack.

Conclusion on molecular characterisation

The molecular data establish that the events stacked in soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 have retained their integrity. The genetic elements and known biological function of the newly expressed proteins do not indicate potential interactions between the events. Protein expression analyses showed that the levels of the newly expressed proteins are similar in the two‐event stack and in the single events, except for 2mEPSPS. The levels of 2mEPSPS tend to be higher in the DAS‐44406–6 × FG72 stack, which could be explained by the combination of two 2mEPSPS gene cassettes, one each in the DAS‐44406‐6 and FG72 in the stack. Therefore, there is no indication of an interaction that may affect the integrity of the events and the levels of the newly expressed proteins in this stack.

Experimental field trial design and statistical analysis

At each field trial site, the following materials were grown in a randomised complete block design with four replicates: the soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 not exposed to the intended herbicides (not treated), the soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 exposed to the intended herbicides (treated), the comparator 6464006‐09 and three non‐GM reference varieties. The agronomic, phenotypic and compositional data were analysed as specified by EFSA GMO Panel (EFSA GMO Panel, 2010b, 2011a). This includes, for each of the two treatments of the soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72, the application of a difference test (between the GM soybean and the non‐GM comparator) and an equivalence test (between the GM soybean and the set of non‐GM reference varieties). The results of the equivalence test are categorised into four possible outcomes (I–IV, ranging from equivalence to non‐equivalence). 9

Suitability of selected test materials

Selection of the test materials

To obtain the soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72, the single events DAS‐44406‐6 and FG72 were introgressed in the 6464006‐09 genetic background by a backcrossing series. This line was used in the comparative analysis. The comparator used in the field trials is the non‐GM soybean variety 6464006‐09, which has a genetic background similar to the two‐event stack soybean (as documented by the pedigree) and is considered to be a suitable comparator.

The two‐event stack soybean and the comparator, both with maturity group 3, are considered appropriate for growing in environments across the US, where the comparative field trials were conducted.

Commercial non‐GM reference varieties with a maturity group ranging from 2.8 to 3.6 were selected by the applicant (see Table 5). Based on the information provided on the maturity group, the GMO Panel considers the selected non‐GM reference varieties appropriate for the comparative assessment.

Seed production and quality

Seeds of soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 and the comparator used in the 2023 field trials were produced from plants free of diseases, harvested and stored under similar conditions, before being sown in the field trial sites. The seed lots were verified for their identity via event specific quantitative polymerase chain reaction analysis.

The seeds were tested for their germination capacity under warm conditions. 10 Germination capacity of the soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 was compared with the one of its comparator and the results 11 of these studies indicate that the seed germination of soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 was not different than that of its comparator.

Conclusion on suitability

The GMO Panel concludes that the soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72, the comparator and the non‐GM reference varieties are of adequate quality. Therefore, the test materials are considered appropriate for the comparative analysis.

Representativeness of the receiving environments

Selection of field trial sites

The selected field trials sites were located in commercial soybean cultivation regions of the United States. The soil and climatic characteristics of the selected fields 12 correspond to optimal, near‐optimal and sub‐optimal conditions for soybean growth (Sys et al., 1993).

The GMO Panel considers that the selected sites, including the subset chosen for the compositional analysis, reflect commercial soybean regions in which the test materials are likely to be grown.

Meteorological conditions

Maximum and minimum mean temperatures and sums of precipitation were provided for each site on a weekly basis. Some exceptional weather conditions were reported at four of the selected sites. 13 However, due to the lack of major impacts on plant growth at these sites, the GMO Panel considers that the exceptional weather conditions did not invalidate the use of data from the field trial sites for the comparative analysis.

Management practices

The field trial sites included plots containing the soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72, plots with the comparator and plots with non‐GM reference varieties, managed according to local agricultural practices. In addition, the field trials included plots with the soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 managed following the same agricultural practices and also exposed to intended herbicides: mesotrione, 2,4‐D, glufosinate and glyphosate. 14

The GMO Panel considers that the management practices, including sowing, harvesting and application of plant protection products, were appropriate for the selected receiving environments.

Conclusion on representativeness

The GMO Panel concludes that the geographical locations, soil and climatic characteristics, meteorological conditions and management practices of the field trial sites are typical for receiving environments where the tested materials could be grown.

Food and feed safety assessment

The two single events included in this stack application have been previously assessed by the EFSA GMO Panel and no safety concerns were identified (Table 1). The soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 will undergo existing production processes used for conventional soybean. No novel production process is envisaged. Based on the outcome of the comparative assessment, processing of the soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 into food and feed products is not expected to result in products being different from those of conventional non‐GM soybean varieties currently in the EU market. 21

In accordance with Regulation (EU) No 503/2013 for the risk assessment of genetically modified food or feed containing stacked events, the GMO Panel evaluated potential interactions resulting from the combination of transformation events impacting on toxicity (Section 3.4.3.1), allergenicity (Section 3.4.3.2) and nutritional assessment (Section 3.4.3.4) described below.

Based on the outcome of the studies considered in the comparative analysis and molecular characterisation, no altered levels of food and feed constituents have been identified in seeds and forage from the soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 except for myristic acid (C14:0), palmitoleic acid (C16:1), heptadecanoic acid (C17:0), total isoflavones in seeds and fat in forage. Testing of the whole genetically modified food and feed

The GMO Panel considers animal studies with food and feed derived from the soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 unnecessary based on the outcome of the molecular characterisation, comparative analysis and toxicological assessment (EFSA GMO Panel, 2011a). There were no concerns regarding the stability and expression of the inserts or interaction between the transformation events, and no changes of toxicological concern in the composition of the two‐event stack soybean were identified (Sections 3.4.1, 3.4.2.6, 3.4.3.1).

In accordance with Regulation (EU) No 503/2013, the applicant provided a 90‐day feeding study in rodents on whole food and feed from each of the soybean single events composing this two‐event stack soybean. In particular, a 90‐day study with soybean DAS‐44406‐6 was previously submitted by the applicant in the context of application EFSA‐GMO‐NL‐2016‐132. The GMO Panel has previously concluded that the study complied with Regulation (EU) No 503/2013 and did not show any adverse effects related to diets incorporating soybean DAS‐44406‐6 (EFSA GMO Panel, 2020).

On the other hand, a 90‐day study with soybean FG72 has been previously assessed by the GMO Panel in the context of the single‐event application (EFSA GMO Panel, 2015b). While it did not raise any safety concerns, it did not meet the requirements of Regulation (EU) No 503/2013. To address this, the applicant spontaneously submitted a new 90‐day feeding study in rats fed with a diet containing meal (toasted, defatted and dehulled) derived from soybean FG72.

This study was also submitted and assessed by the GMO Panel in the context of the renewal of soybean FG72 (EFSA GMO Panel, 2026) concluding that this study is in line with the requirements of Regulation (EU) No 503/2013 and that no treatment‐related adverse effects were observed in rats after feeding diets containing soybean FG72 meals at 30% for 90 days.

Allergenicity 23

The strategies to assess the potential risk of allergenicity focus: (i) on the source of the recombinant protein; (ii) on the potential of the NEP to induce sensitisation or to elicit allergic reactions in already sensitised persons; and (iii) on whether the transformation may have altered the allergenic properties of the modified plant. Furthermore, the assessment also takes into account potential adjuvant properties of the NEPs, which is defined as the ability to enhance an allergic reaction.

Assessment of allergenicity of the newly expressed proteins

The GMO Panel has previously evaluated the safety of AAD‐12, 2mEPSPS, PAT and HPPD W336 proteins individually, and no evidence of allergenicity was identified in the context of the applications assessed (Table 1). No new information on allergenicity of the NEPs in soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 that might change the previous conclusions of the GMO Panel has become available. Based on the current knowledge, and as there is no evidence of allergenicity of the NEPs, there are no expected concerns of allergenicity as a consequence of their simultaneous presence in soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72.

The GMO Panel has previously evaluated the safety of the NEPs, and no evidence of adjuvanticity was identified in the context of the applications assessed (Table 1).

The applicant also provided information on the safety of the AAD‐12, 2mEPSPS, PAT and HPPD W336 proteins regarding their potential to cause a coeliac disease response. For such assessment, the applicant followed the principles described in the EFSA GMO Panel guidance document (EFSA GMO Panel, 2017b). No indications of safety concern were identified by the GMO Panel as the relevant peptides containing the motif fail to mimic gluten sequences.

The GMO Panel considers that there are no indications that the newly expressed AAD‐12, 2mEPSPS, PAT and/or HPPD W336 proteins in this two‐event stack soybean may be allergenic.

Assessment of allergenicity of the whole GM plant or soybean

Soybean is considered a common allergenic food (OECD, 2012). Therefore, any potential change in the endogenous allergenicity of the GM plant should be assessed (Regulation (EU) No 503/2013). The objective of this assessment is to determine whether the genetic modification could lead to biologically relevant changes in the allergenic potential of soybean. For this assessment, the applicant included in the comparative analysis specific allergens relevant for soybean (Section 3.4.2.6) quantified using liquid chromatography with tandem MS, which has been previously considered acceptable (EFSA GMO Panel, 2010c, 2017b; Fernandez et al., 2013; Selb et al., 2017). These allergens were selected based on the list of potential soybean allergens described in relevant documents (OECD, 2012), and the scientific rationale for their selection, as provided by the applicant, was considered acceptable by the GMO Panel. No changes in the levels of endogenous allergens raising concern are identified by the GMO Panel.

In the context of this application, the GMO Panel considers that there is no evidence that the genetic modification would change the overall allergenicity of the soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 when compared with that of the comparator and the non‐GM reference varieties tested.

Human dietary exposure

Chronic and acute estimations of dietary exposure to 2mEPSPS, AAD‐12, HPPD W336 and PAT proteins newly expressed in soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 were provided. The applicant followed the methodology described in the EFSA Statement ‘Human dietary exposure assessment to newly expressed protein in GM foods’ to anticipate human dietary exposure, making use of summary statistics of consumption (EFSA, 2019a).

Human dietary exposure was estimated across European countries on different population groups: young population (infants, toddlers, ‘other children’), adolescents, adult population (adults, elderly and very elderly) and special populations (pregnant and lactating women). Since no specific consumption data were available on commodities containing, consisting of or obtained from soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 seeds, a conservative scenario with 100% replacement of conventional soybean by the GM soybean was considered. Consumption figures for all relevant commodities (e.g. soya bread, textured soy protein, soya drink, tofu, etc.) were retrieved from the EFSA Comprehensive European Food Consumption Database. 26

Mean protein expression values on a fresh weight basis are considered as the most adequate to estimate human dietary exposure (both acute and chronic) when working with raw primary commodities that are commonly consumed as processed blended commodities (EFSA, 2019a). Different recipes and factors were considered to estimate the amount of soybean seeds in the consumed commodities before assigning newly expressed protein levels to the relevant commodities. 27 No losses in the newly expressed proteins during processing were considered.

The highest anticipated acute dietary exposure (highly exposed population) was in the age class ‘Infants’ with estimates up to 613 μg/kg bw per day for AAD‐12, 151 μg/kg bw per day for HPPD W336, 89.4 μg/kg bw per day for 2mEPSPS and 30.5 μg/kg bw per day for PAT. The main contributor to the exposure in the dietary survey with the highest estimates would be follow‐on formula, soya‐based, powder. In the dietary exposure scenario anticipating acute consumption of soybean‐derived protein supplements, the highest estimates would range between 38.3 μg/kg bw per day for PAT and 769 μg/kg bw per day for AAD‐12 proteins (in the adult population).

The highest anticipated chronic dietary exposure (highly exposed population) was in the age class ‘Infants’ with estimates up to 207 μg/kg bw per day for AAD‐12, 50.9 μg/kg bw per day for HPPD W336, 30.2 μg/kg bw per day for 2mEPSPS and 10.3 μg/kg bw per day for PAT. The main contributor to the exposure in the dietary survey with the highest estimates would be follow‐on formula, soya‐based, powder. In the dietary exposure scenario anticipating chronic consumption of soybean‐derived protein supplements, the highest estimates would range between 24.9 μg/kg bw per day for PAT and 500 μg/kg bw per day for AAD‐12 proteins (in the adult population).

The GMO Panel considers pollen supplements as a possible contributor to the dietary exposure to 2mEPSPS, AAD‐12, HPPD W336 and PAT, under the assumption that these supplements might be made of pollen from soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72. Due to the technical challenges related to the collection of pollen from soybean flowers (e.g. close flowers, low amounts produced), the applicant analysed the presence of the newly expressed proteins in flowers as pollen surrogates (see Table 1). Detailed information was provided on how the flowers were collected and the parts included (sepals, petals, pistils and stamens). 28 The GMO Panel acknowledges the substantial uncertainty that the use of flowers as surrogates of pollen adds to the expression levels and, therefore, to the dietary exposure assessment. However, in the absence of identified hazards for the newly expressed proteins, these expression levels are accepted as surrogates.

Consumption data on pollen supplements are available for few consumers across seven different European countries. The low number of consumers available adds uncertainty to the exposure estimations which should be interpreted with care, and only allows the estimation of dietary exposure for average consumers. The highest mean acute dietary exposure would be between 4.77 μg/kg bw per day for PAT and 118 μg/kg bw per day for 2mEPSPS proteins, in the elderly population. Similarly, the highest mean chronic dietary exposure in consumers of pollen supplements would be between 3.2 μg/kg bw per day for PAT and 78.5 μg/kg bw per day for 2mEPSPS proteins, also in the elderly population.

Animal dietary exposure

Anticipated dietary exposure to AAD‐12, 2mEPSPS, PAT and HPPD W336 proteins in soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 was estimated by the applicant across farmed and companion animals, assuming the consumption of soybean products commonly entering the feed supply chain, as below described.

Estimations were based on the recommendations provided in EFSA GMO Panel (2023).

A conservative scenario with 100% replacement of conventional soybean products by soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 products was considered.

Mean levels (dry weight) of the newly expressed proteins in full‐fat seeds and forage/silage from soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 treated with the intended herbicide used for animal dietary exposure are listed in Table 9.

Mean levels (dry weight) of the newly expressed proteins in meal, hull and protein concentrate were calculated to be, respectively, 1.32, 0.34 and 2.07‐fold higher than in seeds, based on conversion factors that take into account the protein content in these feed materials relative to soybean seeds and assuming that no protein is lost during their production or processing. Values are reported in Tables A.1, A.2, A.3, A.4, A.5, A.6, A.7 of Appendix A.

The applicant estimated dietary exposure in ruminants (dairy cow, beef cattle, dairy sheep and dairy goat) and other herbivorous animals (rabbit and horse), in pigs (fattening pig, lactating sow, piglet), in poultry (broiler, laying hens, turkey), in fish (salmon, carp) and in companion animals (cat and dog).

The dietary exposure was estimated via the consumption of soybean forage/silage, full‐fat seeds, meal, hulls and protein concentrate in standard diets or rations.

Estimated dietary exposure to AAD‐12, 2mEPSPS, PAT and HPPD W336 proteins in the concerned animals is reported in Appendix A.

Nutritional assessment of endogenous constituents 29

The intended traits of soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 are to confer tolerance to glyphosate, 2,4‐D, glufosinate‐based and HPPD‐inhibiting herbicides, with no intention to alter nutritional parameters. However, levels of myristic acid (C14:0), palmitoleic acid (C16:1), heptadecanoic acid (C17:0), total isoflavones in seeds and fat in forage, were significantly different from its non‐GM comparator and showed a lack of equivalence or could not be categorised with respect to the set of non‐GM reference varieties (Section 3.4.2.6). The biological relevance of these compounds, the role of the soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 as contributor to their total intake and the magnitude and direction of the observed changes were considered during the nutritional assessment.

Human nutrition

A small increase between 2% and 6% of myristic acid (C14:0) and heptadecanoic acid (C17:0) in treated and not treated soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 was reported compared to its comparator. Current nutritional recommendations for saturated fatty acids indicate that their intake should be as low as possible within the context of a nutritionally adequate diet (EFSA NDA Panel, 2010). Considering the very low levels of these two fatty acids (< 1% total FA each) in soybean, the GMO Panel concludes that their levels in the soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 do not represent a nutritional concern in humans.

A decrease of ~8% of palmitoleic acid (C16:1) both in treated and not treated soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 was reported compared to its comparator. No dietary reference values (DRVs) for cis‐monounsaturated fatty acids are proposed by EFSA since they are synthesised by the body (EFSA NDA Panel, 2010). Considering this information, the very low levels of the fatty acid in soybean, and the fact that many other foods are also a source of palmitoleic acid (C16:1) in the diet, the GMO Panel concludes that the observed decrease does not raise nutritional concerns.

Total isoflavones were reported to be ~8% higher in the treated soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 compared to its comparator. However, the total isoflavones were calculated summing up the individual isoflavones analysed (daidzein, glycitein, genistein) which fell under equivalence categories I or II (see Section 3.4.2.6). Isoflavones show lower estrogenic potencies, as compared to the physiological oestrogen beta‐oestradiol. In addition, oral bioavailability of isoflavones in mammalian species is limited (Messina et al., 2022). Taken together, this indicates that the ingestion of the isoflavones contained in food from soybean does not pose a risk of endocrine disruption in humans. Therefore, the small increase in total isoflavones observed in the two‐event soybean stack does not raise nutritional concerns for humans.

Animal nutrition

A small increase in myristic acid (C14:0) and heptadecanoic acid (C17:0) (ranging from approximately 2% to 6%) was observed in both treated and untreated soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 compared with its conventional comparator. Saturated fatty acids (SFA) are not essential nutrients; they are included in diets as part of total fat, and animals are also capable of synthesising them de novo from carbohydrates. Nutritional recommendations for SFA in farmed and companion animals are less prescriptive than in human nutrition. In most cases, no minimum or maximum dietary requirements have been established for SFA. Instead, they focus on total fat content, essential fatty acids (EFA) and the overall fatty acid profile. From a nutritional standpoint, species differ in their tolerance to SFA. In aquaculture, partial replacement of fish oil with plant or animal fats can increase SFA levels, requiring careful balancing to maintain adequate fatty acid balance. Given the very low levels of these two fatty acids in soybean (each representing < 1% of total fatty acids), and considering that animal diets are typically formulated to be nutritionally balanced, the GMO Panel considers that their presence in the soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 does not raise nutritional concerns for animals.

A decrease of approximately 8% in palmitoleic acid (C16:1) was observed in both treated and untreated soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 compared with its conventional comparator. Palmitoleic acid (C16:1) is a monounsaturated fatty acid (MUFA) present in both plant and animal lipids, but generally at low levels in most common feed crops (e.g. maize and soybean). It is not an essential fatty acid, and animals are capable of synthesising it endogenously from palmitic acid (C16:0) via desaturation; therefore, it is not required in the diet. It is typically present at low levels in feed ingredients and contributes to the overall fatty acid profile rather than to specific nutritional requirements. In most cases, no minimum or maximum dietary requirements have been established for MUFA. In aquaculture species, it contributes to energy supply and membrane lipid composition but is not considered a target nutrient such as EPA and DHA. Given the low levels of this fatty acid in soybean, and considering that animal diets are typically formulated to be nutritionally balanced, the GMO Panel considers that this difference does not raise nutritional concerns for animals.

Total isoflavones were reported to be approximately 8% higher in the treated soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 than in its conventional comparator. Isoflavones are naturally occurring plant secondary metabolites with weak oestrogenic activity (see also Section 3.4.3.4.1) and are common constituents of leguminous feed materials, particularly soybean. Isoflavones naturally occur in soybean meal and other feed ingredients used in compound feeds and are generally present at levels that do not cause adverse effects under standard feeding practices (Deng & Kim, 2024; Grgic et al., 2021; McDonald et al., 2011). Although a small but statistically significant increase in total isoflavones was observed, no statistically significant differences were identified for individual isoflavones. Overall, the isoflavones present in feed derived from soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 do not raise nutritional concerns for farmed or companion animal species.

A decrease of approximately 16% in total fat was observed in treated forage of the soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 compared with its conventional comparator. Considering the inherent variability in forage composition, the limited nutritional role of fat in ruminant diets, and the fact that diets are formulated at the ration level to ensure overall nutritional balance, this difference is not considered to raise nutritional concerns for animals.

Conclusions on the food and feed safety assessment

The NEPs AAD‐12, 2mEPSPS, PAT and HPPD W336 in the soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 do not raise safety concerns for human and animal health. No interactions between NEPs relevant for food and feed safety were identified, and no overall toxicological concerns on the soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 were identified. Similarly, the GMO Panel did not identify indications of safety concerns regarding allergenicity or adjuvanticity related to the presence of the NEPs in this soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72, or regarding its overall allergenicity. Based on the outcome of the comparative assessment and the nutritional assessment, the GMO Panel concludes that the consumption of soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 does not represent any nutritional concern, in the context of the scope of this application.

The GMO Panel concludes that the soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72, as described in this application, is as safe as the comparator and the non‐GM reference varieties tested and no post‐market monitoring of food and feed is considered necessary.

Environmental risk assessment 30

Considering the scope of this application, which excludes cultivation, the environmental risk assessment (ERA) mainly takes into account: (1) the exposure of microorganisms to recombinant DNA in the gastrointestinal tract of animals fed with GM material and of microorganisms present in environments exposed to manure and faeces of these animals; and (2) the deliberate or accidental release into the environment of GM material, including spillage of viable soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 seeds during transportation or processing (EFSA GMO Panel, 2010a).

Persistence and invasiveness of the GM plant

Cultivated soybean (Glycine max (L.) Merr.) is a species in the subgenus Soja of the genus Glycine. The species originated from eastern Asia and is a highly domesticated soybean, generally unable to survive in the environment without proper management (Lu, 2005).

Occasional feral GM soybean plants may occur outside cultivation areas, but survival is limited mainly by a combination of low competitiveness, absence of a dormancy phase and susceptibility to plant pathogens (OECD, 2000). Additionally, soybean is a sub‐tropical species susceptible to cold climatic conditions (Bramlage et al., 1978; Staniak et al., 2021; Szczerba et al., 2021; Tyagi & Tripathi, 1983), although cold tolerance varies across maturity groups and among cultivars (Alsajri et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2023). Soybean can grow as volunteers and the presence of volunteers of G. max was occasionally reported in some areas of Italy where soybean is intensively cultivated (Celesti‐Grapow et al., 2010). However, as for the same reasons mentioned above, soybean seeds usually do not survive during cold winters (Matsushita et al., 2020; Owen, 2005), and any soybean volunteers can be effectively controlled by mechanical methods or appropriate chemical control (Bond & Walker, 2009, Jhala et al., 2013; Soltani et al., 2019). Owing to this, soybean plants are often not considered a problematic volunteer in temperate climates (Jhala et al., 2021). Thus, the establishment and survival of feral and volunteer soybean in the EU is currently limited and transient.

The intended traits of soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 would provide a selective advantage to soybean plants when they are exposed to 2,4‐D‐, glyphosate‐, HPPD‐inhibiting or glufosinate‐containing herbicides.

The GMO Panel considers that neither the fitness advantage provided by the intended traits, nor the observed differences in seed weight (see Section 3.4.2.5) will allow the GM plant to overcome other biological and abiotic factors (described above) limiting the plant's persistence and invasiveness. Therefore, the presence of the intended traits and other observed differences will not affect the persistence and invasiveness of the GM plant.

In conclusion, the GMO Panel considers that it is very unlikely that the soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 will differ from conventional soybean varieties in their ability to survive until subsequent seasons, or to establish occasional feral plants under European environmental conditions in case of accidental release into the environment of viable soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 seeds.

Potential for gene transfer

A prerequisite for any gene transfer is the availability of pathways for the transfer of genetic material, either through horizontal gene transfer (HGT) of DNA or through vertical gene flow via cross‐pollination from feral plants originating from spilled seeds.

Plant‐to‐microorganism gene transfer

The probability and potential adverse effects of HGT of the recombinant DNA have been assessed in previous GMO Panel Scientific Opinions for the single events (see Table 1). This assessment included consideration of homology‐based recombination processes, as well as non‐homologous end joining and microhomology‐mediated end joining. Possible fitness advantages that the bacteria in the receiving environments would gain from acquiring recombinant DNA were considered. No concern was identified as a result of an unlikely, but theoretically possible, HGT of the recombinant genes to bacteria in the gut of domesticated animals and humans fed GM material or other receiving environments, including those exposed to seed spillage or faecal material of animals fed with GM material.

The applicant submitted an updated bioinformatics analysis for each of the single events to assess the possibility for HGT by homologous recombination. This information confirms the assessments provided in the context of previous scientific opinions of the single events (EFSA GMO Panel, 2017a, 2026).

Synergistic effects of the recombinant genes, for instance due to combinations of recombinogenic sequences, which would cause an increase in the likelihood for HGT or a selective advantage were not identified

Therefore, the GMO Panel concludes that the unlikely, but theoretically possible, horizontal transfer of recombinant genes from this soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 to bacteria does not raise any environmental safety concern.

Plant‐to‐plant gene transfer

The potential for occasional feral soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 plants originating from seed import spills to transfer recombinant DNA to sexually compatible plants and the environmental consequences of this transfer were considered.

For plant‐to‐plant gene transfer to occur, imported GM soybean seeds need to germinate and develop into plants in areas containing sympatric wild relatives or cultivated soybean with synchronous flowering and environmental conditions favouring cross‐pollination. It must be noted that most soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 seeds are processed in the countries of production or in ports of importation.

Vertical gene transfer from soybean (G. max) is limited to the species of the subgenus Soja to which G. max belongs to, as well as the wild relatives G. soja and G. gracilis (Zhang et al., 2023). Although wild relatives exist elsewhere, no wild relatives of the subgenus Soja have been reported in Europe so far (Dorokhov et al., 2004; Lu, 2005). Therefore, vertical gene transfer from GM soybean is restricted to cultivated soybean (G. max).

Soybean is an annual, almost completely self‐pollinating crop with a percentage of cross‐pollination usually below 1% (Abud et al., 2007; Lu, 2005; OECD, 2000; Ray et al., 2003; Yoshimura et al., 2006), although natural cross‐pollination rates can fluctuate significantly among different soybean varieties under particular environmental conditions, such as favourable climate for pollination and an abundance of pollinators (Ahrent & Caviness, 1994; Caviness, 1966; Gumisiriza & Rubaihayo, 1978; Kikuchi et al., 1993; Lu, 2005; Ray et al., 2003).

The potential of spilled soybean seeds to establish, grow and produce pollen is extremely low and transient (see Section 3.4.4.1). Therefore, the likelihood/frequency of cross‐pollination between occasional feral GM soybean plants resulting from seed spillage, and weedy or cultivated soybean plants is also extremely low. Even if cross‐pollination would occur, the GMO Panel is of the opinion that the likelihood of environmental effects as a consequence of the spread of genes from occasional feral GM soybean plants in Europe will not differ from that of conventional soybean varieties for the reasons given in Section 3.4.4.1.

In conclusion, the GMO Panel considers that the likelihood of environmental effects as a consequence of the spread of genes from soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 in Europe will not differ from that of conventional soybean varieties.

Interactions of the GM plant with target organisms

Taking the scope of application GMFF‐2025‐34192 (no cultivation) and the absence of target organisms into account, potential interactions of occasional feral soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 plants arising from seed import spills with target organisms are not considered a relevant issue.

Interactions of the GM plant with non‐target organisms

The environmental risk assessment considers potential effects of the GM plant on populations of non‐target organisms, defined as all those species directly or indirectly exposed to the GM plant and which are not targets of the newly expressed metabolite(s) it expresses. The GMO Panel evaluated the potential hazards of the NEPs and considered that the environmental exposure of non‐target organisms to spilled GM soybean material or occasional feral GM soybean plants arising from spilled soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 seeds will be limited. Additionally, ingested proteins are typically degraded before entering the environment through manure and faeces of animals fed with GM soybean (Miner‐Williams et al., 2014; Mok & Urschel, 2020; Santos‐Hernández et al., 2018; van Bruchem et al., 1985). The data provided for the assessment of protein stability in the context of other applications expressing the same NEPs (see Section 3.4.3.1.1) support that also the NEPs will be degraded. As compared to non‐GM modified soybean, the GMO Panel considers that potential interactions of soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 with non‐target organisms do not raise any safety concern.

Interactions with biogeochemical cycles

Biogeochemical cycles encompass the microbiologically mediated movement, transformation and storage of carbon, nitrogen and other compounds within the receiving environments. The GMO Panel evaluated the potential hazards of the NEPs and considered that the environmental exposure to spilled GM soybean material or occasional feral GM soybean plants arising from spilled soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 seeds will be limited, whereas exposure to manure and faeces of animals fed with soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 material is expected to be higher. However, ingested proteins are typically degraded before entering the environment through manure and faeces of animals fed with GM soybean (Miner‐Williams et al., 2014; Mok & Urschel, 2020; Santos‐Hernández et al., 2018; van Bruchem et al., 1985), and the data provided for the assessment of protein stability in the context of other applications expressing the same NEPs (see Section 3.4.3.1.1) support that the NEPs will also be degraded. As compared to non‐GM soybean, the GMO Panel considers that potential interactions of soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 with biogeochemical cycles do not raise any specific safety concern.

Conclusion of the environmental risk assessment

The GMO Panel concludes that it is unlikely that the two‐event stack soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 would differ from conventional soybean varieties in its ability to persist under European environmental conditions. Taking into account the scope of application GMFF‐2025‐34192, interactions of occasional feral soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 plants with the biotic and abiotic environment are not considered to be relevant issues. The analysis of HGT from the soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 to bacteria does not indicate a safety concern. Therefore, considering the introduced traits, the outcome of the agronomic and phenotypic analysis, and the routes and levels of exposure, the GMO Panel concludes that the soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 would not raise safety concerns in the event of release of processed GM soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 or the accidental spillage of viable GM soybean seeds into the environment.

Post‐market monitoring 31

Post‐market monitoring of GM food and feed

The GMO Panel concluded that the soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72, as described in this application, does not raise any nutritional concern and is as safe as the comparator and the non‐GM reference varieties tested (Section 3.4.3). Therefore, the GMO Panel considers that post‐market monitoring of food and feed from the soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72, as described in this application, is not necessary.

Post‐market environmental monitoring 32

The objectives of a post‐market environmental monitoring (PMEM) plan, according to Annex VII of Directive 2001/18/EC, are: (i) to confirm that any assumption regarding the occurrence and impact of potential adverse effects of the GMO, or its use, in the ERA are correct; and (ii) to identify the occurrence of adverse effects of the GMO, or its use, on human health or the environment that were not anticipated in the ERA.

Monitoring is related to risk management, and thus, a final adoption of the PMEM plan falls outside the mandate of EFSA. However, the GMO Panel gives its opinion on the scientific rationale of the PMEM plan provided by the applicant (EFSA GMO Panel, 2011b).

As the ERA did not identify potential adverse environmental effects from soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72, no case‐specific monitoring is required.

The PMEM plan proposed by the applicant for two‐event stack soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 includes: (1) the description of a monitoring approach involving operators (federations involved in import and processing), reporting to the applicant via a centralised system any observed adverse effect(s) of GMOs on human health and the environment; (2) a coordinating system established by CropLife Europe for the collection of information recorded by the various operators; and (3) the review of relevant scientific publications retrieved from literature searches (Lecoq et al., 2007; Windels et al., 2008). The applicant proposes to submit a PMEM report on an annual basis for the duration of the authorisation period.

The GMO Panel considers that the scope of the PMEM plan provided by the applicant is consistent with the intended uses of two‐event stack soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72. The GMO Panel agrees with the reporting intervals proposed by the applicant in its PMEM plan. The PMEM plan and reporting intervals are in line with the intended uses of the soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72.

Conclusions on post‐market monitoring

No post‐market monitoring of food and feed is necessary. The scope of the PMEM plan provided by the applicant and the reporting intervals are in line with the intended uses of the soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72.

OVERALL CONCLUSIONS

The GMO Panel was asked to carry out a scientific assessment of soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 for import, processing and food and feed uses in accordance with Regulation (EC) No 1829/2003.

No new information was identified on the two single soybean events (DAS‐44406‐6 and FG72) that would lead to a modification of the original conclusions on their safety.

The molecular characterisation, the comparative analysis (agronomic, phenotypic and compositional characteristics) and the outcome of the toxicological, allergenicity and nutritional assessment indicate that the combination of the single soybean events and of the newly expressed proteins in the soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 does not give rise to food and feed safety or nutritional concerns. There is no indication of an interaction between the events in the stack that may affect food and feed safety. The GMO Panel concludes that the soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72, as described in this application, does not raise any nutritional concern and is as safe as its comparator and the selected non‐GM reference varieties.

The GMO Panel concludes that additional environmental effects as compared to conventional soybean resulting from the release of soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72 into the environment are unlikely.

The literature searches did not identify any relevant publications on soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72; therefore, the GMO Panel does not identify any safety issue pertaining to the intended uses of soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72.

Given the absence of safety and nutritional concerns for foods and feeds from the soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72, the GMO Panel considers that Post Market Monitoring (PMM) of these products is not necessary. The PMEM plan and reporting intervals are in line with the intended uses of soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72.

In conclusion, the GMO Panel considers that soybean DAS‐44406‐6 × FG72, as described in this application, is as safe as the comparator and the selected non‐GM reference varieties with respect to potential effects on human and animal health and the environment.
