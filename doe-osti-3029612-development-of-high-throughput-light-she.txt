Abstract

This research program will enable new biochemical contrast in the nanosecond lifetime
domain through use of the recently demonstrated electro-optic fluorescence lifetime
imaging technique (EO-FLIM) for wide-field lifetime imaging. The Stanford/Stanford
Linear Accelerator Center multidisciplinary collaboration -- physics, applied physics,
and structural biology -- will develop a light-sheet fluorescence lifetime imaging
microscope for functional studies of microbial and plant metabolic pathways and
dynamic interactions between plants and microorganisms in the rhizosphere.

The proposed approach overcomes the imaging time bottleneck associated with
existing fluorescence lifetime imaging methods. Initial demonstrations have shown a
factor of 100,000 improvement in photon throughput compared to existing methods.
High photon efficiency allowed the first wide-field fluorescence lifetime imaging of single
molecules. Recent work has improved the technique’s repetition rate to enable
compatibility with mode-locked lasers and demonstrated the combination of wide-field
fluorescence lifetime imaging with super-resolution localization microscopy,
observations of single molecule dynamics, and observation of donor lifetime quenching
in single-molecule imaging. These results were achieved on standard camera sensors
and would not have been possible with other wide-field approaches.

The throughput and photon economy of the EO-FLIM method enables new BER-
relevant imaging opportunities. In particular, scanned single- and two-photon light-
sheet excitation will be used to achieve volumetric imaging with time-domain contrast.

Background
Fluorescent microscopy techniques achieve superb performance in the spatial domain,
capturing biological dynamics from thin samples at both high frame-rates and high
resolution. Despite this success, there remain significant obstacles to fluorescence
imaging at-depth in thick samples such as native tissue environments. Further, it is
difficult with current approaches to achieve high levels of chemical specificity that could
enable multiplexing of multiple fluorescent probes or the use of label-free endogenous
contrast for imaging. These problems are especially critical for studying biochemical
pathways like cellular metabolism and molecular interactions. New approaches are
required to make progress on these frontiers. Notably, recent developments in light-
sheet and multi-photon microscopy techniques have allowed for improved imaging at
depth in live cells and tissues for extended observations and with reduced phototoxicity.
However, there remain challenges to achieving improved chemical resolution and
specificity with these techniques.

 4

Fluorescent lifetime microscopy (FLIM) can help close these gaps. The most popular
method for FLIM relies on confocal laser scanning combined with time-correlated single
photon counting (TC-SPC) detectors. The throughput of TC-SPC is limited by the
detector’s maximum count rate, which is typically set by either electronic limitations or
photon pile-up (typically 1–10 MHz). Pile-up is a serious limitation for any photon-
counting based technique, as it dictates that low photon throughput must be used in
order to avoid saturation. As a result, acquiring a wide-field image can be very slow and
imaging fast biological FLIM dynamics is nearly impossible. Further, confocal
microscopy relies on high excitation intensities that can cause non-linear photo-damage
to biological samples. It is desirable to use less invasive methods such as wide-field
detection with light-sheet excitation in order to minimize phototoxicity while imaging.
Wide-field FLIM has been realized in several different modes, but all have significant
technical drawbacks. Gated optical intensifiers rely on low-efficiency photocathodes,
discard un-gated signal, introduce multiplicative noise, and are known to degrade image
quality. Emerging SPAD array technologies are capable of higher throughput than
single-channel TC-SPC. While promising, the technology is currently far from the
performance of standard sCMOS and EMCCD sensors for microscopy.
Given the above, it is not currently possible to perform effective FLIM imaging with the
limited photons provided by many biological samples. The EO-FLIM approach uses
electro-optic crystals to allow single frame FLIM acquisition at the camera framerate
with high photon efficiency. The EO-FLIM approach has many advantages. Notably it
captures all fluorescent photons on the image sensor up to optical losses, it does not
discard non-gated signal, and it is compatible with any scientific image sensor. Because
it collects all sample photons, the approach is self-normalizing for intensity fluctuations
and allows lifetime estimation from each single camera frame.
Combination of FLIM with light-sheet microscopy is a promising direction. Initial
demonstrations have been performed with single-photon counting detectors, gated
intensifiers, and two-tap modulated sensors. As discussed before, these systems have
fundamental limitations. For example, using TC-SPC the acquisition time for a single
slice exceeds 2 minutes, and even with high-noise multi-tap camera detection (vendor
PCO) a single volume acquisition takes 7 minutes which is orders of magnitude slower
than state-of-the art light-sheet fluorescence microscopy (LSFM) systems. At current
sensitivity and noise levels there is no practical path for FLIM-LSFM using standard
detectors. EO-FLIM allows for a practical FLIM + light-sheet microscopy combination.
For imaging at depth, two-photon excitation is desirable for its increased penetration
and for lower total phototoxicity compared to one photon excitation. Two-photon light-
sheet is one promising approach to further improve imaging depth and acquisition
speeds while lowering phototoxicity. By scanning the excitation beam orthogonal to the

detection, only a single plane of the sample is exposed. This technique has been
demonstrated in a variety of applications.

Objectives
Our overall program goal was to develop a high throughput wide-field single and two-
photon excitation light-sheet microscope based on the EO-FLIM method and to
demonstrate its efficacy in proof-of-concept imaging studies. In particular, we expected
this approach to yield unprecedented performance for volumetric FLIM imaging. In the
program performance period, we built three microscopes based on the EO-FLIM
concept in order to demonstrate record FLIM imaging performance. Our instrument
development path was to first demonstrate high throughput imaging in a conventional
two-dimensional wide field configuration, then follow this demonstration with a first
single-photon three-dimensional light-sheet configuration, and finally to realize the full
two-photon light-sheet FLIM imaging system. In each case, we focused on imaging
proof-of-concept image targets which showcased the unique capabilities of the
approach.

Summary of outcomes
We demonstrated state-of-the-art lifetime imaging capabilities in two-dimensional wide-
field and three-dimensional light-sheet imaging modalities.
In the two-dimensional imaging system, we showed that EO-FLIM could capture lifetime
images at unprecedented resolution and speed by imaging previously inaccessible
neuron samples. Neuron voltage imaging provided an excellent, difficult, benchmark
target, as successful imaging requires a difficult combination of temporal resolution (kHz
framerates) and lifetime resolution. This work was published in Science [Publication 1]..
We next extended this capability to three-dimensional imaging through implementation
of a light-sheet system. As our first step in light-sheet imaging, we modified a
commercial system with an EO-FLIM detector. With this system, we captured three
dimensional images of plant roots at unprecedented imaging speeds. This work was
published in Optics Express [Publication 2].
Finally, we have recently realized our program goal of demonstration of a two-photon,
light-sheet, EO-FLIM microscope. For this work, we built on knowledge acquired in the
one-photon commercial set-up to implement a custom light-sheet scanning assembly
compatible with deep field of view two-photon excitation. We confirmed that two-photon
excitation allows deeper sample penetration while enabling high throughput volumetric

 6

imaging. We did this through images of plant root mitochondria and of a beating
zebrafish heart. We showed that the system could effectively resolve multiple lifetime
channels in parallel by imaging plant roots tagged with multiple fluorescent proteins of
differing lifetimes. A manuscript describing this work is currently being prepared for
publication [Publication 3].

EO-FLIM light-sheet microscope
Having demonstrated high thoughput imaging in a conventional wide-field configuration,
we next developed a proof-of-concept light-sheet FLIM microscope. Our approach was
to modify an existing commercial microscope with an electro-optic detection module.
Our rationale was to demonstrate that EO-FLIM capability could be integrated in a
straightforward way into commercial systems. This system would also provide a
benchmark for our second generation two-photon system which we built in-house.

Two-photon light-sheet EO-FLIM

The combination of two-photon Bessel light-sheet microscopy with EO-FLIM detection
can enable rapid, volumetric acquisition of FLIM data with minimal phototoxicity, making
it ideal for live and deep tissue imaging. This integration allows dynamic mapping of
biochemical states across large 3D volumes, broad applications in developmental
biology, neurophysiology, and metabolic profiling with high spatial and temporal
resolution. Here, we have developed an electro-optics-based, two-photon Bessel light-
sheet fluorescence lifetime microscopy (TPLS-EO-FLIM), a tool for examining
specimens with deep penetration depth, large field of view, high resolution, and reduced
phototoxicity, along with minimal scattering and the ability to extract information related
to the local environment of fluorescent molecules through lifetime measurements. The
work described is currently being prepared for publication [Publication 3]..

We first validated this technique using non-biological reference standards—fluorescent
beads and a fluorescent calibration slide—to perform instrument calibration and to
quantitatively compare the fluorescence intensity measurements with FLIM. To
demonstrate biological applications, we imaged the FLIM volume of a live zebrafish
embryo heart and recorded its heartbeat at 50 frames per second with spatial and
temporal lifetime information. The cardiac dynamical imaging led us to estimate the
various biophysical parameters such as heart rate, ventricle volume, atrium volume,
atrioventricular valve dynamics, and fractional area change. Finally, we applied this
technique to image the roots of Arabidopsis thaliana plant.

The implementation of TPLS-EO-FLIM is shown in Figure 7. The depth of the light sheet
is characterized by exciting the fluorescein solution and found to ≳ 300 μm with a 20X
illumination objective. The height of the sheet depends on the voltage applied to the
galvo-scanner and the back aperture of the objective lens and was kept at ∼ 400 μm
during the experiments. The thickness of the light sheet is ∼ 2.5 μm. The total field of
view achieved was ∼ π × 88 × 88 μm2 with 10X and ∼ π × 188 × 188 μm2 with 4X
detection objectives and the corresponding resolution of the imaging system was
observed 1.96 μm and 3.0 μm, respectively. For the acquisition of lifetime imaging, the
Pockels cell is driven with a 40 MHz sinusoidal voltage. The laser pulse is selectively
chosen at a 40 MHz repetition rate to synchronize with the Pockels cell modulation. The
lifetime instrument response function (IRF) is recorded by sweeping the phase delay
between the Pockels cell drive and selective pulse excitation/emission of quenched
fluorescein solution with an extremely short lifetime sample. The fluorescence lifetime
can be computed by the ratio between the gated and ungated fluorescence intensity
images.

The system was tested by imaging a volume filled with yellow-green (1.0 μm diameter)
and Nile red fluorescent beads (1.0 μm diameter) to assess the system’s lifetime-based
contrast performance. The mixture of beads was embedded in the agarose gel and
confined within a rectangular glass capillary attached to the scanning stage. A two-
photon scanning light sheet was employed for excitation, while the sample stage was
systematically moved in 1.0 μm increments to achieve volumetric scanning, with an
exposure time set of 50 ms per frame. Fluorescence emission was collected using a
10X/0.50 NA objective. Analysis confirmed distinct lifetime values of approximately 4.9
ns for yellow-green beads and 3.1 ns for Nile red beads (see Figure 8), validating the
system’s potential to resolve fluorophores based on their unique fluorescence decay
profiles. The histogram illustrates the distribution of both bead’s populations within the
volume, plotted as a function of their lifetimes and provides a quantitative
characterization of the fluorescence lifetime. The corresponding photon-normalized
lifetime sensitivity F-numbers are 3.2 and 2.4, respectively, indicating the relative
measured noise of the lifetime estimates with respect to the fundamental (shot-noise)
limit (F =1).

Discussion and conclusion
Over the performance period of this program, we demonstrated pioneering high-
throughput EO-FLIM methods relevant to imaging of biological samples. Our
instruments achieved performance metrics for lifetime imaging throughput which exceed
the current state-of-the-art. This work has laid the foundation for next generation
studies focused on plant biology.
