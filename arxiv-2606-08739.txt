The Minimal Retroreflective Microfacet Model
Jamie Portsmouth Matthias Raab Laurent Belcour Francis Liu Autodesk NVIDIA Intel NVIDIA

Abstract
We present the Minimal Retroreflective Microfacet (MRM) model, which turns any existing microfacet BSDF into a physically plausible retroreflective one by a single substitution: replacing the view direction with its reflection about the surface normal before evaluating the standard model. Based on the previously published back-vector formulation, MRM requires only minimal code changes and has been adopted in the OpenPBR and MaterialX material standards. We prove reciprocity and energy conservation under the assumption of a reflection-symmetric normal distribution function (NDF), which holds for all commonly used distributions, and validate the model against measured retroreflective material data.

Introduction

For safety applications such as road markings, signs, vehicles, and high-visibility clothing items, retroreflective materials are heavily used (see Figure
The Minimal Retroreflective Microfacet Model
for some example renderings). Materials are typically designed to be retroreflective via a manufactured substructure of elements (for example, sheets of micro-prisms or glass beads), which preferentially scatter light backwards
4

Realistic rendering of such materials is important for design and visualization purposes, as well as in a predictive rendering context, for example, for simulation of visibility in training of autonomous vehicles or for safety certification of road markings.

Previous work

Modeling of retroreflectivity in the surface scattering function has received relatively little attention so far.

15

5

7

None of the aforementioned models can leverage existing microfacet infrastructure (importance sampling of visible normals, energy compensation, layering, pre-integration for real-time renderers) that MRM inherits directly.

Our model

For practical purposes in visual effects, we are interested in a model that is (a) visually plausible and follows measured observations reasonably well, (b) provably energy-conserving and reciprocal (the latter under the mild assumption of a reflection-symmetric NDF), (c) easy to implement, (d) compatible with existing microfacet infrastructure (such as image-based lighting pre-convolutions for real-time renderers), and (e) efficient to run. back-vector

We now detail the mathematical consequences of using the back-vector as the microfacet selector.

Jacobian of back-vector

First we observe that the Jacobian of the change of variable from the back-vector to the light vector
\mathbf{l}
for specular microfacets
(
20
, Equations (11) and (14))
has the same form as the half-vector one:

\left\lVert\dfrac{\partial\omega_{\mathbf{b}_{r}}}{\partial\omega_{\mathbf{l}}}\right\rVert=\left\lVert\dfrac{\partial\omega_{\mathbf{h}_{r}(\mathbf{v^{\prime}},\mathbf{l})}}{\partial\omega_{\mathbf{l}}}\right\rVert=\dfrac{1}{4\left|\mathbf{l}\cdot\mathbf{h}_{r}(\mathbf{v^{\prime}},\mathbf{l})\right|}=\dfrac{1}{4\left|\mathbf{l}\cdot\mathbf{b}_{r}\right|}.

(4)

We also observe that this is true for refractive microfacets
(
20
, Equation (17))
:

\left\lVert\dfrac{\partial\omega_{\mathbf{b}_{t}}}{\partial\omega_{\mathbf{l}}}\right\rVert=\left\lVert\dfrac{\partial\omega_{\mathbf{h}_{t}(\mathbf{v^{\prime}},\mathbf{l})}}{\partial\omega_{\mathbf{l}}}\right\rVert=\dfrac{\eta_{\mathbf{v}}^{2}\lvert\mathbf{l}\cdot\mathbf{h}_{t}(\mathbf{v^{\prime}},\mathbf{l})\rvert}{\bigl(\eta_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{v^{\prime}}\cdot\mathbf{h}_{t}(\mathbf{v^{\prime}},\mathbf{l})+\textit{et al.}\mathbf{l}\cdot\mathbf{h}_{t}(\mathbf{v^{\prime}},\mathbf{l})\bigr)^{2}}.

(5)

The Jacobian has the same form as the half-vector case because we differentiate with respect to the light direction, which is not altered by the back-vector.

3
Plausibility of MRM

We show here that the retroreflective microfacet model of Equation (
10
) is physically plausible, i.e., that it fulfills reciprocity symmetry and energy conservation requirements.

3.1
Reciprocity

We first verify that the MRM model fulfills reciprocity symmetry, i.e.,

f_{\text{MRM}}(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{l})=f_{\text{MRM}}(\mathbf{l},\mathbf{v}),\hskip 10.00002ptf_{t,\text{MRM}}(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{l})=\frac{\eta_{\mathbf{v}}^{2}}{\textit{et al.}^{2}}f_{t,\text{MRM}}(\mathbf{l},\mathbf{v}).

(11)

As outlined by
2
, we can express the reflection around the normal using a matrix transform
\mathbf{R_{\mathbf{n}}}
:

\text{reflect}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{n})=\mathbf{R_{\mathbf{n}}}\mathbf{x}=\left(2\mathbf{n}\mathbf{n}^{T}-\mathbf{I}\right)\mathbf{x},

(12)

where
\mathbf{I}
is the identity matrix.
Note that
\mathbf{R_{\mathbf{n}}}
is the Householder matrix multiplied by
-1
, which means some of its properties are applicable to
\mathbf{R_{\mathbf{n}}}
.
The Householder matrix is orthogonal, which implies that dot products do not change, so we have
\mathbf{R_{\mathbf{n}}}\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{R_{\mathbf{n}}}\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y}
and, in turn, the norm is preserved (
\lVert\mathbf{R_{\mathbf{n}}}\mathbf{x}\rVert=\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert
).
Further, like the Householder matrix,
\mathbf{R_{\mathbf{n}}}
is involutory, i.e., it is its own inverse (
\mathbf{R_{\mathbf{n}}}\mathbf{R_{\mathbf{n}}}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}
).

Using those properties of
\mathbf{R_{\mathbf{n}}}
, we now show that the back-vector gets reflected under the swap
(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{l})\leftrightarrow(\mathbf{l},\mathbf{v})
:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathbf{R_{\mathbf{n}}}\mathbf{b}_{r}(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{l})&=\mathbf{R_{\mathbf{n}}}\mathbf{h}_{r}(\mathbf{v^{\prime}},\mathbf{l})=\mathbf{R_{\mathbf{n}}}\frac{\mathbf{v^{\prime}}+\mathbf{l}}{\lVert\mathbf{v^{\prime}}+\mathbf{l}\rVert}\\
&=\frac{\mathbf{R_{\mathbf{n}}}\mathbf{v^{\prime}}+\mathbf{R_{\mathbf{n}}}\mathbf{l}}{\lVert\cdot\rVert}=\frac{\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{l^{\prime}}}{\lVert\cdot\rVert}=\mathbf{h}_{r}(\mathbf{l^{\prime}},\mathbf{v})=\mathbf{b}_{r}(\mathbf{l},\mathbf{v}),\end{aligned}

(13)

where we omitted spelling out the norm in the denominator (as it continues matching the norm of the numerator, which is unchanged due to norm-preservation) and adopted the notation
\mathbf{l^{\prime}}
for the reflected light direction.
Analogously, the same can be shown for
\mathbf{b}_{t}
, and in the following we use
\mathbf{b}
as all equations apply for both
\mathbf{b}_{r}
and
\mathbf{b}_{t}
.

NDF term

We will need one technical assumption on the NDF
D
: we require that
D
is
reflection-symmetric
around the normal.
Note that this is also the requirement for the applicability of V-cavity masking and that it holds for all commonly used distributions including isotropic and anisotropic GGX, Beckmann, and Phong.
Under that requirement for
D
and using Equation (
13
) we immediately conclude
D\left(\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{l})\right)=D\left(\mathbf{R_{\mathbf{n}}}\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{l})\right)=D\left(\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{l},\mathbf{v})\right)
.

Shadowing and masking

For V-cavity masking, using Equation (
13
), the orthogonality of
\mathbf{R_{\mathbf{n}}}
, and observing that
\mathbf{R_{\mathbf{n}}}\mathbf{n}=\mathbf{n}
, we get

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} G_{1,\text{VC}}(\mathbf{v^{\prime}},\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{l}))&=\min\left\{\frac{2\lvert\mathbf{v^{\prime}}\cdot\mathbf{n}\rvert\lvert\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{l})\rvert}{\lvert\mathbf{v^{\prime}}\cdot\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{l})\rvert},1\right\}\\
&=\min\left\{\frac{2\lvert\mathbf{R_{\mathbf{n}}}\mathbf{v^{\prime}}\cdot\mathbf{R_{\mathbf{n}}}\mathbf{n}\rvert\lvert\mathbf{R_{\mathbf{n}}}\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{R_{\mathbf{n}}}\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{l})\rvert}{\lvert\mathbf{R_{\mathbf{n}}}\mathbf{v^{\prime}}\cdot\mathbf{R_{\mathbf{n}}}\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{l})\rvert},1\right\}\\
&=\min\left\{\frac{2\lvert\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{n}\rvert\lvert\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{l},\mathbf{v})\rvert}{\lvert\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{l},\mathbf{v})\rvert},1\right\}=G_{1,\text{VC}}(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{l},\mathbf{v})).\end{aligned}

(14)

Similarly, using the same properties, for Smith masking we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} G_{1,\text{Smith}}\left(\mathbf{v^{\prime}},\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{l})\right)&=\frac{\chi^{+}\left(\mathbf{v^{\prime}}\cdot\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{l})\right)\lvert\mathbf{v^{\prime}}\cdot\mathbf{n}\rvert}{\int\langle\mathbf{v^{\prime}},\mathbf{x}\rangle D(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}}\\
&=\frac{\chi^{+}\left(\mathbf{R_{\mathbf{n}}}\mathbf{v^{\prime}}\cdot\mathbf{R_{\mathbf{n}}}\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{l})\right)\lvert\mathbf{R_{\mathbf{n}}}\mathbf{v^{\prime}}\cdot\mathbf{R_{\mathbf{n}}}\mathbf{n}\rvert}{\int\langle\mathbf{R_{\mathbf{n}}}\mathbf{v^{\prime}},\mathbf{R_{\mathbf{n}}}\mathbf{x}\rangle D(\mathbf{R_{\mathbf{n}}}\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}}\\
&=\frac{\chi^{+}\left(\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{l},\mathbf{v})\right)\lvert\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{n}\rvert}{\int\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{R_{\mathbf{n}}}\mathbf{x}\rangle D(\mathbf{R_{\mathbf{n}}}\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}}\\
&=\frac{\chi^{+}\left(\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{l},\mathbf{v})\right)\lvert\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{n}\rvert}{\int\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{x}\rangle D(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}}=G_{1,\text{Smith}}\left(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{l},\mathbf{v})\right),\end{aligned}

(15)

where in the last step we simply changed the integration domain to the
\mathbf{R_{\mathbf{n}}}
-transformed hemisphere of directions.

In analogy to the above, we obtain
G_{1}\left(\mathbf{l},\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{l})\right)=G_{1}\left(\mathbf{l^{\prime}},\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{l},\mathbf{v})\right)
for both masking variants. For a separable
G_{2}=G_{1}\cdot G_{1}
(or any
G_{2}
symmetric in its first two arguments), combining both
G_{1}
results gives
G_{2}\!\left(\mathbf{v^{\prime}},\mathbf{l},\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{l})\right)=G_{2}\!\left(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{l^{\prime}},\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{l},\mathbf{v})\right)=G_{2}\!\left(\mathbf{l^{\prime}},\mathbf{v},\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{l},\mathbf{v})\right)
, which completes the proof of reciprocity of the shadowing-masking term.

Fresnel term

For
reflection
,
f_{r}(\mathbf{v^{\prime}},\mathbf{l})
evaluates
F(\eta_{\mathbf{v}}{\to}\textit{et al.},\mathbf{v^{\prime}}\cdot\mathbf{b}_{r}(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{l}))
and the swapped path
F(\eta_{\mathbf{v}}{\to}\textit{et al.},\mathbf{l^{\prime}}\cdot\mathbf{b}_{r}(\mathbf{l},\mathbf{v}))
; since
\mathbf{b}_{r}(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{l})\propto\mathbf{v^{\prime}}+\mathbf{l}
, the forward cosine is
(1+\mathbf{v^{\prime}}\cdot\mathbf{l})/\|\mathbf{v^{\prime}}+\mathbf{l}\|
, and
\mathbf{b}_{r}(\mathbf{l},\mathbf{v})\propto\mathbf{l^{\prime}}+\mathbf{v}
gives the same expression by
\mathbf{R_{\mathbf{n}}}
-orthogonality (
\mathbf{v^{\prime}}\cdot\mathbf{l}=\mathbf{l^{\prime}}\cdot\mathbf{v}
).
For
refraction
, denote by
T_{a\to b}
the Fresnel transmittance of a ray from
a
to
b
through the microfacet. Ray
\mathbf{v^{\prime}}
refracts through
\mathbf{b}_{t}(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{l})
to
\mathbf{l}
(transmittance
T_{\mathbf{v^{\prime}}\to\mathbf{l}}
); the swapped path has
\mathbf{l^{\prime}}
refracting through
\mathbf{b}_{t}(\mathbf{l},\mathbf{v})
to
\mathbf{v}
(transmittance
T_{\mathbf{l^{\prime}}\to\mathbf{v}}
). By reciprocity of light transport,
T_{\mathbf{v^{\prime}}\to\mathbf{l}}=T_{\mathbf{l}\to\mathbf{v^{\prime}}}
. Via Equation (
13
),
\mathbf{l}\cdot\mathbf{b}_{t}(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{l})=\mathbf{l^{\prime}}\cdot\mathbf{b}_{t}(\mathbf{l},\mathbf{v})
, so
\mathbf{l}
and
\mathbf{l^{\prime}}
hit their microfacets at the same angle across the same
\textit{et al.}{\to}\eta_{\mathbf{v}}
boundary, hence
T_{\mathbf{l}\to\mathbf{v^{\prime}}}=T_{\mathbf{l^{\prime}}\to\mathbf{v}}
. Combining,
T_{\mathbf{v^{\prime}}\to\mathbf{l}}=T_{\mathbf{l^{\prime}}\to\mathbf{v}}
.

Summary

In summary, we can conclude the reciprocity symmetry of the BRDF:

f_{r,\text{MRM}}(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{l})=f_{r}(\mathbf{v^{\prime}},\mathbf{l})=f_{r}(\mathbf{l^{\prime}},\mathbf{v})=f_{r,\text{MRM}}(\mathbf{l},\mathbf{v}).

(16)

Analogously, we obtain the reciprocity symmetry requirement of the BTDF:

f_{t,\text{MRM}}(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{l})=f_{t}(\mathbf{v^{\prime}},\mathbf{l})=\frac{\eta_{\mathbf{v}}^{2}}{\textit{et al.}^{2}}f_{t}(\mathbf{l^{\prime}},\mathbf{v})=\frac{\eta_{\mathbf{v}}^{2}}{\textit{et al.}^{2}}f_{t,\text{MRM}}(\mathbf{l},\mathbf{v}).

(17)

3.2
Energy Conservation

We now verify that the retroreflective microfacet model fulfills energy conservation, i.e., that the directional albedos fulfill

\rho_{r,\text{MRM}}(\mathbf{v})+\rho_{t,\text{MRM}}(\mathbf{v})\leq 1
for all
\mathbf{v}
.
For the directional albedos we simply have (where
\Omega_{\mathbf{x}}
denotes the hemisphere containing
\mathbf{x}
)

\displaystyle\rho_{r,\text{MRM}}(\mathbf{v})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{\mathbf{v}}}f_{r,\text{MRM}}(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{l})\lvert\mathbf{l}\cdot\mathbf{n}\rvert\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{l}=\int_{\Omega_{\mathbf{v^{\prime}}}}f_{r}(\mathbf{v^{\prime}},\mathbf{l})\lvert\mathbf{l}\cdot\mathbf{n}\rvert\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{l}=\rho_{r}(\mathbf{v^{\prime}}),

\displaystyle\rho_{t,\text{MRM}}(\mathbf{v})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{-\mathbf{v}}}f_{t,\text{MRM}}(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{l})\lvert\mathbf{l}\cdot\mathbf{n}\rvert\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{l}=\int_{\Omega_{-\mathbf{v^{\prime}}}}f_{t}(\mathbf{v^{\prime}},\mathbf{l})\lvert\mathbf{l}\cdot\mathbf{n}\rvert\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{l}=\rho_{t}(\mathbf{v^{\prime}}).

(18)

That is, the directional albedo of the MRM is equivalent to the directional albedo of the regular microfacet BSDF, but evaluated at the reflected direction.
As the original microfacet BSDF fulfills energy conservation, the retroreflective modification does, too.

We further note that under our requirement of a reflection-symmetric microfacet normal distribution, the geometric
configurations for
\mathbf{v}
and
\mathbf{v^{\prime}}
are identical.
Hence we can conclude
\rho_{r}(\mathbf{v})=\rho_{r}(\mathbf{v^{\prime}})
and
\rho_{t}(\mathbf{v})=\rho_{t}(\mathbf{v^{\prime}})
, which in turn implies that the albedos of forward- and retroreflection are the same, i.e.,
\rho_{r,\text{MRM}}(\mathbf{v})=\rho_{r}(\mathbf{v})
and
\rho_{t,\text{MRM}}(\mathbf{v})=\rho_{t}(\mathbf{v})
.

Results and Discussion

Highlight appearance on curved surfaces

Although the MRM BRDF has the same lobe shape as GGX at any single surface point, the apparent highlight on
curved
surfaces is significantly broader under retroreflection (Figure
3
).
When
\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{l}
, the back-vector
\mathbf{b}_{r}=\mathbf{n}
exactly at every surface point, so
D(\mathbf{b}_{r})
stays at its peak across the entire visible surface; the half-vector, by contrast, is approximately constant for distant illumination, so
D(\mathbf{h}_{r})
peaks only where
\mathbf{n}\approx\mathbf{h}_{r}
.
This is a fundamental property of retroreflective materials in general, not specific to MRM.

Energy is conserved—MRM merely redirects each point’s specular lobe toward the camera (Section
3.2
).
From an artist’s perspective, roughness controls how closely the light source must be aligned with the camera to produce the retroreflective effect,
not
the apparent size of the highlight.

6
Conclusion

We have presented the Minimal Retroreflective Microfacet (MRM) model, which turns any existing microfacet BSDF into a retroreflective one by a single substitution: reflecting the view direction about the surface normal. We have shown that this modification preserves reciprocity and energy conservation, making MRM a physically plausible drop-in replacement. A comparison to measured retroreflective material data confirms that the model can represent materials with strong retroreflective properties—such as safety tape, certain fabrics, or retroreflective coatings—with reasonable accuracy and negligible implementation cost.

Due to its simplicity and compatibility with existing microfacet infrastructure, MRM has been adopted as part of the OpenPBR material standard
1
and the MaterialX shading framework
17
. Future work could explore fitting to a wider range of measured retroreflective materials and validating the retroreflective BTDF component against physical data.
