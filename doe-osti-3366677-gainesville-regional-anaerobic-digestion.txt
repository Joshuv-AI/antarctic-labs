Executive Summary
Project Background and Objectives
The City of Gainesville (City) and Gainesville Regional Utilities (GRU) are exploring the feasibility of
developing a Regional Anaerobic Digestion (AD) Facility to support the City’s environmental goals,
including organic waste landfill diversion and greenhouse gas (GHG) emission reductions in alignment
with the 2040 Zero Waste Initiative.

This study assessed the technical, environmental, and economic potential for co-digestion of municipal
sludge, food waste, and fats, oils, and grease (FOG), with the objective to:

▬ Identify and characterize available feedstocks.

▬ Assess suitable digestion technologies and configurations.

▬ Evaluate biogas production and end-use options.

▬ Recommend siting, sizing, and phasing strategies.

▬ Estimate capital and operating costs.

▬ Understand potential revenue streams and partnerships.

▬ Determine environmental impacts.

Methodology Overview
The approach included a comprehensive data collection and review phase involving:

▬ Biosolids and sludge data from Kanapaha Water Reclamation Facility (KWRF), Main Street Water
 Reclamation Facility (MSWRF), and University of Florida Water Reclamation Facility (UFWRF).
▬ Organic waste and FOG availability assessments.

▬ EPA’s Excess Food Opportunities Map validation.

▬ Biomethane potential testing in partnership with the University of Florida (UF).

▬ Mass balance development for digester sizing.

▬ High-level conceptual design and siting evaluation.

▬ Economic and lifecycle modeling.

Site Evaluation
Three locations for the AD Facility were evaluated for technical feasibility, integration with existing
infrastructure, and permitting constraints:

 1) Kanapaha WRF (KWRF)
 2) Deerhaven Generating Station
 3) EcoLoop
KWRF presents the most favorable option due to its existing solids handling systems including
dewatering system and ability to treat centrate onsite, the potential to repurpose existing solids
handling infrastructure, and utility coordination opportunities. KWRF is also the largest contributor of
solids of the three WRFs and has the most available land for the proposed anaerobic digestion facilities.

Biogas Production and Utilization
Biogas production potential is tied directly to the volatile solids loading and feedstock composition.
Methane yields were estimated using biomethane potential (BMP) testing for various food waste and
waste activated sludge (WAS) blends.

The proposed facility offers two primary options for biogas utilization:

▬ Upgrading to Renewable Natural Gas (RNG) for injection into pipelines or use as vehicle fuel.

▬ Direct use in Combined Heat and Power (CHP) systems.

To evaluate economic feasibility, a financial model was developed for the RNG system, incorporating
lifecycle costs based on capital investment, regulatory incentives, and environmental credit market
pricing. A sensitivity analysis was conducted on key variables—Renewable Identification Numbers
(RINs), tipping fees, power costs, food waste and FOG flows, and OPCC—as fluctuations in these factors
are critical to project viability. To represent possible variations in these factors, three pricing scenarios
for the proposed system were created: an extreme worst-case, extreme best-case, and a most likely
case. The payback period was compared against the financial projection for continuing the existing
system. Figure ES.1 presents the projected Net Present Values (NPV) under all three scenarios through
2050.

Financial Performance and Lifecycle Value
As shown in Figure ES.1, the projected NPV outcomes vary significantly by scenario. Under the extreme
worst-case scenario, the proposed system exhibits a steadily declining NPV over the evaluation period,
indicating that unfavorable assumptions for market pricing, costs, and revenues would result in a
materially worse financial outcome than continuing the existing system. In contrast, the extreme best-
case scenario shows a strong upward NPV trajectory, transitioning from negative values in the early
years to positive NPV over the long term, demonstrating the potential for substantial economic benefit
under favorable market and operating conditions. The most likely case remains negative throughout the
planning horizon but shows a gradual improvement over time, narrowing the gap relative to the existing
system or base case. This trend suggests that while the proposed RNG system may not fully outperform
the existing system under expected conditions, its long-term financial performance improves and
remains sensitive to key market drivers.

Despite the lack of payback within the analysis horizon, the most-likely scenario does deliver improved
lifecycle value compared to maintaining current operations, indicating that the AD Facility offers a
significant long-term advantage. Additionally, the proposed organics waste diversion program and AD
Facility aligns with wider community strategic goals such as sustainability and greenhouse gas emissions
reductions. From a financial standpoint, the proposed program has long term benefits considering many
of the facility investments have an anticipated life well beyond 20-years and would provide GRU
increased flexibility in solids handling and risk avoidance of escalating landfill hauling costs and tipping
fees.

creates a more resilient community infrastructure.

Preliminary Recommendations
The following recommendations are intended to guide the next phase of project development and
decision-making. These actions build upon the findings of this feasibility study and are focused on
reducing project risk, confirming technical and financial assumptions, and supporting stakeholder
alignment if the project moves toward implementation.

▬ Finalize stakeholder coordination with UF, Alachua County, GRU, and haulers.

▬ Validate feedstock quantities through expanded organic waste diversion pilot programs and supply
 agreements with large generators.
▬ Advance conceptual design and select preferred digestion configuration.

▬ Establish a phasing strategy for implementation and future expansion.

▬ Continue to investigate federal and state grant programs for organic waste diversion, resource
 recovery, and renewable energy programs to offset capital costs

Conclusion
This feasibility study demonstrates that a Regional Anaerobic Digestion (AD) Facility offers significant
environmental benefits, including a substantial reduction in greenhouse gas emissions compared to the
existing system, and enhanced capacity to meet Gainesville’s 2040 Zero Waste goals. While financial
modeling indicates that the project is unlikely to achieve profitability within a 20-year timeframe,
external funding could support a project with viable payback. Additionally, the facility investments have
an anticipated life well beyond 20-years and would provide GRU increased flexibility in solids handling
and risk avoidance of escalating landfill hauling costs. Acquisition of adequate food waste feedstock is
a highly critical factor in the financial metrics of the proposed system.

agreements, implementing pilot programs for source separation, and exploring partnerships with food
processors and agricultural stakeholders. Additionally, pursuing external funding opportunities will be
essential to improve financial viability.

1.0 Introduction
The Gainesville Regional AD Feasibility Study was initiated to address environmental concerns within
Gainesville and Alachua County, particularly regarding the management of organic waste streams. This
study aims to assess the feasibility of developing a regional facility to process organic waste, including
food waste, fats, oils, grease (FOG), and wastewater residuals, thereby reducing GHG emissions,
diverting organics from the landfill, and producing renewable biogas. This section provides an overview
of the project objectives and outlines past evaluations and studies that have contributed to the planning
and development of this project.

1.1 Project Overview and Objectives
In line with Gainesville’s 2040 Zero Waste Initiative and the City’s long-term sustainability goals, the
Gainesville Regional AD Facility is envisioned as a key infrastructure project to support the region’s
environmental and waste management priorities. The primary objective of this feasibility study is to
evaluate technical, economic, and environmental benefits and cost implications of a regional facility that
will handle both biosolids and organic waste generated within Gainesville and the surrounding region.

Key objectives include:

▬ Optimizing Feedstock Management: Identify and quantify available wastewater residuals; organic
 waste (including food waste); and Fats, Oils, and Greases (FOG); this includes sources such as KWRF
 and MSWRF, the Alachua County-operated Leveda Brown Transfer Station, the University of Florida
 (UF), and FOG haulers. Determine the most suitable mix and rates for these feedstocks to maximize
 biogas production and minimize residual waste.
▬ Assessing Technology and Site Viability: Analyze suitable AD technologies for processing varied
 feedstock compositions. Evaluate potential sites, considering factors such as proximity to feedstock
 sources, biogas utilization infrastructure, and ease of permitting.
▬ Evaluating Environmental and Social Impact: Project the GHG emission reduction potential of the AD
 Facility compared to traditional waste disposal practices. Consider social impacts, particularly in
 relation to local waste diversion goals and community involvement in waste management.
▬ Estimating Economic Feasibility: Calculate probable capital and operating costs, analyze potential
 revenue from biogas sales and tipping fees, and estimate the project’s return on investment (ROI).
 Develop a preliminary financial model to project long-term economic viability and sustainability.
The feasibility study serves as a foundational analysis for developing the Gainesville Regional AD Facility,
offering insights into the benefits, costs, and challenges of implementing this sustainable waste solution.

 1.0 │ INTRODUCTION

Anaerobic Digestion and Co-Digestion
Anaerobic digestion is a proven method of sludge stabilization and is employed at numerous water
resource recovery facilities across the country. Anaerobic digestion reduces the mass of the influent
feedstocks while generating valuable resources: biogas and digestate. Biogas is a form of energy,
consisting of approximately 60% methane, which can be used to generate power or heat, or can be
upgraded to RNG. Digestate is stabilized sludge that can form Class B biosolids, suitable for land
application. Anaerobic digestion also produces less digestate on average than aerobic digestion.

Co-digestion is the process of combining different feedstocks (e.g., wastewater residuals and outside
organic waste) in the anaerobic digestion process, as shown in Figure 1.2. Combining feedstocks
promotes increased biogas production while diverting organic waste from landfills. To implement
regional co-digestion for Gainesville, an AD Facility could be constructed at Kanapaha WRF, as shown in
Figure 1.3. In addition to sludge from Kanapaha, the digester could process:

▬ Sludges from MSWRF and the University of Florida Water Reclamation Facility (UFWRF).

▬ FOG collected from local grease traps.

▬ Commercial, industrial, or residential food waste.

▬ Other organic waste sources.

1.2 Previous Evaluations and Programs
The following evaluations and studies contributed to this feasibility and planning study for a regional
anaerobic digestion facility for the City and Gainesville Regional Utilities (GRU).

▬ EcoEngineers Study: Conducted an initial assessment of AD technology suitable for handling regional
 waste streams, including a review of feedstock composition, AD system requirements, and
 anticipated biogas yields. This study provided baseline information on the technical potential for
 biogas production from local organic waste sources.
▬ Envirosim Waste Analysis: Focused on detailed waste characterization at local water reclamation
 facilities, this analysis identified critical factors impacting co-digestion, such as variations in waste
 properties and volumes, seasonal fluctuations, and transportation logistics. Recommendations
 included feedstock pre-processing options to enhance biogas yield and facility efficiency.
▬ Community-Based Composting Programs: Prior community efforts in organic waste diversion,
 including local composting programs, offer a perspective on community engagement and waste
 separation practices. These programs illustrate the community’s willingness to participate in waste
 diversion efforts and potential for collaboration with local businesses and institutions for AD
 feedstock sourcing.
These prior evaluations have informed the current feasibility study by identifying Gainesville’s unique
waste management needs, the types of organic waste available, and community interest in sustainable
waste solutions. The insights gained from these studies and programs are integrated into this feasibility
assessment to ensure that the proposed AD Facility is tailored to the region’s specific environmental,
economic, and social objectives.

2.0 Data Collection and Review
To understand the current system operations and limitations and estimate the efficiency of an anerobic
digester, data was collected, reviewed, and analyzed for different AD feedstocks, including wastewater
residuals, food waste, and FOG. The sources of this data were the City of Gainesville/GRU, the Florida
Department of Environmental Protection (FDEP) Oculus Database, and the Environmental Protection
Agency’s (EPA) Food Waste Map. A mass balance was developed to evaluate the potential digester sizing
and biogas production based on the available data. UF also conducted preliminary laboratory testing to
evaluate potential biomethane potential (BMP).

2.1 Solids and Waste Activated Sludge Data
KWRF is one of the sites being considered in this study to implement anaerobic co-digestion on-site at
the facility to generate biogas. KWRF currently receives WAS from MSWRF and the UFWRF. The
combined WAS streams are mixed with KWRF WAS and the combined mixture is fed to a series of
aerobic digesters and dewatered prior to off-site composting.

2.1.1 Kanapaha WRF
The Kanapaha WRF has approximately 11 million gallons per day (MGD) average annual day flow
(permitted for 14.9 MGD) and is a secondary treatment facility providing biological treatment,
clarification, filtration, sodium hypochlorite disinfection, and biosolids processing through aerobic
digestion.

The KWRF Primary Aerobic Digester WAS data was used to obtain weekly total solids concentration by
volume (%TS), volatile solids concentration by volume (% VS), and volatile solids to total solids (VS:TS)
ratio of KWRF sludge going to the digester, prior to being blended with sludge from UFWRF and MSWRF
for the time period from January 2021 through October 2024. On average, the KWRF WAS consisted of
2.08% TS and 1.64% volatile solids, and the average VS:TS ratio was 0.79. This data can be seen in Figure
2.1 below. As a note, a higher VS:TS ratio implies a better AD feedstock, as there is more organic
material available for digestion.

2.2 Organic Waste Data
Organic waste materials, such as food waste, will be a feedstock to the AD system. Therefore, a
preliminary estimate of available excess food waste for AD was developed through an analysis of organic
waste generation from industrial, commercial, and institutional (ICI) sources within the City. This section
compiles data from local solid waste audits, state and federal food waste studies, and industry
benchmarks to assess the potential volume of food waste that could be diverted to the proposed AD
Facility.

The City has indicated plans to implement third-stream recycling for food waste exclusively at the ICI
level, meaning businesses, restaurants, food processors, institutions, and other large-scale generators
will be encouraged to participate. The majority of ICI facilities have existing contracts with food waste
haulers, while the food waste of most residential properties goes to Leveda Brown Transfer Station.
Tipping fees for this waste hauling are further discussed in Section 8 of this report. However, there are
no firm plans to implement food waste separation for single-family or multi-family residential
properties. A residential program may be introduced in the future, but its implementation timeline and
participation rates remain uncertain for this study. Subsequently, this analysis focuses primarily on ICI
food waste generation, with residential contributions considered as a potential source rather than a
reliable component of the anaerobic digestion feedstock supply.

2.2.1 Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) Excess Food Opportunities
CDM Smith reviewed the Excess Food Opportunities Map Version 3.0 and the accompanying Technical
Methodology (EPA 2023) report to estimate quantities of excess food that may be available in the
region. The Excess Food Opportunities map includes data from 2021 onward for selected industrial,
commercial, and institutional sources that can potentially generate excess food. Target facilities were
determined by their North American Industry Classification System (NAICS) code and include food
manufacturers and processors, food wholesale and retail, educational institutions, the hospitality

industry, correctional facilities, healthcare facilities, restaurants and food services, and farmers markets.
The database includes low and high estimates of food waste production for each type of facility using
industry specific equations gathered from a database of scientific publications. More information about
these equations and calculations can be found in the EPA Excess Food Opportunities technical
methodologies report.

All data in this section are estimated values using the equations listed in the EPA Excess Food
Opportunities technical methodology report. The EPA data uses multiple sources and studies to
estimate excess food for a nation-wide data set. While validating the data, it was noted that some of the
estimates are higher or lower than what would be expected for the specific facilities. Due to these
inaccuracies, top producers noted by EPA in each category were verified along with a selection of
additional facilities in each category.

Since EPA data in the Excess Food Opportunities report is from 2021, it is likely that a few facilities on
the list may have closed while others may have opened within this timeframe; thus a net neutral change
in the number of facilities was assumed.

2.2.1.1 EPA Food Waste Technical Methodology
Each facility type, and sub-category within each facility, has a different method and equation for
calculating excess food waste. All equations use literature-derived generating factors as a part of these
calculations. For example, for educational facilities, literature was used to determine the food waste
generating factors from elementary and secondary schools. Generating factors from the literature found
that 18 to 25.9 pounds per student per year of food waste are generated (EPA 2023). This value was
then multiplied by the numbers of students at the school to determine the total excess food waste
generated. All equations and examples for other facility types can be found in the EPA Excess Food
Opportunities report.

calculated as total digester feed. GRU provided Low/Medium/High projections for population growth

ratio of Municipal Sludge to HSW (FOG and food waste) was used). A slight modification to this method,
referred to as Method 1a, followed the same analysis steps but used the max month conditions for
municipal solids instead of annual average.

Method 2 to size the digester used the max month data and the population projections noted above but
took a hydraulic loading approach instead, assuming an SRT of 15 days.

3.2 Mesophilic Anaerobic Digestion
Mesophilic anaerobic digestion is a biological process that occurs at temperatures between 30°C to 38°C
(with the optimum range being 35-37°C). This process involves the decomposition of organic matter in
biosolids into methane gas and carbon dioxide by microorganisms in the absence of oxygen. Mesophilic
digestion is the most commonly used anaerobic digestion process at wastewater treatment plants,
representing approximately 90 percent of all facilities in North America. It results in Class B stabilized
biosolids. Class B biosolids are minimally treated and require strict site permits and setbacks (Rule 62-
640.700, F.A.C.), Class A biosolids undergo advanced treatment to eliminate pathogens but still face
some application restrictions (Rule 62-640.200(11) and 62-640.700, F.A.C.), while Class AA biosolids
meet the highest treatment and pollutant standards, allowing them to be freely marketed and used like
commercial fertilizer without site-specific controls (Rule 62-640.850, F.A.C.). The process is considered
more stable than thermophilic digestion due to the greater number of mesophilic bacteria and archaea
species and their tolerance to environmental changes. Mesophilic systems are also less susceptible to
upset conditions caused by toxicity or high organic loading rate. However, the digestion kinetics are
slower, requiring larger system volumes compared to thermophilic systems.

3.3 Thermophilic Anaerobic Digestion
Thermophilic anaerobic digestion operates at higher temperatures, typically between 50°C (122°F) to
57°C (135°F) and can reach up to 70°C (158°F). This elevated temperature increases the metabolic rates

and growth rates of the microbial population, allowing for higher volatile solids reduction and more
biogas production compared to mesophilic digesters for the same amount of solids. Thermophilically-
digested biosolids tend to have better dewatering characteristics but require more energy input for
heating, resulting in higher operating costs. Thermophilic digestion produces solids with fewer
pathogens, but achieving Class A pathogen reduction status can be challenging due to regulatory
provisions. The process is more sensitive to temperature changes and requires careful control of
digester gas and odors. Despite these challenges, thermophilic digestion is capable of meeting Class A
pathogen reduction requirements when properly managed and approved by regulatory authorities.

For the purposes of this evaluation, mesophilic anaerobic digestion was assumed as the basis for the
new co-digestion facility.

3.4 Co-Digestion Considerations
Co-digestion could have many economic, environmental, and community benefits for the City of
Gainesville by combining municipal sludge with outside organic waste to produce energy while diverting
solid waste from landfills. The products of co-digestion (e.g., biogas and biosolids) could be used on the
same site where it is produced, or, if further processed, can be sold, thus stimulating revenue. In
addition, co-digestion reduces the amount of organic waste (e.g., food waste) going to landfills,
providing an environmental benefit to the overall community.

This report goes into detail about the considerations for co-digestions for the City of Gainesville
specifically and discusses the feasibility and costs associated with implementation. In summary, these
considerations include:

▬ Capacity and space for additional infrastructure at the facility to accept new food waste, equipment
 for anaerobic digestion and biosolids management (deliveries, etc.).
▬ Availability and quality of feedstocks; feasibility of transferring feedstocks to the co-digestion
 facility.
▬ Costs associated with additional infrastructure and feedstocks and net revenue generated from the
 process.
▬ Regulatory implications to existing permits and the need for additional permits.

▬ RIN credits are reduced for a co-digestion process compared to digestion of solely municipal solids
 for biogas production.
The most promising feedstocks for co-digestion for the City of Gainesville include food/beverage waste
and fats, oils, and greases (FOG), as previously discussed.
While co-digestion can be a great option for a utility, there are several barriers to implementation. Some
of these include the uncertainty in feed stock availability and price, site constraints, capital funding,
operational and maintenance complexities, and public support of the projects. These factors are further
discussed in the below sections of this report.
One facility that has successfully implemented a co-digestion program is the Victor Valley Regional
Water Reclamation Facility in Victorville, California. This project, initially started in 2006, has gone

3.4.1 Waste Receiving
A consideration for a co-digestion facility is the need for a waste receiving facility, including storage
tanks, pipelines and connections, and access roads. Depending on the sources of the feedstocks, there
may be requirements for grit and plastic removal system. Odor control systems are also recommended
for the waste receiving facility.

An example of this is the Central Marin Sanitation Agency (CMSA) Treatment Plant in San Rafael,
California, which implemented a co-digestion program at the 7.5 million gallons per day (mgd)
treatment plant. Part of this project included an organic waste receiving station, costing approximately
$2 million (2018 Dollars). This station included a 300,000-gallon receiving tank, mixing pumps, grinders,
a paddler finisher, and odor control system.

For Gainesville and Alachua County, it is recommended to include the ability to offload tanker trucks by
gravity into a below ground storage tank, which eliminates the need to pump HSW out of receiving
trucks, which can extend unloading times. However, due to the relatively shallow groundwater in
Gainesville, this approach will likely introduce significant geotechnical considerations, including
dewatering and over excavation during construction. The below grade storage tank would be mixed with
a pumped recirculation mixing system equipped with in-line grinders. HSW is recommended to be fed
into the digesters consistently and slowly; too much HSW can disrupt anaerobic digester kinetics.
Automated valves can be used to control digester feed and evenly distribute HSW across multiple
digesters operating in parallel.

3.4.2 Pre-Processing and Depackaging
In addition to waste receiving stations, some systems may require a depackaging system for food waste
to remove bags and packaging, unless this is provided by waste haulers prior to delivery at the site.
These systems typically consist of a horizontal or vertical screw auger designed to tear open packaging,
followed by grinding or shredding equipment to reduce particle size, with the goal of producing a slurry
of less than 10% TS to feed to the digester. These systems separate unprocessed food waste into slurry,
light rejects like plastic packaging, and grit. The location of the depackaging system is discussed in
Section 5.1 of this report.

The Hermitage Water Reclamation Facility in Pennsylvania receives food waste from local industries as
feedstock, specifically from a dairy industry client. In a past project, the WRF purchased a solid waste
depackager, capable of removing packaging and slurrying the food waste. This has allowed them to
expand the range and amounts of feedstocks they can accept at the facility. As of 2018, they were
accepting between 60 to 100 tons per week of food waste. Tipping fees for each feedstock provider are
determined based on the labor required for depackaging, but average around $30 per ton of liquid and
slurried waste.

The three main types of digester tanks are conventional plug flow reactors, two-stage mixed plug flow,
and a complete mix reactor. These tank constructions are summarized below, with considerations for
each.

3.5.1.1 Conventional Plug Flow Reactor (PFR)
PFRs are typically used for a scenario with high feed solids (>10% TS). High feed solids are optimal for a
PFR since particles flow through with little-to-no mixing and thick solids will not settle in the tank as
easily. The tanks are typically buried concrete with a high L:W ratio (>5). The configuration of a PFR
spatially separates anaerobic reactions and does not allow for neutralization of acids, resulting in a low
pH at the head end of the digester.

3.5.1.2 Two-Stage Mixed Plug Flow Reactor (PFR)
DVO Inc., of Chilton WI, specializes in the Two-Stage Mixed Plug Flow Reactor (PFR). The PFR utilizes a u-
shaped plug flow system with acidogenic and methanogenic chambers. There is convective, mechanical,
and gas mixing which allows it to operate at a lower range of TS. They are typically buried concrete tanks
with an internal heat exchanger.

3.5.1.3 Complete Mix Reactor (CMR)
A CMR is either a concrete or steel, above or below ground tank with an internal or external heat
exchanger. They use mechanical mixing to retain particles in suspension throughout the reactor, all four
reactions occur simultaneously, and they usually operate at a range of 3-11% TS in the feed.

3.5.5 Biogas Storage
Biogas storage provides a buffer for biogas production and biogas use volumes. Biogas storage can be
accomplished on the low-pressure side in a dual membrane cover (as discussed above) or a ground-
mounted membrane. Alternatively, biogas can be stored at medium pressure in a gas sphere or gas
cylinder. For flexibility, it is recommended that at least one digester be equipped with a dual membrane
cover for variable biogas storage. With biogas storage, downstream biogas utilization equipment (e.g.,
RNG processing system) can be controlled to match biogas production with flexibility due to the gas
storage.

3.6 Thickening
WAS thickening is a crucial process in wastewater treatment aimed to reduce the volume of sludge by
increasing its solids concentration. This process is especially advantageous upstream of anaerobic
digestion, as it reduces the volume of WAS, allowing space for additional feedstocks and HSW.
Thickening is typically achieved through physical means such as Gravity Belt Thickeners (GBTs), dissolved
air flotation thickeners (DAFTs), and rotary drum thickeners (RDTs).

RDTs are specialized equipment used for thickening WAS and other types of sludge and are
recommended as the thickening process for this application. An RDT consists of a rotating cylindrical
drum screen that separates solids from liquids through coagulation and flocculation, followed by
drainage of free water through a porous media. The drum is internally fed with sludge conditioned with
polymer, which is then transported by internal flights to the discharge end. As the drum rotates at slow
speed (~5 rpm), free water passes through the drum perforations into a collection trough, while
thickened solids are retained inside and conveyed to a discharge chute. RDTs are known for their high
capture rate, low polymer consumption, and compact design, making them efficient and cost-effective.
Figure 3.2 shows an installation of a 400-gpm RDT from Alfa Laval as a reference for a full-scale
application.

4.0 Biogas Purification and Utilization Technology
Assessment
Biogas produced through anaerobic digestion consists of approximately 60-percent methane (CH4), trace
amounts of hydrogen sulfide (H2S), siloxanes, volatile organic compounds (VOCs), and the balance
carbon dioxide (CO2). Some level of purification is required to remove undesired components,
particularly siloxanes. With an energy density of 600 British thermal units (BTU) per cubic foot (cf),
purified biogas can be used as a fuel source to power boilers or engines. Biogas can also be purified
further to produce pure methane which can be used as RNG. Several processes exist for biogas
purification, as will be outlined later in this section. These processes can require significant investment
and have intense operations and maintenance costs (O&M). However, their economic feasibility can be
improved through a number of federal and voluntary programs, which provide incentives for renewable
energy generation.

4.1 Industry Drivers
The economic feasibility of biogas generated from anaerobic digestion is impacted by industry drivers
including current market conditions, regulatory requirements, federal energy programs, availability of
third-party contracts, and spot market energy pricing. The following section describes these drivers,
including regulatory requirements, federal drivers, and voluntary market opportunities.

4.1.1 Regulatory Requirements
The FDEP has established regulatory requirements for biosolids, as discussed in Section 3.1. Anaerobic
digestion serves as a viable method to reach Class B biosolids designations, which can be land applied.
The biogas produced from anaerobic digestion can be used for a variety of functions which are discussed
in Sections 4.2, 4.3 and 4.4. Biogas not used must be flared according to local and federal air
regulations.

4.1.2 Renewable Fuel Standards Program
The Renewable Fuel Standard (RFS) program, authorized under Energy Policy Act of 2005, is a federal
initiative to reduce GHG emissions and expand the renewable fuel sector. The RFS promotes biofuel
production by providing financial credits in the form of renewable identification numbers (RINs) based
on fuel volumes. RINs are categorized based on biofuel type, with each type having a different D-code.
RNG produced from wastewater residuals (primary and waste activated sludges) is considered a
cellulosic fuel and has the designation of a D3 RIN. RNG produced from high strength waste is
considered an advanced biofuel and has the designation of a D5 RIN. As a note, high strength waste is
defined as food waste and FOG by EPA. Figure 4.1 shows historical D3 and D5 RIN prices. (Note: 11.727
RIN is equivalent to one MMBTU).

The EPA has historically dictated that fuels derived from co-digestion are advanced fuels (D5 RINs),
which are valued lower than cellulosic fuels (D3 RINs). However, the EPA announced with the “set rule”
in June 2023 a revised method for differentiation between the two. The methodology requires facilities
to track their cellulosic fuel feedstocks that are converted to renewable fuels and apply a 50-percent
discount on the calculated D3 RINs, with the remainder still being considered D5 RINs. This pathway has
been completed at several facilities recently, however as a conservative assumption, CDM Smith
considered all theoretical biogas produced to generate D5 RINs (see Section 7).

4.1.3 Voluntary Fixed Price Contracts
Alternatively, RNG-producing facilities may consider voluntary fixed price contracts to sell renewable
energy credits to third parties outside the RFS program for longer fixed periods. These parties are
generally large companies interested in buying renewable energy credits to meet internal or external
sustainability goals. The purchasing party does not need to use the purchased energy, nor does it need
to be geographically close to the RNG-producing facility. These contracts are usually on five-to-ten-year
timelines.

Fixed price contracts can be utilized to reduce risk to the RNG-producing facility by providing stable
revenue and reducing exposure to fluctuating RINs prices. A biogas producer may also consider a hybrid
revenue model, where an RNG-producing facility uses a portion of their RNG produced to enter into a
fixed price contract and uses their remaining RNG to enter into the RFS system.

Raw biogas is saturated with moisture, which can be corrosive and impact utilization performance. This

both CHP and RNG systems.

4.2.1.3 Siloxane Removal
Siloxanes are silicone-based compounds found in many consumer products. Upon combustion, they can
turn into small grains of sand or glass (silicon dioxide) and can accumulate within combustion systems.
This can lead to equipment fouling and reduce heat transfer efficiencies.

Typically, siloxanes are removed using activated carbon or other forms of media, which can be single use
or regenerated. Additionally, siloxane treatment requires H2S removal upstream, as H2S can plug the
siloxane removal media. Siloxane removal systems can operate at low pressure (3 to 5 psig) or high
pressure (100 psig+), depending on the application. Siloxane removal is required for RNG equipment and
is recommended for CHP equipment.

and disadvantages which impact capital cost, methane purity, and O&M efforts. Since the primary
purpose of these systems is to remove CO2 from biogas, pretreatment is typically required to remove

4.2.2.1 Liquid Scrubbing
Liquid scrubbing uses a transfer solvent in an absorption process, such as water or an amine solution.
This solvent absorbs CO2 and other biogas contaminants that are more soluble than CH4. The product
gas then exits the scrubber and is dried. A basic process flow diagram (PFD) of a liquid scrubbing process
is shown in Figure 4.5. Liquid scrubbing has a relatively low capital cost and a high methane recovery.
However, liquid scrubbing has a high-water consumption due to the nature of the treatment process,
requires drying of the exit gas, and may add oxygen to the CH4. Furthermore, these systems have
potential clogging concerns from bacterial growth and require tall towers.

4.2.2.2 Pressure Swing Adsorption
Pressure swing adsorption uses sets of vessels filled with media, which only allow CH4 to pass through.
These vessels cycle through pressure swing stages: adsorption (active treatment), depressurization,
recycling, regeneration (vacuum purge gas), and re-pressurization. A basic PFD of a PSA process is shown
in Figure 4.6. PSA facilities have been operating for longer periods compared to the other two treatment
methods, and their media can be regenerated. Some PSA systems can remove all impurities (H2S,
siloxanes, VOCs, and CO2) in a single step, eliminating the need for upstream treatment. However, PSA

4.2.2.3 Membrane Scrubbing
Membrane scrubbing employs a physical diffusion process, using thin membranes made of polymer.
These films are designed to selectively retain CH4, separating it from CO2 and other contaminants. These
systems usually consist of two or three stages to increase methane recovery. A basic PFD of a membrane
scrubbing process is shown in Figure 4.7. Membrane scrubbers are generally compact systems with
modular configurations allowing for easy scaling and have a high methane recovery. However,
pretreatment of the biogas is required for H2S and siloxane removal, multiple stages are needed, and
the membranes lose potency over time (>10 years), requiring replacement.

For GRU, a three-stage membrane scrubbing system is recommended due to the system size, high

4.2.2.4 Thermal Oxidizers
When cleaning biogas, all aforementioned technologies will produce a process waste stream consisting
of primarily CO2 with trace amounts of methane. This waste may need further treatment depending on
local air quality regulations. Due to the low methane concentration of the waste gas, a traditional flare
may not be used. The waste gas is recommended to be treated by a thermal oxidizer (TO). Thermal
oxidizers operate at a temperature of approximately 1,500°F and have an efficiency of > 99%.

Thermal oxidizers consist of a burner stack, gas train, and control valves. In a TO, waste gas is mixed with
natural gas or propane to combust trace amounts of methane in the waste gas. Supplemental natural
gas is required to heat the combustion chamber to 1500°F during the startup of the TO. Once a
sufficient internal temperature has been reached, the supplemental natural gas is adjusted to maintain
the operating temperature. Generally, for every 100 standard cubic feet per minute (scfm) of waste gas,
15 scfm of natural gas is required to maintain operating conditions.

With a three-stage membrane scrubbing system, a thermal oxidizer is likely not required and would not
be needed for GRU.

4.3 Combined Heat and Power Systems
CHP systems generate power and recover waste heat. The electricity produced can be used onsite or
implemented into the electrical utility, while the heat produced can be used to heat digesters or used
elsewhere onsite. CHP systems can have overall efficiencies as high as 80 percent.

There are three primary options for CHP systems: internal combustion engines, microturbines, or fuel
cells. CHP systems require biogas pretreatment, generally requiring the removal of moisture, H2S, and
siloxanes.

4.3.1.1 Internal Combustion Engines
Internal combustion engines function as traditional, piston driven reciprocating engines. The electricity
produced from the engine can be used for a variety of plant functions, and the excess heat produced can
be used for digester heating.

4.3.1.2 Microturbines
Microturbines employ small, controlled combustion systems where fuel combustion drives a turbine.
This electricity and heat produced can serve similar functions to those as the internal combustion
engines.

4.3.1.3 Fuel Cells
Fuel cells are composed of three primary subsystems: A fuel stack that generates direct current
electricity, a fuel processor that converts the biogas into a hydrogen-rich feed stream, and a power
conditioner that processes the electric energy into alternating or regulated direct current. However, the
biogas must be processed before being used as a fuel.

5.2 Anaerobic Digester Building
A new building is proposed to house the anaerobic digestion equipment, as shown in Figure 5.3 and

A section of the main processing floor within this building will be provided for thickening of KWRF WAS.

5.4.1.1 Kanapaha WRF Geotechnical Considerations
The Kanapaha WRF site is likely underlain by sandy soils with high infiltration capacity and shallow
groundwater (<5 ft). The site sits above an unconfined aquifer with limited confining material, increasing
susceptibility to karst-related settlement. Historical sinkhole incidents within 0.5 to 1 mile reinforce the
need for caution. Lineament analysis indicates multiple intersecting fracture traces suggesting potential
zones of weakness or subsurface void migration. These features, combined with the site's hydrogeologic
setting, elevate the risk of differential settlement and localized ground loss.

Foundation Recommendations
All structures should be supported on engineered foundations designed to span voids and tolerate
differential movement. Mat foundations may be suitable for lighter structures, provided subsurface
variability is low and ground improvement is confirmed. Heavier tanks and process equipment should be
supported on deep foundations advanced to competent bearing strata or on ground improvement (e.g.,
compaction grouting, rigid inclusions) to densify loose zones and mitigate karst risk. Foundation systems
must be compatible with groundwater fluctuations and designed to resist settlement induced by
subsurface instability.

Surface Water Management
Site grading must ensure positive drainage away from all building footprints. Roof runoff should be
routed to remote discharge points. If water is allowed to infiltrate near structures, there is increased
potential for triggering karst activity.

Excavation and Dewatering Challenges
Excavations will encounter shallow groundwater and highly permeable soils, making dewatering difficult
and potentially unstable. Traditional sump pumping may be ineffective; wellpoint systems or deep wells
may be required. Due to the high permeability of sandy soils, large excavations may be difficult to
maintain in the dry without additional ground modification. Dewatering efforts must be carefully staged
to avoid inducing settlement or destabilizing adjacent soils. Existing structures within the zone of
influence of dewatering systems are susceptible to settlement caused by groundwater drawdown and
should be monitored during construction.

increase the risk of instability and may exacerbate subsurface movement in karst-prone zones.

Excavation and Dewatering Challenges
Shallow groundwater and permeable soils will complicate excavation and dewatering. Traditional sump
pumping may be ineffective. Wellpoint systems or deep wells may be required. Large excavations may
be difficult to maintain in the dry without additional ground modification. Excavation support must
resist hydraulic uplift and lateral inflow. Dewatering efforts must be carefully staged to avoid inducing
settlement or destabilizing adjacent soils. Existing structures within the zone of influence should be
monitored for groundwater drawdown effects during construction.

7.1.1.1 Wastewater Sludge Biogas Modeling
The foundational calculation for estimating biogas production consisted of converting each feedstock’s
average daily flow in gallons per day to pounds per day. Then, this feedstock was multiplied by its
percent TS, then by its percent VS, then by its percent VSR to yield pounds per day of VSR. This was then
multiplied by an assumed biogas per pound VSR to yield the estimated biogas output in standard cubic
feet per day for each feedstock.

To create a range of daily flows from the plants, a normal distribution was created based on each
feedstock’s average flow and standard deviation, with upper and lower bounds of two times the
standard deviation. For example, the average daily flow and standard deviation from KWRF were 92,051
gal/day and 16,634 gal/day. A normal distribution from this plant would have upper and lower bounds
of 125,319 gal/day and 58,783 gal/day respectively. The Monte Carlo simulation evaluated 10,000
values that were selected randomly from this normal distribution to yield a range of feasible daily flows.
These values were then grown at a yearly rate based on population growth projections for 21 years to
yield 21,000 values of theoretical flow, with 10,000 data points at each year.

To create a range of percent TS values, a normal distribution was created based on each feedstock’s
average and standard deviation, with upper and lower bounds of two times the standard deviation. The
model randomly selected 10,000 values to create a list of possible percent TS values. This technique was
repeated to create lists of percent VS. Since percent VSR was not directly calculated, normal
distributions were created using assumed upper and lower bounds were. Each normal distribution
contained 10,000 data points.

To create theoretical biogas values, the feedstock data points were each multiplied by their respective
percent TS, percent VS, percent VSR, and biogas per pound VSR to yield a list of 21,000 theoretical
biogas values, with 10,000 values per year. The average and standard deviation were then calculated at

8.1 Sensitivity Analysis
The purpose of a sensitivity analysis is to identify and evaluate key variables of an RNG system that
could significantly impact the financial viability and operational performance of the proposed Regional
AD Facility. Recognizing that certain inputs are subject to variability and uncertainty, this analysis aims to
highlight the most influential factors affecting operating costs and revenue streams.

The sensitivity analysis consisted of two major components: static and variable. Static components were
not changed throughout analysis, as their values were considered consistent enough to not greatly
impact the financial viability of the AD Facility. Variable components were changed throughout the
analysis, as their values were considered critical to the viability of the system.

To determine the payback period of the proposed system, the Net Present Value of both the base
aerobic process and the proposed anaerobic digestion process and RNG system was calculated on a 20-
year time frame. A 20-year time frame was selected since the financial analysis did not include future
capital costs, as these would need to be accounted for beyond 20 years. The present value of an asset is
the current worth of a future cash flow that the asset is expected to produce. This method of calculation
accounts for inflation and provides the value of a future cash flow in today’s terms.

9.1.3 Greenhouse Gas Emissions
GHG emissions are typically quantified and reported by scope. The most accepted definitions of
emission Scopes 1, 2 and 3 were introduced by the GHG Protocol Corporate Standard (Greenhouse Gas
Protocol, 2015) to categorize emissions by ownership levels, i.e., direct (Scope 1) and indirect (Scopes 2
and 3) emissions. The relevant international best practice for reporting these emissions defines scopes
and example emissions for the waste sector (including wastewater treatment) as follows:

 ▬ Scope 1 – Direct GHG emissions from sources owned or controlled by the corporation from
 stationary combustion (e.g., boilers, flaring, standby generators), process fugitive emissions
 from treatment of wastewater and/or sludge (e.g., N2O and CH4 emissions), and mobile
 combustion (e.g., vehicles for transportation of waste/products).

 GAINESVILLE REGIONAL ANAEROBIC DIGESTION FACILITY FEASIBILITY STUDY │ PAGE 9-2

 9.0 │ GREENHOUSE GAS EMISSIONS ANALYSIS

 ▬ Scope 2 – Indirect GHG emissions from the generation of purchased electricity, heat, or steam
 that is consumed in its owned or controlled equipment or operations.
 ▬ Scope 3 – Indirect GHG emissions that, based on the selected control approach used in setting
 its organizational boundaries, are not owned or controlled by the corporation. Examples of
 upstream Scope 3 emissions include materials and consumables such as chemicals, purchased
 goods and services (including those for capital infrastructure works), waste generated by
 corporate operations, and employee travel and commuting. Examples of downstream Scope 3
 emissions include emissions associated with the use of treated wastewater, biosolids recycled to
 land, or sludge products (e.g., biogas) used as fuel at offsite processes.
For the context of this study, the following GHG emission sources and sinks were quantified to allow for
comparative evaluation of alternatives:

 ▬ Fugitive CH4 emission from anaerobic digestion facilities - potential sources include leakages
 from digesters, downstream storage tanks from associated pressure relief valves and pipework,
 and as a result of methane slip through inefficient flares or engines. A portion of the CH4
 generated is dissolved in the digestate, which can be released during the downstream
 dewatering process. In the absence of site-specific leak detection or measurement data, the
 fugitive CH4 emission is typically estimated as a fraction of the biogas generated. Environmental
 and Climate Change Canada (ECCC) uses a default emission factor of 2.1% (of biogas generated)
 for the National Inventory Report (NIR) (ECCC, 2023).
 ▬ Fugitive CH4 emission from biogas upgrading – a small fraction of CH4 can escape unprocessed
 during the biogas purification process. 0.5% of total biogas fed to the upgrading system is a
 typical value for CH4 slip in a 3-stage membrane system.
 ▬ Electricity – based on the net electricity purchased from the grid (i.e., subtracting the CHP
 electricity output).
 ▬ Polymers – for thickening and dewatering.

 ▬ Haulage – based on haulage distance and associated diesel fuel consumption.

 ▬ Biosolids management emissions – fuel use to spread biosolids/fertilizer on land, emissions (CH4
 or N2O) when biosolids are land applied or landfilled.
 ▬ Biosolids management offsets – carbon sequestration and commercial fertilizer offset resulted
 from beneficial use of biosolids or biosolids products.
For most of the GHG emission sources, the associated emission factors (EFs) are consistent with the
latest Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) 2019 Refinement to the 2006 IPCC Guidelines
for National Greenhouse Gas Inventories (referred to as the 2019 Refinement), or 2025 GHG Emission
Factors Hub unless specified otherwise. For electricity, the GHG EF is based on the e-Grid emission
factors for Florida Reliability Coordinating Council (FRCC). The 2024 Biosolids Emissions Assessment
Model (BEAM) update (referred to as BEAM2024) was used to estimate GHG emissions/offsets
associated with the biosolids management options (offsite) (Biosolids GHGs).

12.0 Conclusions and Recommendations
The feasibility study confirms that a Regional AD Facility is technically viable and represents a feasible
long-term solution for managing Gainesville’s wastewater residuals and organic waste streams while
advancing the City’s environmental sustainability objectives.

The financial analysis indicates that, under current market conditions and cost assumptions, the project
is not expected to achieve a positive net present value over a 20-year evaluation period. This outcome is
primarily driven by upfront capital investment requirements, uncertainty in long-term feedstock
availability, and variability in energy and byproduct markets. While these factors limit near-term
financial returns, they do not preclude future viability as funding mechanisms, market conditions, and
operational strategies evolve.

The Key Findings of the feasibility study are listed below:

 • Feedstock Availability: Adequate organic waste appears to exist within the region; however,
 long-term reliability and quality will require confirmation through formal supply agreements and
 pilot programs. Initial pilot efforts focused on source separation at restaurants, schools, and
 food wholesale establishments represent a logical first step toward securing dependable
 feedstock. As participation increases, phased enforcement mechanisms may be considered to
 improve capture rates, which have a direct and significant impact on biogas production and
 overall system performance. Additional opportunities may exist through partnerships with food
 processing facilities and the agricultural sector to further enhance co-digestion potential.

 • Site Evaluation: KWRF is the preferred site for the AD Facility due to existing infrastructure.
 KWRF is already the centralized biosolids producer for GRU and accepts thickened biosolids
 from MSWRF and UFWRF. There would be significant capital and operational costs associated
 with the other potential sites that limit their financial viability.

 • Financial Viability: Under current assumptions, only the extreme best-case scenario, which is
 an unlikely outcome, would achieve a positive NPV without external funding support. Sensitivity
 analyses indicate that partial capital cost participation—on the order of 50 percent—could
 materially improve long-term financial performance. While the availability and scale of future
 federal or state funding programs remain uncertain, these results highlight the importance of
 continued monitoring of grant, incentive, and partnership opportunities.

 • Environmental Impacts: Transitioning from aerobic digestion to anaerobic digestion would
 result in a significant reduction in greenhouse gas emissions, equivalent to removing
 approximately 6,000 gasoline-powered vehicles from the road annually. In addition, accepting
 food waste and fats, oils, and grease (FOG) would substantially expand the City’s capacity to
 meet its 2040 Zero Waste goals.

Overall, the updated feasibility study demonstrates that a Regional AD Facility offers meaningful
environmental and operational benefits that align with the City of Gainesville’s long-term sustainability
objectives, even though near-term financial returns are constrained under current conditions.

Executive Summary
CDM Smith partnered with the University of Florida Agricultural and Biological Engineering department
to complete biomethane potential (BMP) testing. BMP testing was used to determine the methane
production potential from anaerobic digestion using various scenarios expected at full scale. The
experimental set up included using 1-liter bottles dosed with an active inoculum to activate the
digestion process. Waste activated sludge (WAS) from the three WRFs in Gainesville (KWRF, MSWRF,
and UFWRF) were mixed using the proportion of WAS that would be expected at a full-scale facility.
Synthetic food waste was then added to the digester bottles at different ratios to simulate different
food collection scenarios based on community food waste availability and capture. The scenarios include
0/100 food waste to WAS ratio, 10/90, 30/70, and 50/50. Testing lasted 18 days during which time
biogas production was monitored continuously and methane content was measured once per week.
Results indicated a positive correlation with food waste content and methane production. The scenario
with 50% food waste produced the most methane and achieved the greatest solids removal. BMP
testing with feedstocks representing fats, oils and grease (FOG) was also carried out in a similar fashion,
and results indicating a strong positive correlation between the addition of FOG during co-digestion with
wastewater biosolids. These results highlight the benefit of co-digesting food waste and/or FOG with
wastewater biosolids to enhance methane production.

5.0 Methane Production Related to Fraction of
Food Waste or FOG Added
The correlations between maximum specific methane production and fraction of food waste or FOG
added in each BMP test is shown in Figures 5.1, 5.2, 5.3 and 5.4. Generally, a strong positive correlation
can be seen between methane production and the fraction of food waste or FOG added. Addition of
FOG as grease trap waste resulted in the highest observed methane production. Results also indicate
that addition of FOG may lead to a greater positive impact to methane production than addition of food
waste, highlighting the high energy content of FOG. These findings are consistent with published
literature and demonstrate the benefit of co-digesting food waste and/or FOG with wastewater
biosolids to enhance methane production.

Methane production from digestion of biosolids alone (the 0% condition) in each BMP test varied from
an average of 97 – 332 mL/g VS added. This variation could be due to compositional differences in the
biosolids and/or inoculum that were used in each BMP test. Each test was set up at a different time,
using a different inoculum and slightly different biosolids mixture. The variation could also be due to
differences in the food to microorganism ratio that was applied in each BMP test. The food waste BMP
test and FOG BMP Test 3 using grease trap waste had the most similar loading conditions among the
four BMP tests (500 mL of inoculum from bench-scale digesters, and 1.6-1.75 F:M ratio). However, the
biosolids used in these two tests were different. A composite from all three wastewater treatment
plants collected in February 2025 was used in the food waste BMP, while only biosolids collected from
Kanapaha in September 2025 were used in the grease trap waste BMP.

Further work could investigate the impact of inoculum and biosolids composition as well as food to
microorganism ratio on methane production. Additionally, further work should investigate BMP testing
using real food waste and co-digestion of both food waste and FOG with biosolids.

Appendix C – Full Sensitivity Analysis

 GAINESVILLE REGIONAL ANAEROBIC DIGESTION FACILITY FEASIBILITY STUDY │ PAGE C-1

Lifecycle and Operating Cost Analysis
To estimate the financial outcomes of anaerobic digestion, a sensitivity analysis was conducted on the
biogas outputs from the bioenergy modeling, as discussed in Section 7.2. The financial modeling was
conducted on an RNG system. Details of this system are discussed in Section 4.2.

Sensitivity Analysis
The purpose of a sensitivity analysis is to identify and evaluate key variables of an RNG system that
could significantly impact the financial viability and operational performance of the proposed Regional
AD Facility. Recognizing that certain inputs are subject to variability and uncertainty, this analysis aims to
highlight the most influential factors affecting operating costs and revenue streams.

The sensitivity analysis consisted of two major components: static and variable. Static components were
not changed throughout analysis, as their values were considered consistent enough to not impact the
financial viability of the AD facility. Variable components were changed throughout the analysis, as their
values were considered critical to the viability of the system. The definitions and assumptions used for
static and variable costs are included in Section 8 of this report.

To determine the payback period of the proposed system, the Net Present Value of both the base
anaerobic process and the proposed aerobic digestion and RNG system was calculated on a 20-year time
frame. A 20-year time frame was selected since the financial analysis did not include capital costs, as
these would need to be accounted for beyond 20 years. The present value of an asset is the current
worth of a future cash flow that the asset is expected to produce. This method of calculation accounts
for inflation and provides the value of a future cash flow in today’s terms.

The sensitivity analysis was conducted assuming a high capture rate of the high estimate of food waste
in Alachua County, which is approximately 13,000 gallons/day. The financial impacts of food waste
capture on the RNG system are discussed in detail in Section 8.3 of this report.

Net Present Value
The Net Present value of each system, as discussed in Section 8.1 of this report, was calculated for both
the base aerobic digestion system and proposed anaerobic digestion system. Each static cost and
revenue stream for the respective base and proposed systems were included in the calculation to create
an all-inclusive value for the payback period.

Since three variables were changed throughout the sensitivity analysis, RIN price, tipping fees, and
electricity rate, three different plots were produced. In each case, only one value was changed,
corresponding to the low, medium, and high values seen in Table 8.6. As a note, when each variable
component was changed, the other two variable components were kept at their medium value. For
example, when testing low, medium, and high power costs, RIN and tipping fee revenues were kept at
their medium value. The results of this analysis for RINs, tipping fees, and power costs can be seen in
