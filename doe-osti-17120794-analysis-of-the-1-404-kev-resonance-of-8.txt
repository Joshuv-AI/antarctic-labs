 Abstract
 The DANCE array was used to investigate neutron captures on 84 Kr as a
 function of neutron energy using the time-of-flight method. The multiplic-
 ity and total-energy information was used to determine the ratio of neutron
 captures populating the isomeric state versus the ground state of 85 Kr. This
 ratio is of importance for the understanding of the s-process nucleosynthesis
 in the krypton region. Our preliminary ratio of captures to ground state to
 total captures for the s-wave resonance at 1.404 keV is 0.195 ± 0.015, which
 is in good agreement with reported values in other energy regimes.

1 Astrophysical Motivation
When examining the origin of heavy elements in the universe, the observable
chemical signatures in our solar system originate from several different nucleosyn-
thesis processes and stellar sources. While not all of these are fully understood yet,
one important contributing factor is the accurate knowledge of the nuclear physics
data that form the basis of all stellar simulations and nucleosynthesis calculations.

Figure 1: Reaction network during s-process nucleosynthesis around the impor-
tant branch point 85 Kr. The s-process path closely follows the valley of stability
(black squares, stable isotopes in the chart of nuclei) since neutron capture time
scales (typically ten years) are much slower than most of the β-decay time scales
of the involved nuclei (blue squares).

Most of the elements heavier than iron are produced by the slow (s) and the rapid
(r) neutron capture processes. The s-process takes place during stellar He and C
burning phases with neutron densities between 108 and 1011 cm−3 . The reaction
path closely follows the valley of stability, since neutron capture times of typically
ten years are much slower than most of the β-decay times of the involved nuclei
(Figure 1) [1].
The s-process can be further split into its weak and main components [2]. The
main differences lie in the neutron-to-seed ratios, the temperatures as well as the
neutron densities. The main component of the s-process takes place at about 5 to

 3

25 keV in low-mass asymptotic giant branch (AGB) stars and produces the nuclei
with mass numbers above A ≈ 90 [3], which includes in particular the end point
of the s-process [4]. The weak component of the s-process takes place at about
90 keV in massive stars with Mstar > 8 M⊙ [5]. The time-integrated neutron flux
is lower compared to the main component producing nuclei with mass numbers
A ≤ 90.
The region around krypton is particularly interesting because the branching at
85
 Kr affects different pairs of observable abundance ratios with largely different
astrophysical impact (Figure 2). First, in particular, the abundance ratio 86 Kr/84 Kr
found in presolar mainstream SiC grains varies strongly as a function of grain size
[6]. It is therefore well-suited to test models of AGB stars [7]. Since the produc-
tion ratio 85 KrISO /85 KrGS affects the 86 Kr/84 Kr abundance ratio, this opportunity
to test the assumption of the stellar model can not be realized yet.

Figure 2: 85 Kr is a branch point in the s-process path. If the fraction of
84
 Kr(n,γ)85 Kriso is increased, the β − -branch becomes stronger and the corre-
sponding isotopes (green) will be produced more. At the same time, the (n,γ-
branch becomes weaker and the affected isotopes (orange) will be less produced.
Isotopes before and after the branching (black) are (almost) not affected.

The second interesting abundance ratio affected by the production branching is
87
 Rb/87 Sr [8, 9]. Because of the long half-life of 87 Rb, the solar 87 Rb/87 Sr abun-
dance ratio could, in principle, be used as a cosmochronometer for the determina-
tion of the age of the universe. However, the 85 KrISO /85 KrGS production ratio is
one of the important missing nuclear physics parameters for this approach.

2 DANCE
The Detector for Advanced Neutron Capture Experiments (DANCE) [10] is an
assembly of 160 BaF2 crystals of 4 different shapes (Figure 3). A 4π coverage
would require 162 crystals, but 2 opposite crystals (regular hexagons) are left out
to allow neutrons to pass through the array (Figure 4). A sample can be placed
in the center of the array. Neutron capture events can be detected based on the
emitted γ-rays.

Figure 3: Four different shapes of BaF2 crystals are the fundamental constituents
of the DANCE array (Figure 4). They are designed to cover the entire solid angle
with 12 regular pentagons (red), 60 irregular hexagons of 2 shapes each (yellow,
blue), and 30 regular hexagons (green) [11]. The dimensions of the bare crystals
are designed to form a BaF2 shell with an inner radius of 17 cm and a thickness
of 15 cm.

 5

Figure 4: The actual DANCE array does not cover the entire solid angle. Two
(sometimes even more) crystals are always left out to leave space for the beam
pipe (gray). The crystals are covered with material and the resulting gaps lead to
a further reduction in angular coverage. In total, about 90% of the solid angle is
covered by BaF2 crystals.

DANCE is located on the 20 m neutron flight path 14 (FP14) at the Manuel Lu-
jan Jr. Neutron Scattering Center at the Los Alamos Neutron Science Center
(LANSCE) [12]. The neutrons are produced via spallation reactions caused by an
800 MeV proton beam impinged upon a tungsten target with typical beam currents
of 80-100 µA.
Most of the energy released after the neutron capture will be emitted as γ-rays,
which means that a cut on the sum of the energies deposited in all of the 160 de-
tector modules provides a sensitivity to the populated state. The energy difference
for captures populating isomer and ground state of 85 Kr is 305 keV. The possibil-

ity of distinguishing reaction channels based on the Q-value of the reaction has
been described earlier [13] and performed at DANCE for isomeric states higher
than 1 MeV [14, 15], and therefore makes this analysis promising.

energy. This can be done using known peaks from γ-calibration sources, shown
in Table 1. One such source used was 60 Co, which has two peaks at close energies.
However, due to the resolution of DANCE, these two peaks 60 Co spectrum could

A linear calibration was chosen due to the stability of the fit at extremes and when
extrapolating. Each crystal was examined independently to find the channel where
the γ-energies in Table 2 were located. Then, a linear fit was applied between
the channel number and known energies. A calibration function of one of the
crystals is shown in Fig 8. Parameters were then extracted from the fit (p0 and
p1 in Figure 8) and the data were calibrated according to these parameters. It is
important to note that (0,0) was not used as a calibration point nor a restriction on
the offset to give the fitting code greater freedom. Despite this, 0 is still within
the error bounds of the offset parameter, and given this fact and the χ2 value, the
calibration was accepted.

5 DICEBOX-GEANT Simulations
DICEBOX [17] is a Monte Carlo code meant to simulate γ-deexcitations from an
excited level in a nucleus. It is especially useful to simulate deexcitations from
levels that are at a high enough energy that the level structure is not well known,
e.g. after a neutron capture. GEANT is a Monte-Carlo-based detector simulation
code capable of simulating all the necessary interactions of elementary particles
with very complex volumes [18, 19].
Combinations of DICEBOX and GEANT have been used very successfully used
over the last decades to infer the structure of compound nuclei by comparisons of
simulated and experimental data. This holds true, in particular for nuclei produced
via neutron captures [15, 20].
The first step was to simulated the DANCE response to DICEBOX cascades fol-
lowing s-wave neutron captures on 84 Kr. Cascades populating the isomeric state
were treated separately from those bypassing the isomer. In this particular case,
the difference is not only the energy of the highest-energy peak of the total energy
spectrum (Figure 9); the entire shape of the response is different due to the huge
difference in the cluster multiplicity distribution (Figure 10). All adjacent crystals
that have fired during an event are combined into a cluster and the number of clus-

 11

ters in an event is called cluster multiplicity [11]. The cluster multiplicity closely
reflects the number of γ-rays emitted during the de-excitation of the nucleus.

Figure 9: Simulated total energy distribution in the DANCE array for one DICE-
BOX model. The data were not treated for pile-up. 106 cascades were simulated
for captures into ground (blue) and isomeric (red) state respectively.

Figure 10: Cluster multiplicity distribution in the DANCE array for the same

The second step was to emulate the effect of pile-up. This step is very important
since the data rate for resonance energies is rather high and the integration time
of the digitizers is rather long (800 ns). The effect of pile-up on the total-energy
spectrum is two-fold. If 2 events are pile-up outside the coincidence window
(5-10 ns), the first event can only gains energy if the second event contributes
to crystals that have already fired during the first event. The second event can
only lose energy, because some crystals are already assigned to the first event.
Figure 11 shows how this mechanism affects the total-energy distribution for an
average of 10 events randomly distributed over the 800 ns integration window.
Figure 12 shows the multiplicity distribution for the same data. While the total-
energy distribution changes significantly, the effect on the multiplicity distribution
is only minor.

The final step was to perform the pile-up simulation for a mixture of cascades
leading to the ground and isomeric state. This mixture is based on ratio of the
neutron captures populating the ground state to the total number of captures and
rages from 0 to 1. The pile-up emulation was done for the entire possible ratio
range in steps of 0.01.

Other measurements of this ratio report values of 0.32±0.026 at thermal energies
[21] and 0.18 [22]. Since the neutron captures at thermal energies are dominated
by s-wave captures and the investigated resonance at 1.404 keV is also due to s-
wave captures, the ratio of ground state to total capture might be expected to be
very similar. This is indeed the case for the recommended value by Holden [22].
That being said, there are competing physics effects which could lead to different
feeding ratios for different resonances, even of the same J π . The level density in
85
 Kr is not particularly high compared to mid-mass rare-earth elements, e.g. Past
studies have shown there can be significant variation in the primary transitions in
nuclei with lower level densities [23]. Past studies of statistical approaches to pre-
dicting isomer population showed difficulties in predicting the isomer ratio when
the primary transition did not populate a state in the statistical regime [15]. This
makes the results of this study particularly revealing as it tests the competition
between these different effects.
There are different sets of measurements in the astrophysically interesting energy
regime, which is a mixture of mostly s- and p-wave captures. The value with
the smallest systematic uncertainty comes from an activation measurement where
both the number of atoms in the isomeric state and the number of atoms in the
ground state were independently detected. This ratio is therefore not affected
by the neutron flux determination [24]. For a broad neutron spectrum, roughly
corresponding to a Maxwellian spectrum with kT = 42.5 keV, the authors found
0.26 ± 0.03. This value is very close to our value.
Independent measurements of the total cross section at the s-process reference
temperature of kT = 30 keV based on the time-of-flight method [25, 26] in com-
bination with measurements of the population of the isomer via activation in the
keV-regime [27, 28] results in values around 0.5 [29].

7 Future Work
While the ratio for the 1.404 keV resonance has been extracted, further work is
still necessary to extract the branching ratio for other resonances in 84 Kr. Since
statistics were low, the experimental data runs need to be added together before
any reasonable fitting can be performed. In addition, more precise simulations

 19

involving p-waves need to be simulated in DICEBOX to accurately capture the
physics at higher energy resonances. Further, expanding the number of reso-
nances for testing the competition between the role of the primary transition and
the angular momentum will provide additional insight into our understanding of
the nucleus.
More work is necessary to extract the 78 Kr(n,γ) branching ratio. The difference
in γ cascades to the ground state and isomer are much smaller compared to in
85
 Kr which makes it more challenging to disentangle the ground state and isomer
feedings from each other. In addition, whereas the 85 Kr isomer multiplicity spec-
trum was almost entirely multiplicity one and the ground state dominated higher
multiplicities, the multiplicity spectrum in 79 Kr is less stratified, which increases
the difficulty of disentangling the feedings to both states.

8 Summary
Previous 84 Kr(n,γ) data were first calibrated and then fit to DICEBOX-GEANT
simulations. From these simulations, a preliminary value for the branching ratio
between ground state and isomer feeding of the compound nucleus was deter-
mined for the 1404 eV resonance in 84 Kr.
