Single-atom Cu catalyst in a zirconium MOF for highly selective hydrogenation of unsaturated aldehydes to unsaturated alcohols
Authors: Yushan Wu, Yao Luo, Mingyue Ding, Anmin Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Achieving nearly 100% selectivity in the hydrogenation of α,β-unsaturated aldehydes to unsaturated alcohols, while maintaining complete conversion, remains a significant challenge in heterogeneous catalysis, especially with non-noble metals. Herein, we report an efficient single-atom Cu catalyst (Cu1@MOF-808) constructed by covalently anchoring Cu atoms onto defective sites of Zr oxide clusters in MOF-808, where Cu species are chelated and stabilized via coordination with oxygen atoms from dangling –OH/–OH2 groups. This synergistic, spatially dispersed adsorption mechanism drives highly selective CO bond hydrogenation. The resultant Cu1@MOF-808 catalyst achieves nearly 100% selectivity toward cinnamyl alcohol (COL) with full conversion of cinnamaldehyde (CAL). Mechanistic studies revealed that the high oxidation state of single-atom Cu sites, coupled with electron-rich oxygen coordination and Lewis acid sites, enables spatial separation between reactant adsorption and proton transfer, thereby effectively regulating the product desorption and optimizing the reaction pathway.

Introduction

Chemoselective hydrogenation of α,β-unsaturated aldehydes to unsaturated alcohols is of fundamental and practical significance in both scientific research and industry applications, as these compounds are valuable and versatile intermediates in the production of pharmaceuticals, cosmetics, resins, fragrances, and dyes.1–5 Nevertheless, the intrinsic thermodynamic and kinetic favorability of CC hydrogenation over CO reduction poses a persistent selectivity challenge for heterogeneous catalysts (Scheme 1A).6–12 Conventional supported metal catalysts (Pt, Au, Pd, Ni, Co, etc.) have been employed for this transformation,13–23 but they often suffered from low selectivity due to the preferential adsorption and activation of the CC bond on extended metal surfaces.

Representative catalytic systems for the hydrogenation of α,β-unsaturated aldehydes to unsaturated alcohols. (A) Monometal-supported catalysts. (B) Modified catalysts, including bimetallic catalysts, sterically hindered catalysts, encapsulated catalysts, and confined catalysts. (C) This work: single-atom Cu catalyst, Cu1@MOF-808.

To circumvent this limitation, two classical strategies have been developed: modification of electronic properties by introducing second metals24–33 and construction of spatial steric hindrance34–38 or a confinement effect6,39–41 (Scheme 1B). In addition, non-contact hydrogenation42 and an oscillation potential strategy43 have also been employed to improve the hydrogenation selectivity. While these approaches have achieved some success, they are largely empirical and offer limited capacity for the precise, bottom-up design of the catalytic environment. More fundamentally, these strategies still operate within the paradigm of modifying metal-based active sites, rather than rationally engineering the entire catalytic microenvironment.

Single-atom catalysts (SACs), with their well-defined coordination structures and maximum atom utilization efficiency, provide an ideal model system for probing structure–selectivity relationships.44,45 However, the local environment of the isolated metal centers is inherently constrained by the support's surface chemistry, making it difficult to independently tune the microenvironment without altering the metal's intrinsic electronic state. We hypothesized that metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) could offer a transformative solution to this problem. Unlike conventional supports, MOFs have chemically tunable pore surfaces, adjustable coordination geometries, and programmable functionality at the molecular level.46–50 Such construction, which leverages the highly tunable nature of MOF matrices for atomic-level precision in regulating the coordination environment and electronic states of active metal species, may offer a pathway to enhance the selectivity of the targeted catalytic reactions toward desired products.

Herein, we demonstrate a MOF-based microenvironment engineering strategy to achieve highly chemoselective hydrogenation of α,β-unsaturated aldehydes. The isolated Cu atoms were covalently attached to the defect sites of the Zr clusters in MOF-808, forming abundant Zr–O–Cu–O–Zr structures in the frameworks, which facilitated the exposure of catalytic sites (Scheme 1C). The precise spatial confinement of MOF and atomically dispersed positively charged Cu species, as well as electron-rich coordination O atoms, coupled with the Lewis acid sites, enabled the single-atom Cu1@MOF-808 catalyst to selectively promote CO bond hydrogenation while completely suppressing CC bond hydrogenation. Consequently, the catalyst achieved 99.7% selectivity to cinnamyl alcohol (COL) at full conversion of cinnamaldehyde (CAL), outperforming other non-precious metal-based catalysts and rivaling Pt-based systems. The Cu1@MOF-808 catalyst showed no significant decay in terms of activity and selectivity over five consecutive cycling tests, indicating the robust nature of the catalyst. Moreover, it effectively catalyzed the hydrogenation of diverse α,β-unsaturated aldehydes to their corresponding unsaturated alcohols with satisfactory selectivity. This work demonstrates MOF-enabled microenvironment engineering as a general and rational design strategy for selectivity control in single-atom catalysis.

Results

Synthesis and characterization of catalysts

MOF-808 was synthesized by a modified solvothermal method, and it possesses a 6,3-connected 3D structure with spn topology, featuring a long-range ordered framework comprising both tetrahedral and adamantine-type cages (Fig. 1A and S1).51 Cu chelated MOF-808 (Cu1@MOF-808) was prepared by a facile and scalable post-modification method. The activated MOF-808 powder was dispersed in DMF solution containing a certain amount of copper salt under constant heating, during which the Cu ions were coordinated to the oxygen atoms of dangling –OH or –OH2 ligands on Zr6 clusters of the MOF. The Cu species were precisely chelated and stabilized by the nodes of MOF-808 (Fig. 1B). Further activation under dynamic vacuum was performed to remove guest molecules and generate active Cu-anchored MOF-808. Finally, reduction treatment in a H2/Ar mixed atmosphere was conducted, resulting in the creation of reduced Cu1@MOF-808. The accurate Cu loading in Cu1@MOF-808 was calculated to be 1.37 wt%, which was determined by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES, Table S1).

MOF-808 displayed an octahedral shape with an average particle size of approximately 700 nm (Fig. S2), as determined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The morphology of reduced Cu1@MOF-808 crystals could be maintained after chelation of Cu species and reduction treatment (Fig. S3 and S4), without obvious Cu nanoparticles or clusters on the surface of MOF, implying the even distribution of Cu species. Moreover, high-angle annular dark-field scanning TEM (HAADF-STEM) was used to analyze the structures of reduced Cu1@MOF-808. No apparent lattice fringes assigned to metallic Cu or CuOx species were present, further suggesting the encapsulation of Cu atoms in the pores of MOF-808 (Fig. 1C–E). Additionally, the elemental mapping of Cu matched well with C, O, and Zr elements, suggesting the even distribution of Cu species throughout the entire single MOF crystal (Fig. 1F–J), as evidenced by energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS).

Cu1@MOF-808 showed similar sharp powder X-ray diffractions (PXRD) compared with the as-synthesized MOF-808 and the simulated one, indicating the high crystallinity of Cu1@MOF-808 (Fig. 2A). Moreover, no characteristic reflections assigned to Cu species were observed, suggesting the high dispersion of Cu species over the MOF support. Cu1@MOF-808 displayed high thermal stability up to 290 °C (Fig. S5), as evidenced by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA). The N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms revealed a Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) surface area of 1586 m2 g−1 for Cu1@MOF-808, which was lower than that of MOF-808 (2158 m2 g−1) as predicted (Fig. S6a). In addition, the pore size distribution revealed a small reduction after Cu chelation from 1.53 nm to 1.26 nm (Fig. S6b). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was performed to test the chemical valence state of Cu1@MOF-808 (Fig. 2B and S7).The binding energies centered at 934.6 eV (Cu 2p3/2) and 953.6 eV (Cu 2p1/2), along with two distinct satellites, were visible for fresh Cu1@MOF-808 (Fig. 2B). These characteristic satellites belonged to Cu2+ ions.52,53 Meanwhile, Cu 2p3/2 and Cu 2p1/2 at binding energies of 932.8 and 952.8 eV were present in fresh Cu1@MOF-808, corresponding to Cu1+ or Cu0 species. However, the existence of Cu0 was ruled out by Auger electron spectroscopy (AES) of Cu LMM at a binding energy of 572.6 eV (Fig. 2C), indicating the existence of Cu1+ in fresh Cu1@MOF-808.53–55 The Cu1+ species might result from partial reduction of Cu2+ during the synthetic process due to the reducibility of the DMF solvent.56 These results manifested the co-presence of both Cu1+ and Cu2+ in fresh Cu1@MOF-808. After reduction treatment, only Cu1+ species were present in reduced Cu1@MOF-808, which was confirmed by the combination of the disappearance of characteristic satellites of Cu2+ and the observation of a typical Cu1+ signal in Cu LMM spectra (Fig. 2B and C).

Furthermore, X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) and extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) were employed to identify the electronic structure and local coordination environment of Cu species in reduced Cu1@MOF-808 (Fig. 2). The normalized XANES spectra of absorption edges for Cu1@MOF-808 shifted to higher energy as compared with Cu foil (Fig. 2D and S8), revealing the positively charged state of Cu atoms. Moreover, the absorption edge of Cu species in Cu1@MOF-808 was very close to that of the Cu2O reference, implying that the average oxidation state of Cu atoms was around +1.57,58 In the EXANES spectra of Cu2O, CuO, and Cu foil, the peaks at ∼2.66, ∼2.42, and ∼2.23 Å were attributed to the Cu–O–Cu and Cu–Cu bonds (Fig. 2E and S9–S11).48 Besides, the peaks at ∼1.47 and ∼1.53 Å were assigned to the Cu–O bond for Cu2O and CuO,58,59 respectively. In contrast, only a single peak located at approximately 1.50 Å was observed in Cu1@MOF-808, which is attributed to Cu–O coordination (Fig. 2E). The absence of a Cu–Cu bond peak further confirmed the atomic dispersion of Cu species.58 One prominent peak located at around 1.42 Å, a fingerprint peak attributed to Cu–O coordination,58,59 was also found in the Fourier transformed EXAFS spectra in the R and K space in Cu1@MOF-808 (Fig. 2F and G). Subsequently, the detailed atomic structures of Cu atoms were analyzed by fitting the EXAFS spectra (Table S2). The coordination number of the Cu–O bonding in the first coordination sphere was 2.15 ± 0.30 at a distance of 1.97 ± 0.01 Å, suggesting that one Cu atom was coordinated with two O atoms. To further elucidate the atomic dispersion of Cu species, the wavelet transform (WT) of the Cu K-edge EXAFS oscillation was analyzed (Fig. 2H). The WT contour plots of Cu1@MOF-808 exhibited only one intensity maximum at approximately 5.2 Å−1, which corresponds to the Cu–O coordination. In contrast to the reference samples (Cu foil, Cu2O, and CuO), no intensity maxima associated with Cu–Cu or Cu–O–Cu contributions were observed.59

Selective CO bond hydrogenation

The selective hydrogenation of α,β-unsaturated aldehydes to unsaturated alcohols was performed, and cinnamaldehyde (CAL) was used as the model chemical compound (Table 1). First, the hydrogenation of CAL was conducted in isopropanol solvent at 100 °C and 2 MPa H2 pressure. Control experiments confirmed that no reaction occurred either in the absence of a catalyst or when using only MOF-808 as a catalyst (Table 1, entries 1 and 2). In marked contrast, the reduced Cu1@MOF-808 catalyst could effectively catalyze the selective hydrogenation of CAL toward target cinnamyl alcohol (COL) with 99.6% selectivity at 71.6% conversion (Table 1, entry 3). When the reaction time was prolonged to 10 hours, the CAL conversion reached nearly 100% while preserving high selectivity to COL (99.7%) (Table 1, entry 4). For comparison, other catalysts (Pt1@MOF-808, Pd1@MOF-808, Cu1@UO-66, and Cu/ZrO2, which are carefully characterized, see the SI, Fig. S12–S22) were also systematically evaluated under identical reaction conditions. Pt1@MOF-808 and Pd1@MOF-808 displayed full CAL conversion, but no COL was obtained; instead, the over-hydrogenation product hydrocinnamyl alcohol (HCOL) was obtained (Table 1, entries 5 and 6). Moreover, neither Cu1@UiO-66 nor Cu/ZrO2 exhibited catalytic activity for this reaction (Table 1, entries 7 and 8). Notably, when the temperature was raised to 150 °C, Cu1@UiO-66 gave 73.5% conversion of CAL with excellent selectivity toward COL (>99%, Table S3). In contrast, even at an elevated temperature of 200 °C, Cu/ZrO2 showed ultralow activity (<3% conversion) and produced a mixture of COL, hydrocinnamaldehyde (HCAL), and HCOL with a selectivity of 51.4%, 34.8%, and 13.8% (Table S3), respectively.

Furthermore, the effects of pressure and temperature on CAL hydrogenation over the Cu1@MOF-808 catalyst were investigated. The catalytic performance showed minimal dependence on hydrogen pressure (Table S4), while the reaction temperature exerted a pronounced effect on both activity and selectivity (Table S5). At 80 °C, the reaction achieved high COL selectivity (>99%) with only 27.5% CAL conversion. Remarkably, when the temperature increased to 100 °C, the conversion increased to nearly 100% without compromising the product selectivity (Table S5). Upon further elevation of temperature, a gradual decline in COL selectivity was observed, accompanied by the formation of side products, ascribed to the occurrence of a condensation reaction at high temperature,60 as confirmed by GC-MS (Fig. S26). The solvent effect was studied over the Cu1@MOF-808 catalyst (Fig. S27). In comparison with nonpolar solvent (n-hexane) and aprotic polar solvent (tetrahydrofuran), protic solvent (isopropyl alcohol) was more beneficial for the CAL hydrogenation reaction. Under the optimized conditions, Cu1@MOF-808 delivered complete CAL conversion, 99.7% COL selectivity, and a superior productivity of 23.2 h−1, outperforming non-precious metals by an order of magnitude (Table S6) and rivaling precious metal-based catalysts (Table S7).

The evolution of reactant and product distributions over Cu1@MOF-808 as a function of time was investigated. The CAL conversion increased gradually over time, reaching full conversion after 10 h. Throughout the reaction, COL remained the sole product, with no observable changes in selectivity even after prolonged reaction times (Fig. S28). These results demonstrated the excellent selectivity of Cu1@MOF-808 toward CO bond hydrogenation, while effectively suppressing the hydrogenation of the CC bond. Moreover, the catalyst was reusable for at least five consecutive cycles without significant decay in activity and selectivity (Fig. S29), implying outstanding stability and durability. After cycling, PXRD confirmed the well-retained crystallinity and structure of MOF-808 (Fig. S30), and HAADF-STEM showed no observable Cu clusters or nanoparticles (Fig. S31). A negligible leaching of Cu species was detected, as evidenced by ICP-OES analysis (Table S8). XPS revealed new features assigned to Cu2+ ions (Fig. S32), implying partial oxidation of Cu1+ to Cu2+ during cycling. All these results demonstrated the robustness of the Cu1@MOF-808 catalyst for selective CO bond hydrogenation, an industrially desirable feature.

To further demonstrate the universality of Cu1@MOF-808, a series of α,β-unsaturated aldehyde substrates were investigated (Table 2). As anticipated, cinnamyl aldehyde and its various phenyl derivatives, such as p-methylcinnamaldehyde, p-fluorcinnamaldehyde, and p-bromocinnamaldehyde, were all converted to their corresponding unsaturated alcohols and achieved high selectivity (97–99%, Table 2, entries 1–4). Furthermore, when the heterocyclic substrate 3-(2-furyl)acrolein was employed, the corresponding unsaturated alcohol was obtained with 92% selectivity (Table 2, entry 5). In particular, aldehydes containing a benzoyl group, for instance benzalacetophenone, could be transformed into the corresponding unsaturated alcohols (Table 2, entry 6). It is generally difficult to achieve linear selective synthesis of unsaturated alcohols from chain unsaturated aldehydes due to the fact that they lack conjugated functional groups on their CC bond, resulting in a negligible effect of spatial steric hindrance, which makes it easy for CC bond hydrogenation. In our catalytic system, the hydrogenation of the CC bond was restrained and the target linear unsaturated alcohols were achieved in high selectivity (Table 2, entries 7–9). All these results suggested that Cu1@MOF-808 exhibited excellent tolerance to a variety of substrates.

Reaction mechanism investigation

To gain more insight into the reaction mechanism, several control experiments were conducted. To rule out the possibility that the reaction pathway proceeds via a catalytic transfer hydrogenation (CTH) route, we conducted a comparative experiment under an inert atmosphere. The reaction did not proceed under an N2 atmosphere (Table S9), confirming that the hydrogen originated from H2. When HCAL was used as the substrate, HCOL was identified as the main product over both Cu1@MOF-808 and Pt1@MOF-808 catalysts (Fig. 3A and Table S10). However, when COL was employed as the reactant, Pt1@MOF-808 hydrogenated the CC bond to form HCOL, whereas Cu1@MOF-808 completely suppressed this reaction (Fig. 3A and Table S11). These results demonstrated that Cu1@MOF-808 exhibited excellent hydrogenation selectivity for the CO bond while fully inhibiting CC bond hydrogenation.

Temperature programmed desorption (TPD) was performed to study the H2 activation capacity of catalysts (Fig. 3B). The temperature for the TPD test increased from 50 to 250 °C at a rate of 5 °C min−1 and was then held at 250 °C for 1 h. Three desorption peaks located at around 175, 230, and 250 °C were observed for Cu1@MOF-808 and Cu1@UiO-66, which may be attributed to the desorption of hydrogen spilled over onto the Zr-oxide cluster (Fig. S33).61 Note that the total H2 desorption amounts of Cu1@MOF-808 were higher than those of Cu1@UiO-66, suggesting the higher hydrogenation ability of Cu1@MOF-808, which was consistent with the high activity (Table 1). However, Pt1@MOF-808 displayed much lower desorption peaks at about 122 °C and 198 °C, suggesting a weak interaction between the catalyst and H2 molecules. This result demonstrated the higher activation ability for H2 over Pt1@MOF-808, which accounts for its highest activity. For comparison, Cu/ZrO2 showed no noticeable H2 desorption peaks, explaining its poor performance (Fig. S33).

Pyridine-IR spectroscopy was conducted to identify the acid type and the amount of catalysts. Generally, the bands located at around 1610/1450 and 1540 cm−1 were assigned to the Lewis and Brønsted acid sites,62,63 respectively. However, the bands in the range of 1400–1700 cm−1 were difficult to distinguish from those of MOFs with strong peaks because of the benzene ring and carboxylate vibrations. As a result, we focused on the signal peaks in the interval of 1000 to 1100 cm−1.64 Three characteristic peaks at around 1069, 1043, and 1013 cm−1 were observed (Fig. 3C), corresponding to the ν18, ν12, and ν1 ring models of pyridine adsorbed on Zr clusters, respectively, which acted as Lewis acid sites.65 The Lewis acid sites would facilitate the adsorption and activation of the carbonyl group, thus enhancing the reaction selectivity.11,14 Cu1@MOF-808 exhibited the highest Lewis acid amount of 140.7 µmol g−1 at 200 °C, which was higher than those of Pt1@MOF-808 (122.5 µmol g−1), Cu1@UiO-66 (79.8 µmol g−1), and Cu/ZrO2 (11.9 µmol g−1) (Fig. S34 and Table S12). This result was generally consistent with the catalytic performance, demonstrating that Lewis acid sites played a key role in the selective hydrogenation of the CO bond.

Furthermore, attenuated total reflectance infrared (ATR-IR) spectra were recorded to evaluate the adsorption of CAL on the surfaces of different catalysts (Fig. 3D). The pure CAL displayed two characteristic vibration peaks at 1677 cm−1 (CO) and 1625 cm−1 (CC).66 Upon adsorption on Cu1@MOF-808 and Pt1@MOF-808, both the CO and CC peaks exhibited a slight but consistent blueshift (1680 cm−1vs. 1677 cm−1; 1627 cm−1vs. 1625 cm−1, respectively). These results indicated that CAL adsorbed on Cu1@MOF-808 and Pt1@MOF-808 through both the CC and CO bonds. By correlating the distinct hydrogenation products over Cu1@MOF-808 and Pt1@MOF-808 with ATR-IR analysis, we proposed that the reaction pathway depends on factors beyond just CAL's adsorption configuration. To elucidate the origin of Cu1@MOF-808's superior performance and underlying reaction mechanism, we conducted density functional theory (DFT) calculations.

We first established stable structural models of Cu and Pt atoms anchored on MOF-808 (Fig. 4A and S35). The intrinsic Lewis acidity of the MOF-808 node originated from its Zr6–O clusters, creating an electron-withdrawing coordination environment for the anchored metal atoms. Atomic charge distribution analysis revealed distinct oxidation states induced by oxygen coordination, with Cu and Pt losing 0.59 and 0.26|e| to the coordinated oxygen atoms (Fig. 4B), respectively. The substantially greater electron transfer from Cu (0.59 vs. 0.26|e|) indicated that the Lewis-acid-driven metal–support interaction is markedly stronger for Cu sites, resulting in a higher-valence Cu center with depleted d-electron density. Guided by these electronic structure differences, we systemically evaluated competitive adsorption and hydrogen atom capture at these single-atom centers—key factors governing the selective hydrogenation mechanism of CAL. For both Cu and Pt sites, the adsorption of CC, CO, and H atoms was considered. The calculated adsorption energies followed the order CC > CO > H (Fig. 4C), suggesting that both CO and CC bonds are preferentially activated on Cu1@MOF-808 and Pt1@MOF-808. Bader charge analysis further confirmed that greater charge transfer promotes stronger adsorption (Fig. S36). Critically, the Lewis-acid-induced electron depletion at the Cu site led to weaker CC adsorption relative to Pt, because the diminished d-electron density at Cu reduced the back-donation from the metal d-orbitals to the π* antibonding orbitals of CC. This electronic effect is the microscopic origin of the experimentally observed suppression of CC hydrogenation on Cu1@MOF-808.

Further analysis addressed the origin of H atoms in CAL hydrogenation. On Cu1@MOF-808, H* preferentially adsorbs on the Lewis-basic coordinated oxygen (adjacent to the Lewis-acidic Zr center), benefiting from its high electron density (−1.00|e|). This creates a spatial separation between CAL adsorbed on Cu and H* on oxygen. On Pt1@MOF-808, the lower oxidation state of Pt enables a co-adsorption configuration of CC and H*, with an adsorption energy as low as −3.05 eV (Fig. 4D and S37). These distinct behaviors reflect different underlying mechanisms: in Cu1@MOF-808, the Lewis acidity of the node enforces substantial electron transfer between Cu and coordinated oxygen, creating a synergistic bifunctional environment where Cu captures CAL and the oxygen captures H in the initial stage. In contrast, the electron-rich Pt site in Pt1@MOF-808 retains its metallic character and higher electron density, thus acting as an independent active center.

Based on experimental observations and product distribution analysis, two possible reaction pathways were proposed. Path 1 involves the preferential attack of H* on the CC bond, generating HCAL as an intermediate, which undergoes complete hydrogenation to yield HCOL. Path 2 proceeds via selective addition of H* to the CO group to form COL as the primary product, followed by hydrogenation to HCOL (Fig. 4E). The energy profiles and intermediate structures of CAL hydrogenation on the two catalytic models are presented in Fig. 4F and G. Building upon the established preferential activation of the CC bond, the CO bond and the H* captured by coordinated oxygen adopt a vertically aligned configuration over Cu1@MOF-808 (inset in Fig. 4D), requiring only a 0.61 eV migration barrier. Subsequent attack on the C atom exhibited a comparable barrier of 0.63 eV, with a maximum adsorption energy change of 0.47 eV for these two elementary steps. In contrast, initial hydrogenation of the CC bond encountered a considerable barrier of 1.12 eV, making this pathway unfavorable. This selective low-barrier process benefits from steric effects induced by the coordination structure that activates H species, thereby facilitating target product formation. For Pt1@MOF-808, the first elementary step of CO bond hydrogenation showed a strong adsorption energy (−3.36 eV); however, further hydrogenation was precluded by a high energy barrier of 1.96 eV, rendering this pathway unviable. We then evaluated the feasibility of Path 1 for CC bond hydrogenation. The second protonation step during HCAL formation was endothermic by 1.18 eV with a 1.54 eV barrier and is considerably more favorable than Path 2. Consequently, HCAL was thermodynamically driven to further hydrogenate to HCOL on Pt1@MOF-808. These findings aligned with the established hydrogenation selectivity of noble metals, where the combined effect of CC adsorption and H* activation on Pt surfaces favors saturated alcohol production. Therefore, comparative simulations identify three superior features of Cu1@MOF-808: (1) a unique electronic structure with strongly interacting high-valence Cu sites and electron-rich coordinated oxygen, both originating from the Lewis acidity of the MOF-808 node; (2) dispersed adsorption of reactants and H* that lowers the initial protonation barrier and optimizes the reaction pathway; and (3) moderate adsorption strength that facilitates product desorption.

Conclusions

In summary, we successfully engineered atomically dispersed Cu species stabilized in MOF defects through Cu–O coordination, which effectively prevented their aggregation and leaching. The resulting catalyst achieved exceptional performance in the selective hydrogenation of cinnamaldehyde to cinnamyl alcohol, demonstrating 99.7% selectivity at complete conversion. Combined with experiments and theoretical calculations, the study revealed that the precisely controlled geometric and electronic structures of these isolated Cu sites and Lewis acid sites in the MOF are the key factors for the high selectivity and efficiency. The low Cu electron density and high O electron density precisely regulated the adsorption of both reactants and H* and the desorption capability of the product, ultimately improving the selectivity. The present defect-engineering strategy for stabilizing non-precious single-atom catalysts in MOFs offers a versatile platform for designing high-performance catalysts for a wide range of selective hydrogenation reactions.
