ABSTRACT
This project was motivated by the opportunity to use solar-driven photocatalysis for scalable, low cost
domestic production of energy and commodity chemicals such as fertilizers. Of the various
approaches, photocatalysis using nanoparticle photocatalyst suspensions are projected to be among
the most cost effective. To improve the efficiency of photocatalysts, a detailed mechanism of the
photoreduction/photooxidation processes is necessary to guide the catalyst design. To develop such
mechanistic knowledge, we used a specialized time-of-flight mass spectrometry techniques, including
velocity map imaging, and laser desorption mass spectrometry, to selectively identify photocatalytic
reaction products, intermediates, and reaction rates as well as to correlate nanoparticle photocatalyst
synthesis, structure, composition, and dimension. By coupling this approach with established
spectroscopic and microstructural characterization on the same photocatalysts, we will enable the
identification of features that result in high water splitting rates, as well as the mechanisms that degrade
photocatalysis. In this report, we build upon previous results of photocatalytic water splitting where
H2 ions were detected with low signal to noise. Additionally, we conduct laser desorption mass
spectroscopy on the Nb doped SrTiO3 sample and measure its time-of-flight where we observe
intermediates and products of the reaction.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
 Currently, the efficiency of photocatalytic production of energy and commodity chemicals is
not sufficient to make such processes economically viable on large scale. To improve the efficiency,
details of the photochemical reactions, their rates, and how they depend on the composition and
structure of the photocatalyst nanoparticles is required. Correlative microscopy offers an avenue to
address important questions about these photocatalytic systems that can allow for improved and
informed decision making during the iterative process of designing photocatalysts, synthesis, and
experimental measurements. In this report, we discuss how we used a specialized time-of-flight mass
spectrometry techniques to study a hydrogen evolution photocatalytic system and we present the
experimental results.
 First, we use velocity map imaging to study a Nb doped SrTiO3 sample. In these preliminary
results, we observed H2 ions and mapped them on the detector. We demonstrated that this velocity
map imaging can be used to study products coming off a photocatalyst surface. Unfortunately, we
faced an issue with low signal-to-noise levels, so findings from this study is limited.
 Additionally, we used laser desorption mass spectrometry to study the ions created at the
surface of the Nb doped SrTiO3. By controlling the timing and potential applied to the repeller plate,
we observed 4 peaks in the time-of-flight plots, which we were unable to definitively assign. We used
SIMION to calculate the ions arrival times given the experimental parameters. When comparing the
experimental and calculated arrival times of the ions, we determined that the ions must have an
additional large repulsive force.
 Future efforts will be focused on comparing a Nb doped SrTiO3 sample with an area with and
without Pt dots patterned. These Pt dots will form a Schottky barrier with the SrTiO3 substrate and
improve catalysis. Additionally, Pt is an excellent co-catalyst for water splitting, and could change the
amount and ratio of the intermediates and products measured.

1. SECTION 1: VELOCITY MAP IMAGE MICROSCOPY FOR
 EFFICIENT PHOTOCATALYSIS
1.1. Introduction
 Water splitting (WS) using direct solar to hydrogen (STH) conversion is economically the most
promising route to clean H2 production according to a DOE commissioned study carried out in 2009.1
Of the possible schemes, approaches based on nanoparticle photocatalyst (nPC) suspensions are
projected to be the most cost–effective, including when compared to photovoltaic-electrolytic
systems.2, 3 However, these projections are based on a ~10% STH conversion efficiency, well above
the current state-of-the-art efficiency of ~1%.3 This gap is due to the poor performance of the nPCs,
despite decades of intense research. The largely ad hoc strategy employed to date has failed due to
insufficient understanding of how the composition, dimensions, morphology and defects of the nPCs
relate to their performance. We propose a new route to nPC discovery, based on several recent
developments in our laboratory. Specifically, we propose to extend a new capability at the Laboratory,
the Velocity-Selected, Spatially-Mapped, Ion-Imaging (VSSMII) microscope, to uncover the detailed
mechanisms of photocatalytic water splitting reactions. With this approach, we propose to identify
photocatalytic reaction products and their rates with high spatial resolution for select oxygen evolution
reaction (OER) and hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) nanoparticle photocatalysts and correlate
these measurements with structural, compositional and liquid cell characteristics. We hypothesize that
with a combined approach we will identify the rate limiting steps and propose a scientifically validated
strategy for improving OER and HER efficiencies.
 The basic principle of VSSMII microscopy is shown in Figure 1(a). Briefly a quantum-state-
resolved laser is utilized for ionization of a product of a catalytic reaction, such as H2 + D2 occurring
over a platinum strip. The technique is able to accurately determine where the ions are formed, and
to correlate the location of the products in the gas phase directly above the surface with features on
the surface. For OER and HER characterization, water vapor at pressures of ~10 Torr will be
introduced into the system and an optical pump laser with excite photocarriers in the nanoparticle
photocatalysts. A time delayed voltage pulse will be used to force the OER or HER products toward
the detector. Laser ionization will be used to probe for neutral reaction intermediates. The VSSMII
microscopy measurements will be combined with photoelectrochemical, microstructural and charge
transport characterization on the same nanoparticle photocatalysts with the aim of unraveling the
complex WS mechanisms and revealing paths toward achieving substantially enhanced efficiency for
solar hydrogen production.
 Photocatalytic water splitting comprises three basic steps: 1) light absorption, 2) transport of
photocarriers to the surface of the nanoparticle, and 3) H2 and/or O2 evolution, often aided with a
co-catalyst. Whether using a single type of nPC or separated H2 and O2 reactions (z-scheme),
optimizing all three steps within a single material system is a considerable challenge. Traditional
semiconductors are unstable in the aqueous media. Various transition metal oxides are low cost, stable,
and have appropriately positioned conduction band minima and valence band maxima to drive H2 and
O2 evolution, respectively. However, many of these compounds absorb only a small fraction of the
solar radiation due to their large bandgaps (>3eV).4 To increase visible absorption, dopants like N, C,
S and various transition metals are introduced to alter the electronic structure to form an impurity
band.5 This approach has yet to work mainly due to formation of deep traps that severely reduce
photocarrier lifetime6. One promising exception is BiVO4 (BVO). With a bandgap (Eg) of ~2 eV,
BVO absorbs substantially in the visible. However, because BVO is naturally n-type and its band
structure is only well suited for the oxygen evolution reaction (OER), it requires a z-scheme type
approach for solar WS. Thus, further improving BVO OER rates and finding an effective HER
photocatalyst, could be a promising solution for low cost solar WS.
 Herein we propose to extend a recently developed technique at SNL, the Velocity-Selected,
Spatially-Mapped, Ion-Imaging (VSSMII) microscopy, to uncovering the detailed mechanisms of PC
WS reactions. Specifically, we propose to identify photocatalytic reaction products and their rates with
high spatial resolution for selective OER and HER nanoparticle photocatalysts and correlate these

 10

measurements with structural, compositional and liquid cell characteristics. We hypothesize that with
a combined approach we will identify the rate limiting steps and propose a scientifically validated
strategy for improving OER and HER efficiencies.
 The basic principle of VSSMII microscopy is shown in Figure 1(a). In this approach,
developed at SNL by D. Chandler and C. Kliewer, a quantum-state-resolved laser is utilized for
ionization of a product of a catalytic reaction, such as H2 + D2 occurring over a platinum strip at
500oK.7 The goal is to accurately determine where the ions are formed, in order to correlate the
location of the products in the gas phase directly above the surface with features on the surface. In
order to measure the exact location of the products, velocity mapped ion imaging is used to form a
velocity image of the ions. This image is located at a plate with a small hole that selects out a single
velocity group to pass onto an imaging detector 1 meters away. For a pinhole placed such that only
ions moving perfectly perpendicular to the surface are allowed to pass and to expand onto the detector,
the location of ions at the detector accurately reflects the location that the desired product (HD in this
example) exits the surface. It turns out that one can pick any subset of velocities and get an equivalent
image simply translated on the detector (assuming all locations have the same angular distribution of
products).

 A schematic of our proposed approach to extending VSSMII microscopy to PC WS is shown
in Fig. 1b. We will initially focus on the oxygen evolution reaction OER, 2H2O + 4h+  O2 + 4H+.
We anticipate that we will be able to detect the charged particles produced, protons, and/or the
hydronium ions (formed when the protons react with the adsorbed H2O), just with the application of
the repeller plate voltage, without laser ionization. By controlling the time delay between the optical
pump pulse and when the voltage is applied to the repeller plate, we will determine the time constants
for the OER as a function of the pump power, crystal orientation, and co-catalyst composition and
coverage. Optical ionization of O2 near 370 nm has been extensively studied and will be used for O2
molecule detection.8 The BVO crystals will be dispersed on commercially purchased, alphanumerically

labeled Si substrates with 50 nm thick Si3N4 wells and Au metal lines. This will enable us to identify
specific nanoparticle PCs and correlate the data with other complimentary measurements (see Fig. 2),
including photoelectrochemical (PEC), spectroscopic (Raman/photoluminescence), transmission
electron microscopy with electron energy loss spectroscopy (TEM/EELS), and charge transport. This
type of correlative microscopy/spectroscopy is needed to understand how the basic steps in the PC
WS are linked and identify which step is rate limiting. The TEM/EELS characterization will be done
in collaboration with Josh Sugar and will be supported by the Ensembles of Photocatalytic
Nanoreactors Energy Frontiers Research Center (EPN EFRC). We will particularly focus on better
understanding the role of cocatalyst in assisting the OER efficiency. To this end, we will select BVO
PC nanoparticles with the highest crystal quality (judged using x-ray diffraction and scanning electron
microscopy) and have highest intensity of near band-edge luminescence, indicative of lower charge
recombination trap density. These BVO nanoparticle PCs will be decorated with either Ir or FeOOH
or CoOOH cocatalysts using in situ facet-selective photodeposition and characterized using VSSMII
microscopy, followed by TEM/EELS. By relating specific aspects of the BVO nPCs such as their
dimension, the cocatalyst structure and location, and the electrical properties of its contact with the
BVO nanoparticle, with the quantitatively measured OER rates, we will formulate a model for the
reaction and use this understanding to design improved nPCs.

1.2. Experimental Methodology
 The surface velocity map ion imaging experiments were conducted within a custom-built time-
of-flight mass spectrometer setup with a low-pressure water vapor leaking into the sample chamber.
When the sample chamber is closed off, the pressure is consistently at 1.1 x 10-7 Torr. Pure hydrogen

 12

from a gas cylinder source was connected to a regulator and leaked into the chamber at a very low
flowrate to calibrate the operating conditions for the experiment. The vacuum chamber would be
evacuated for several minutes, and the lines would be evacuated separately with a vacuum pump to
prevent cross-contamination in the gas lines. When water vapor is leaked in from a sealed water
bubbler (that is evacuated of air), the chamber pressure is able to be controlled via a micrometer leak
valve, and the chamber pressure is brought up 1 x 10-5 Torr. This chamber pressure is measured via a
digital high vacuum gauge. The vacuum chamber was maintained at a constant measured vacuum
pressure via a turbomolecular pump. Three microchannel plates were used in combination with a high
voltage pulser box to gate the first plate with a pulse width of 300 ns. An Andor Sona sCMOS camera
was used to obtain raw images. A custom LabVIEW software program was used to alternate
measurements with a foreground and background measurement switching between two preset timings
for the laser pulses. The experimental data was acquired using a custom built VMI apparatus and the
resulting images were processed using python and MATLAB scripts. The spectra data were collected
with a SRS boxcar averager model SR250 and the laser intensity was measured with a photodiode
(ThorLabs model DET10A). The spectra were collected three times, averaged, and then normalized
by dividing by the squared intensity of the photodiode.
 The photocatalyst sample was mounted with double sided copper tape to the stainless steel
repeller plate in the vacuum chamber. The frequency-tripled output of a 100 Hz Lumibird Nd:YAG
was used to illuminate the photocatalyst sample on the repeller plate with 355-nm light. The 355-nm
light had a low energy range of 5 to 100 μJ/pulse as to not damage or degrade the sample. The 355-
nm pump light was positioned to be grazing over the sample surface. Another 100 Hz Lumibird
Nd:YAG was used to pump a Sirah Precision Scan dye laser with 532-nm light. A mixture of
Rhodamine 610 (0.17g/L) and Rhodamine 640 (0.04 g/L) in methanol were used in the Sirah Precision
Scan dye laser to produce 202-nm light with pulse energies ranging from 70 to 100 μJ/pulse and a line
width of 0.03 cm-1. The time delay between the 355-nm and the 202-nm light was controlled
throughout the experiment with a Stanford research DG645 timing box.
 The 355-nm light is used to illuminate the photocatalyst surface and generate electron-hole
pairs, which further react into forming the hydrogen product. The hydrogen from the gas cylinder
sources or product of the reaction would be ionized with a 202-nm laser pulse using a (2+1) REMPI
scheme via the E, F 1Σ+X 1Σ+ transition near 202-nm. The hydrogen is ionized when it reaches the
202-nm laser beam centered and focused between the repeller plate and the extractor (1.1 cm from
the photocatalyst surface on the repeller plate). The laser was focused into the center of a traditional
ion imaging apparatus between a repeller plate and extractor plate; the ions were propelled through
an Einzel lens, focused through a field-free flight region onto the imaging detector to accomplish
velocity mapping of the H2+ photoproduct. The ion lensing electrodes and repeller plate voltages
were adjusted to conform the ions into circular shapes and these parameters was assumed to be the
best velocity mapped imaging conditions. The front plate of the microchannel plate detector was gated
for a pulse width of 300 ns to ensure only detection of H2 ions.

1.3. Discussion
 Pure hydrogen was used to calibrate the velocity map imaging conditions and to collect
REPMI spectra. As shown in appendix A, the REMPI spectra collected has 4 sharp peaks
corresponding to the Q0, Q1, Q2, and Q3 peaks, which is agreeance with literature reports. This REMPI
spectra is important for confirming that hydrogen in the vacuum chamber is being ionized, and to
position the dye laser wavelength on a Q branch to get selective ionization.
 The first sample to be tested was a 1 cm x 1 cm x 0.05cm bulk crystalline Nb doped SrTiO3
(STO). The vacuum chamber was saturated with water vapor leaked in from the head space of a water
source. The Sirah dye laser was set to the Q1 peak wavelength to ionize the hydrogen. With a 5 μs time
delay between the 355-nm and 202-nm laser pulses, H2 was clearly detected and velocity mapped onto
the detector. The theoretical thermal average velocity of hydrogen at room temperature is
approximately 1800 m/s. The measured velocity of the hydrogen is 1850 m/s. This measured velocity
is calculated using the ratio of diameter of the camera in pixels and real diameter, cm, and dividing by
the arrival time.
 An averaged image was generated by combining 60 individual images, each acquired with an
accumulation time of 30 seconds, as illustrated in Figure 5. The signal intensity is quite low for these
averaged images once the foreground is subtracted from the background. Figure 6 was generated by
binning across the x-axis and y-axis of velocity mapped image of H2 ions in Figure 5.

 Other time delays were tested and measured as well, but due to the low signal to noise ratio,
the data is unreliable to make definitive conclusions. There is suspicion that the photocatalyst sample
is degrading over the course of the measurement due to the decreasing signal over time. The signal

attenuated to a lower power, or a lower frequency.

1.4. Conclusion
 In conclusion, preliminary results indicate that hydrogen was velocity map imaged using water
vapor and a photocatalyst sample, Nb doped STO. With the current results, no definitive conclusions
can be made and it is imperative to continue future measurements to uncover the water splitting
mechanism on these photocatalysts. Due to low signal-to-noise, the experimental results were able to
map H2 ions hitting the detector, but the signal decreases as experimentation continued.
 Future experimentation will include controlling the sample temperature to study the
temperature effects on photocatalytic rates as well as different photocatalysts, dopants, and co-
catalysts. Additionally, future VMI systems will be designed to selectively measure different ions like
H+, OH-, or O2- which could be important intermediates that provide crucial information for the water
splitting mechanism. With a pinhole design as presented previously, a spatial resolution can be
achieved to determine differences in photocatalyst facets, structures, and compositions that can be
correlated back to photocatalytic activity rates.

2. SECTION 2: LASER DESORPTION MASS SPECTROSCOPY ON NB
 DOPED STO
2.1. Introduction

 Laser desorption mass spectrometry (LDMS) is an advanced analytical technique that enables
the direct investigation of molecular species adsorbed on surfaces by employing a focused laser pulse
to desorb and ionize analytes for mass spectrometric detection.9-13 This approach offers significant
advantages in surface analysis due to its minimal sample preparation, high sensitivity, and ability to
analyze complex and heterogeneous materials.9, 14, 15 Semiconductors, in particular, represent an
excellent class of substrates for LDMS studies because of their well-defined and technologically critical
surfaces, which are often functionalized or modified in applications such as microelectronics, catalysis,
and biosensing.10, 16, 17

 The interaction of laser energy with semiconductor surfaces induces rapid thermal desorption
of surface-bound molecules without extensive fragmentation, preserving molecular integrity for
accurate mass analysis. This capability allows researchers to obtain detailed information on surface
species, including adsorbates, thin films, and reaction intermediates, which are crucial for
understanding surface chemistry and material properties. For example, surface-assisted laser
desorption/ionization (SALDI) on silicon substrates has been demonstrated to enhance ionization
efficiency and reduce background noise, improving detection limits for a variety of analytes.16, 18, 19
SALDI is a subset of LDMS that replaces conventional organic chemical matrices with inorganic
nanostructured surfaces or nanoparticles to mediate energy transfer.

 Moreover, coupling laser-induced thermal desorption with high-resolution mass spectrometry
techniques such as Fourier transform ion cyclotron resonance (FT-ICR) mass spectrometry enables
time-resolved analysis of surface molecular composition. This approach provides dynamic insights
into catalytic processes17, 20 and biomedical implant surface modifications by monitoring molecular
changes in real time.21, 22 The ability to perform spatially and temporally resolved surface analysis has
broadened the applicability of LDMS in fields ranging from materials science to biomedical
engineering.

 Recent advances have also focused on optimizing laser parameters and substrate properties to
improve desorption efficiency and ionization selectivity, further enhancing the analytical performance
of LDMS.13, 20, 23 The integration of LDMS with complementary surface characterization techniques
continues to expand its utility in elucidating complex surface phenomena on semiconductor materials,
supporting the development of next-generation devices and functional coatings. Laser adsorption
mass spectrometry potentially offers unique insight into photocatalysis by providing key details into
the short-lived reactive intermediates on the surface of these water splitting photocatalysts.17 Herein
this study, we use a n-type semiconductor sample, Nb doped SrTiO3¸to study the photocatalytic
process of water splitting. Additionally, we compare a bare STO sample to an area with patterned with
Pt dots. Comparing with and without the Pt will provide a comparison of reactive intermediates and
products as Pt is a common co-catalyst used in multiple photocatalytic systems.24-26 By studying a
complex system with water splitting photocatalysts with this laser desorption mass spectrometry
technique, we enable at Sandia another complex tool capable to furthering Sandia National
Laboratory’s missions.

2.2. Experimental Methodology

2.2.1. Laser desorption mass spectrometry measurements
 The chosen samples were placed into the square 1 cm2 inset on the repeller plate. The ionic
optics stack was housed inside a custom-built time-of-flight mass spectrometer setup with a needle
valve leaking low-pressure water vapor from the head space of a bubbler filled with deionized water.
When chamber was enclosed completely, and the pressure was constant at 1.1 x 10-9 Torr. When the
chamber is exposed to water vapor, the pressure is brought up to 3 x 10-6 Torr by controlling the
needle valve. This chamber pressure is measured via a digital high vacuum gauge. Three microchannel
plates were used in combination with a high voltage pulser box to gate the first plate with a pulse
width of 300 ns. An Andor Sona sCMOS camera was used to obtain raw images. A custom LabVIEW
software program was used to alternate measurements with a foreground and background
measurement switching between two preset timings for the laser pulses. The experimental data was
acquired using a custom built VMI apparatus and the resulting images were processed using python
and MATLAB scripts.
 The frequency-tripled output of a 10 Hz Continuum Surelite Nd:YAG was used to illuminate
the photocatalyst sample on the repeller plate with 355-nm light. The 355-nm light had a low energy
range of 20-40 uJ/pulse as to not damage the sample. The 355-nm light was positioned as to graze
over the sample surface.

2.2.2. I-V measurements

2.3.2. Pt dots deposited onto Nb doped SrTiO3
 Pt is a common co-catalyst for photocatalytic water splitting. Pt enhances charge separation
and facilitates specific surface reactions, such as hydrogen and oxygen evolution. The Pt/STO
interface forms a Schottky junction, which helps trap and extract photoelectrons from the STO
substrate, thus reducing electron-hole recombination rates. Additionally, the Pt serves as a catalytic
reaction site for specific surface reactions as Pt is a known catalyst that is highly active for proton

 22

reduction. In this system, the Nb doped STO will absorb the photons, and generate an electron-hole
pair. The electron will flow towards the Pt. The sample was patterned with a thin layer of Pt (~ 5 nm)
in a dot pattern with varying diameters, 500 μm, 400 μm, 250 μm, and 125 μm. The Pt dots were
spaced equidistant from each other at 1000 μm.

2.4. Conclusion
 In this report, we present some preliminary results of conducting laser desorption mass
spectrometry with a Nb doped SrTiO3 sample. We used 355-nm in one-color experiments to
illuminate the STO sample and do photocatalysis and create ions. The 355-nm is absorbed by the STO

 24

sample and the water vapor leaked into the chamber is then photocatalyzed into intermediates and
product ions at the repeller plate surface where the 355-nm is illuminating. We hypothesize that there
could be localized plasma generation or a hot spot of charge that is causing a strong repulsion force
at the repeller plate when conducting one-color experiments with 355-nm. This hypothesis is
supported by the SIMION calculations that suggest that a higher repeller plate voltage is required to
get close to the experimental arrival times of the ions. From the time-of-flight plots, we assigned the
H- and H2- ion peaks. We used a high voltage pulser to separately pulse the MCP and the repeller plate
to observe changed in the time of flight and observe changes in the population of ions.
 Future experiments will focus on comparing the results measured on the areas with and
without the Pt co-catalyst to observe differences in the time-of-flight plots. Due to the highly active
nature of the Pt as an excellent water splitting catalyst, we hypothesized that there will be a difference
in activity between the areas with and without the Pt dots. In future experimentation, we aim to
spatially resolve where the photocatalytic activity is located with this Pt – STO interface.
 Despite only having preliminary results, from this report, we are confident that we have
demonstrated successfully that we have increased Sandia National Laboratory’s capabilities of
advanced techniques. Laser desorption mass spectrometry has the potential to further Sandia’s
missions and support current projects by providing crucial information.
