The Vacuolar Ca2+/H+ Exchanger AaVCX Regulates Calcium Homeostasis, the Differentiation of Infection Structures and Pathogenicity in Alternaria alternata Under the Transcriptional Control of AaCrz1
Authors: Yangyang Yang, Pengdong Xie, Qili Liu, Yuanping Nan, Miao Zhang, Xiaojing Wang, Yang Bi, Yongcai Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCalcium ion (Ca2+) signalling is crucial for multiple biological processes in fungi, including growth, stress adaptation and pathogenic behaviour. The vacuolar Ca2+/H+ exchanger (VCX) is vital for maintaining calcium homeostasis within fungal cells, yet its specific biological functions in phytopathogenic fungi remain poorly understood. This study focused on the role and transcriptional regulation of AaVCX in Alternaria alternata , the organism responsible for pear black spot disease. By employing a split‐marker strategy, we generated AaVCX knockout (ΔAaVCX) and complemented (ΔAaVCX‐C) strains. Phenotypic assessments revealed that ΔAaVCX exhibited reduced mycelial growth, abnormal accumulation of intracellular Ca2+ and heightened sensitivity to various stressors. Deletion of AaVCX severely hindered spore germination, differentiation of appressoria and invasive hyphae in A. alternata triggered by pear peel wax. The ΔAaVCX nearly lost its ability to penetrate cellophane, and its virulence on pear fruits and leaves was markedly diminished. Molecular docking analysis indicated that AaCrz1, a key transcription factor in the calcium signalling pathway, can bind to the promoter of AaVCX. Dual‐luciferase and yeast one‐hybrid assays demonstrated that AaCrz1 binds to the AaVCX promoter in vitro and positively regulates its expression, with a predicted GCC‐core motif identified in the promoter region. Furthermore, transcript levels of AaVCX were significantly reduced in the ΔAaCrz1 background, indicating that AaVCX is positioned downstream of AaCrz1 in a regulatory cascade. Our findings enhance the understanding of calcium signalling networks in phytopathogenic fungi and propose that components involved in vacuolar calcium storage may serve as potential targets for disease management.Keywords: Alternaria alternata , Ca2+/H+ exchanger, calcium homeostasis, infection structure differentiation, pathogenicity, transcriptional regulation

Introduction

Alternaria alternata is a widespread fungal pathogen that affects a variety of fruit and vegetables, leading to diseases like black spot disease (Gatto et al. 2011; Kustrzeba‐Wójcicka et al. 2014; Li et al. 2006; Soylu and Kose 2015). The pathogen's ability to infect relies on the accurate differentiation of its infection structures. When it detects specific physical and chemical cues from the host, including hydrophobic signals and fruit wax extracts, A. alternata progresses through stages of spore germination, appressorium and invasive hyphal differentiation, allowing it to breach the host's outer layer and establish infection (Tang et al. 2017). Previous studies have confirmed that certain wax constituents on the host surface, like octacosanal, play a crucial role in guiding the differentiation of these infection structures in fungi (Reisige et al. 2006). Additionally, signaling pathways such as MAPK, cAMP‐PKA and calcium ion pathways in A. alternata are implicated in the formation of infection structures triggered by pear wax (Liu et al. 2024; Zhang, Li, et al. 2021; Zhang, Zong, et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2022). Understanding the regulatory mechanisms that govern the differentiation of infection structures in phytopathogenic fungi is essential for creating effective disease management strategies.

The system responsible for maintaining calcium homeostasis is a highly conserved signalling network found in eukaryotic organisms. As a ubiquitous second messenger, the fluctuating levels of intracellular Ca2+ concentration play a crucial role in various physiological functions in fungi, such as their growth and development, stress responses, differentiation of infection structures and pathogenicity (Carafoli 2002; Cui et al. 2009; Yang, Xie, Yuan, et al. 2022). Phytopathogenic fungi maintain intracellular calcium levels via a sophisticated system comprising calcium transporters, calcium channel proteins and calcium‐binding proteins. Dysfunction in these components can result in excessive intracellular Ca2+ levels, leading to disruptions in normal metabolic processes within fungi (Antebi and Fink 1992; Axelsen and Palmgren 1998; Cunningham and Fink 1994; Jiang et al. 2014; Kmetzsch et al. 2010; Sze et al. 2000). The calcineurin‐Crz1 pathway represents a core module in calcium homeostasis regulation. In response to calcium signalling, the transcription factor Crz1 translocates to the nucleus, where it influences the expression of downstream target genes involved in calcium stress tolerance, cell wall stability and the differentiation of infection structures in fungi (Cunningham and Fink 1996; Yang, Xie, Li, et al. 2022; Yang, Xie, Yuan, et al. 2022). In A. alternata , the gene AaCrz1, which is homologous to Crz1, has been shown to play a role in regulating calcium homeostasis and pathogenicity, although the specific downstream target genes and regulatory mechanisms involved are still not fully understood.

The VCX protein, a Ca2+/H+ exchanger, plays a crucial role in maintaining calcium homeostasis within fungi. This ion transporter, located in the vacuolar membrane, facilitates the exchange of Ca2+ and H+, enabling the absorption and storage of cytoplasmic Ca2+ in vacuoles, thereby finely tuning the levels of Ca2+ in the cytoplasm (Cunningham and Fink 1996; Kmetzsch et al. 2010; Miseta et al. 1999; Pittman et al. 2004). The first identified member of the VCX family, Vcx1p from Saccharomyces cerevisiae , has its function inhibited by calcineurin. In conditions of elevated calcium, Vcx1p helps transport Ca2+ into vacuoles, lowering cytoplasmic Ca2+ levels and mitigating calcium‐related toxicity (Cunningham and Fink 1996; Miseta et al. 1999). In Cryptococcus neoformans , Vcx1 contributes to calcium tolerance that is dependent on calcineurin, and its absence notably reduces the pathogen's virulence (Kmetzsch et al. 2010). VCX proteins work in conjunction with other calcium transporters like Pmc1 to collectively uphold intracellular calcium homeostasis within fungal cells (Pittman 2011). However, research on VCX genes in pathogenic fungi has primarily concentrated on yeasts and cryptococci, leaving the biological roles and regulatory pathways of VCX in phytopathogenic fungi, such as A. alternata , largely unexplored.

This study focused on the vacuolar Ca2+/H+ exchanger gene AaVCX in A. alternata . We generated AaVCX knockout mutants and complementation strains using the split‐marker PCR method to systematically examine how the deletion of AaVCX affects the growth, development, intracellular calcium balance, and responses to abiotic stress in A. alternata . Additionally, we explored the role of AaVCX in infection and pathogenicity through assays involving pear wax‐induced differentiation, cellophane penetration and host inoculation. To investigate the regulatory interaction between AaCrz1 and AaVCX, we conducted molecular docking, dual‐luciferase reporter assays and yeast one‐hybrid (Y1H) assays. Our study reveals that AaVCX is crucial for maintaining calcium homeostasis, which is essential for the differentiation of infection structures. We demonstrate that the expression of AaVCX is directly activated by AaCrz1. This research fills a significant conceptual gap in the calcium signalling network of phytopathogenic fungi. The results advance our insight into the molecular regulation of calcium homeostasis in A. alternata , highlight the biological roles of VCX proteins in plant pathogens, and suggest a potential molecular target for the creation of low‐toxicity, high‐efficiency fungicides aimed at the calcium signalling pathway.

Results

Deletion of AaVCX Impairs the Growth and Development of A. alternata

Figure 1A,B illustrates that the ΔAaVCX‐1 and ΔAaVCX‐2 mutants displayed notably altered colony characteristics, including darker colouration and significantly smaller colony size at 3, 5 and 7 days post‐inoculation (dpi) when compared to the wild type (WT). Analysis of biomass revealed that at 5 dpi, the ΔAaVCX‐1 and ΔAaVCX‐2 mutants had a considerably lower biomass than both the WT and ΔAaVCX‐C (Figure 1C). Additionally, the accumulation of extracellular melanin was significantly greater in the ΔAaVCX mutants relative to the WT and ΔAaVCX‐C (Figure 1D). Overall, these findings suggest that the deletion of AaVCX hampers the vegetative growth of A. alternata , resulting in diminished colony growth and biomass accumulation, while also affecting the production of extracellular melanin.

AaVCX Is Crucial for Calcium Homeostasis in A. alternata

Deletion of AaVCX Enhances Sensitivity to High Ca2+ Concentration

To explore the role of AaVCX under calcium stress, growth experiments were conducted using potato dextrose agar (PDA) with varying concentrations of CaCl2 and Na2EDTA. The results indicated that the ΔAaVCX mutants were significantly more sensitive to exogenous Ca2+. At a concentration of 0.1 M Ca2+, the growth inhibition rates of ΔAaVCX‐1 and ΔAaVCX‐2 were 30.6% and 35.1%, respectively, which were considerably higher than those of the WT (19.1%) and ΔAaVCX‐C (14.1%). When subjected to Na2EDTA stress, the growth inhibition rates of the ΔAaVCX mutants were slightly higher than those of the WT, though this difference lacked statistical significance (Figure 2A, Table 1). These findings imply that AaVCX is crucial for A. alternata 's ability to withstand high calcium stress.

Deletion of AaVCX Causes Abnormal Intracellular Calcium Accumulation

To assess the role of AaVCX in modulating calcium levels within A. alternata , we quantified the cytosolic calcium contents (Figure 2B). The results showed that the intracellular calcium levels in ΔAaVCX‐1 and ΔAaVCX‐2 were significantly higher than those of the WT. In contrast, the calcium content in ΔAaVCX‐C returned to a level comparable to that of the WT. These findings indicate that AaVCX plays a crucial role in maintaining calcium homeostasis within A. alternata , and its absence results in excessive calcium accumulation inside the cells.

AaVCX Is Involved in the Response of A. alternata to Cell Wall and Osmotic Stresses

The impact of AaVCX on the reaction to abiotic stressors was examined by assessing the growth of various strains subjected to Congo Red (CR), Calcofluor White (CFW), NaCl and H2O2 stress conditions. The results showed that under cell wall stress, the growth inhibition rates of ΔAaVCX‐1 and ΔAaVCX‐2 under CR stress were 23.0% and 23.9%, respectively, which were considerably elevated relative to the WT (4.8%) and ΔAaVCX‐C (8.4%). Similarly, the ΔAaVCX mutants exhibited significantly higher growth inhibition rates compared to the WT and ΔAaVCX‐C when exposed to CFW. Under NaCl stress, the ΔAaVCX mutants also showed distinctly increased growth inhibition rates (Figure 3, Table 2). These findings indicate that the deletion of AaVCX significantly increases the sensitivity of A. alternata to both cell wall and osmotic stresses, while it does not appear to have a significant effect on the response to oxidative stress.

AaVCX Is Essential for the Early Infection Process of A. alternata

AaVCX Deletion Impairs Infection Structure Formation in A. alternata Under Hydrophobic Surface and Pear Wax Induction

The regulatory role of AaVCX in the infection process of A. alternata was evaluated by analysing spore germination and appressorium formation on both hydrophobic surfaces and those coated with pear peel wax extract. After 2 h incubation on pear peel wax extract coating surface, the spore germination rates of ΔAaVCX‐1 and ΔAaVCX‐2 were reduced by 31.2% and 29.2%, respectively, in comparison to the WT. By 8 h post‐incubation, the formation rates of appressorium in the mutants were decreased by 50.2% and 46.2%, respectively. Furthermore, the impact of pear peel wax on appressorium formation was significantly greater than that of a hydrophobic surface alone (Figure 4A,B). These findings indicate that AaVCX is essential for sensing host wax signals and is vital for the initiation of spore germination and appressorium formation during the early infection stages of A. alternata .

AaVCX Deletion Disrupts Differentiation of Infection Structure in A. alternata on Wax‐Coated Onion Epidermis

The differentiation of infection structures in A. alternata was examined by applying spore suspensions of the WT, ΔAaVCX and ΔAaVCX‐C strains onto onion epidermis treated with pear wax extract. The results showed that, after 2 h incubation, the germination rate of spores from the ΔAaVCX strain was decreased by 46.5% compared to the WT (Figure 4C). By 8 h incubation, the rates of appressorium formation and invasive hyphal formation of the ΔAaVCX mutants were diminished by 56.2% and 68.5%, respectively (Figure 4D,E). Collectively, these findings indicate that AaVCX plays a crucial role in regulating the differentiation of infection structures and pathogenicity of A. alternata .

AaVCX Deletion Reduces the Penetration Capability of A. alternata

Due to the significant impairment of the invasive hyphae in the ΔAaVCX mutant, spore suspensions from the WT, ΔAaVCX and ΔAaVCX‐C strains were applied to PDA covered with cellophane to assess their spore penetration abilities. After 48 h of incubation, the cellophane was removed, and the cultures were incubated for a further 48 h. The ΔAaVCX mutants exhibited a significant reduction in their ability to penetrate cellophane (Figure 4F). Quantitative analysis demonstrated that the diameter of the colonies that penetrated the cellophane in the ΔAaVCX mutants was markedly smaller compared to those of the WT and the complemented strain ΔAaVCX‐C (Figure S2). This finding indicates that the deletion of AaVCX substantially impairs the invasive capability of A. alternata .

AaVCX Deletion Reduces the Pathogenicity of A. alternata

The regulatory role of AaVCX on the pathogenicity of A. alternata was assessed through both wound‐inoculated pear fruits and non‐wounded inoculated pear leaves. After 4 dpi, the lesions on the wounded fruits caused by the ΔAaVCX‐1 and ΔAaVCX‐2 mutants were significantly smaller than those observed in the WT (Figure 5A). Statistical evaluations indicated a significant reduction in lesion size for both mutants when compared to the WT and the complemented strain ΔAaVCX‐C (Figure 5B). In the case of non‐wounded leaves examined 3 dpi, no obvious differences in lesion appearance were noted on the upper surface among the various strains. However, on the lower surface, lesions were prominent in the WT but absent in the ΔAaVCX mutants (Figure 5C). The ΔAaVCX‐C strain was able to restore pathogenicity to levels comparable to the WT.

AaCrz1 Binds to the AaVCX Promoter and Positively Regulates Its Transcription

Docking of AaCrz1 Protein With AaVCX Promoter Nucleic Acid

Molecular docking conducted with HDOCK indicated that the best model (model_1) achieved a docking score of −264.77 and a confidence score of 0.9085, indicating a strong potential for stable interactions between the protein and DNA (Figure 6A). The binding interface was visualized in surface mode (Figure 6B). Figure 6C illustrated that DG‐396 from the AaVCX promoter A‐chain established a salt bridge (yellow dashed line) with Arg‐458 of AaCrz1. Ser‐459 of AaCrz1 formed hydrogen bonds (blue solid lines) with DG‐404 and DG‐403 of the AaVCX promoter A‐chain. Arg‐457 of AaCrz1 created a hydrogen bond with DC‐401 of the AaVCX promoter A‐chain, while Tyr‐469 of AaCrz1 bonded with DA‐399 of the AaVCX promoter A‐chain. DT‐27 from the AaVCX promoter B‐chain formed a hydrogen bond with Ser‐528 of AaCrz1. These interactions collectively contribute to the stability of the binding, establishing a structural foundation for a strong connection between AaCrz1 and the AaVCX promoter.

Regulation of the AaVCX Promoter by AaCrz1 Analysed by Dual‐Luciferase Reporter Assay

In order to investigate how AaCrz1 influences the AaVCX promoter, a dual‐luciferase reporter assay was conducted. This involved inserting AaCrz1 into the pGreenII 62‐SK vector and the AaVCX promoter into the pGreenII 0800‐LUC vector (Figure 6D). The results showed that AaCrz1 effectively enhanced the activity of the AaVCX promoter, achieving an activation fold of around 3.5 (Figure 6E).

Binding of AaCrz1 to the AaVCX Promoter Analysed by Y1H Assay

The interaction of the transcription factor AaCrz1 with the AaVCX promoter was evaluated through a Y1H assay. The self‐activation test showed that the lowest concentration of aureobasidin A (AbA) suitable for screening the AaVCX promoter was 420 ng/mL. The results revealed that the Y1H Gold strain with AD‐AaCrz1 and the AaVCX promoter thrived on SD/−Leu plates with 420 ng/mL AbA, whereas the strain with the empty AD vector and the AaVCX promoter exhibited growth inhibition on plates containing AbA (Figure 6F). To further investigate the binding specificity of AaCrz1 to the AaVCX promoter, potential binding sites within the AaVCX promoter region were identified using the JASPAR database (Figure 6G). The predictions indicated that the AaVCX promoter region features a predicted GCC core motif, recognized as a conserved binding site for C2H2 zinc finger transcription factors. Moreover, the surrounding sequences of this motif closely matched the binding preferences of Crz1‐like transcription factors in JASPAR, implying that this motif may serve as a key binding site for AaCrz1.

Analysis of the Expression Regulatory Relationship Between AaCrz1 and AaVCX

The interaction between AaCrz1 and AaVCX at the transcriptional level was investigated by measuring gene expression in both deletion mutants and complementation strains. The results revealed that AaVCX levels in the ΔAaCrz1 strain were markedly reduced compared to the WT (Figure 7A), while AaCrz1 expression in the ΔAaVCX showed no significant difference compared with that in the WT strain (Figure 7B). Collectively, these findings suggest that AaCrz1 functions as an upstream regulator that influences AaVCX expression positively, while its own expression remains unaffected by AaVCX.

Discussion

Calcium ions, recognized as a crucial second messenger, are integral to various biological functions in eukaryotes (Berridge et al. 2000; Carafoli 2002). Fungi regulate intracellular Ca2+ levels through a sophisticated calcium homeostasis mechanism, which includes calcium channel proteins (Cch1, Figure 1) in the plasma membrane, Ca2+‐ATPases (PMR1, PMC1) in organelle membranes, and Ca2+/H+ exchangers (VCX) in vacuolar membranes (Axelsen and Palmgren 1998; Liu et al. 2006; Pittman 2011; Sze et al. 2000; Zelter et al. 2004). It is believed that vacuoles store approximately 90% of the total calcium within cells (Miseta et al. 1999). The vacuolar Ca2+/H+ exchanger (VCX) is a vital transmembrane protein that facilitates the antiport of Ca2+/H+ across the vacuolar membrane, effectively sequestering excess cytoplasmic Ca2+ into the vacuole and playing a significant role in maintaining cytoplasmic calcium balance. Its activity is closely linked to the calcineurin‐Crz1 signalling pathway, forming a fundamental network for calcium signalling regulation in fungi (Cunningham and Fink 1996; Dunn et al. 1994; Miseta et al. 1999; Ohsumi and Anraku 1983). In S. cerevisiae, the first identified member of the VCX family, Vcx1p, has been shown to contribute to tolerance against high‐calcium and the meticulous control of cytoplasmic Ca2+ concentration (Cunningham and Fink 1996; Pittman 2011). In C. neoformans , Vcx1 is implicated in regulating fungal virulence in a calcineurin‐dependent manner (Kmetzsch et al. 2010). However, there is a lack of functional research on VCX family genes in phytopathogenic fungi.

This study marks the initial identification of the Ca2+/H+ exchanger gene AaVCX in A. alternata . Our findings indicate that the growth of ΔAaVCX mutants was notably hindered, resulting in a significant decrease in biomass accumulation. This aligns with the growth defects observed from the deletion of VCX1 in S. cerevisiae (Miseta et al. 1999) and the impaired mycelial development due to the deletion of the calcium transporter gene in Aspergillus fumigatus (Jiang et al. 2014). These observations support the idea that the function of Ca2+/H+ exchangers in regulating fundamental fungal growth is evolutionarily preserved. Melanin production and release, which serve as important virulence factors in various phytopathogenic fungi, are often influenced by environmental and cellular signalling mechanisms (Zhang, Li, et al. 2021; Zhang, Zong, et al. 2021). Melanin is generally attached to the fungal cell wall. Yang, Xie, Yuan, et al. (2022) reported that the deletion of AaCrz1 compromised the cell wall integrity of A. alternata , resulting in melanin leakage. In line with this, the ΔAaVCX mutants in our study exhibited a significant increase in extracellular melanin levels, suggesting a possible link between AaVCX and cell wall integrity that warrants further investigation.

This study indicated that the ΔAaVCX mutants exhibited a significantly higher rate of growth inhibition compared to the WT when subjected to high‐calcium stress (0.1 M Ca2+). However, there was no significant difference noted under Na2EDTA stress, and the intracellular Ca2+ levels in the mutants were markedly higher. This observation aligns closely with the VCX1 deletion mutant phenotypes observed in S. cerevisiae and C. neoformans (Cunningham and Fink 1996; Kmetzsch et al. 2010), reinforcing the idea that VCX family genes play a crucial role in enabling fungi to withstand high‐calcium stress. VCX facilitates the transport of excess cytoplasmic Ca2+ into vacuoles by mediating Ca2+/H+ antiport across the vacuolar membrane, thereby protecting intracellular enzymes and membrane structures from damage due to elevated calcium levels (Dunn et al. 1994; Ohsumi and Anraku 1983). The ΔAaVCX mutants' minimal response to Na2EDTA stress can be explained by the fact that Na2EDTA, a calcium chelator, lowers the concentration of available extracellular Ca2+, while AaVCX is primarily involved in the storage of cytoplasmic Ca2+ and does not directly influence the uptake of extracellular Ca2+. Consequently, its absence has little effect on fungal growth in low‐calcium environments. Furthermore, the ΔAaVCX mutants exhibited a marked increase in sensitivity to cell wall disruptors (CR, CFW) and an osmotic stressor (NaCl) but showed no obvious response to oxidative stress (H2O2). We hypothesize that the disruption of cytoplasmic Ca2+ homeostasis affects the regulation of genes related to cell wall synthesis (Caracuel et al. 2003; Jiang et al. 2014; Yang, Xie, Yuan, et al. 2022), leading to an incomplete cell wall structure that diminishes the pathogen's ability to withstand cell wall stress. Meanwhile, the imbalance in Ca2+ homeostasis, which is crucial for osmotic regulation, disrupts the intracellular ion balance and osmotic pressure regulation (Cui et al. 2009), making it difficult for the pathogen to manage hyperosmotic conditions effectively. The absence of a response to oxidative stress implies that A. alternata 's oxidative stress response is governed by alternative signalling pathways that do not involve AaVCX, contrasting with the regulatory mechanisms of calcium transporter genes in A. fumigatus (Jiang et al. 2014) and highlighting the functional divergence of calcium signalling pathways in response to abiotic stress among various phytopathogenic fungi.

The infection process of phytopathogenic fungi is contingent upon accurately detecting physical and chemical cues on the surface of the host, along with the systematic differentiation of infection structures. Pear peel wax serves as a crucial recognition signal for A. alternata (Tang et al. 2017; Zhang, Li, et al. 2021; Zhang, Zong, et al. 2021), prompting the pathogen to undergo the full cycle of spore germination, the differentiation of appressorium and invasive hyphal, all of which are significantly influenced by calcium signalling (Bircher and Hohl 1999; Liu and Kolattukudy 1999; Hoch et al. 1987). Numerous inhibitors targeting various components of the Ca2+ signalling pathway have been employed to obstruct this pathway, validating its involvement in regulating conidial germination and appressorium formation (Ahn and Suh 2007; Huang et al. 2020; Lee and Lee 1999; Shaw and Hoch 2000; Uhm et al. 2003). In this study, the ΔAaVCX mutants showed significantly reduced rates in both spore germination and appressorium formation rates when induced by pear peel wax, alongside a significant reduction in the formation of invasive hyphae, and nearly completely lost the ability to penetrate cellophane. This aligns with the impaired differentiation of infection structures observed in mutants lacking calcium signal‐related genes such as AaCrz1 (Yang, Xie, Yuan, et al. 2022) and AaCaM (Jiang et al. 2022) in A. alternata . We propose that the abnormal buildup of cytoplasmic Ca2+ due to the deletion of AaVCX disrupts the precise regulation of calcium signals during conidial germination and appressorium formation (Berridge et al. 2000; Carafoli 2002). It is plausible that this disruption may affect the proper activation of G protein‐coupled receptors and MAPK signalling pathways linked to host signal detection (Liu et al. 2021, 2024), thereby hindering appressorium maturation and the differentiation of invasive hyphae (Figure 8). However, direct evidence linking elevated cytoplasmic Ca2+ to altered GPCR/MAPK activity in the ΔAaVCX mutant is currently lacking. Pathogenicity tests revealed that the ΔAaVCX mutants exhibited significantly lower virulence towards Zaosu pear fruits and leaves, mirroring the reduced virulence observed with the Vcx1 deletion mutant in C. neoformans (Kmetzsch et al. 2010), suggesting a conserved role of Ca2+/H+ exchangers in the virulence of phytopathogenic fungi across different species. In non‐wounded inoculations on pear leaves, the mutants produced only minor lesions on the upper surface, while no lesions appeared on the lower surface, suggesting that AaVCX is crucial for regulating A. alternata 's penetration through the leaf epidermis.

AaVCX, a vacuolar Ca2+/H+ exchanger regulated by AaCrz1, maintains calcium homeostasis. Its deletion elevates cytoplasmic Ca2+, disrupting signals essential for spore germination, appressorium formation and infection hyphae differentiation, thereby blocking infection structure differentiation and reducing pathogenicity in Alternaria alternata .

The calcineurin‐Crz1 signalling pathway is crucial for the regulation of calcium signals in fungal species. In response to calcium stimulation, the transcription factor Crz1 translocates to the nucleus, where it binds to specific promoter motifs to control the expression of target genes, thus facilitating the maintenance of calcium homeostasis, growth, development and pathogenicity in fungi (Soriani et al. 2010; Yang, Xie, Li, et al. 2022; Yang, Xie, Yuan, et al. 2022). This study used molecular docking, dual‐luciferase reporter assays and Y1H assays to demonstrate that AaCrz1 can bind to the AaVCX promoter in vitro and positively regulate its transcription. The promoter region contains a predicted GCC core motif, which is typical of C2H2 zinc finger transcription factors (Dinamarco et al. 2012; Iuchi 2001). Notably, the function of this predicted GCC‐core motif was not verified by site‐directed mutagenesis in the present study, and the exact contribution of this motif to the AaCrz1‐mediated transcriptional regulation of AaVCX remains to be elucidated further. Gene expression analysis indicates that AaCrz1 acts upstream of AaVCX to positively regulate its transcription; however, functional epistasis between the two genes has not yet been tested. This regulatory mode aligns with the role of CrzA in regulating calcium transporter genes in A. fumigatus (Soriani et al. 2010) and the influence of FgCrz1A on calcium signal‐related genes in Fusarium graminearum (Li et al. 2018), suggesting that positive regulation of calcium transporter genes by Crz1 may be conserved in some phytopathogenic fungi. However, in S. cerevisiae , Crz1 inhibits the expression of VCX1 (Cunningham and Fink 1996), highlighting the evolutionary divergence in the calcium signalling regulatory networks among fungi. As a saprophytic organism, S. cerevisiae maintains calcium homeostasis through the repression of VCX1 and the activation of Ca2+‐ATPases mediated by Crz1 (Cunningham and Fink 1996; Cunningham and Fink 1994). Conversely, A. alternata, a phytopathogenic fungus, exhibits positive regulation of AaVCX expression through Crz1. Such contradictory regulatory mechanisms reflect the functional adaptation of the conserved Crz1‐VCX module to distinct fungal lifestyles and survival strategies. S. cerevisiae adapts to saprophytic growth and stable nutritional environments, requiring negative feedback regulation to maintain calcium homeostasis. In contrast, plant‐pathogenic fungi encounter complex and hostile host immune pressures, leading to the evolution of a positive regulatory mechanism to ensure efficient infection. However, this hypothesis awaits direct experimental validation.

Collectively, this study represents the inaugural effort to elucidate the role of the Ca2+/H+ exchanger gene AaVCX in A. alternata . Our data show that AaVCX is essential for modulating vegetative growth, tolerance to high‐calcium stress, cell wall stability and responses to osmotic stress by maintaining intracellular calcium homeostasis. Additionally, it facilitates the pathogen's ability to detect host wax signals and develop infection structures, marking it as a vital gene for pathogenicity. Our findings also indicate that AaCrz1 enhances the expression of AaVCX by interacting with its promoter, forming a significant regulatory module within the calcium signalling network of A. alternata . Future research should directly validate the vacuolar localization of AaVCX through the use of fluorescent fusion proteins and confirm the in vivo binding of AaCrz1 to the AaVCX promoter via chromatin immunoprecipitation‐quantitative PCR (ChIP‐qPCR). The potential of AaVCX as an antifungal target requires chemical‐genetic validation. Additionally, the interactions between the AaCrz1‐AaVCX module and other signalling pathways, such as MAPK and G protein, warrant further investigation to fully elucidate the pathogenicity of A. alternata and to enhance strategies for controlling pear black spot disease.

Experimental Procedures

Pathogens

The strain JT‐3 of A. alternata , responsible for the black spot disease in pears, was obtained from infected Zaosu pear tissues in Jingtai, Gansu Province. It underwent purification through single‐spore isolation and was identified using ITS rDNA sequencing. The ΔAaCrz1 knockout mutant and its complemented strain ΔAaCrz1‐C were constructed in our laboratory in a previous study (Yang, Xie, Yuan, et al. 2022).

Construction of AaVCX Knockout Mutants and Complemented Strains

The hygromycin phosphotransferase (hph) gene knockout fragment was generated using the split‐marker PCR method, with the fundamental concept of this gene knockout strategy illustrated in Figure S1. Flanking sequences both upstream and downstream of AaVCX were amplified from the genomic DNA of A. alternata using the primer pairs AaVCX‐up‐F/R and AaVCX‐down‐F/R, respectively. The upstream (H1) and downstream (H2) fragments of the hph gene were amplified from plasmid pCB1003 using primer pairs HYG‐F/HY‐R and YG‐F/HYG‐R (Table S1). Two rounds of PCR were subsequently performed for fragment fusion, with the PCR systems and procedures detailed in Tables [Link], [Link]. The gene knockout vector was obtained by ethanol precipitation (Dai et al. 2020). Protoplasts of A. alternata were prepared and transformed with the knockout cassette following the protocol of Yang, Xie, Yuan, et al. (2022). In this study, two independent knockout mutants, designated ΔAaVCX‐1 and ΔAaVCX‐2, were generated using the split‐marker strategy.

For construction of the complemented strain, the full‐length coding sequence (CDS) of AaVCX was amplified by PCR from A. alternata cDNA using the primers listed in Table S1. The binary vector pCAMBIA1300‐NEO, carrying the OliC promoter, TrpC terminator and resistance genes for hygromycin B and geneticin, was used for vector construction (Zhang, Li, et al. 2021; Zhang, Zong, et al. 2021). In this study, we used a validated complemented strain ΔAaVCX‐C, which was constructed by reintroducing the full‐length coding sequence of AaVCX into the ΔAaVCX‐1 mutant.

Determination of Colony Diameter and Morphology of WT, ΔAaVCX and ΔAaVCX ‐C

A 2 μL aliquot of spore suspension (106 spores/mL) of WT, ΔAaVCX and ΔAaVCX‐C was inoculated onto PDA plates. Colony diameters were measured by the cross method and photographed at 3, 5 and 7 dpi (Xu et al. 2018). Three independent biological replicates were performed for the experiment, with three Petri dishes in each replicate.

Biomass Determination

Cellophane sheets were cut to match the diameter of Petri dishes, sterilized and laid flat on the surface of PDA medium using sterile forceps. A 2 μL aliquot of spore suspension was inoculated at the centre of the cellophane. After incubation, the cellophane with mycelia was gently peeled and weighed with an electronic balance. Each independent biological replicate consisted of three cellophane‐covered plates per strain. The entire experiment was conducted with three independent biological replicates.

Extracellular Melanin Determination

Melanin was extracted according to the method of Wang et al. (2020). WT, ΔAaVCX and ΔAaVCX‐C strains were cultured in potato dextrose broth. The culture filtrate was adjusted to pH 2 with HCl, then centrifuged, and the supernatant discarded. The extracellular melanin precipitate was resuspended in HCl and incubated in a boiling water bath. After cooling, the mixture was centrifuged, and the supernatant was discarded. The precipitate was dissolved in NaOH, and absorbance at 400 nm was measured with an ultraviolet spectrophotometer, using NaOH as the blank. Each independent biological replicate comprised three individual flasks per strain. The entire experiment was performed with three independent biological replicates.

Calcium Stress Assay

The calcium stress assay was performed as described by Yang et al. (2024). PDA supplemented with different concentrations of CaCl2 or Na2EDTA (calcium chelator) was prepared, with non‐supplemented PDA as the control. Colony diameters were measured by the cross method, and the growth inhibition rate was calculated using the formula: Growth inhibition rate (%) = (Colony diameter on control medium − Colony diameter on stress medium)/Colony diameter on control medium × 100. Each independent biological replicate consisted of three plates for each strain under each stress condition. The entire experiment was performed with three independent biological replicates.

Intracellular Calcium Content Determination

Inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP‐MS) was used for the quantitative analysis of intracellular calcium content. Precisely 0.1 g of dried mycelia was placed in a polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) digestion tank, and ultrapure nitric acid was added. The tank was sealed and placed in a microwave digester for digestion. Ultrapure nitric acid of the same volume was used as the blank control. The sample digests and blank control were analysed by ICP‐MS with optimal instrument parameters. Each independent biological replicate consisted of three individual culture flasks per strain. The entire experiment was performed with three independent biological replicates.

Abiotic Stress Response Assay

The abiotic stress response assay was performed with minor modifications according to Nan et al. (2025). Different stress factors were added to molten PDA, mixed thoroughly, and poured into plates to prepare stress media. The stress factors and their concentrations were as follows: 1 M NaCl (osmotic stress), 0.02% H2O2 (oxidative stress), 0.1 M Congo Red (CR, cell wall disruptor) and 80 μg/mL Calcofluor White (CFW, cell wall disruptor). Each independent biological replicate consisted of three plates per strain per stress condition. The entire experiment was performed with three independent biological replicates.

Infection Ability Assays of A. alternata

Infection Structure Assay of A. alternata Under Various Induction Conditions

Silanized glass slides were coated with Gelbond PAG films to create two inductive surfaces: the hydrophobic surface of the film and the surface precoated with Zaosu pear peel wax extract. A 20 μL aliquot of spore suspension was dropped onto each inductive surface. For each independent biological replicate, three glass slides were prepared for each strain under each induction condition. At 2, 4, 6 and 8 h post‐inoculation, the spores on the films were stained with lactophenol cotton blue. Five randomly selected fields of view were examined under a light microscope for each slide. In each field, at least 100 spores were counted to assess spore germination and appressorium formation. The entire experiment was conducted using three independent biological replicates. The spore germination rate and appressorium formation rate were calculated using the following formulas: Spore germination rate (%) = (Number of germinated spores/Total number of counted spores) × 100; Appressorium formation rate (%) = (Number of spores with appressoria/Total number of counted spores) × 100.

Invasive Hyphal Formation Assay of A. alternata

Onion epidermis was used to simulate the infection process of A. alternata in vivo according to the method of Tang et al. (2017). Fresh onions were cut into small pieces measuring 5 × 2 cm for subsequent infection structure assays and the inner epidermis was carefully peeled off with sterile forceps and laid flat on clean glass slides. Zaosu pear wax solution was evenly spread on the onion epidermis using a sterile spreader. For each independent biological replicate, three onion epidermis pieces were prepared per strain. A 20 μL aliquot of spore suspension was dropped onto the treated onion epidermis. The culture conditions and observation methods were identical to those described in Section 4.9.1.

Penetration Ability Assay of A. alternata

Sterilized cellophane sheets were laid flat on the surface of solidified PDA without air bubbles. A 2 μL aliquot of spore suspension was inoculated at the center of the cellophane. The colony morphology on the cellophane surface was observed and recorded. The cellophane sheets were then carefully peeled off, and the plates were further incubated in the dark at 28°C for another 48 h. The colony growth on the PDA was observed and recorded again. Each independent biological replicate consisted of three cellophane‐covered plates per strain. The entire experiment was performed with three independent biological replicates.

Virulence Assay of A. alternata

The pathogenicity of each strain was evaluated through non‐wounded inoculation on pear leaves and wounded inoculation on pear fruits. For non‐wounded inoculation (Liu et al. 2019), a 20 μL aliquot of spore suspension was dropped onto the surface of Zaosu pear leaves. For each independent biological replicate, six leaves were used per strain. The experiment was performed with three independent biological replicates. For wounded inoculation, three wounds (3 mm in diameter and 5 mm in depth) were made on the equator of each Zaosu pear fruit using a sterile puncher, and a spore suspension was injected into each wound. For each independent biological replicate, nine fruits were used per strain, with three wounds per fruit. The experiment was performed with three independent biological replicates (Huang et al. 2020).

Molecular Docking

Protein Structure Acquisition

The three‐dimensional (3D) structure of AaCrz1 protein was predicted by AlphaFold3 based on its amino acid sequence and the promoter sequence of AaVCX. The structure with the highest predicted local distance difference test (pLDDT) score was selected as the model for subsequent analysis.

AaCrz1 Protein‐ AaVCX Promoter Nucleic Acid Docking

The protein–nucleic acid docking was performed using the local version of HDOCKlite v. 1.1 server. HDOCK develops an efficient molecular docking algorithm and accurate scoring function for biomolecular interactions by combining physics‐based and bioinformatics‐based methods.

Analysis of AaCrz1 Protein– AaVCX Promoter Nucleic Acid Interactions

Protein–nucleic acid complexes are formed through non‐covalent interactions between molecules, mainly including hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonds and electrostatic interactions. The PLIP platform was employed to conduct comprehensive characterization and systematic analysis of the binding interface in the protein–nucleic acid complex, and PyMOL was used to supplement the detailed information of the interactions.

Dual‐Luciferase Reporter Assay

The transcription factor AaCrz1 and the promoter AaVCX were amplified using cDNA and DNA of A. alternata as templates, respectively. The specific primers are shown in Table S6. The pGreenII 62‐SK and pGreenII 0800‐LUC vectors were double‐digested with the corresponding restriction enzymes, and the amplified AaCrz1 fragment was ligated into the pGreenII 62‐SK vector, while the AaVCX promoter fragment was ligated into the pGreenII 0800‐LUC vector. The constructed dual‐luciferase vectors were transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101 (pSoup) chemically competent cells (AC1002). Positive transformant A. tumefaciens strains were cultured to the logarithmic phase and infiltrated into the abaxial side of Nicotiana benthamiana leaves. After 3 days of incubation, the activities of firefly luciferase (LUC) and renilla luciferase (REN, internal reference) were detected to analyse the regulatory effect of the transcription factor on the target gene promoter (Xu et al. 2014).

Y1H Assay

The pGADT7 (AD) and pAbAi vectors were double‐digested with the corresponding restriction enzymes; the AaCrz1 fragment was ligated into pGADT7 as the prey protein, and the AaVCX promoter fragment was ligated into pAbAi as the bait protein. The specific primers are shown in Table S7. The linearized AaVCX‐pAbAi recombinant plasmid was transformed into Y1H Gold yeast competent cells, which were subsequently inoculated onto SD−Ura medium supplemented with varying concentrations of AbA for the analysis of the AaVCX promoter's self‐activation activity. Yeast competent cells were prepared according to the instructions of the Pointe Yeast Competent Cell Preparation Kit. The AD‐AaCrz1 plasmid was transformed into the prepared Y1H Gold (AaVCX‐AbAi) competent cells, and the transformed bacterial solution was spread on SD−Leu screening plates and incubated at 28°C for 3–5 days. A positive control (AD‐Rec‐53 transformed into Y1H [p53‐AbAi]) and a negative control (empty AD vector transformed into Y1H Gold [AaVCX‐AbAi]) were set up to verify the interaction between AaCrz1 and the AaVCX promoter (Wei et al. 2022).

Data Analysis

Microsoft Office 2010 was used to calculate the mean and standard deviation (SD). Origin 8.5 was used for graph plotting, and SPSS 26.0 was used for Duncan's multiple range test, with a level of α = 0.05 adopted to determine significant differences.
