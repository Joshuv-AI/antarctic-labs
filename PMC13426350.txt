Neuro-Inclusion in Medical Education: Are We Providing Neuro Affirmative Experiences to Include Neurodivergent Learners? An Exploration of Tutor Learning Needs
Authors: Kelly Fletcher, Enam Haque, Clare Clarke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGrowing numbers of medical students now identify as neurodivergent. Educators must therefore adapt their teaching practices within clinical placements in healthcare settings to promote a more inclusive learning environment. However, there are no studies to explore how this can be done. This study explored the faculty perspective on teaching neurodivergent medical students during their clinical placements, as well as what their needs as educators are. Methods of improving placement experience were identified and proposed.ApproachDoctors involved in undergraduate medical education teaching and supervision at a UK medical school were invited by email to participate in this qualitative study. Interested educators read a participant information sheet and returned a signed written consent form by email. Six participants were recruited take part in online semi-structured interviews exploring the educator perspective. The transcripts obtained from Zoom recordings were analysed, and the data thematically analysed using NVivo 12 software.EvaluationFaculty were aware of neurodivergence and were committed to continuous learning, to improve teaching methods for neurodivergent students. 83% highlighted a clear difference in learning style within neurodivergent students, with 100% referencing a requirement to adapt their teaching style to suit neurodivergent needs. Interestingly, 83% of participants disclosed varying forms of personal experience with neurodivergent people outside of the medical education context. Key barriers to supporting students included student reluctance to share their diagnosis, practical challenges to implementing reasonable adjustments, and the shadow of stigma. 67% of participants felt completely unprepared in terms of understanding neurodivergent learner needs and how to best support them, with the remaining 33% relying on their own self-directed learning. All participants highlighted critical gaps in the support systems available to tutors who teach neurodivergent learners and 83% expressed a significant desire for at least one form of additional support.ConclusionMedical education remains behind the curve in engaging with the neurodivergence movement. These findings show that this issue is not just associated with individual educators but provides a system wide challenge. Faculty members lack knowledge, confidence and institutional support needed to effectively meet the needs of neurodivergent learners. Medical schools must therefore provide practical training, clearer guidance and consistent support structures to help educators create a neuro-affirmative learning environment.

Neurodivergence has gained significant attention in recent years, with an estimated 15–20% of the global population identifying as neurodivergent.1 The term describes variations in neurodevelopment and encompasses various conditions within the literature.2 Figure 1 illustrates this meaning within the context of the study. Unfortunately, the medical profession has been slow to accommodate neurodivergent individuals, despite this growing recognition.4 Data remains limited on the number of neurodivergent medical students, and available estimates likely underrepresent the true picture.5 Fear of stigma may contribute to this lack of data, with many medical students reluctant to share disabilities.4

Medical school can present particular challenges for neurodivergent students, as they face coordination, organisation and communication barriers within an environment that fails to accommodate their needs.6,7 The “double empathy problem” further highlights communication barriers that may arise between neurodivergent students and their neurotypical peers and educators.8 Progress remains limited, despite calls from the Medical Schools Council for inclusivity.6,9

There is a clear mismatch between the learning needs of neurodivergent individuals and the support currently provided by medical schools. This study therefore explored the faculty perspectives on teaching neurodivergent medical students to facilitate the creation of a neuro-affirmative learning environment.

Methods

Participants

General Practitioners (GPs) and Hospital Consultant Clinical Placement Supervisors (CPSs), as well as hospital doctors teaching undergraduate medical students at a northern medical school, were invited to take part in the study.

Recruitment

An email invitation, including a participant information sheet and consent form, was sent to eligible participants through the central teaching team at the medical school. The consent form included participant consent for publication of anonymised responses and direct quotes. Six individuals returned a signed consent form via email. They were invited to select from a list of dates to attend an individual online interview with the lead author. The author sent individualised links with passwords to the participants before each interview.

Study Design

Semi-structured interviews were created and conducted by a third-year medical student, who was a member of the research team, in May 2024. This occurred via Zoom, a communication platform licensed by the university. A copy of the interview sheet is available on Figshare. Semi-structured interviews were selected for their flexible methodology, allowing participants the freedom to expand on different focus areas that were most important to them. The lead author confirmed the participants identity, purpose of the interview and obtained verbal consent at the beginning of the interview. Each interview lasted 1 hour, with interview questions exploring faculty experiences with neurodivergent medical students.

Data Collection

Zoom audio recordings were obtained for each interview and subsequently deleted once the lead author had reviewed and anonymised the automatic transcription generated by Zoom. Anonymised transcripts were finalised within 2 weeks. These transcripts were stored securely in a university data storage system.

Data Analysis

The main author conducted thematic analysis of the transcripts using NVivo 12 software, generating codes to identify themes (Table 1). Ethics

This study had ethical approval from the University of Manchester Research Proportionate Ethics Committee (Reference: 2024–19,008-34,055).

Limitations

A potential limitation of this study was interviewer bias, whereby interviewer phrasing, tone or non-verbal reactions may have influenced participant response. Semi-structured interviews allow flexibility to improve depth but may create inconsistency in how questions are asked and examined. This risk is mitigated through the use of a single interviewer, a consistent interview guide and reflexive awareness throughout the interview process.

Discussion

This study explored educators’ experiences of teaching neurodivergent medical students. The key finding was that while participants demonstrated growing awareness and motivation to be inclusive, they identified significant gaps in practical guidance, training and institutional support, leading to inconsistent implementation of neuro-affirmative practise. These findings are consistent with existing research showing limited staff confidence and training around neurodivergence and disability.10 However, this study also highlights an increasing recognition of systemic barriers, including unclear frameworks for balancing fairness and accommodation. Additionally, participants offered suggestions to improve learning experiences, an area that has not previously been explored. Overall, this study emphasises that a shift towards acknowledging meaningful inclusion requires not only individual effort, but co-ordinated institutional responsibility and evidence informed approaches.

Increasing Recognition of Neurodivergence in Medical Education

This study found that medical educators were increasingly aware of neurodivergence and committed to improving teaching practises. This contrasts with earlier studies which found widespread misunderstanding and negative views of autism within medical education, indicating a shift towards deeper understanding.6,11 Despite this, increased recognition does not always equate to neuro-affirmative practise. Studies have shown that awareness can remain deficit-focused, emphasising labels and reinforcing stereotypes.12 The MSC’s endorsement of communication guidance with autistic individuals in healthcare represents a key step to address this, but findings from this study show that this has not yet been translated into consistent, practical tools.9 As a result, educators are often required to improvise, leading to variability in practise and continuation of inequalities in learning experiences.

Challenges of Stigma and Non-Disclosure

Educators in this study linked student non-disclosure of neurodivergence to fears around privacy and data protection breaches, as well as lack of confidence that there would be tailored support for them after disclosure. Studies have framed disclosure as a pre-requisite for reasonable adjustments, emphasising that barriers to disclosure need to be addressed.13 Research with autistics and ADHD medical trainees shows that decisions about disclosure are shaped by fear of impact on career progression, perceived stigma and prior negative experiences, and not misunderstanding of potential benefits.4,6 This suggest that the “stalemate” between perceived need for disclosure and respecting individual privacy is driven by a culture that discourages disclosure. Universal Design for Learning (UDL) could mitigate the need to share diagnosis as it encourages pre-emptive flexibility, shifting responsibility to the organisation to be adaptable, rather than individuals having to face the stigma of sharing.14 Encouraging educators to use UDL principles to support individuals learning needs, rather than relying on sharing diagnostic information could improve support, though staff training remains essential to overcome biases.14,15 Currently, there are no studies that explore the staff perspective on this matter, and more research is therefore needed to better influence training frameworks.

The Impact of Personal Experience

Most educators reported personal experience with neurodivergent students, which is often valued by students as it enhances empathy and confidence.16 However, relying primarily on personal experience risks overgeneralisation and inconsistent practise. The heavy presence of personally invested educators is both a strength and a limitation. It is a strength because they are often early adopters of inclusive practise, but a limitation as more sceptical colleagues, who may post the greatest barrier to inclusion, are under-represented. These findings suggest that personal experience should be complemented by structured, evidence-informed training.

Balancing Support with Professional Demands – A Systemic Issue

The study highlighted challenges in balancing support for neurodivergent learners with the fast-paced demands of medical practice. This reflects a systemic issue, where educators are positioned within environments that prioritise productivity over inclusion.5 These findings suggest that faculty development alone is insufficient without broader changes to workload, assessment, and service pressures. Addressing these issues aligns with the General Medical Council’s Welcomed and Valued guidance.17

Navigation of Positive Discrimination and Fairness

Participants described the tension between supporting neurodivergent students and maintaining fairness. This aligns with previous research indicating that students may avoid support to prevent appearing advantaged.18 Concerns about positive discrimination are commonly discussed within the disability accommodation literature and may reflect strong beliefs in merit-based fairness within medical education.6 However, research on accommodation policies suggest that treating everyone the same can reinforce existing inequalities, as it assumes all learners experience the environment in the same way.19 These findings suggest that educators need clearer, institutionally endorsed guidance on how to balance individual support with consistent standards, shifting the focus from individual attitudes to how fairness and competence are defined. Medical schools must support educators in balancing accommodations with practice demands through flexible scheduling, resource allocation, and inclusive culture-building. However, exact solutions to this problem are beyond the scope of this study.

Essential Role of the Institution

Educators consistently reported feeling under-prepared to support neurodivergent students and highlighted limited, fragmented institutional support. This reflects wider evidence that disability-related training is often minimal and compliance-focused.10 In contrast, structured frameworks and clear guidance leads to improve confidence and consistency.20–22 These findings shift responsibility from individual educators to institutions, suggesting current gaps are a predictable result of under-resourced, decentralised support systems.

Exploration of Effective Support Mechanisms for Educators

Whilst existing research has focused on supporting neurodivergent students, this study emphasises the need to consider the support needs of educators.23,24 Participants in this study identified support groups, accessible materials, dedicated contact points, and staff education as potential solutions, recognising the importance of ongoing practical training guided by experts. In contrast, individual-led models may place too much work on a small group of committed staff, which may not be sustainable and may unintentionally reduce wider institutional accountability.25,26

Implications, Limitations and Future Directions

This study suggests that educators are increasingly aware of neurodivergence and are willing to engage, but they operate within systems that constrain neuro-affirmative practise. It supports targeted, co-produced faculty development that integrates UDL, critical disability perspectives and practical tools, underpinned by institutional policies that address accountability for inclusive practise. The small sample size and lack of secondary care educators limits generalisability, and researcher positionality may have shaped both data generation and interpretation, however the use of semi-structured interviews allowed exploration of nuanced tensions.27–29 Future research should purposively sample educators with varying levels of engagement and scepticism, include secondary-care and post-graduate settings, and examine the impact of specific institutional interventions on both learner outcomes and staff experiences.

Conclusion

This study explored the experiences of tutors educating neurodivergent medical students. While educators are motivated to supporting neurodivergent students, their efforts are constrained by stigma, disclosure dilemmas, and challenges in implementing reasonable adjustments. Educators tended to rely on personal experience rather than structured, evidence-informed training, and reported a lack of coherent institutional frameworks or resources to support neuro-affirmative practise. These findings suggest that responsibility for inclusion cannot rest with individual educators. This under-researched area warrants further study to better understand the needs of both students and educators, guiding a structured approach to support neurodivergent learners. With proper preparation, training, and institutional support, medical education can become a more neuro-affirmative experience.
