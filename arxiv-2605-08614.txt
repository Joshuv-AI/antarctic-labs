DiagnosticIQ: A Benchmark for LLM-Based Industrial Maintenance Action Recommendation from Symbolic Rules
Devin Yasith De Silva, Dhaval Patel, Christodoulos Constantinides, Shuxin Lin, Nianjun Zhou, Paul J Adams, Sal Rosato, Nicolas Constantinides, Deborah L. McGuinness, Jayant Kalagnanam, [1pt] IBM Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute Independent Contributor

Abstract
Monitoring complex industrial assets relies on engineer-authored symbolic rules that trigger based on sensor conditions and prompt technicians to perform corrective actions. The bottleneck is not detection but response: translating rules into maintenance steps requires asset-specific knowledge gained through years of practice. We investigate whether LLMs can serve as decision support for this rule-to-action step and introduce DiagnosticIQ, a benchmark of 6,690 expert-validated multiple-choice questions from 118 rule-action pairs across 16 asset types. We contribute (i) a symbolic-to-MCQA pipeline normalizing rules to Disjunctive Normal Form with embedding-based distractor sampling, (ii) five variants probing distinct failure modes (Pro, Pert, Verbose, Aug, Rationale), and (iii) a benchmark of 29 LLMs and 4 embedding baselines. A human evaluation (9 practitioners, mean 45.0%) confirms DiagnosticIQ requires specialist knowledge beyond operational experience. Three findings stand out. The frontier has closed: the top three LLMs lie within one Macro point, with Bradley-Terry Elo placing claude-opus-4-6 30 points above the next model. Yet DiagnosticIQ Pro exposes brittleness, with every model losing 13–60% relative accuracy under distractor expansion. DiagnosticIQ Aug exposes pattern-matching: under condition inversion, frontier models still select the original answer 49–63% of the time. The deployment bottleneck is not capability but calibration: frontier models handle template-style fault detection but break under structural perturbation.

Consider Bob, a facility manager responsible for HVAC systems in a data center. Bob authors rules that raise alerts based on asset-specific logical conditions, such as
{\color[rgb]{0,0.6,0}\text{Temperature}}>80^{\circ}\text{F}
(Condition 1) or

The bottleneck is not detection, but the action:
The rules that translate raw sensor data into triggered conditions are already authored by domain experts using standard guidelines
[
3
]
; the open problem is deciding what to do once a rule fires. Determining the right inspection or repair step often exceeds Bob’s expertise, and the challenge compounds in environments with heterogeneous assets from different manufacturers, with different operating modes and hundreds of sensors. For each abnormal condition, identifying what to inspect or repair requires asset-specific knowledge of failure modes typically gained through years of hands-on experience. Can LLMs help bridge this gap? In our human evaluation, frontier LLMs answered 10 of 12 questions on which no engineering practitioner in our panel could identify the correct response (Appendix Section
C
; although at N=40, no LLM significantly exceeds the best human).

To address this gap, we present DiagnosticIQ, a benchmark suite for industrial maintenance action recommendation. Figure

Grounded in real-world rules and expert-validated actions, the suite features a primary multiple-choice question-answering (MCQA) dataset along with several variants, each evaluating a specific capability such as reasoning, generalization, or robustness. Our contributions are:

•

Symbolic-to-MCQA pipeline.
A deterministic pipeline maps expert-authored symbolic rules, including temporal predicates, into MCQA. Rules are normalized into Disjunctive Normal Form (DNF) to expand rule-to-action coverage, and an embedding-based sampling mechanism constructs a diverse distribution of plausible distractors.

•

DiagnosticIQ benchmark.
We build DiagnosticIQ, a benchmark of 6,690 expert-validated multiple-choice questions derived from 118 rule-action pairs across 16 heterogeneous asset types.

•

Extensive empirical evaluation.
We benchmark 29 LLMs across 9 families and 4 embedding baselines on DiagnosticIQ and five targeted variants (DiagnosticIQ Pro, DiagnosticIQ Aug, DiagnosticIQ Verbose, DiagnosticIQ Pert, DiagnosticIQ Rationale), with analyses spanning question complexity, asset type, robustness, fine-tuning, rationale validation, and operational impact.

Summary of Empirical Insights.
DiagnosticIQ remains unsaturated for current frontier models. The leading model reaches 73.6% Macro and loses 13.8 points on DiagnosticIQ Pro (hard dataset). The three latest-generation closed-source releases (claude-opus-4-6, gpt-5.4, gemini-3.1) cluster within one point of each other. Note that
model selection was determined in part by our monthly compute budget ($5000)
alongside model availability and family coverage priorities. Beyond aggregate accuracy, two failure modes emerge. A logical-inversion probe shows the leader selects the now-incorrect original answer 63% of the time, indicating that current LLMs treat structured rules as templates to recognize rather than conditions to evaluate. Paraphrasing rules into natural language costs 4.3 Macro points on average (up to
-9.6
for smaller models), exposing a
symbolic-to-linguistic gap
. Two further findings bear on deployment: fine-tuning bifurcates by base architecture (SFT favors reasoning-tuned models; GRPO favors non-reasoning ones), and correct recommendations from the top model account for an estimated USD 3.13–4.82M in operational impact.

3
Symbolic Conditions to MCQA

We present a pipeline that converts a single expert-authored rule into multiple grounded multiple-choice questions. The pipeline composes four ingredients into a single deterministic procedure: (i) parsing rule documents into structured condition trees with associated actions and metadata; (ii) normalizing trees into Disjunctive Normal Form (DNF) so that each conjunction yields a logically valid trigger scenario; (iii) generating two complementary question types,
selection
and
elimination
, each drawing distractors from rules at opposite ends of a rule-rule semantic similarity scale; and (iv) preserving asset-level metadata (asset name, description, triggering duration) in the prompt so that questions are answerable only by reasoning over both rule and asset context.
While each component is established, their composition into a deterministic rule-to-MCQA pipeline is, to our knowledge, novel for industrial maintenance.

Symbolic Conditions to MCQA Pipeline. “Met for 2 Hours” applies to any condition.

3.1
Input: Expert-Curated Rule Documents

The rule documents originated from the Smarter Buildings initiative. Reliability Engineers, System Administrators, and a Rules Logic committee collaboratively developed the fault-detection logic using ASHRAE guidelines
[
3
]
, refining it iteratively over several years across multiple equipment types (e.g., Air Handlers, Chillers, Boilers) to expand coverage and maintain diagnostic accuracy. A detailed description of the rule development process is provided in Appendix
A.2
. As shown in Figure
3
, each rule
\mathcal{R}^{i}
comprises three components:

Conditions:
A set
\mathcal{C}^{i}=\langle c^{i}_{1},c^{i}_{2},\dots,c^{i}_{n}\rangle
of atomic boolean predicates over sensor readings or asset states (e.g.,
\text{Temperature}>80^{\circ}\text{F}
,
\text{Enthalpy}<15\,\text{BTU/lb}
), which must be jointly satisfied for a duration
t^{i}
to activate the rule. The duration
t^{i}
is preserved as an explicit field in the question prompt (Section
3.2.1
) so that temporal sensitivity is testable.

Maintenance actions:
A set
\mathcal{O}^{i}=\{o^{i}_{1},o^{i}_{2},\dots,o^{i}_{N_{O}^{i}}\}
, each hypothesized for verification once the rule triggers.

Metadata:
Rule identifier (
rule_id
), rule name, asset type (
asset_type
), rule description, and estimated cost savings achieved by applying the rule.

We extract and assemble these conditions into a structured logical formula, the
condition tree

\mathcal{T}\mathcal{R}^{i}
: a boolean expression constructed from
\mathcal{C}^{i}
using
\land
,
\lor
, and optionally
\lnot
, where each leaf is an atomic condition and the root evaluates to
True
iff the rule fires. We define a rule as the tuple

\mathcal{R}^{i}=(\textit{rule\_id},\;\textit{asset\_type},\;\mathcal{TR}^{i},\;\mathcal{O}^{i},\;\mathcal{C}^{i},\;t^{i}),

and the full corpus as
\mathcal{DS}_{\mathcal{R}}=\{\mathcal{R}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{\mathcal{R}}}
with
N_{\mathcal{R}}=118
. Rules span 16 asset types (Appendix Table
5
summarizes the per-asset count of rules, disjunction operators, atomic conditions, and observations). The number of disjunctions is informative because it bounds the branching complexity of
\mathcal{TR}^{i}
and therefore the number of distinct trigger scenarios derivable from a single rule.

Asset descriptions:
Industrial assets of the same type can differ substantially in configuration (e.g., horizontal vs. vertical pumps, manufacturer, age, sensor suite). To anchor questions in this context, we developed concise textual descriptions
Desc
for each asset type in collaboration with industry experts (Appendix Table
4
), inserted into the prompt as the
AD
field. An ablation in Appendix
A.7
shows that removing
AD
reduces accuracy by 8.6–10.2% across three models, confirming that asset context is load-bearing for this task and not a presentational addition.

3.2
QA Generation Pipeline

We design two complementary question types that probe different reasoning skills.
Selection
questions ask the model to identify the
most relevant
action given the question conditions
QC
, testing whether the model can prioritize the correct root cause from operationally adjacent alternatives.
Elimination
questions invert this: the model must identify the action that does
not
belong to
\mathcal{R}^{i}
, testing whether it can recognize when a condition-action mapping is unsupported. The two types share question structure but use opposite distractor-sampling strategies, exposing the model to complementary failure modes. Before question generation, we precompute the two artifacts.

Rule-Rule Similarity (RRSim):
For each rule
\mathcal{R}^{i}
, we build a textual representation of its condition tree
\mathcal{TR}^{i}
from the expert documentation, embed each representation with
all-mpnet-base-v2

[
30
]
, and compute pairwise cosine similarities. RRSim allows us to retrieve semantically similar or dissimilar rules at distractor-sampling time, which is the lever that makes
selection
and
elimination
probes inequivalent. The full RRSim matrix and analysis are given in Appendix Section
A.5.1
.

Unique Observations (UO):
We manually curate and categorize observations and actions across
\mathcal{DS}_{\mathcal{R}}
into a universal candidate pool. The benchmark contains
|UO|=193
unique observations with a mean length of approximately 20 tokens; four examples appear in Figure
3
under
Options
, and the full word-count distribution is given in Appendix Figure
8
.

3.2.1
Question-Answer Structure

Each question
\mathcal{Q}^{i}
is a tuple
(AD,QC,QP,OPT,A)
, where
AD
is the asset name and description,
QC
specifies the observed conditions for the question (including the triggering duration
t^{i}
where applicable),
QP
is the question prompt drawn from
N_{QT}
templates,
OPT
is the set of answer options (4 in DiagnosticIQ, 10 in DiagnosticIQ-Pro), and
A
is the ground-truth answer (single-correct).

3.2.2
Rule Representation to Disjunctive Normal Form

For each rule
\mathcal{R}^{i}
, we convert the condition tree
\mathcal{TR}^{i}
into Disjunctive Normal Form,
\mathcal{TR}^{i}_{\text{DNF}}=\bigvee_{k=1}^{K}\left(\bigwedge_{j=1}^{m_{k}}c^{i}_{kj}\right)
, where each conjunctive clause is a complete observation pattern sufficient to activate the rule. This yields
K
distinct, logically valid trigger scenarios per rule, each used as an independent
QC
, rather than a single example per rule. For example, a rule
\mathcal{TR}^{0}=(c_{1}\lor c_{2})\land c_{3}
expands to DNF
(c_{1}\land c_{3})\lor(c_{2}\land c_{3})
, yielding two questions. We use conjunctive (DNF-expanded) presentation rather than the full condition tree because it matches how rules execute in the production monitoring system, where technicians see one fired conjunction at a time. An ablation study in Appendix Section
A.13
confirms that condition-tree presentation yields only a small accuracy gain (1–2 points) without changing the leaderboard ordering.

3.2.3
Selecting Sets of Actions

For a
selection
question, we retrieve the
N_{sel\_topk}
rules
least similar
to
\mathcal{R}^{i}
under RRSim and collect their observations into
INC^{i}=\{inc^{i}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N_{inc}}
as candidate distractors. We then form answer tuples
\{(QP_{j}^{i},OPT_{j}^{i},A_{j}^{i})\}_{j=1}^{N_{sel}}
by (i) taking each
o_{j}^{i}\in\mathcal{O}^{i}
as the correct option, (ii) sampling
\alpha
incorrect options from
INC^{i}\setminus\mathcal{O}^{i}
, and (iii) composing the prompt
QP_{j}^{i}
from a template pool of size
N_{QT}
. Sampling distractors from
dissimilar
rules ensures they are operationally adjacent (same general failure family) without being semantically correct alternatives.

For an
elimination
question, the polarity inverts: the correct option is one that does
not
belong to
\mathcal{R}^{i}
. We retrieve the
N_{ele\_topk}
rules least similar to
\mathcal{R}^{i}
and collect their observations as
COR^{i}=\{cor_{j}^{i}\}_{j=1}^{N_{cor}}
. We sample
\min(N_{cor},\beta)
observations from
COR^{i}
as correct options, and pair each with incorrect options drawn from
\mathcal{O}^{i}
. This forces the model to recognize which actions are
not
supported by the rule’s conditions, a complementary skill to selection.

The hyper-parameters
\alpha
and
\beta
control the number of distractors sampled per rule for selection and elimination, respectively. Larger values increase question count but also increase option overlap across questions derived from the same rule. We use
\alpha=\beta=10
throughout, selected as the elbow of the size-vs-diversity curve (Appendix Section
A.4
).

DiagnosticIQ

Figure 4:
DiagnosticIQ Pro
composition by asset type (outer ring), option count (inner ring), and question type.

We apply the pipeline described in Section
3
on 118 expert-curated rules to construct DiagnosticIQ. We set the hyper-parameters
N_{sel\_topk}=25
,
N_{eli\_topk}=25
,
N_{QT}=10
,
\alpha=10
, and
\beta=10
. The resulting dataset contains 6690 questions, with composition shown in Figure
4
. Selection questions account for 77.4% of the dataset and elimination questions for the remaining 22.6%. The predominance of selection questions is a structural consequence of the pipeline: incorrect options for elimination questions are sampled from
\mathcal{O}^{i}
for a given
\mathcal{R}^{i}
, which is small relative to the cross-rule pool used for selection distractors. Setting
\alpha\ll\beta
would rebalance the ratio but at the cost of generating many near-duplicate elimination questions, reducing diversity.

By asset type, DiagnosticIQ is dominated by Air Handling Unit (AHU) scenarios (58%), followed by Chiller and Boiler (7% and 6% respectively). This skew is intentional and reflects operational reality: AHUs are the most prevalent equipment at most facility sites and account for the largest share of energy savings opportunities, so domain experts authored proportionally more rules for them. Rather than resampling to equalize coverage, we preserve the imbalance so that benchmark performance reflects the operational distribution practitioners actually face. Similar dataset imbalance is documented in other domain benchmarks such as GPQA
[
31
]
and HLE dataset
[
6
]
.

A second structural property of the dataset is variable option count for elimination questions. Selection questions have a fixed option set; elimination questions draw their incorrect options from the source rule’s own action set
\mathcal{O}^{i}
, which varies in size across rules. We report this transparently rather than truncate, since rule-specific action-set size is itself diagnostic information about the asset.

4.1
Variants for Targeted Evaluation

DiagnosticIQ Pro:
A higher-difficulty variant of DiagnosticIQ. We expand each question from 4 to 10 answer options to stress-test discrimination among many plausible distractors. The 10-option design follows FailureSensorIQ
[
8
]
and MMLU-Pro
[
39
]
.

DiagnosticIQ Pert:
A robustness variant of DiagnosticIQ Pro. We apply PertEval
[
17
]
to perturb each question’s surface form (option ordering, wording) while preserving semantics, isolating sensitivity to formatting and position bias from reasoning ability. Construction details in Appendix
A.6.2
.

DiagnosticIQ Rationale:
A reasoning-trace variant of DiagnosticIQ. For each question, we collect the chain-of-thought rationale the model produces alongside its answer. Domain experts rate these rationales on knowledge accuracy and operational relevance (Section
5.2
). The rationales also serve as supervision signal for the fine-tuning experiments (Section
5.3
).

DiagnosticIQ Verbose:
A linguistic-paraphrase variant of DiagnosticIQ. Symbolic question conditions (
QC
) are rewritten into natural language. Comparing DiagnosticIQ against DiagnosticIQ Verbose isolates the
symbolic-to-linguistic gap
. Construction details in Appendix
A.6.3
.

DiagnosticIQ Aug:
A logical-stress variant inverting all temporal comparison operators (
<\!\leftrightarrow\!>
,
\leq\!\leftrightarrow\!\geq
) and adding “None of the above” as a new option. It helps in isolating sensitivity to logical content from rule-shape recognition. Manual verification is conducted to check validity.

Experimental Results

We evaluate models via direct zero-shot prompting (representative prompts in Appendix Figure

where
q(x)
is the generated prompt,
M(\cdot)
the model’s response,
\mathbb{1}[\cdot]
the indicator,
D_{\mathcal{Q}}
the full dataset,
A
the set of asset classes, and
D_{a}
the questions for asset
a
.

Latest-generation and larger models lead within their families. But, gpt-oss-120b and granite-3-3-8b (above several frontier-prior models at 8B) show that training-data composition and instruction tuning contribute alongside scale and recency.

5.2
Detailed Analysis

Beyond aggregate accuracy, we ask
how
models fail and
which
questions are hard. This section predominantly uses the dataset variants introduced in Section
4.1
.

Models reason competently within templates.
We conducted an expert study with five experts, who rated mistral-large rationales on 27 questions sampled from DiagnosticIQ Rationale. We find that 85% of paired annotations reached consensus at the favorable threshold, with quadratic-weighted Cohen’s Kappa of 0.22–0.28 across pairs (moderate agreement; full breakdown in Appendix
A.9.2
). Inter-rater agreement was lowest for assets requiring fine-grained operational knowledge (Plate & Frame heat exchangers). Overall, even mid-tier models produce explanations consistent with domain reasoning on our prepared dataset. This sets up the central question:
what happens when the rule structure is preserved but the logical content is altered?

Yet models pattern-match more than reason about conditions.
The DiagnosticIQ Aug variant probes this directly. After temporal-operator inversion, the original action is no longer logically supported, so a reasoning-grounded model should select “None of the above.” Across the three models we evaluated on DiagnosticIQ Aug, only 15.5–16.6% do so, while half to two-thirds (48.8–63.4%) persist with the original answer. Strikingly, claude-opus-4-6, the leaderboard leader on DiagnosticIQ, shows the
strongest
logical inertia (63.4%), suggesting more capable models are more confidently wrong on this probe rather than more careful. A complementary per-rule analysis (Appendix
D
) corroborates this: accuracy is sharply bimodal across the 118 rules, the signature of rule-shape recognition.
Current LLMs treat structured industrial rules more as templates to recognize than as conditions to evaluate.
See Appendix
A.9.1
for details.

Models lose accuracy under paraphrasing.
The pattern repeats when we paraphrase symbolic conditions into prose (DiagnosticIQ Verbose). Across 17 models, paraphrasing reduces Macro accuracy by 4.2 points on average (range
-1.8
to
-9.6
). The drop indicates that current LLMs in this domain rely on symbolic structure as reasoning scaffolding, and paraphrasing erodes that structure. Per-model breakdowns are available in Appendix
A.12
.

Performance varies along structural axes.
Across the 16 asset types, claude-opus-4-6 exceeds 85% on UPS, Plate & Frame, Boiler, and HXU, but drops below 35% on Pump and Cooling Tower under the Pro variant, with an average Pro drop of 13.8 points. The largest Pro drops occur on assets whose rules span interrelated subsystems. Question type matters as well: elimination questions consistently yield higher accuracy than selection (mistral-large: 71.0% vs. 63.9% on DiagnosticIQ), and the selection gap widens sharply on DiagnosticIQ Pro. Per-asset and per-question-type breakdowns are in Appendix

and Appendix Table

Performance drops under prompt-level perturbation.

Cross-Asset/Rule Generalization

We partition DiagnosticIQ into AHU rules (the dominant class, 58%) and Other (all remaining asset types). We fine-tune three 8B-scale base models on the AHU split using two methods, supervised fine-tuning on rationales from DiagnosticIQ Rationale and GRPO technique on the correctness signal, and evaluate on Other. Both methods improve over the base model, but the optimal choice depends on whether the base model is reasoning-tuned (Table
3

+3.6

+10.5

This bifurcation suggests that supervised distillation works best when the base model already exposes structured reasoning traces, while preference-based optimization is more effective when starting from a model without reasoning structure. Conclusion and Limitations

Our DiagnosticIQ benchmark and its variants reveal a consistent brittleness arc: the leading model scores
73.6%
Macro,
59.8%
on Pro, and
\sim
48%
consistency on Pert. Paraphrasing and operator-inversion experiments show that current LLMs treat structured rules as templates rather than conditions to evaluate. This
\sim
48% robustness floor sits near frontier accuracy on Humanity’s Last Exam
[
27
]
(
\sim
46%, May 2026), suggesting that gains on general-knowledge benchmarks overstate readiness for high-stakes operational deployment where consistency across phrasings is non-negotiable.

Limitations:
Our scope covers English-language rules under zero-shot evaluation, leaving multilingual rules, agentic settings, and the full 800+ industrial asset taxonomy as future work.

We analyze the effect of varying

\beta
(the per-rule selection and elimination distractor counts) on dataset option diversity. We quantify diversity using the question-to-question Intersection over Union (IoU) of generated options, computed across questions from rules with ids
\{1,4,9,16,25\}

\mathrm{IoU}_{\mathrm{mean}}=\frac{1}{|D_{Q}|}\sum_{x_{1},x_{2}\in D_{Q}}\frac{OPT_{x_{1}}\cap OPT_{x_{2}}}{OPT_{x_{1}}\cup OPT_{x_{2}}}

Figure

Pareto frontier: smaller values yield higher

stable accuracy estimates (
\sim

\alpha=\beta=10

A.6
DiagnosticIQ Variants

This subsection details the construction procedure for each released variant.

A.6.1
DiagnosticIQ Pro

DiagnosticIQ Pro extends DiagnosticIQ to evaluate model performance with larger answer sets. For each question
\mathcal{Q}^{i}
in DiagnosticIQ, we retain the asset description
AD^{i}
, observed conditions
QC^{i}
, prompt
QP^{i}
, and ground-truth answer
A^{i}
, while expanding the option set
OPT^{i}
from 4 to 10 by adding plausible distractors. For
selection
questions, additional distractors are sampled from observations of semantically similar but distinct rules. For
elimination
questions, both correct and incorrect option sets are augmented using RRSim to maintain logical coherence. The expanded variant mimics real-world scenarios where practitioners must consider many potential failure causes and tests model robustness in high-option environments.

A.6.2
DiagnosticIQ Pert

DiagnosticIQ Pert evaluates response sensitivity to surface-level perturbations. We apply four transformations to each DiagnosticIQ question: random shuffling of conditions and options, parenthesizing option labels (e.g.,
A\to(A)
), changing label characters (e.g.,
A,B,C\to P,Q,R
), and substituting question prompts
QP^{i}
with alternates from the template pool. These perturbations preserve semantic content while varying surface form.

A.6.3
DiagnosticIQ Verbose

DiagnosticIQ Verbose paraphrases the symbolic conditions of each question into natural language. We embed conditions of each question using
all-mpnet-base-v2
and cluster via cosine similarity to identify 10 representative questions across the rule corpus. We manually convert these representative cases to natural language, then prompt mistral-large with these as in-context examples to generate paraphrases for the remaining questions (Figure
16
).

A.6.4
DiagnosticIQ-Aug

DiagnosticIQ-Aug applies a logical inversion to each rule: all temporal comparison operators are inverted (
<\!\leftrightarrow\!>
,
\leq\!\leftrightarrow\!\geq
), and “None of the above” is added as a new answer option. After inversion, the original action is no longer logically supported, so a reasoning-grounded model should select “None of the above.” Construction details and the full probe analysis are in Section
A.9
.

A.8
Real-World Application: Estimated Energy Savings

Accuracy is a useful proxy for benchmark performance, but practitioners care about
operational impact
: how much an LLM-recommended action saves when deployed against a real maintenance workload. Each rule in DiagnosticIQ carries an associated cost-savings range (Min, Max) authored by the same domain experts who developed the rules over seven years of deployment, capturing the energy and downtime savings observed when correct actions were taken on triggered conditions.

Estimation procedure.
For each LLM, we sum the cost-savings ranges of every question the model answered correctly on DiagnosticIQ. Incorrect answers, whether false positives or false negatives, are conservatively assigned zero savings. This is a deliberately strict accounting: a wrong action may still incidentally save some energy in practice, but quantifying suboptimal-action savings would require infeasible combinatorial analysis. The zero-savings convention treats only verified-correct recommendations as value-generating.

Result.
Table
7
reports estimated total savings for the top five models alongside Micro accuracy on DiagnosticIQ. Two findings emerge. First, the leading model contributes USD 3.13–4.82M in estimated savings, demonstrating LLM-based action recommendation is operationally meaningful. Second, ranking by savings differs from ranking by accuracy: gemini-3.1-pro-pre achieves the highest savings despite ranking second on Micro accuracy (75.92% vs. claude-opus-4-6’s 77.76%), because it answers correctly on a higher proportion of high-savings rules. Conversely, claude-3-7-sonnet (prior-generation accuracy leader at 72.66%) ranks fifth on savings, indicating its correct answers concentrate on lower-impact rules.

A.9
Reasoning vs. Pattern Matching: Full Probes

A natural concern with DiagnosticIQ is that, because all questions are generated from 120 source rules, models may achieve high accuracy by recognizing rule
structure
rather than reasoning about conditions. We design two complementary probes.

A.9.1
Logical inversion via condition flipping.

For each rule in DiagnosticIQ, we construct a perturbed variant in which all temporal comparison operators are inverted (
<\!\leftrightarrow\!>
,
\leq\!\leftrightarrow\!\geq
), reversing the rule’s truth condition. To this perturbed dataset, we add “None of the above” as a new answer option. After inversion, the rule no longer fires under the original conditions; the original correct action is no longer supported, and the only logically valid answer is “None of the above.” A reasoning-grounded model should select the new option; a pattern-matching model will select the original answer despite the inversion.

Result.
Table
8
reports answer distributions for three models. Across all three, the dominant choice is the previously correct answer, even though that answer is now logically inconsistent with the inverted conditions. Claude-Opus-4-6 selects the original answer 63% of the time and “None of the above” only 21% of the time. Mistral-Medium and Mistral-Small show the same systematic failure mode (50% and 49% old-answer selection). The old action dominates by a 3
\times
to 4
\times
margin.

Rationale evaluation by domain experts.

As a complementary check, we collect chain-of-thought rationales from mistral-large on the unperturbed DiagnosticIQ and have five domain experts rate them along two axes: knowledge accuracy (does the explanation cite the right operational principles?) and operational relevance (does the explanation match how a technician would reason about the alarm?). What the two probes together tell us.
Models can produce expert-acceptable reasoning when the rule structure aligns with the underlying logic, but they fail to detect when that structure has been logically inverted. The condition-flipping result is the kind of finding that a benchmark of this design
enables

A.10
What Makes a Question Hard? Difficulty Decomposition

We ask which structural properties of a question predict whether an LLM will answer it correctly. This decomposition characterizes the kinds of reasoning current LLMs handle well or poorly and helps benchmark consumers identify which subsets of DiagnosticIQ pose the greatest difficulty.

Setup.
We extract four features from every question in DiagnosticIQ core (
n=6{,}690
):

•

Logic complexity index
: number of logical symbols (AND, OR, NOT) in the rule.

•

Special function count
: number of mathematical or comparison operations (e.g., SUM, AVG, IF,
<
,
>
,
\neq
).

•

Length of question text
: total prompt character count, normalized.

•

Number of options
: 4 in DiagnosticIQ core; included as a confounding control.

We fit a logistic regression to predict per-question correctness for the leaderboard-leading model (claude-opus-4-6, accuracy 73.59%). Significance is assessed via Wald
z
-test.

Result.
Three of four features are highly significant (
p<0.001
); see Table
9
. First, special-function count is the strongest predictor of failure: each additional arithmetic or comparison operation reduces the odds of correctness by approximately 47% (odds ratio
e^{-0.627}\approx 0.53
). Rules dense with arithmetic and threshold comparisons, common in HVAC and chiller diagnostics, are systematically harder than rules dominated by simple state predicates. Second, and counterintuitively, logical complexity
helps
: each additional AND/OR connective increases the odds of correctness (odds ratio
\approx 2.34
). One plausible explanation: longer Boolean expressions provide structural scaffolding that helps anchor the model’s reasoning, whereas terse rules with few connectives leave more room for misinterpretation. This flips a common assumption that compositional logic is universally harder. Question text length has a small positive effect (
+0.122
,
p=0.002
); option count is not significant (
p=0.449
), confirming Pro-variant difficulty stems from set-size brittleness, not per-question complexity.

For benchmark interpretation, aggregate accuracy can obscure structural failure modes: a model scoring 73% may be near-perfect on simple rules and barely above chance on math-heavy ones. Why we use conjunctive scenarios.
Two reasons. A.14
Few-Shot Generalization to Unseen Rules

To test whether models can generalize the rule-to-action mapping to entirely new rules, we collect 11 SME-authored rules with ground-truth actions held out from DiagnosticIQ. For each new rule, we sample 10 candidate actions using
all-MiniLM-L6-v2
to construct realistic distractors, and measure MAP@1 (whether the ground-truth action is the top-1 recommendation). We provide mistral-large with 0–5 in-context examples drawn from DiagnosticIQ.

Result.
In-context examples improve MAP@1 over zero-shot, but the gain is non-monotonic: 41.67% (0-shot), 58.33% (1-shot), 50.00% (2-shot), 50.00% (3-shot), 50.00% (4-shot), 50.00% (5-shot). 1-shot is strongest, suggesting a single example is sufficient to anchor the model on the rule-to-action format; additional demonstrations neither help nor hurt, indicating diminishing returns. We caveat these results with the small sample size (11 rules); a larger held-out corpus would give firmer conclusions.

Appendix B
Extended Empirical Analyses

This appendix presents four analyses derived from per-question evaluation results across all 29 LLMs on DiagnosticIQ simpleV.

B.1
Universal-Failure Questions

We define a
universal-failure
question as one that no LLM in our 29-model evaluation answered correctly. These questions are diagnostically interesting because they reveal failure modes shared across the full LLM population, not specific to one family or scale.

Figure
17
summarizes the universal-failure population. Panel (A) shows the full distribution of question difficulty. Panel (B) shows per-asset universal-failure rate.

Figure 17:
Question difficulty across 5,180 positive-type simpleV questions.
(A)
Histogram of n_correct values. Bars colored from red (0 correct) to green (29 correct). 144 questions are universal failures (0 of 29 correct); 178 are universal passes (all 29 correct).
(B)
Per-asset universal-failure rate. CRAC stands out at 11.4% universal-failure rate, more than 1.7
\times
the next-highest asset class.

Universal failures are 2.8% of the benchmark.

Of 5,180 simpleV positive-type questions, 144 are universal failures (2.8%) and 178 are universal passes (3.4%). The bulk of questions sit between these extremes. The mid-range distribution is roughly U-shaped, with small bumps at both ends and a heavier population on the easy side (more questions where 20+ models succeed than where 5 or fewer succeed).

CRAC dominates the universal-failure population.

Per-asset universal-failure rate (Figure
17
B) ranges from 0% (UPS, HXU, Plate & Frame) to 11.4% (CRAC). CRAC questions account for 32 of 144 total universal failures despite representing only 280 of 5,180 questions in the benchmark. Three other asset classes show elevated rates: PDU (6.7%), Air Compressor (5.5%), and Lighting/Fan (5.0%). The remaining classes sit below 5%.

What makes CRAC questions hard for all models.

We inspected a sample of CRAC universal-failure questions. Three patterns recur. First, CRAC rules frequently involve nested temporal conditions (“temperature exceeded threshold for 3 hours after maintenance flag cleared”) that require tracking multiple stateful variables. Second, the correct diagnoses often involve compound causal chains (“sensor fault upstream caused compressor cycling”) rather than single-pattern matches. Third, the distractor options for CRAC questions tend to be syntactically similar to the correct answer, making lexical-match heuristics unhelpful. Together these patterns suggest CRAC is the asset class most demanding of genuine multi-step diagnostic reasoning, and that current LLMs are uniformly weak at this style of inference.

Universal failures map to a small set of rules.

Of the 144 universal-failure questions, the majority concentrate on a small set of rules. We provide the full universal-failure question list as supplementary material (
universal_failures_full.csv
). Researchers seeking targeted training data for industrial diagnostic reasoning may find these questions a useful starting point.

B.2
Wilson 95% Confidence Intervals on the Main Leaderboard

We compute Wilson 95% confidence intervals for every cell of the main leaderboard (Table
2
) from the existing per-question correctness data. No model re-evaluation is required.

Method.

Wilson score intervals are the recommended interval for binomial proportions and have better coverage near 0 and 1 than Wald intervals
[
5
]
. Micro accuracy is a single Bernoulli proportion (correct over total questions); its CI is direct Wilson. Macro accuracy is the mean of per-asset Bernoulli proportions; we compute each per-asset CI via Wilson, then propagate to a Macro CI via the delta method, treating per-asset accuracies as independent. The delta-method CI is normal-approximation in form but uses the per-asset Wilson variances rather than Wald variances.

Result.

Table

Top-3 Macro CIs overlap.

\pm
2.21), gpt-5.4 (73.27
\pm
2.01), and gemini-3.1-pro-pre (72.74
\pm

B.3
), which pools 633,252 pairwise comparisons across questions and places claude-opus-4-6 30 Elo points above gemini-3.1-pro-pre with non-overlapping CIs.

Micro CIs are roughly half as wide as Macro CIs.

B.3
Bradley-Terry Elo Ratings

Aggregate accuracy treats each question equally, regardless of how many models found it solvable. A question that all 29 models answer correctly contributes the same signal as a question only the top model can solve. Bradley-Terry Elo ratings address this by reducing each pairwise model comparison to a win or loss, fitting a latent rating that explains the observed pairwise outcomes.

Method.

For each pair of models
(i,j)
and each question, we count one win for
i
if
i
is correct and
j
is incorrect (and vice versa). Ties (both correct or both incorrect) carry no signal. We fit Bradley-Terry maximum-likelihood ratings
\theta
via L-BFGS-B optimization, then convert to the Elo scale (mean 1500,
\theta
scaled by
400/\ln 10
). We compute 95% confidence intervals via question-level bootstrap with
B=500
resamples.

Total comparisons.

Across all questions and 29 models, we observe 633,252 pairwise win/loss outcomes. The remaining pairs are ties.

Figure 18:

r=0.993
and Spearman correlation is
\rho=0.997
, indicating BT-Elo and accuracy rankings agree closely while BT-Elo additionally provides bootstrap-based uncertainty.

The top three are tightly clustered, but Opus has a clean lead.

Claude-opus-4-6 reaches Elo 1727 (95% CI [1711, 1742]). Gemini-3.1-pro-preview at 1698 [1684, 1714] and gpt-5.4 at 1692 [1678, 1708] sit in overlapping CIs with each other but separated from claude-opus-4-6 by 30 to 35 Elo points with non-overlapping bootstrap CIs. This is consistent with the main-paper finding that the frontier has closed within 1 Macro point but that claude-opus-4-6 retains a small but statistically distinguishable advantage over the other two.

Open-weight models hold their own.

Qwen3.6-35B reaches Elo 1662 (rank 4 of 29), within 65 Elo points of the top model and within 30 points of the third-place gpt-5.4. Two Gemma 4 variants (31B and 26B) place at ranks 6 and 10. This mirrors the leaderboard finding that the closed-source-vs-open-weight gap has narrowed.

Strong agreement with Macro accuracy.

The Pearson correlation between Elo and Macro accuracy is

This divergence reflects that llama-3-1-405b answers many easy questions correctly (boosting Macro) but loses pairwise comparisons against models that solve harder questions it cannot. Reporting both metrics provides complementary views of model capability.

Why we report Elo alongside Macro.

Appendix C
Human Evaluation: Benchmark Difficulty Validation

C.1
Motivation

We conducted a human evaluation to validate that DiagnosticIQ requires genuine HVAC fault-detection expertise rather than general engineering knowledge. Our hypothesis was simple. Practitioners with hands-on experience in AHU and Chiller asset classes should still find the questions challenging. If they did, the benchmark discriminates between specialist and practitioner-level knowledge.

C.2
Study Design

Question subset.

We sampled 40 positive-type questions from DiagnosticIQ spanning 10 asset categories. The asset distribution is shown in Table
21
. AHU and Chiller dominate the question set with 23 of 40 questions across both classes.

Recruitment.

We recruited 9 engineering practitioners from our research network. Recruitment targeted practitioners with several years of working exposure to AHU and Chiller assets. Most respondents worked in operational or monitoring roles similar to the Bob persona introduced in Section
1
. They observe sensor data, respond to triggered conditions, and escalate when faults exceed their diagnostic experience. None held certified HVAC fault-detection credentials such as those held by the rule authors (Appendix
A.2
).

Entry questionnaire.

On entry each respondent confirmed (i) experience with reliability and engineering, and (ii) the specific asset classes they worked with. Six respondents marked “Yes, some experience” and three marked “Basic familiarity.” Five respondents listed AHU and Chiller explicitly in their asset experience. Profiles appear in Table
19
.

Protocol.

Each respondent independently answered all 40 questions in a single session via a web form. Each question presented the rule conditions, four answer options, and the question stem (“Review the listed conditions and identify which option MOST accurately accounts for them”). External resources were not allowed. All 9 respondents completed all 40 questions. There was no missing data (360 of 360 cells answered).

Scoring.

Responses were matched to the ground-truth answer using normalized string matching with abbreviation expansion. For example “BMS” was matched to “Building Management System.” Each (respondent, question) cell was scored binary correct or incorrect against the rule-author’s designated correct answer.

C.7
Inter-Rater Agreement

Fleiss’ kappa across the 9 raters is 0.40 (fair agreement, Landis & Koch convention). Mean pairwise simple-agreement is 55.4% with standard deviation 15.5%. Both statistics were computed over all 360 cells using normalized string matching with abbreviation expansion. The kappa value indicates agreement above chance but well below the level expected for trained specialists on a calibrated test. We read this as consistent with the panel’s diverse and limited specialist exposure leading to high variance in answer choice.

LLM performance on universal failures is bimodal.

Of the 12 questions, 5 are answered correctly by at least 75% of evaluated LLMs (mean 86%). Another 4 are answered by at most 45% of LLMs (mean 31%). The questions that are easy for LLMs share a pattern. The correct answer text contains domain vocabulary that overlaps with the conditions. For example one answer reads “Pump problems (VFD problems)” when conditions reference coolant pressure differential. 5.2

C.10
Limitations

We disclose three limitations.

Sample size.
The 9-respondent panel is small relative to professional benchmark studies. A larger panel with more diverse asset specializations would strengthen the per-asset breakdowns particularly for asset classes with single questions such as Boiler, CRAC, and PDU.

Recruitment scope.
We recruited working practitioners with hands-on exposure to AHU and Chiller assets. We did not require certified HVAC fault-detection credentials. The respondents are working practitioners. They are not certified specialists like the rule authors who held 10 to 15 years of operational experience and certifications including CFM, CEM, and CMRP (Appendix
A.2
).

Comparison framing.
We do not claim that frontier LLMs match certified fault-detection specialists. We claim that they exceed the level of working practitioners represented in our panel. On 10 of 12 questions where every panel member failed, the top frontier model identified the correct diagnosis even though every practitioner with self-reported AHU and Chiller experience could not. A larger study with credentialed specialists is left for future work.

C.11
Implications for the Benchmark

The human-evaluation results support three claims about DiagnosticIQ.

The benchmark requires specialist knowledge beyond working exposure.
Practitioners with self-reported AHU and Chiller experience scored 43.8% on AHU questions and 41.7% on Chiller questions. Working familiarity with the asset class did not transfer to fault-pattern recognition.

The questions are not impossibly hard.
Of 40 questions, 19 saw 6 or more humans correct. The panel’s best respondent reached 67.5%. The benchmark discriminates between specialist and practitioner-level knowledge rather than requiring credentials no human possesses.

LLMs encode partial specialist knowledge.
Frontier LLMs solve a substantial fraction of the questions practitioners cannot. The bimodal LLM performance on universal-failure questions suggests current frontier models capture surface vocabulary patterns of HVAC fault-detection more reliably than they capture inferential diagnostic reasoning.

D.3

contribute independent information. We compute the intraclass
correlation coefficient (ICC) per model using rule as the cluster:

\rho\;=\;\frac{\sigma_{\text{between rules}}^{2}}{\sigma_{\text{between}}^{2}+\sigma_{\text{within}}^{2}},\qquad N_{\text{eff}}\;=\;\frac{N}{1+(\bar{m}-1)\rho},

where
\bar{m}\!\approx\!45
is the mean cluster size. Across all 30
models, ICC ranges from
0.872
to
0.914
(mean
0.896
), giving a
design effect of approximately
40
and an effective sample size of

N_{\text{eff}}\!\approx\!127
–
133

(Fig.
23
). Most of the apparent statistical mass
of the benchmark sits in the rule structure, not in question variants.

D.6
Reporting Recommendations

Based on this analysis, we adopt the following conventions throughout
the paper:

•

Confidence intervals.
All accuracy estimates are
reported with rule-clustered (design-effect-corrected) Wald 95%
CIs unless otherwise stated.

•

Pairwise model comparison.
Significance claims
between models use rule-level paired tests, treating per-rule
accuracies as the unit of analysis.

•

Per-model diagnostic.
The bimodality coefficient and
excess-over-binomial-null factor are reported alongside aggregate
accuracy as indicators of rule-recognition reliance.

These conventions do not invalidate DiagnosticIQ as an evaluation
instrument, but they materially change how its results should be
read: the benchmark resolves
rule-shape coverage
at an
effective resolution of
\approx 118
independent rules
