Reduction of Multiple Orthogonal Polynomials to Standard Orthogonal Polynomials
Roozbeh Gharakhloo, Maksim Kosmakov, Kenta Miyahara

Abstract
. In this article, we derive explicit formulae expressing multiple orthogonal polynomials in terms of standard orthogonal polynomials. We treat both the real-line and unit-circle settings: multiple orthogonal polynomials on the real line (MOPRL) are reduced to orthogonal polynomials on the real line (OPRL), while multiple orthogonal polynomials on the unit circle (MOPUC) are reduced to orthogonal polynomials on the unit circle (OPUC). These formulae also yield corresponding reductions of the Christoffel–Darboux kernels, from the MOPRL kernel to the OPRL kernel and from the MOPUC kernel to the OPUC kernel. In particular, they recover Zinn-Justin’s kernel for the external-source random matrix model [ 43 ] and Baik’s kernel reduction formula in the spiked source model [ 6 ] . We also apply our general results to concrete examples: in the real-line setting, we obtain an explicit expression for the multiple Hermite Christoffel–Darboux kernel in terms of classical Hermite polynomials, while in the unit-circle setting, we use arc-indicator weights to exhibit resonance-type zero escape phenomena for type II MOPUC. Keywords : Multiple orthogonal polynomials on the real line, Multiple orthogonal polynomials on the unit circle, Christoffel-Darboux kernel. 2020 Mathematics Subject Classification: Primary 42C05; Secondary 33C45, 33C47, 60B20.



1.
Introduction

Multiple orthogonal polynomials are a natural extension of ordinary orthogonal
polynomials in which a single orthogonality measure is replaced by a finite
collection of measures. They were originally developed in connection with
Hermite-Padé approximation, analytic number theory, and the theory of special
functions; see, for example,
[
2
,
34
,
42
]
and the references therein.
Given weights
w^{(1)},\ldots,w^{(r)}
and a multi-index

\vec{n}=(n_{1},\ldots,n_{r})
, the type II multiple orthogonal polynomial is the
monic polynomial of degree
|\vec{n}|
satisfying
n_{j}
orthogonality
conditions with respect to the weight
w^{(j)}
. The dual type I object is a
linear combination of the weights
w^{(1)},\ldots,w^{(r)}
, with
polynomial coefficients, satisfying corresponding moment conditions. When

r=1
, these objects reduce to the usual orthogonal polynomials.

In this paper we derive explicit reduction formulae for multiple orthogonal polynomials on the real line and on the unit circle when
r\geq 2
. These formulae express the type II polynomials, the type I linear forms, and the associated Christoffel–Darboux kernels in terms of usual orthogonal polynomials. As discussed in Section
7
, a closely related reduction at the level of Christoffel–Darboux kernels was obtained by Baik
[
6
]
in connection with the finite-rank external-source random matrix model. From this perspective, the present work extends Baik’s finite-rank reduction formula from the special external-source multi-index
(n-r,1,\ldots,1)
to general perfect systems of multiple orthogonality on the real line. It also develops the parallel unit-circle theory. A further feature of our approach is that these reductions are obtained by finite-dimensional linear algebra, without using Riemann–Hilbert machinery.

More precisely, we choose one of the weights
w^{(1)},\ldots,w^{(r)}
,
and, after relabeling if necessary, denote it by
w^{(1)}
. We regard this
weight as the
reference

weight and use the corresponding ordinary orthogonal polynomials as a basis.
In this basis, the mixed-moment matrix for the multiple orthogonality conditions has a
block upper-triangular structure. The lower-right block, denoted
M_{22}
,
contains precisely the information coming from the companion weights

w^{(2)},\ldots,w^{(r)}
. Our main results show that the type II multiple
orthogonal polynomial, the type I linear form, and the corresponding
Christoffel–Darboux kernel can all be written
explicitly
in terms of the
ordinary orthogonal polynomials for
w^{(1)}
, the ordinary
Christoffel-Darboux kernel, and the inverse of
M_{22}
. In the finite-rank external-source setting, this recovers Baik’s kernel
reduction formula. The same formalism also recovers Zinn-Justin’s earlier
external-source kernel; see
Section
7
.

We also prove analogues of these reduction formulae for multiple orthogonal
polynomials on the unit circle. Multiple orthogonal polynomials on the unit
circle were introduced by Mínguez Ceniceros and Van Assche
[
37
]
, who
related them to rational approximation of Carathéodory functions,
formulated Riemann–Hilbert problems for the type I and type II objects, and
used this formulation to derive recurrence relations for MOPUC. Further
structural results, including normality and recurrence relations obtained
without relying on the Riemann–Hilbert formulation, were developed in

[
21
]
. More recently, Kozhan and Vaktnäs established Szegő-type
recurrences for MOPUC
[
30
]
. Our MOPUC
formulae reduce type I and type II MOPUC, and their Christoffel–Darboux kernel,
to ordinary OPUC data plus a finite-sized mixed-moment matrix. This gives a direct unit-circle counterpart of the real-line reduction.

The results of this paper are intended as a first step in a broader research program. In forthcoming work, we plan to apply these reduction formulae to several problems, including some unexplored aspects of external-source GUE and CUE models; ensembles of non-intersecting random walks on
\mathbb{Z}
, in both discrete and continuous time, with consecutive initial points and multiple packets of consecutive end points
[
26
]
; and ensembles of non-intersecting Brownian bridges on the circle
[
18
]
with one starting point and multiple ending points.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
MOPRL preliminaries

Let
\vec{n}=(n_{1},\cdots,n_{r})\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
be a multi-index of size
n=|\vec{n}|=\sum_{j=1}^{r}n_{j}
, and let
\{\mu_{j}\}_{j=1}^{r}
be a sequence of positive measures on the real line such that all moments
\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{k}\mathrm{d}\mu_{j}(x)
exist.
Furthermore, we assume each
\mu_{j}
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure. Thus, we have a sequence of weight functions
\{w^{(j)}(x)\}_{j=1}^{r}
such that

\mathrm{d}\mu_{j}=w^{(j)}(x)\mathrm{d}x,\quad j=1,2,\cdots,r.

In this setting, we define two types of multiple orthogonal polynomials on the real line.

Definition 2.1
.

A type II MOPRL
P_{\vec{n}}(x)
is a monic polynomial of degree
n
satisfying

(2.1)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}P_{\vec{n}}(x)x^{k}w^{(j)}(x)\mathrm{d}x=0,\quad k=0,\cdots,n_{j}-1,\quad j=1,\cdots,r.

Definition 2.2
.

Type I MOPRL are a nonzero vector of polynomials
(A_{1}(x),\cdots,A_{r}(x))
where each
A_{j}(x)
has degree at most
n_{j}-1
and the linear form

Q_{\vec{n}}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{r}A_{j}(x)w^{(j)}(x)

satisfies

(2.2)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}Q_{\vec{n}}(x)x^{k}\mathrm{d}x=\begin{cases}0,&k=0,\cdots,n-2,\\
1,&k=n-1.\end{cases}

Condition (
2.2
) gives a linear system of
n
equations for the
n
unknown coefficients of
A_{j}(x)
,
j=1,\cdots,r
.
Thus, the existence and uniqueness of type I MOPRL depend on the non-singularity of this linear system.
In other words, if the moment matrix

m=\begin{pmatrix}m^{(1)}&m^{(2)}&\cdots&m^{(r)}\end{pmatrix}

where each
m^{(j)}
is an
n\times n_{j}
Hankel matrix whose
(a,b)
-entry is given by
\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{a+b-2}w^{(j)}(x)\mathrm{d}x
, has a nonzero determinant, then type I MOPRL uniquely exists. This condition also guarantees the existence and uniqueness of type II MOPRL.

Definition 2.3
.

Given weights
\{w^{(j)}(x)\}_{j=1}^{r}
, if for every multi-index
\vec{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
, the associated type I and II MOPRL uniquely exist,
\{w^{(j)}(x)\}_{j=1}^{r}
is called a perfect system.

Throughout this article, we assume that the underlying system of weights is perfect, so that MOPRL exists uniquely for every multi-index. This condition is satisfied, for example, by Angelesco systems, AT systems, and Nikishin systems, see for example
[
42
]
. Also, in this article, we refer to
w^{(1)}(x)
as the reference weight and to the remaining weights as the companion weights.
For the reference weight
w^{(1)}(x)
, we write the corresponding monic orthogonal polynomials by
\{p_{k}(x)\}_{k\geq 0}
, where they satisfy

(2.3)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{n}(x)p_{k}(x)w^{(1)}(x)\mathrm{d}x=h_{n}\delta_{nk},\quad k,n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}.

We refer them to the standard orthogonal polynomials on the real line.
Also, we define the Christoffel-Darboux kernel for this OPRL by

(2.4)

\displaystyle K_{0}(x,y)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\frac{p_{j}(x)p_{j}(y)}{h_{j}}w^{(1)}(y).

A
monotonic path
is a sequence of multi-indices
\{\vec{n}_{j}\}_{j=0}^{n}\subset\mathbb{N}^{r}
from
\vec{n}_{0}=\vec{0}
(all zero vector) to
\vec{n}_{n}=\vec{n}
such that
\vec{n}_{j+1}
is obtained from
\vec{n}_{j}
by increasing exactly one component by
1
.
In other words, for each
j=0,1,\cdots,n-1
, the path must satisfy

(2.5)

|\vec{n}_{j}|=j,\quad\text{and}\quad\vec{n}_{j+1}\geq\vec{n}_{j},

where the inequality is taken component-wise.
A
natural path
is an example of the monotonic path, where the components of the multi-index are filled in sequential order.
That is, the path first increments the first component from
0
to
n_{1}
, then increments the second component from
0
to
n_{2}
, and so on, until the final index
\vec{n}_{n}=(n_{1},\cdots,n_{r})
is reached. In particular, the natural path is unique for a fixed multi-index
\vec{n}
.

For a fixed monotonic path
\{\vec{n}_{j}\}_{j=0}^{n}\subset\mathbb{N}^{r}
, we define the associated sequences of type II MOPRL and type I linear forms by

(2.6)

P_{j}(x):=P_{\vec{n}_{j}}(x)\quad\text{and}\quad Q_{j}(x):=Q_{\vec{n}_{j+1}}(x).

These sequences are the building blocks for the Christoffel-Darboux kernel for MOPRL,

(2.7)

K(x,y)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}P_{j}(x)Q_{j}(y).

A crucial result, shown by Daems and Kuijlaars (see
[
22
, Theorem 1.1, Remark 1.2]
), is that this sum is independent of the specific monotonic path chosen to connect
\vec{0}
and
\vec{n}_{n}
.
This path independence allows us to select the most convenient monotonic path, i.e., the natural path.
Therefore, whenever we mention
P_{j}(x)
and
Q_{j}(x)
defined in (
2.6
) throughout this paper, we assume that the multi-index
\vec{n}
is fixed and the sequences
P_{j}(x)
and
Q_{j}(x)
are understood to be defined along the natural path from
\vec{0}
to
\vec{n}
.

Another important fact about (
2.6
) is that they satisfy the bi-orthogonality, see
[
22
, Lemma 3.1]
and
[
27
, Theorem 23.1.6]
. For later use, we formulate it as the following lemma.

Lemma 2.4
.

One has

(2.8)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}P_{k}(x)Q_{m}(x)\mathrm{d}x=\delta_{km},\quad\forall k,m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}.

Before formulating our main results, we introduce some notations.
First, we denote the space of polynomials on the real line with real coefficients of degree at most
n
by
\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\mathbb{R}}=\mathrm{span}\{1,x,\cdots,x^{n}\}
.
For us, the most interesting basis is the set of orthogonal polynomials
\{p_{k}(x)\}_{k=0}^{n}
associated with
w^{(1)}
.
Let
\bm{p}(x)
be the
n
-column vector of
basis polynomials
,

(2.9)

\bm{p}(x):=(p_{0}(x),\cdots,p_{n-1}(x))^{\top},

\bm{q}(x)
be the
n
-column vector of
weighted basis polynomials
,

(2.10)

\bm{q}(x):=\bigg(p_{0}(x)w^{(1)}(x),\cdots,p_{n_{1}-1}(x)w^{(1)}(x),\\
p_{0}(x)w^{(2)}(x),\cdots,p_{n_{2}-1}(x)w^{(2)}(x),\bm{\cdots},p_{0}(x)w^{(r)}(x),\cdots,p_{n_{r}-1}(x)w^{(r)}(x)\bigg)^{\top},

and
\bm{v}
be the
n
-column vector of
mixed-moments of
p_{n}(x)
,

(2.11)

\bm{v}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{n}(x)\bm{q}(x)dx.

By (
2.3
), one can immediately observe that the first
n_{1}
components of
\bm{v}
are zero.

Next, we construct the
n\times n

mixed-moment matrix

\bm{M}
associated with the basis
p_{m}(x)
and the weighted family
p_{k}(x)w^{(j)}(x)
,

(2.12)

\bm{M}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bm{p}(s)\,\bm{q}(s)^{\top}\,\mathrm{d}s.

For
k=1,\cdots,r
, if we write a
n\times n_{k}
matrix
M^{(k)}
whose
(a,b)
-entry is given by

M^{(k)}_{a,b}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{a-1}(x)p_{b-1}(x)w^{(k)}(x)\mathrm{d}x,

one can readily see that
\bm{M}
is of the form,

(2.13)

\displaystyle\bm{M}=\begin{pmatrix}M^{(1)}&M^{(2)}&\cdots&M^{(r)}\end{pmatrix}.

We shall also define a
n\times n
matrix of the norms of
p_{k}(x)
,

(2.14)

\bm{H}=\operatorname{diag}(h_{0},h_{1},\ldots,h_{n-1}).

For later use, it is convenient to partition these objects according to the split
between the reference system, consisting of the first
n_{1}
components, and the
companion system, consisting of the remaining
n-n_{1}
components. We use the
block decompositions

(2.15)

\bm{p}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{p}_{1}(x)\\
\bm{p}_{2}(x)\end{pmatrix},\qquad\bm{q}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{q}_{1}(x)\\
\bm{q}_{2}(x)\end{pmatrix},\qquad\bm{v}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{0}\\
\bm{v}_{2}\end{pmatrix},

and

(2.16)

\bm{M}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{M}_{11}&\bm{M}_{12}\\
\bm{M}_{21}&\bm{M}_{22}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\bm{H}=\operatorname{diag}(\bm{H}_{1},\bm{H}_{2}).

We note that the top-left
n_{1}\times n_{1}
block matrix
\bm{M}_{11}
is, in fact,
\bm{H}_{1}
, and the bottom-left
(n-n_{1})\times n_{1}
block matrix
\bm{M}_{21}
is the zero matrix
\bm{0}
; by the orthogonality condition (
2.3
). The right blocks,
\bm{M}_{12}
and
\bm{M}_{22}
, involve integrals against the companion weights
w^{(j)}(x)
for
2\leq j\leq r
, and cannot be simplified immediately. Thus,
\bm{M}
has the block upper-triangular structure,

(2.17)

\bm{M}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{H}_{1}&\bm{M}_{12}\\
\bm{0}&\bm{M}_{22}\end{pmatrix}.

Remark 1
.

Observe that using this notation the Christoffel-Darboux kernel for OPRL (
2.4
) can be written as

(2.18)

K_{0}(x,y)=\bm{q}_{1}(y)^{\top}\bm{H}_{1}^{-1}\bm{p}_{1}(x)+w^{(1)}(y)\bm{p}_{2}(y)^{\top}\bm{H}_{2}^{-1}\bm{p}_{2}(x).

2.2.
MOPUC preliminaries

As we present the results for multiple orthogonal polynomials on the unit circle (MOPUC) later, we shall give a similar preparation now for MOPUC as we did for MOPRL in the above three subsections.
We start by reviewing a few facts about the orthogonal polynomials on the unit circle (OPUC).
Let
\nu_{1}
be a positive measure on the unit circle
\mathbb{T}
such that all moments exist. With an inner product,

(2.19)

\langle f(z),g(z)\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{T}}f(z)\overline{g(z)}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{1},

L^{2}(\nu_{1})
is a Hilbert space.
Let
\mathcal{P}^{\mathbb{T}}_{n}=\mathrm{span}\{1,z,\cdots,z^{n}\}
be the space of polynomials with complex coefficients and of degree at most
n
. Clearly,
\mathcal{P}^{\mathbb{T}}_{n}
is a subspace of
L^{2}(\nu_{1})
.
Applying the Gram-Schmidt process to the standard basis
\{z^{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
of
\mathcal{P}^{\mathbb{T}}_{n}
, one obtains the
monic
orthogonal basis
\{\varphi_{n}(z)\}_{n\geq 0}
that satisfy

(2.20)

\int_{\mathbb{T}}\varphi_{n}(z)\,\overline{\varphi_{m}(z)}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{1}=h_{n}\delta_{nm},\quad h_{n}>0,

which are called the OPUC.
Assuming
\nu_{1}
is absolutely continuous with respect to the normalized arclength measure, i.e.,

\mathrm{d}\nu_{1}(z)=\omega^{(1)}(z)\frac{|\mathrm{d}z|}{2\pi},

with a nonnegative weight function
\omega^{(1)}(z)
defined on
\mathbb{T}
, we define the Christoffel-Darboux kernel for OPUC by

(2.21)

\mathcal{K}_{0}(z,\xi)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\frac{\varphi_{j}(z)\,\overline{\varphi_{j}(\xi)}}{h_{j}}\,\omega^{(1)}(\xi).

As with the generalization of OPRL to MOPRL, we consider the multiple orthogonal polynomials on the unit circle (MOPUC) following
[
37
,
21
,
30
]
, and derive reduction formulae of MOPUC to OPUC.
To this end, we introduce a few notations and definitions here.
Let
\vec{n}=(n_{1},n_{2},\cdots,n_{r})\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
be a multi-index of size
n=|\vec{n}|=\sum_{j=1}^{r}n_{j}
.
Define measures
\nu_{j}
on
\mathbb{T}
for
j=1,\cdots,r
such that all moments exist, and we assume each
\nu_{j}
is absolutely continuous with respect to the normalized arclength measure,

(2.22)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\nu_{j}(z)=\omega^{(j)}(z)\frac{|\mathrm{d}z|}{2\pi}

where each
\omega^{(j)}(z)
is a nonnegative
1

1

Following
[
37
]
and the subsequent studies on MOPUC
[

,

, we restrict our attention in this work to nonnegative measures, although the theory can also be developed for complex measures.
weight function defined on
\mathbb{T}
.
Now, we are ready to define the type I and II MOPUC.

Definition 2.5
.

A type II MOPUC
\Phi_{\vec{n}}(z)
is a monic polynomial of degree
n
such that

(2.23)

\int_{\mathbb{T}}\Phi_{\vec{n}}(z)\,\overline{z^{k}}\,\omega^{(j)}(z)\frac{|\mathrm{d}z|}{2\pi}=0,\quad k=0,1,\cdots,n_{j}-1,\quad j=1,2,\cdots,r.

Definition 2.6
.

Type I MOPUC are a nonzero vector of polynomials
(\Lambda_{1},\cdots,\Lambda_{r})
with
\deg\Lambda_{j}\leq n_{j}-1

such that the linear form

(2.24)

\Psi_{\vec{n}}(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\Lambda_{j}(z)\,\omega^{(j)}(z),

satisfies

(2.25)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}}\Psi_{\vec{n}}(z)\,\overline{z^{k}}\,\frac{|\mathrm{d}z|}{2\pi}=\begin{cases}0,&k=0,\cdots,n-2,\\
1,&k=n-1.\end{cases}

Definition 2.7
.

Given weights
\{\omega^{(j)}(z)\}_{j=1}^{r}
, if
\Phi_{\vec{n}}(z)
and
\Psi_{\vec{n}}(z)
uniquely exist for every multi-index
\vec{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
, then the system is called perfect.

Remark 2
.

Replacing
\{z^{k}\}_{0\leq k\leq n_{j}-1}
by any invertible lower-triangular change of basis (for example,
\{\varphi_{k}(z)\}_{0\leq k\leq n_{j}-1}
) in (
2.23
) and (
2.25
) leaves the linear systems defining
\Phi_{\vec{n}}(z)
and
\Psi_{\vec{n}}(z)
unchanged.

A monotonic path is a sequence of multi-indices
\{\vec{n}_{j}\}_{j=0}^{n}
from
\vec{n}_{0}=\vec{0}
to
\vec{n}_{n}=\vec{n}
such that
|\vec{n}_{j}|=j
and
\vec{n}_{j+1}\geq\vec{n}_{j}
.
Along a fixed path, we define the associated sequences of type II polynomials and type I linear forms as

(2.26)

\Phi_{j}(z):=\Phi_{\vec{n}_{j}}(z),\quad\Psi_{j}(z):=\Psi_{\vec{n}_{j+1}}(z),\quad j=0,1,\dots,n-1.

We then define the Christoffel-Darboux kernel for MOPUC by

(2.27)

\mathcal{K}(z,\xi)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\Phi_{j}(z)\,\overline{\Psi_{j}(\xi)}.

Remark 3
.

This Christoffel-Darboux kernel is slightly different from the one considered in

\nu_{j}

2.27

2.7

Similarly to
[
30

\mathcal{K}(z,\xi)
is independent of the specific monotonic path chosen to connect
\vec{0}
and
\vec{n}_{n}
. Thus, we use the natural path hereafter.
For this reason, whenever we mention
\Phi_{j}(z)
and
\Psi_{j}(z)
defined in (
2.26
) for the rest of the paper, we assume that the multi-index
\vec{n}
is fixed and the sequences
\Phi_{j}(x)
and
\Psi_{j}(x)
are understood to be defined along the natural path from
\vec{0}
to
\vec{n}
.

As with Lemma

To illustrate the explicit formulae obtained above and to further investigate
zeros of type II MOPUC, we consider weights given by characteristic functions
of arcs of the unit circle. This is a natural class of examples in the
Toeplitz/OPUC setting, whose connection with random-matrix gap probabilities
is well known. For instance, if
U
is drawn from CUE, then the probability
that all eigenangles of
U
lie in a measurable set

is
the Toeplitz determinant generated by
\chi_{B}
; see

[
36

20

. Similarly, the GUE bulk gap probability on

is obtained as the large-

determinant generated by the characteristic function of the arc

[
32

and also
[
35

.

In the setting of multiple orthogonality on the unit circle, systems of
measures supported on arcs were considered by Mínguez Ceniceros and
Van Assche in
[
37
]
. Recall that, for ordinary OPUC associated
with a positive measure on
\mathbb{T}
with infinite support, all zeros of
the corresponding monic orthogonal polynomials lie in the open unit disk.
The examples in
[
37
]
show that the zero behavior of type II MOPUC
can differ substantially from this classical picture. For two disjoint arcs

[
37
, Example 1]
, the zeros appear to lie on curves connecting the
endpoints of the supporting arcs, rather than accumulating close to the arcs
themselves. For overlapping arcs
[
37
, Example 2]
, the zeros may
even leave the unit disk. In the case where one measure is supported on the
full circle and the other on a single arc, the type II polynomial has a zero
of large multiplicity at the origin, while the remaining zeros are governed
by the geometry of the arc. In the diagonal case, this phenomenon is made
explicit in
[
37
, Proposition 3.1]
, where the nontrivial factor is
expressed in terms of a Legendre polynomial.

The examples below are in the same spirit, but are chosen so that our general formulae lead to completely explicit expressions. In
particular, they make visible a simple resonance mechanism by which zeros
of type II MOPUC can escape to infinity.

Remark 4
.

Here we make some observations about the zeros of arc-indicator type II MOPUC under the equal-length assumption.
newline

(1)

The estimate (
3.28

r
and
\Delta\notin\pi\mathbb{Q}
are fixed. Since the sequence

such that

\sin\left(\frac{m_{k}\Delta}{2}\right)\to 0.

Setting

Moreover,

\sin\left(\frac{n_{k}\Delta}{2}\right)\to\sin\left(\frac{r\Delta}{2}\right),

in (

\Phi_{\vec{n}_{k}}
escapes to infinity.

(2)

At the same time, for a fixed finite degree

\Delta
so that
\sin\left(\frac{(n-r)\Delta}{2}\right)
is very small while
\sin\left(\frac{n\Delta}{2}\right)
remains nonzero. In this finite-

interpretation, the natural parameters are the arc centers
x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}
, the number of arcs

, and the common arc length
\Delta
; see Figures
1A
and
2A
.

(3)

In
[
37
, Example 2]
, it is observed that some zeros of the
type II MOPUC of fixed degree lie outside the unit disk. However, their
overlapping-arc example does not address the possibility that, under a
limiting choice of geometric parameters, some zeros may tend to infinity.
This phenomenon appears to be observed here for the first time in the MOPUC
setting.

0<\Delta<2\pi/r
and
\Delta/\pi\notin\mathbb{Q}
.
Write the set of
r
nontrivial zeros of the degree
n
polynomial (
3.34
) as
\mathcal{Z}^{(n)}_{r}
.
Then, these
r
zeros of
\Phi_{\vec{n}}(z)
are asypmtotically dense in the
2r
rays on the complex plane, i.e.,

\bigcap_{m=r+1}^{\infty}\overline{\bigcup_{n\geq m}\mathcal{Z}^{(n)}_{r}}=\bigcup_{\ell=0}^{2r-1}\{\varrho\,e^{i(\theta_{1}+\pi\ell/r)}:\varrho\geq 0\}.

Proof.

Put
k=n-r
. By (
3.35
), the nontrivial zeros are the roots of

z^{r}=A_{k+r,r}(\Delta)x_{1}^{r}
, where

A_{k+r,r}(\Delta)=\frac{k}{k+r}\frac{\sin((k+r)\Delta/2)}{\sin(k\Delta/2)}=\frac{k}{k+r}\left(\cos\frac{r\Delta}{2}+\sin\frac{r\Delta}{2}\cot\frac{k\Delta}{2}\right).

Since
\Delta/\pi
is irrational,
k\Delta/2\pmod{\pi}
is dense in
(0,\pi)
. Also

\sin(r\Delta/2)\neq 0
. Thus, the last parenthesis takes every real value
along subsequences of
k
, and since
k/(k+r)\to 1
, every
y\in\mathbb{R}
is a
subsequential limit of
A_{k+r,r}(\Delta)
.

Let
\varrho\geq 0
and
\ell\in\{0,\ldots,2r-1\}
, choose a subsequence with

A_{k_{\ell}+r,r}(\Delta)\to(-1)^{\ell}\varrho^{r}
.
Then,
z^{r}\to(-1)^{\ell}\varrho^{r}x_{1}^{r}
and the
\ell
-th root satisfies
z_{\ell}\to\varrho\,e^{i(\theta_{1}+\pi\ell/r)}
.
Conversely,
A_{k+r,r}(\Delta)
is real for all
k
, so every nontrivial zero lies on one of these
2r
rays.
∎

Moreover, one can also show that
\bigcup^{\infty}_{r=1}\bigcap_{m=r+1}^{\infty}\overline{\bigcup_{n\geq m}\mathcal{Z}^{(n)}_{r}}

4.
Proof of Theorems (MOPRL)

4.1.
Proof of Theorem
3.1

By Definition
2.1
, type II MOPRL
P_{\vec{n}}(x)
is a monic polynomial of degree
n=|\vec{n}|
.
As the space of polynomials of degree up to
n
is a Hilbert space spanned by the monic orthogonal polynomials
\{p_{k}(x)\}_{k=0}^{n}
with respect to the reference weight
w^{(1)}(x)
, one can uniquely express
P_{\vec{n}}(x)
by

(4.1)

P_{\vec{n}}(x)=p_{n}(x)+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}c_{k}p_{k}(x)

for some
c_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
. Recall
P_{\vec{n}}(x)
satisfies (
2.1
) as part of the definition.
Since one can integrate
P_{\vec{n}}(x)
against the orthogonal polynomial basis
\{p_{k}(x)\}_{k=0}^{n_{j}-1}
instead of the monomial basis
\{x^{k}\}_{k=0}^{n_{j}-1}
to check the orthogonality, we observe that the first
n_{1}
orthogonality conditions,
\int_{\mathbb{R}}P_{\vec{n}}(x)p_{k}(x)w^{(1)}(x)\mathrm{d}x=0
for
k=0,\cdots,n_{1}-1
, implies that
c_{0}=\dots=c_{n_{1}-1}=0
.
This simplifies the expansion to

(4.2)

P_{\vec{n}}(x)=p_{n}(x)+\sum_{k=n_{1}}^{n-1}c_{k}p_{k}(x).

The remaining coefficients can be specified from the rest of the orthogonality conditions now for
\{w^{(j)}(x)\}_{j=2}^{r}
.
For each
j\in\{2,\dots,r\}
and
b\in\{0,\dots,n_{j}-1\}
, we have

(4.3)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}P_{\vec{n}}(x)p_{b}(x)w^{(j)}(x)\mathrm{d}x=0.

Substituting (
4.2
) into this condition gives

(4.4)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(p_{n}(x)+\sum_{k=n_{1}}^{n-1}c_{k}p_{k}(x)\right)p_{b}(x)w^{(j)}(x)\mathrm{d}x=0.

Rearranging this gives a linear system for the coefficient vector
\bm{c}_{2}=(c_{n_{1}},\dots,c_{n-1})^{\top}
,

(4.5)

\sum_{k=n_{1}}^{n-1}c_{k}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{k}(x)p_{b}(x)w^{(j)}(x)\mathrm{d}x\right)=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{n}(x)p_{b}(x)w^{(j)}(x)\mathrm{d}x.

In matrix notation, the linear system for the coefficients becomes

(4.6)

\bm{c}_{2}^{\top}\bm{M}_{22}=-\bm{v}_{2}^{\top}

where we used (
2.11
), (
2.12
), (
2.15
), and (
2.16
).

Since (
4.2
) can be written as
P_{\vec{n}}(x)=p_{n}(x)+\bm{c}_{2}^{\top}\bm{p}_{2}(x)
, we obtain
P_{\vec{n}}(x)=p_{n}(x)-\bm{v}_{2}^{\top}(\bm{M}_{22})^{-1}\bm{p}_{2}(x)
from (
4.6
).
Moreover, by Schur’s formula for the determinant of a block matrix, we have

(4.7)

\det\begin{pmatrix}\;\bm{M}_{22}&\bm{p}_{2}(x)\\
&\\
\;\bm{v}_{2}^{\top}&p_{n}(x)\end{pmatrix}=\det\bm{M}_{22}\left(p_{n}(x)-\bm{v}_{2}^{\top}\bm{M}_{22}^{-1}\bm{p}_{2}(x)\right).

Dividing by
\det\bm{M}_{22}
, we obtain the determinantal formula for
P_{\vec{n}}(x)
, which proves Theorem
3.1
.

4.2.
Proof of Theorem
3.2

Similar to type II MOPRL, defining condition (
2.2
) are entirely equivalent to testing against the orthogonal polynomial basis
\{p_{m}(x)\}_{m=0}^{n-1}
,

(4.8)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}Q_{\vec{n}}(x)p_{m}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\delta_{m,n-1},\quad\text{for }m=0,\dots,n-1.

We now build a linear system using these equivalent conditions.
Since type I MOPRL
A_{j}(x)
for
j=1,\cdots,r
can be written as a linear combination of
\{p_{k}\}_{k=0}^{n-1}
, one has

A_{j}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n_{j}-1}d_{k}^{(j)}p_{k}(x)

for some
d_{k}^{(j)}\in\mathbb{R}
, and
Q_{\vec{n}}(x)
is expressed by

(4.9)

Q_{\vec{n}}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\sum_{k=0}^{n_{j}-1}d_{k}^{(j)}p_{k}(x)w^{(j)}(x)=\bm{d}^{\top}\bm{q}(x),

where
\bm{d}=\left(d_{0}^{(1)},\cdots,d_{n_{1}-1}^{(1)},d_{0}^{(2)},\cdots,d_{n_{2}-1}^{(2)},\bm{\cdots},d_{0}^{(r)},\cdots,d_{n_{r}-1}^{(r)}\right)^{\top}
, recall (
2.10
).
Substituting this into (
4.8
) gives

(4.10)

\bm{d}^{\top}\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{m}(x)\bm{q}(x)\mathrm{d}x=\delta_{m,n-1}

for
m=0,\cdots,n-1
. In matrix notation, recalling (
2.12
), this gives

(4.11)

\displaystyle\bm{d}^{\top}\bm{M}^{\top}=\bm{e}_{n}^{\top}\iff\bm{M}\bm{d}=\bm{e}_{n},

where
\bm{e}_{n}=(0,\cdots,0,1)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Thus, we have
Q_{\vec{n}}(x)=\bm{e}_{n}^{\top}\bm{M}^{\top-1}\bm{q}(x)
, and similarly to the discussion in the previous section, we obtain the determinantal formula for
Q_{\vec{n}}(x)
, which proves Theorem
3.2
.

4.3.
Proof of Theorem
3.3

We first prove a simple uniqueness lemma for the finite-dimensional kernel. This lemma also explains why the kernel can be written in the weighted basis
\bm{q}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

For each fixed
x
, there is a unique function
L(x,\cdot)
in

(4.12)

\mathcal{W}_{\vec{n}}^{\mathbb{R}}:=\operatorname{span}\{q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}\},

where
q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}
are the entries of
\bm{q}
, such that

(4.13)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}L(x,y)\pi(y)\,\mathrm{d}y=\pi(x),\quad\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{n-1}^{\mathbb{R}}.

Moreover, the MOPRL kernel can be written as

(4.14)

K(x,y)=\bm{q}(y)^{\top}\bm{M}^{-1}\bm{p}(x).

Proof.

Suppose that
L_{1}(x,\cdot)
and
L_{2}(x,\cdot)
both belong to
\mathcal{W}_{\vec{n}}^{\mathbb{R}}
and both satisfy (
4.13
). Then, for fixed
x
, their difference can be written as

(4.15)

L_{1}(x,y)-L_{2}(x,y)=\bm{a}(x)^{\top}\bm{q}(y)

for some
n
-column vector
\bm{a}(x)
. Since the difference integrates to zero against every polynomial in
\mathcal{P}_{n-1}^{\mathbb{R}}
, we may test it against the basis
p_{0},\ldots,p_{n-1}
. Thus, for
m=0,\ldots,n-1
,

(4.16)

0=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{m}(y)\bm{q}(y)^{\top}\,\mathrm{d}y\right)\bm{a}(x).

Equivalently,

(4.17)

\bm{M}\bm{a}(x)=\bm{0}.

Since the multi-index is normal, the mixed-moment matrix
\bm{M}
is nonsingular. Hence
\bm{a}(x)=\bm{0}
, and the function satisfying (
4.13
) is unique.

We now check that the MOPRL kernel satisfies the same property. Recall (
2.7
); for each fixed
x
,
K(x,\cdot)
is a linear combination of
Q_{0},\ldots,Q_{n-1}
. Since the natural path remains componentwise bounded by the final multi-index
\vec{n}
, each
Q_{j}
is a type I linear form whose polynomial coefficients have degrees bounded by
n_{k}-1
in the
k
-th weight, therefore
Q_{j}\in\mathcal{W}_{\vec{n}}^{\mathbb{R}}
. Thus
K(x,\cdot)\in\mathcal{W}_{\vec{n}}^{\mathbb{R}}
. Moreover, since
P_{0},\ldots,P_{n-1}
are monic polynomials of degrees
0,\ldots,n-1
, they form a basis of
\mathcal{P}_{n-1}^{\mathbb{R}}
. Therefore, for any
\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{n-1}^{\mathbb{R}}
, we can write

(4.18)

\pi(y)=\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}a_{m}P_{m}(y).

Using the biorthogonality relation (
2.8
), we obtain

(4.19)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(x,y)\pi(y)\,\mathrm{d}y=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}P_{j}(x)Q_{j}(y)\right)\left(\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}a_{m}P_{m}(y)\right)\mathrm{d}y=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}a_{j}P_{j}(x)=\pi(x).

Thus
K
satisfies (
4.13
).

On the other hand, define

(4.20)

\widehat{K}(x,y):=\bm{q}(y)^{\top}\bm{M}^{-1}\bm{p}(x).

Clearly
\widehat{K}(x,\cdot)\in\mathcal{W}_{\vec{n}}^{\mathbb{R}}
. It is enough to test (
4.13
) on the basis
p_{0},\ldots,p_{n-1}
. For
m=0,\ldots,n-1
, by the definition of
\bm{M}
, we have

(4.21)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widehat{K}(x,y)p_{m}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{m}(y)\bm{q}(y)^{\top}\,\mathrm{d}y\right)\bm{M}^{-1}\bm{p}(x)=\bm{e}_{m+1}^{\top}\bm{M}\bm{M}^{-1}\bm{p}(x)=p_{m}(x).

Thus
\widehat{K}
also satisfies (
4.13
). By uniqueness,
K=\widehat{K}
, which proves (
4.14
).
∎

We now prove Theorem
3.3
. Recall the block decompositions (
2.15
) and (
2.17
). Since
\bm{M}
is nonsingular by normality, its inverse is

(4.22)

\bm{M}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{H}_{1}^{-1}&-\bm{H}_{1}^{-1}\bm{M}_{12}\bm{M}_{22}^{-1}\\
\bm{0}&\bm{M}_{22}^{-1}\end{pmatrix}.

Substituting (
4.22
) into (
4.14
) gives

(4.23)

K(x,y)=\bm{q}_{1}(y)^{\top}\bm{H}_{1}^{-1}\bm{p}_{1}(x)+\left(\bm{q}_{2}(y)^{\top}-\bm{q}_{1}(y)^{\top}\bm{H}_{1}^{-1}\bm{M}_{12}\right)\bm{M}_{22}^{-1}\bm{p}_{2}(x).

Thus, using (
2.18
), we obtain

(4.24)

K(x,y)-K_{0}(x,y)=\left(\bm{q}_{2}(y)^{\top}-\bm{q}_{1}(y)^{\top}\bm{H}_{1}^{-1}\bm{M}_{12}-w^{(1)}(y)\bm{p}_{2}(y)^{\top}\bm{H}_{2}^{-1}\bm{M}_{22}\right)\bm{M}_{22}^{-1}\bm{p}_{2}(x).

It remains to identify the row vector in parentheses with
\bm{r}(y)^{\top}
. By definition,

(4.25)

\bm{r}(y)^{\top}=\bm{q}_{2}(y)^{\top}-\int_{\mathbb{R}}K_{0}(s,y)\bm{q}_{2}(s)^{\top}\,\mathrm{d}s.

Using (
2.4
), we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}K_{0}(s,y)\bm{q}_{2}(s)^{\top}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=w^{(1)}(y)\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\frac{p_{j}(y)}{h_{j}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{j}(s)\bm{q}_{2}(s)^{\top}\,\mathrm{d}s

(4.26)

\displaystyle=\bm{q}_{1}(y)^{\top}\bm{H}_{1}^{-1}\bm{M}_{12}+w^{(1)}(y)\bm{p}_{2}(y)^{\top}\bm{H}_{2}^{-1}\bm{M}_{22}.

Substituting (
4.3
) into (
4.25
), we get

(4.27)

\bm{r}(y)^{\top}=\bm{q}_{2}(y)^{\top}-\bm{q}_{1}(y)^{\top}\bm{H}_{1}^{-1}\bm{M}_{12}-w^{(1)}(y)\bm{p}_{2}(y)^{\top}\bm{H}_{2}^{-1}\bm{M}_{22}.

By (
4.24
) and (
4.27
),

(4.28)

K(x,y)-K_{0}(x,y)=\bm{r}(y)^{\top}\bm{M}_{22}^{-1}\bm{p}_{2}(x),

which completes the proof of Theorem
3.3
.

4.4.
Proof of Corollary
3.4

We apply the general reduction formula to the multiple Hermite weights

(4.29)

w^{(1)}(x)=e^{-x^{2}},\quad w^{(j)}(x)=e^{a_{j}x-x^{2}},\quad j=2,\cdots,r.

It is a well-known fact that the monic orthogonal polynomials
p_{k}(x)
with respect to the weight
w^{(1)}(x)=e^{-x^{2}}
are the monic Hermite polynomials,

(4.30)

\displaystyle\widehat{H}_{k}(x)=\frac{(-1)^{k}}{2^{k}}e^{x^{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{k}}{\mathrm{d}x^{k}}e^{-x^{2}}.

Throughout this subsection, we use several facts about Hermite polynomials, see for example,
[
24
, Sections 18.3, 18.5, 18.17, 18.18]
.

4.4.1.
Explicit Expression of
\bm{M}

Recall that the mixed-moment matrix
\bm{M}
admits the block structures (
2.13
) and (
2.17
), i.e.,

M^{(1)}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{H}_{1}\\
\bm{0}\end{pmatrix}\in\operatorname{Mat}_{n\times n_{1}},\quad\begin{pmatrix}M^{(2)}&\cdots&M^{(r)}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{M}_{12}\\
\bm{M}_{22}\end{pmatrix}\in\operatorname{Mat}_{n\times(n-n_{1})}.

By (
2.14
),
\bm{H}_{1}
is a diagonal matrix of norms
h_{k}
for
k=0,\cdots,n_{1}-1
, where each
h_{k}
is given by

(4.31)

\displaystyle h_{k}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widehat{H}_{k}^{2}(x)e^{-x^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\,k!}{2^{k}}.

By (
2.12
), each
n\times n_{j}
sub-block matrix of the right
n\times(n-n_{1})
block matrix of
\bm{M}
,

\big(\underbrace{M^{(2)}}_{n\times n_{2}}\;\cdots\;\underbrace{M^{(r)}}_{n\times n_{r}}\big),

has entries

(4.32)

\displaystyle M^{(j)}_{m,b}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widehat{H}_{m}(x)\widehat{H}_{b}(x)e^{a_{j}x-x^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x,\quad 0\leq m\leq n-1,\quad 0\leq b\leq n_{j}-1.

Since it holds that

\widehat{H}_{m}(x)\widehat{H}_{b}(x)=\sum_{q=0}^{\min(m,b)}2^{-q}q!\binom{m}{q}\binom{b}{q}\widehat{H}_{m+b-2q}(x)

and

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widehat{H}_{k}(x)e^{a_{j}x-x^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x=\sqrt{\pi}\,e^{t_{j}^{2}}t_{j}^{k},\quad t_{j}=\frac{a_{j}}{2},

it follows that

(4.33)

M^{(j)}_{m,b}=\sqrt{\pi}\,e^{t_{j}^{2}}\sum_{q=0}^{\min(m,b)}2^{-q}q!\binom{m}{q}\binom{b}{q}t_{j}^{m+b-2q}=\sqrt{\pi}\,e^{t_{j}^{2}}\sum_{q=0}^{\min(m,b)}2^{-q}\binom{b}{q}t_{j}^{b-q}\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}^{q}}{\mathrm{d}t^{q}}t^{n_{1}+s}\right]_{t=t_{j}}.

4.4.2.
Decomposition of
\bm{M}_{22}

Since we need to compute
\bm{M}_{22}^{-1}
explicitly to apply Theorem
3.3
, we shall further study structure of
\bm{M}_{22}
.
Let
N=n-n_{1}
. Then, one may write
\bm{M}_{22}
as

\big(\underbrace{\bm{M}_{22}^{(2)}}_{N\times n_{2}}\;\cdots\;\underbrace{\bm{M}_{22}^{(r)}}_{N\times n_{r}}\big),

where the
(s,b)
-entry of
\bm{M}_{22}^{(j)}
is given by

(4.34)

\displaystyle\left(\bm{M}_{22}^{(j)}\right)_{s,b}=M^{(j)}_{n_{1}+s,b},\quad 0\leq s\leq N-1,\quad 0\leq b\leq n_{j}-1.

One can readily notice that (
4.33
) gives the matrix decomposition,

\bm{M}_{22}=\bm{V}\bm{R},

where
\bm{V}
is an
N\times N
matrix,

(4.35)

\bm{V}=\big(\underbrace{\bm{V}^{(2)}}_{N\times n_{2}}\;\cdots\;\underbrace{\bm{V}^{(r)}}_{N\times n_{r}}\big),\quad\bm{V}^{(j)}_{s,q}=\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}^{q}}{\mathrm{d}t^{q}}t^{n_{1}+s}\right]_{t=t_{j}},\quad 0\leq s\leq N-1,\quad 0\leq q\leq n_{j}-1,

and
\bm{R}
is an
N\times N
block diagonal matrix,

(4.36)

\bm{R}=\operatorname{diag}(\underbrace{\bm{R}^{(2)}}_{n_{2}\times n_{2}},\cdots,\underbrace{\bm{R}^{(r)}}_{n_{r}\times n_{r}}),\quad\bm{R}^{(j)}_{q,b}=\sqrt{\pi}\,e^{t_{j}^{2}}2^{-q}\binom{b}{q}t_{j}^{b-q},\quad 0\leq q,b\leq n_{j}-1,

with a convention
\binom{b}{q}=0
if
q>b
(that is each
\bm{R}^{(j)}
is upper triangular). Thus, all we need to compute is
\bm{R}^{-1}
and
\bm{V}^{-1}
.

4.4.3.
Inverse of
\bm{R}

For
\bm{R}^{-1}
, since the inverse of a block diagonal matrix is the matrix formed by the inverses of each individual block matrix, we need to compute
\big(\bm{R}^{(j)}\big)^{-1}
.
Notice one can write
\bm{R}^{(j)}
as a product of matrices,

\bm{R}^{(j)}=\sqrt{\pi}e^{t_{j}^{2}}\operatorname{diag}(2^{-b})_{b=0}^{n_{j}-1}\,S(t_{j}),

where
S(t_{j})
is a shifted generalized Pascal matrix with entries

(S(t_{j}))_{b,q}=\begin{cases}\binom{q}{b}t_{j}^{q-b},&b\leq q,\\
0,&b>q,\end{cases}

whose inverse formula is well-known, see
[
19
, Equation (7)]
for example,

S(t_{j})^{-1}=S(-t_{j}).

Thus, again with the convention
\binom{q}{b}=0
if
b>q
, one has

(4.37)

\left(\bm{R}^{(j)}\right)^{-1}_{b,q}=\frac{e^{-t_{j}^{2}}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\binom{q}{b}(-t_{j})^{q-b}2^{q}.

4.4.4.
Inverse of
\bm{V}

To compute
\bm{V}^{-1}
, we first notice that
\bm{V}
is a confluent Vandermonde matrix. By computing the associated generalized Hermite interpolation polynomials, see (
4.39
), we shall find
\bm{V}^{-1}
. For the general construction of such polynomials, see
[
38
]
.
For
j=2,\ldots,r
, define

(4.38)

L_{j}(z)=\left(\frac{z}{t_{j}}\right)^{n_{1}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=2\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}^{r}\left(\frac{z-t_{i}}{t_{j}-t_{i}}\right)^{n_{i}},

the Lagrange-type factor associated with the node
t_{j}
. Using this factor, introduce, for
0\leq q\leq n_{j}-1
, the interpolation polynomials

(4.39)

\Pi_{j,q}(z)=L_{j}(z)\,\frac{(z-t_{j})^{q}}{q!}\left(\sum_{\mu=0}^{n_{j}-1-q}\frac{1}{\mu!}\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}^{\mu}}{\mathrm{d}z^{\mu}}\frac{1}{L_{j}(z)}\right]_{z=t_{j}}(z-t_{j})^{\mu}\right),

where the expression in parentheses is the Taylor polynomial of
1/L_{j}(z)
at
t_{j}
of degree
n_{j}-1-q
. The polynomial
\Pi_{j,q}
satisfies the interpolation conditions

(4.40)

\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}^{\nu}}{\mathrm{d}z^{\nu}}\Pi_{j,q}(z)\right]_{z=t_{i}}=\delta_{ij}\delta_{q\nu},\qquad 2\leq i,j\leq r,\qquad 0\leq\nu\leq n_{i}-1.

In the special case
n_{j}=1
, only
q=0
occurs at the node
t_{j}
, and the construction reduces to
\Pi_{j,0}=L_{j}
.

Let us verify that condition (
4.40
) indeed holds. First, observe that
L_{j}(z)
has a zero of order
n_{i}
at
z=t_{i}
for
i\neq j
, while the remaining factors of
\Pi_{j,q}(z)
are analytic at
z=t_{i}
. Hence,
\Pi_{j,q}(z)
has a zero of order
n_{i}
at
z=t_{j}
for
i\neq j
, establishing (
4.40
) for
i\neq j
. For the
i=j
case, Taylor’s theorem gives

(4.41)

\frac{1}{L_{j}(z)}-\left(\sum_{\mu=0}^{n_{j}-1-q}\frac{1}{\mu!}\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}^{\mu}}{\mathrm{d}z^{\mu}}\frac{1}{L_{j}(z)}\right]_{z=t_{j}}(z-t_{j})^{\mu}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left((z-t_{j})^{n_{j}-q}\right)

as
z\to t_{j}
, and combining this with (
4.39
), we have

(4.42)

\Pi_{j,q}(z)=\frac{(z-t_{j})^{q}}{q!}+O\bigl((z-t_{j})^{n_{j}}\bigr).

This proves (
4.40
) at the node
t_{j}
.

Now, we shall rewrite
\Pi_{j,q}(z)
as

\Pi_{j,q}(z)=\sum_{s=0}^{N-1}v_{s}^{(j,q)}z^{n_{1}+s}

where the coefficients
v_{s}^{(j,q)}
can be computed from expanding (
4.40
) at
z=0
.
Using this expression with (
4.40
), one has

(4.43)

\sum_{s=0}^{N-1}v_{s}^{(j,q)}\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}^{\nu}}{\mathrm{d}z^{\nu}}z^{n_{1}+s}\right]_{z=t_{i}}=\sum_{s=0}^{N-1}v_{s}^{(j,q)}\bm{V}^{(i)}_{s,\nu}=\delta_{ij}\delta_{q\nu},

where we also recall (
4.35
).
Equation (
4.43
) implies that we can form
n_{j}\times N
block matrices
\bm{W}^{(j)}
where the
(q,s)
-entry of
j
-th block is given by
v_{s}^{(j,q)}
and put these block matrices together to construct
\bm{V}^{-1}
,

(4.44)

\bm{V}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{W}^{(2)}\\
\vdots\\
\bm{W}^{(r)}\end{pmatrix},\quad\bm{W}^{(j)}_{q,s}=v_{s}^{(j,q)},\quad 0\leq q\leq n_{j}-1,\quad 0\leq s\leq N-1.

4.4.5.
Explicit Expression of

Recall

(4.45)

\bm{M}_{22}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{U}^{(2)}\\
\vdots\\
\bm{U}^{(r)}\end{pmatrix},\quad\bm{U}^{(j)}_{b,s}=U^{(j)}_{b,s},\quad 0\leq b\leq n_{j}-1,\quad 0\leq s\leq N-1.

Applying (

4.4.6.

Now, we are ready to compute the multiple Hermite kernel

Similarly, the current setup shows that the vector

Applying (

-e^{-y^{2}}\sum_{s=0}^{N-1}\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}\frac{\widehat{H}_{n_{1}+s}(x)\widehat{H}_{m}(y)}{h_{m}}\sum_{j=2}^{r}\sum_{b=0}^{n_{j}-1}U^{(j)}_{b,s}M^{(j)}_{m,b}.

We shall denote the inner sum on the last line by

(4.52)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{m,s}:=\sum_{j=2}^{r}\sum_{b=0}^{n_{j}-1}U^{(j)}_{b,s}M^{(j)}_{m,b}.

For

where

(4.53)

\Gamma_{m,s}=\delta_{st},

which is readily obtained from writing out

\Gamma_{m,s}=\sum_{j=2}^{r}\sum_{q=0}^{n_{j}-1}\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}^{q}}{\mathrm{d}t^{q}}t^{m}\right]_{t=t_{j}}v_{s}^{(j,q)}=\sum_{j=2}^{r}\sum_{q=0}^{\min(m,n_{j}-1)}q!\binom{m}{q}t_{j}^{m-q}v_{s}^{(j,q)}.

Using (
4.53
), (
4.54
), and (
4.47

\displaystyle K(x,y)

\displaystyle=K_{0}(x,y)+\sum_{s=0}^{N-1}\sum_{j=2}^{r}\sum_{b=0}^{n_{j}-1}U^{(j)}_{b,s}\widehat{H}_{n_{1}+s}(x)\widehat{H}_{b}(y)e^{a_{j}y-y^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\quad-e^{-y^{2}}\sum_{s=0}^{N-1}\left(\sum_{m=0}^{n_{1}-1}\frac{\widehat{H}_{n_{1}+s}(x)\widehat{H}_{m}(y)}{h_{m}}\Gamma_{m,s}+\sum_{t=0}^{N-1}\frac{\widehat{H}_{n_{1}+s}(x)\widehat{H}_{n_{1}+t}(y)}{h_{n_{1}+t}}\delta_{st}\right)

\displaystyle=e^{-y^{2}}\sum_{m=0}^{n_{1}-1}\frac{\widehat{H}_{m}(x)\widehat{H}_{m}(y)}{h_{m}}+e^{-y^{2}}\sum_{t=0}^{N-1}\frac{\widehat{H}_{n_{1}+t}(x)\widehat{H}_{n_{1}+t}(y)}{h_{n_{1}+t}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\quad+\sum_{s=0}^{N-1}\sum_{j=2}^{r}\sum_{b=0}^{n_{j}-1}U^{(j)}_{b,s}\widehat{H}_{n_{1}+s}(x)\widehat{H}_{b}(y)e^{a_{j}y-y^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\quad-e^{-y^{2}}\sum_{s=0}^{N-1}\sum_{m=0}^{n_{1}-1}\frac{\widehat{H}_{n_{1}+s}(x)\widehat{H}_{m}(y)}{h_{m}}\Gamma_{m,s}-e^{-y^{2}}\sum_{t=0}^{N-1}\frac{\widehat{H}_{n_{1}+t}(x)\widehat{H}_{n_{1}+t}(y)}{h_{n_{1}+t}}

\displaystyle=e^{-y^{2}}\sum_{m=0}^{n_{1}-1}\frac{\widehat{H}_{m}(y)}{h_{m}}\left(\widehat{H}_{m}(x)-\sum_{s=0}^{N-1}\Gamma_{m,s}\widehat{H}_{n_{1}+s}(x)\right)+\sum_{s=0}^{N-1}\sum_{j=2}^{r}\sum_{b=0}^{n_{j}-1}U^{(j)}_{b,s}\widehat{H}_{n_{1}+s}(x)\widehat{H}_{b}(y)e^{a_{j}y-y^{2}}.

Using (
4.31

Since OPUC is a basis of

.

7.
External Source Models

In this section, we explain how the kernel reduction formula in Theorem
3.3

recovers two earlier formulae for random matrix models with external source.
First, we recover Baik’s finite-rank reduction formula
[
6
, Theorem 1]
. We then explain how
the same mixed-moment formalism also contains the earlier formula
of Zinn-Justin
[
43
, Equation (3.10)]
for the kernel in terms of external source parameters and the orthogonal polynomial with respect to the reference weight.

7.1.
A review of the external source model

The connection between multiple orthogonal polynomials and random matrix theory
became especially prominent through Hermitian random matrix models with external
source. In such models, introduced by Brezin and Hikami
[
16
,
17
]
, one considers probability measures of the form

(7.1)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Z_{n}}\exp\{-\operatorname{Tr}(V(M)-AM)\}\,\mathrm{d}M

on Hermitian matrices, where
A
is a fixed Hermitian matrix. If
A
has
distinct eigenvalues
a_{1},\cdots,a_{r}
with multiplicities

n_{1},\cdots,n_{r}
, then the average characteristic polynomial

\mathbb{E}[\det(zI-M)]

is characterized by multiple orthogonality with respect to the weights

e^{-(V(x)-a_{j}x)},\qquad j=1,\cdots,r.

This observation, due to Bleher and Kuijlaars
[
11
]
, showed that the
external-source model is governed by multiple orthogonal polynomials. Moreover,
the eigenvalue correlation functions form a determinantal point process whose
kernel can be written in terms of type I and type II multiple orthogonal
polynomials. The Christoffel-Darboux formula of Daems and Kuijlaars

[
22
]
then provided a compact expression for this kernel, generalizing the
ordinary Christoffel-Darboux formula for orthogonal polynomial ensembles.

The Gaussian external-source model with two eigenvalues
\pm a
of equal
multiplicity has played a central role in this development. In the
subcritical regime
0<a<1
, analyzed by Aptekarev, Bleher, and Kuijlaars,
the limiting spectrum is supported on a single interval, as in the case with
no external source
[
3
]
. As
a
increases, the external field pushes the spectrum
toward a splitting transition; in the supercritical regime
a>1
, analyzed by
Bleher and Kuijlaars, the support consists of two intervals

[
12
]
. In both non-critical regimes, the local correlations have
the standard universal limits: the sine kernel in the bulk and the Airy kernel
near regular edges. At the critical value
a=1
, however, the two regimes
meet at the point where one band is about to split into two, and the local
correlations are governed instead by the Pearcey kernel
[
14
]
. For more general even polynomial
potentials, Bleher, Delvaux, and Kuijlaars later showed that the limiting
eigenvalue distribution is described by a constrained vector equilibrium
problem
[
10
]
. The analysis of these models was based on a
3\times 3
Riemann-Hilbert
problem as established in
[
41
]
. In this sense, these works provided some of the first large-
n

steepest descent analyses beyond the classical
2\times 2
setting, and
helped inform subsequent extensions of the Deift-Zhou nonlinear steepest
descent method to higher-dimensional Riemann-Hilbert problems

[
23
,
25
,
33
,
9
]
.

A different, but closely related, line of work concerns finite-rank, or
spiked, external sources. In this setting, the external source
A
has only
finitely many nonzero eigenvalues, or more generally, a number of nonzero
eigenvalues that grows slowly with
n
. Bertola–Buckingham–Lee–Pierce
considered the small-rank external source

A=\operatorname{diag}(\underbrace{a,\cdots,a}_{r},\underbrace{0,\cdots,0}_{n-r}),

with
r
fixed or slowly growing with
n
. For general analytic potentials,
they proved universality in the subcritical and supercritical regimes, and, in
the critical and near-critical regimes with
r=\mathcal{O}(n^{\gamma})
, obtained
the corresponding
r
-Airy edge statistics
[
7
,
8
]
.

Another important perspective was developed by Baik, who showed in

[
6
]
that for

(7.2)

\displaystyle A=\operatorname{diag}(\underbrace{a_{1},\cdots,a_{r}}_{r},\underbrace{0,\cdots,0}_{n-r}),

the multiple-orthogonal Christoffel-Darboux kernel can be expressed as a finite-rank perturbation of the ordinary Christoffel-Darboux kernel associated with the reference weight
e^{-V(x)}
.
Baik and Wang used this perspective in their analysis of the largest eigenvalue of Hermitian matrix models with spiked external source, first in the rank-one case and then in higher rank
[
4
,
5
]
.
Their results exhibit the familiar subcritical, critical, and supercritical regimes: below the critical value, the largest eigenvalue remains at the edge of the referenced equilibrium measure, while above it one or more outliers detach from the bulk.

\displaystyle=\mathbf{v}(y)w^{(1)}(y)-\int_{\mathbb{R}}K^{B}_{0}(s,y)\frac{w^{(1)}(y)}{w^{(1)}(s)}\mathbf{v}(s)w^{(1)}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=w^{(1)}(y)\left(\mathbf{v}(y)-\int_{\mathbb{R}}K_{0}^{B}(s,y)\mathbf{v}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\right).

The term in the parenthesis is exactly Baik’s vector

, leading to the simple relation,

(7.11)

\bm{r}(y)=w^{(1)}(y)\mathbf{w}(y).

Therefore, Theorem
3.3
becomes

(7.12)

\displaystyle K(x,y)-K_{0}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\bm{r}(y)^{\top}\bm{M}_{22}^{-1}\bm{p}_{2}(x)=(w^{(1)}(y)\mathbf{w}(y))^{\top}B^{-1}\mathbf{t}(x)=w^{(1)}(y)\mathbf{w}(y)^{\top}B^{-1}\mathbf{t}(x).

Using (
7.5
), we recovered Baik’s formula,

(7.13)

\left(K^{B}(x,y)-K^{B}_{0}(x,y)\right)e^{V(x)}=\mathbf{w}(y)^{\top}B^{-1}\mathbf{t}(x).

7.3.
Recovery of Zinn-Justin’s formula

The same mixed-moment formalism also contains the finite-
N
external-source
kernel of Zinn-Justin
[
43
]
, up to a scalar which does not affect the correlation determinant.
Since this work appeared before the aforementioned work
of Bleher and Kuijlaars
[
11
]
, the kernel was not expressed in terms of
multiple orthogonal polynomials.

Let us introduce this kernel and show its correlation determinant agrees with that of
K(x,y)
given by (
2.7
). Write

W_{N}(x):=e^{-NV(x)}

and consider an external source

A=\operatorname{diag}(a_{0},a_{1},\cdots,a_{N-1}).

Let
p_{k}
be the monic orthogonal polynomials associated with

W_{N}(x)
, and define

(7.14)

F_{k}(x)=h_{k}^{-1/2}p_{k}(x)W_{N}(x)^{1/2},\quad G_{\ell}(x)=e^{Na_{\ell}x}W_{N}(x)^{1/2},

where

h_{k}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{k}(x)^{2}W_{N}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

Let
\bm{\alpha}
denote Zinn-Justin’s inverse moment matrix, namely

(7.15)

\bm{\alpha}=\bm{E}^{-1},\quad E_{\ell k}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}G_{\ell}(x)F_{k}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x,\quad 0\leq\ell,k\leq N-1.

Zinn-Justin’s kernel is then expressed in terms of the external source data
and the ordinary orthogonal polynomials as

(7.16)

K_{\mathrm{ZJ}}(x,y)=\sum_{k,\ell=0}^{N-1}F_{k}(x)\alpha_{k\ell}G_{\ell}(y).

We now compare this kernel with ours. After replacing
V(x)
by

V(x)-a_{0}x
and replacing
a_{j}
by
a_{j}-a_{0}
, we may assume
a_{0}=0
.
If we take

(7.17)

\vec{n}=(1,1,\cdots,1)\in\mathbb{N}^{N},\quad w^{(j+1)}(x)=e^{Na_{j}x}W_{N}(x),\quad j=0,\cdots,N-1,

then, recalling (
2.10
), the vector
\bm{q}(x)
in our
mixed-moment construction is

\bm{q}(x)=\bigl(W_{N}(x),e^{Na_{1}x}W_{N}(x),\cdots,e^{Na_{N-1}x}W_{N}(x)\bigr)^{\top}.

Thus

M_{k\ell}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{k}(s)e^{Na_{\ell}s}W_{N}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s,\quad k,\ell=0,\cdots,N-1.

We have
E_{\ell k}=h_{k}^{-1/2}M_{k\ell}
and Therefore,

\bm{E}=\bm{M}^{\top}\bm{H}^{-1/2},\mbox{ \ and thus \ }\bm{\alpha}=\bm{H}^{1/2}\bm{M}^{-\top}.

Substituting this relation into Zinn-Justin’s formula (
7.16
), we get

(7.18)

K_{\mathrm{ZJ}}(x,y)=W_{N}(x)^{1/2}W_{N}(y)^{1/2}\sum_{k,\ell=0}^{N-1}p_{k}(x)\left(\bm{M}^{-\top}\right)_{k\ell}e^{Na_{\ell}y}.

Now let us focus on the kernel

(
2.7
) corresponding to the multi-index and weights given by (

).
Recall Lemma
4.1
where we obtain

K(x,y)=\bm{q}(y)^{\top}\bm{M}^{-1}\bm{p}(x).

Since

\bm{q}(y)^{\top}=W_{N}(y)\bigl(1,e^{Na_{1}y},\cdots,e^{Na_{N-1}y}\bigr),

the kernel can be written as

K(x,y)=W_{N}(y)\sum_{k,\ell=0}^{N-1}p_{k}(x)(\bm{M}^{-\top})_{k\ell}e^{Na_{\ell}y}.

Comparing this with (

) gives

Consequently, for every

because the scalar factors cancel between rows and columns.
