ABSTRACT
This report documents independent testing and validation, by Sandia National Laboratories, of a
supercapacitor technology developed by Capybara Energy. This technology is built with Electric
Double Layer Capacitor cells that use activated carbon as electrodes, which are immersed in Sodium
Sulfate electrolyte. A laboratory-scale prototype of this supercapacitor energy storage system
was subjected to comprehensive testing and evaluated against design specifications for potential
application in multi-hour grid services and providing auxiliary power. The key performance
metrics benchmarked through the experiments are Coulombic efficiency, round trip efficiency,
self-discharge, degradation, and self-healing of a standard module. In addition, system integration
requirements were optimized through this evaluation.
Energy storage technologies that can perform as alternatives to Lithium-based systems are being
actively investigated for de-risking the supply chain, alongside their potential advantages in safety,
multi-hour discharge, and low degradation. The supercapacitor technology presented in this report
diversifies storage chemistry beyond Lithium, has potential for multi-hour operation with low
degradation, and uses electrodes derived from abundantly available waste feedstock.
Performance of a single cell does not capture all the dynamics of a complete energy storage system
that comprises modules of multiple cells in series-parallel combination, various power electronic
converters, battery management system, energy management system, communication protocol,
protection circuits, and connection architecture. Commercially available system integration com-
ponents for batteries are designed for Lithium-based technologies. Retrofitting such components
with non-Lithium storage systems renders sub-optimal performance, which may increase degrada-
tion and operating cost, while delaying field deployment. Hence, it is crucial to understand system
integration needs at laboratory-scale, and develop a modular and scalable platform for large scale
field deployments.
In this work, a 50Wh system was designed with 32 cells of the Capybara Energy supercapacitor
technology. The fully integrated system was subjected to cycling at different C-rates, number of
cycles, and rest periods to evaluate Coulombic efficiency, round trip efficiency, self-discharge, and
degradation.

1. INTRODUCTION

The power grid accommodates a diverse range of loads and services with widely varying ramp-
rate, number of cycles, power, and energy requirements. An energy storage technology can be
grid deployable only if it supports these different grid services reliably, efficiently, safely, and
cost-effectively. A single storage technology does not possess all these attributes and tradeoffs are
generally made. Lithium-ion remains the predominant energy storage technology deployed across
a broad spectrum of grid applications. Table 1-1 shows that grid services that require a high depth
of discharge cause faster degradation in Lithium technologies [1]. Capacity fade resulting from
cycle aging reduces the usable lifetime of the system, necessitating oversizing, replacement, or
augmentation, thereby increasing the levelized cost of storage.

 Application Cycling Degradation
 Frequency regulation Shallow Low
 Peak shaving Deep High
 Energy arbitrage Deep Very High
 Backup power Rare Very Low

In addition, high-rate charge/discharge cycling accelerates electrochemical degradation and elec-
trode microstructural deterioration, leading to a reduction in battery state of health. The resulting
degradation lowers the thermal stability of lithium-ion cells, decreases the energy required to initiate
thermal runaway, and increases the rate of thermal runaway propagation within battery modules [2].
Thermal runaway remains a critical safety concern, particularly for large-scale battery installations,
requiring sophisticated battery management systems equipped with thermal management, and fire
protection measures [3].
Furthermore, the supply chain for lithium-ion batteries depends on critical minerals such as lithium,
cobalt, and nickel, whose geographically concentrated production, price volatility, and environmen-
tal impacts have raised concerns regarding long-term resource security and sustainable large-scale
deployment [4].
Some grid services, such as T&D upgrade deferral, time shifting of intermittent resources, electric
supply capacity, and intraday grid support require multi-hour operation. Using energy storage for
such applications will improve the return-on-investment for these large-scale installations that incur
considerable capital and operating expenses. Currently, BESS deployed with lithium technologies
cannot operate for a long duration. However, hybridizing Lithium batteries with technologies such
as supercapacitors enables power and energy demands to be distributed according to the strengths
of each technology [5, 6, 7].

All these aforementioned factors have motivated research into alternative energy storage technolo-
gies based on more earth-abundant materials that can provide improved lifetime, enhanced safety,
and lower lifecycle costs for specific grid applications.

anism: electric double layer capacitors (EDLCs), pseudocapacitors, and hybrid supercapacitors as
shown in Fig. 1-1 [8, 9, 10].

A traditional capacitor consists of two conductive electrodes separated by a solid dielectric material,
which stores energy through electrostatic polarization. In contrast, an EDLC employs two high-
surface-area porous electrodes immersed in an electrolyte and separated by a porous, ion-permeable
separator. EDLCs store energy through the electrostatic adsorption of electrolyte ions at the
electrode–electrolyte interface without involving Faradaic reactions. This enables high power
density, rate capability, and cycle life. Electrodes are typically comprised of high-surface-area
carbon materials such as activated carbon, carbon nanotubes, and graphene.
Pseudocapacitors store charge through fast and reversible surface or near-surface Faradaic redox
reactions, enabling higher specific capacitance and energy density. Common pseudocapacitive
electrode materials include transition metal oxides (e.g., 𝑅𝑢𝑂 2 and 𝑀𝑛𝑂 2 ) and conducting polymers
such as polyaniline and polypyrrole.
Hybrid or asymmetric supercapacitors combine an EDLC electrode made of carbonaceous materials
at the cathode with a Faradic mode of charge storage using transition metals (carbides and oxides)
at the anode, in aqueous electrolytes. Thus, a hybrid supercapacitor can simultaneously utilize
the high power capability of EDLCs and the higher energy density of Faradaic materials, thereby
improving the overall electrochemical performance.
Conventional EDLCs demonstrate pseudo-infinite cycle life (typically over 1 million cycles) among
the three supercapacitor technologies and have a simple design that enables cost effective imple-
mentation. These are widely recognized for their exceptional power capability and rapid charge-
discharge response. However, their low energy density (< 8 Wh/kg) and high self-discharge char-
acteristics have limited their application to short-duration operations, such as, frequency regulation
and power quality. These limitations can be improved by innovative designs in system architecture
and electrolyte composition. Capybara Energy has designed a large format aqueous technology

 18

that utilizes the advantages of the electric double layer architecture to extend charge/discharge
duration from ~0.2 to >100 hours. This technology utilizes thick carbon electrodes derived from
low-cost waste feedstock, an aqueous sulfate electrolyte, and a modular prismatic cell architecture.
This approach enables economically scalable manufacturing, while eliminating critical minerals
and flammable organic solvents [11, 12]. Precursor materials include agricultural residues, forestry
byproducts, paper waste, and biosolids, which enable flexible raw material sourcing while removing
dependence on geographically constrained critical minerals.
Single cell cycling data cannot accurately predict the performance and degradation of a battery
module, which may be connected in different series-parallel combinations [13] and connection
architecture [14, 15, 16]. Therefore, cycling a standard prototype module prior to field demon-
stration is essential for validating the integration of balance-of-system (BOS) components and
characterizing their coupled interactions within the overall system architecture.
This report characterizes the performance of a 50 Wh EDLC-based energy storage module, com-
prising 32 cells in series, by subjecting the system to various operating conditions. Furthermore, this
process facilitated the standardization of BoS components and associated maintenance procedures.
The objective of this evaluation was to determine whether an electric double layer charge storage
system could fundamentally achieve performance typically associated with battery technologies
while preserving the defining characteristics of conventional EDLCs.

2.0.1. Cell Technology

Capybara Energy’s technology is a large-format aqueous EDLC that stores energy through the
reversible electrostatic adsorption of ions at the electrode-electrolyte interface. The cell utilizes
porous activated carbon electrodes and a neutral-pH aqueous sodium sulfate electrolyte. During
charging, sodium cations adsorb onto the negatively polarized electrode while sulfate anions adsorb
onto the positively polarized electrode, forming electric double layers at each electrode surface.
During discharge, these ions desorb and return to the electrolyte. Unlike battery systems that rely on
Faradaic reactions, phase transformations, or ion insertion into electrode materials, charge storage
within the Capybara Energy platform occurs through a fundamentally physical process.

Because the charge storage mechanism is electrostatic rather than electrochemical, the cell avoids
many of the degradation pathways that limit conventional battery technologies, including repeated
structural changes within the electrode materials and electrolyte decomposition under normal op-
erating conditions. The aqueous electrolyte is nonflammable, noncorrosive, and composed of
nontoxic constituents, eliminating many of the safety concerns associated with flammable organic
electrolyte systems or alkaline/acidic-pH aqueous systems. Collectively, these characteristics pro-
vide a foundation for exceptionally long cycle life, high operational reliability, and an inherently
safe electrical storage platform suitable for stationary energy storage applications.

A distinguishing characteristic of the Capybara Energy architecture is its ability to provide substan-
tial operational flexibility while preserving the intrinsic advantages of EDLC technology. A system
can accommodate discharge durations ranging from 20 minutes to more than 100 hours, enabling
a single system to respond to second-by-second grid disturbances, hourly load fluctuations, and
multi-day energy shifting without sacrificing the long cycle life, operational safety, or low-cost
material composition. Higher power operation results primarily in increased resistive losses and a
corresponding reduction in round-trip efficiency, while the fundamental safety profile and expected
cycle life remain unchanged. Consequently, system sizing is driven principally by the power and
energy requirements of the application rather than competing degradation mechanisms associated
with different operating regimes.

In addition to its electrochemical advantages, the platform is designed around abundant, domes-
tically available materials. The activated carbon electrodes can be produced from a variety of
waste-derived feedstocks, including agricultural residues, forestry byproducts, paper waste, and
biosolids, providing flexibility in raw material sourcing while reducing dependence on critical
mineral supply chains. Combined with an aqueous sulfate electrolyte and other readily avail-
able materials, this approach supports a resilient domestic supply chain and aligns with broader
objectives for secure, scalable, and affordable energy storage manufacturing.

whereas the Round Trip Efficiency varied from 82% to 50% for C-rates ranging from C/4 to 2C,

4. CONCLUSION

Sandia National Laboratories’ evaluation of the Capybara Energy’s supercapacitor technology
validates that their aqueous EDLC architecture can deliver near-perfect Coulombic efficiency, and
intrinsic safety, while extending discharge durations into the multi-hour regime needed for grid
support. The 50Wh prototype evaluated for this report is entirely sourced from a sustainable
material base that mitigates critical-mineral dependencies. The core electrochemical performance
of the technology shows potential for multi-hour storage without any degradation, and the use
of a benign non-flammable electrolyte requires reduced system integration components, which in
turn improves robustness of a complete system. Roundtrip Efficiency losses at higher C-rates are
primarily resistive and are expected to diminish with scaling up to larger cell formats. Overall, the
results from lab-scale testing establish a solid foundation for scaling-up and field demonstration,
positioning Capybara Energy’s aqueous supercapacitor system as a promising candidate for safe,
durable, and cost-effective stationary energy storage in an evolving power grid.
