 Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

 This project represents a continuation of the collaboration between iProTech and NLR to
 simulate, optimize and design the iProTech Pitching Inertial Pump (PIP) device. The objectives
 of this TEAMER project are twofold:

 1. Refining the physical characteristics of the existing iProTech PIP WEC-Sim model to
 enhance the model’s fidelity and include controllable components.

 Key model enhancements target the inclusion of Coulomb friction, the introduction of a
 controllable bypass valve, and the replacement of traditional check valves with advanced
 motorized ones.

 2. Exploring traditional and advanced control algorithms.

 From traditional methods like latching control to cutting-edge reinforcement learning
 (RL) algorithms, the goal is to ensure the PIP device's adaptability and optimal
 performance across a range of ocean conditions.

 NLR is tasked with augmenting the WEC-Sim model and implementing the control algorithms,
 culminating in performance comparison analyses. iProTech will update their existing 3D models,
 advise on model improvements, and determine crucial system metrics.

 WEC-Sim, developed in MATLAB/SIMULINK with Simscape Multibody, is the main piece of
 software that will be used in this project. Coupled with the MATLAB RL Toolbox, it offers a
 robust platform for in-depth simulation and optimization of the iProTech PIP device.

 Building on previous work to explore the PIP design space and optimize its geometry, mass
 distribution, center of gravity and other key parameters, this project aims to refine iProTech’s
 existing numerical models and develop effective control algorithms that can seamlessly integrate
 into their future hardware testing campaigns.

 Issues with the models developed in RFTS-1

 The RFTS-1 phase developed two types of models:

 1. A simplified PTO model, where the internal water coil was always treated as a rigid body
 interacting with the outer hull with a rotary spring and damper.
 2. A hydraulic PTO model, where the internal water coil was modeled as a fluid or as a rigid
 body coupled with a hydraulic circuit composed of a low-pressure accumulator and a
 high-pressure accumulator.

 The simplified PTO model was used in the later phase, RFTS-6, and the hydraulic PTO model
 and the flaws therein were not discovered until RFTS-10 started. When the RFTS-10 phase
 started, the TSR pointed out that there may have been issues with the models developed during
 the RFTS-1 phase. Specifically, the TSR noted that the power generated by the hydraulic PTO
 model was higher than expected and on par with the simplified PTO model. This outcome would
 be wrong because the hydraulic PTO model should have inherent power losses, as the power it
 generated used a hydraulic circuit and not a rigid and idealized spring damper. The TSR
 suggested that the hydraulic PTO model should incorporate the relevant pipe frictions to account
 for the discrepancy. Part of the team investigated this lead, but after modeling the pipe frictions,
 it was unclear how pipe friction alone could explain almost 2 times the expected power the
 RFTS-1 models were making.

 Following this discovery, the multibody dynamics of the model itself were revisited. Figure 1
 shows an overview of the WEC-Sim model developed during RFTS-1 for the hydraulic PTO
 model.

 5

 Figure 1 An overview of the role of the different subsystems of the RFTS-1 hydraulic PTO model.

 WEC-Sim is based on the Simscape multibody solver that uses “Joints” blocks to articulate
 dynamics relationships. These joints can be further acted upon by external forces/moments to
 simulate the effects of actuators in the system. The fundamental challenge in modeling this
 device is to model the internal water coil as a rigid body when its pressure is below a
 prespecified “cracking pressure,” 𝑃𝑐𝑟𝑎𝑐𝑘 , and then as a fluid when the valves open and flow of
 the internal water coil is permitted. In other words, the model should be able to arrest the motion
 of the internal water coil such that its velocity is the same as the overall hull when the fluid
 pressure is below the threshold 𝑃𝑐𝑟𝑎𝑐𝑘 , or it should effectively lock the revolute joint between the
 hull and the water coil while allowing the revolute joint to operate as a revolute joint when the
 water coil allows flow. On closer inspection, it was observed that the RFTS-1 model tried to
 achieve the above by imposing an external torque on the revolute joint based on the dynamical
 motion of the coil. It can be speculated that this was an attempt to cancel out the revolute joint
 internal mechanics, effectively “latching” the water coil. However, this in turn imposes an extra
 moment and perhaps explains the 2-times the expected power from the RFTS-1 hydraulic PTO
 model. Equation 1 shows a simplified representation of the external torque calculations in the
 RFTS-1 hydraulic PTO model as implemented within the PTO-Sim block, shown in Figure 2.

 In the RFTS-1 model, when the inertia contributions were removed, the power generated was
 approximately halved, and the results were therefore somewhat reasonable. However, the
 parameters of the physical components were not based on specific off-the-shelf or carefully
 engineered components. The pressure of the water when flow was allowed was based on the flow
 rates, which were based on arbitrary accumulator sizes. The overall model performance remained
 sensitive to the accumulator sizes—with volumes set at hundreds of cubic meters. For
 perspective, the accumulators for the hydraulic systems used to operate ports and piers
 infrastructure are less than 5 m3.

 To summarize, the major flaws in the RFTS-1 hydraulic PTO model were as follows:

 1. Counting the inertial moments twice—once by Simscape Multibody and then by the model.
 2. Not modeling the latching and de-latching modes of the internal water coil.

 Ensuring the numerical stability of the model

 The numerical stability of the model was an ongoing challenge, as it introduced ambiguity about
 whether the modeling issues were caused by erroneous implementation or the complexity of the
 problem being solved. Figure 3 shows the WEC-Sim solver configuration used to model the
 RFTS-10 models. This enabled increasing the model sampling time, from requiring 10−4 s to a
 sampling rate of 10−1 s.

 8

 Figure 3 WEC-Sim solver configuration used for the RFTS-10 models.

 Modeling the revolute joint to have the ability to latch and de-latch

 The inability of the revolute joint to be able to latch and de-latch was a shortcoming of the
 previous modeling attempts. In models from RFTS-1, the water coil was considered to be a rigid
 body for the purposes of multibody dynamics; an inertial moment was exerted on the water coil
 (using conservation of angular momentum) at all time steps, which was an incorrect modeling
 approach that resulted in substantially high moments measured at the PTO joint, which in turn
 resulted in inflated PTO torques and associated power captured. The new approach used in
 RFTS-10 utilizes a customized WEC-Sim PTO block where the revolute joint block is modified
 to have an external Boolean trigger that can lock/unlock the revolute joint. This enabled
 effectively opening/closing the valves and flow control in the model such that the water coil
 could be modeled as a fluid when the flow was unlatched and as a rigid body when the flow was
 latched. Figure 4 shows the customized WEC-Sim model developed for this application. The
 state of the variable valve_logic changes during the model run-time. This feature enables the
 model to allow or restrict the motion of the inner water coil. The user has the choice to run the
 model in “manual” mode such that the revolute joint is always latched/de-latched or in
 “automatic” mode, where the states calculated during run-time change the revolute joint from
 latched/de-latched depending on the model state.

 9

 Figure 4 A view inside the WEC-Sim Revolute PTO block that allows external torques. By enabling
 the “Mode,” the revolute joint can be latched or de-latched.
 Note, the latching/de-latching of the revolute joint does not introduce numerical instabilities in
 the overall model; however, if the switching happens such that there is a significant difference
 (> 106 Nm) in the external torque to the revolute joint, the revolute joint block has significant
 solver issues, and the entire model suffers numerical breakdown. Some strategies such as using
 tan h(𝑦) to smooth the abrupt state changes—as suggested by the TSR—can help alleviate some
 of the numerical sensitivity. Overall, it is important when and how often these switches are
 performed for the overall model’s stability and accuracy. The accuracy of the model could be
 affected if the simulation is run for very long durations such as more than 2,000 s, as the
 numerical integration error will accumulate, especially because the model can never truly
 achieve a steady state due to continuous latching/de-latching of the internal water coil.
 Anecdotally, however, the time series is periodic for simulations of up to ~4,000 s.

 Calculation of the external torque due water coil

 The two options explored to calculate the external torque from the water coil were to use:

 1. The flow rates, such that the torques were 𝜏𝑣𝑎𝑙𝑣𝑒 = (𝑄𝐴 + 𝑄𝐵 )𝜌𝑅𝑐𝑜𝑖𝑙 , as shown in Figure 7.

 Figure 7 Calculation of external torque from the water coil treated as a fluid bulk.

 2. The pressures calculated for ports A and B, such that the bulk of the fluid is considered a
 rigid body exerting a torque. In the implementation shown in Figure 8, the torques exerted on
 the revolute joint are proportional to the pressures developed in the water coil, and the
 constant of proportionality is 𝐾 = 10−4 such that 𝜏𝑣𝑎𝑙𝑣𝑒 = 𝐾(𝑃𝐴 − 𝑃𝐵 )𝐴𝑐𝑜𝑖𝑙 𝑅𝑐𝑜𝑖𝑙 . The
 proportionality constant ensures the numerical stability of the model by not imposing torques
 so high that the numerical stability of the model is compromised.

 12

 Figure 8 Calculation of the external torque on the PTO using the pressures generated in the water
 coil.

 For the final numerical models, the second approach, i.e., the pressure-based approach, was used
 because there was inadequate confidence in the models for the accumulators, and the flow rates
 predicted by the model were too high.

 Using the second approach ensured a direct coupling from the angular momentum of the water
 coil body to the corresponding pressure calculations to the corresponding torque such that a
 swirling water coil would impose on the multibody dynamics of the system. Furthermore, the
 flow-based torque will be subject to flow losses; however, from a dynamic’s standpoint, the
 pressure method will capture all of the dynamic effects that the dynamical coupling between the
 hull and the inner water coil impose on each other. In other words, the additional torque
 experienced by the hull and water coil dynamic coupling will be proportional to the pressure
 developed within the water coil, whether the revolute joint of the PTO is latched or unlatched.
 Additional torque on the revolute joint will be experienced in the hull and water coil coupling,
 even if the flow was arrested in the latched mode.

 Introducing a hydraulic motor and bypass valve with basic PID controls

 The controls for the hydraulic motor and the bypass valve were implemented using the
 MATLAB-built off-the-shelf blocks. While a deeper control design exploration was the original
 objective of this project, as explained in other places in this document, the time and funds ran out
 due to issues with existing models and the limited information on the physical components of the
 device.

 In the implementation shown in Figure 9, a PID block has been added to the pressure differences
 of the water coil and accumulators. The variables throttle_control and FlowRate_CD represent
 the control action on the flow, effectively modulating the flow rate of the different ports.
 Additionally, a sinusoidal function represents a simple hydraulic motor that draws flow from the
 accumulators to create hydraulic power. This was a rudimentary stand-in for what would have
 been a control co-design of the hydraulic circuit.

 Pressure calculation of internal water coil

 The power extraction by the device is supposed to happen when the water coil has built up
 enough pressure to exceed a cracking pressure such that the water coil is de-latched and can flow
 through the overall hydraulic circuit. Figure 10 shows the three main components of the PTO
 assembly: (1) water coil, (2) hydraulic circuit, and (3) high-pressure and low-pressure
 accumulators.

 Figure 10 A high-level representation of the main components being modeled in the hydraulic PTO
 block. The water coil as it pitches is supposed to create additional pressure, supplying its flow to
 accumulators.
 Overall, for pressure calculations, three different approaches were explored, based on whether
 the water coil is considered a fluid-bulk or a rigid body:

 1. Assuming a fluid-bulk, the pressure calculations are made using flow rates and bulk
 modulus.
 2. Assuming a rigid body, pressures are calculated by considering the internal water coil to
 be a rigid body and using the torque that a rigid body (water coil) would experience and
 dividing it by the coil radius and the area of aperture.
 3. Assuming a fluid-bulk, imposing mass-flow conservation, energy conservation, i.e., using
 Bernoulli’s equation to convert the kinetic energy of the water coil to the pressure.

 accurate, which meant the pressures and flow rates associated with the accumulators had a
 downstream effect on the calculations of the water coil pressure calculations as well.

 Note, the RFTS-1 models used a very similar approach (see Figure 2), where these forces were
 calculated under the Flux Force subsystem block. However, in addition to the flux forces, the
 RFTS-1 models added additional contributions from inertia forces, which resulted in much
 higher inertial forces (as they were already being calculated by Simscape), while still not being
 able to have any effective way of latching and de-latching the flow. The friction force
 calculations were explored in detail during this project and will be discussed. However, when it
 comes to pressure calculations, the canonical WEC-Sim block was not used, and two other
 approaches were explored.

 Results and Discussion

 As mentioned in Section 3, the two important requirements/expectations that the numerical model
 was able to satisfy were:

 1. The capability to latch and de-latch the inner water coil during the run-time of the model
 based on the state of the pressures in the hydraulic circuit.
 2. Calculate the pressures developed within the water coil as the device pitches in response
 to the incoming waves.

 Implement the latching and de-latching of the inner water coil

 The final model iteration can latch and de-latch the inner water coil based on the pressure states
 of the hydraulic circuit during the simulation run-time. Figure 16 shows the pitch velocities of
 the inner water coil, the outer hull “duck” velocity, and the valve_logic that controls the latching
 and de-latching of the water coil. The Boolean 1 corresponds to the latching condition, and the
 Boolean 0 corresponds to the de-latched condition, or stated simply, the valve_logic Boolean
 indicates the latching on or off. When the hull and the water coil are latched, they have the same
 pitch velocity, whereas in the de-latched state, the inner water coil velocity slows down, i.e., the
 water coil uncouples as it experiences reduced forces.

 (watercoil_vel).

 General results

 Figure 18 shows the general results from the WEC-Sim model. It can be observed that the model
 has the capability to latch and de-latch the inner water coil. It can be further observed that the
 overall average power generated by the model is in the range of ≈40–60 kW (bottom-right plot in
 Figure 18), and the torque on the joints ranges from ≈ 1 × 106 to 1 × 107Nm (bottom-left plot
 in Figure 18)—similar ranges were predicted by linear models and the initial calculations by the
 TSR. Furthermore, by calculating the flow dynamic pressures using Bernoulli’s equation, the
 pressures in the water coil are in phase with the relative velocity. This is true for pressures in
 flows and is the basis for aeronautics and nautical engineering applications. Intuitively, this
 phasing suggests that the pressure in the water coil is generated from the energy of the flow of
 the inner fluid-bulk as the system is excited by the incoming waves. The top-right plot of Figure
 18 shows the flow rates in the simulation. These flow rates are sensitive to the accumulator
 parameters.

 Test-plan deviations and lessons learned

 The work deviated from the initial plan because of a misunderstanding of the existing numerical
 models of the device. The existing models of the device had some significant deficiencies that
 resulted in this project trying to first fix and then create new models from scratch for the work
 done in RFTS-1. The device concept also suffers from ambiguity in some important aspects of its
 operational principles, which may have caused flaws in the models developed in RFTS-1.
 Furthermore, the project was delayed for approximately 3 months due to personnel issues caused
 by the departure of a senior researcher in January 2024. This resulted in the PI for the project
 being reallocated to several time-sensitive merit reviews for some of the key projects in the NLR
 portfolio. These delays and reasons were communicated to the TSR, and they showed flexibility
 in the matter.

 24

 For further context, the TSR was awarded two previous awards:

 1. Creating a numerical model of the device in WEC-Sim (RFTS-1).
 2. Numerical optimization of the device’s hull to improve its hydrodynamic performance
 (RFTS-6).

 The objective of this phase (RFTS-10) was supposed to be the implementation of advanced
 control laws for the device. However, on close inspection of the older model, some significant
 issues with the old model were observed. Specifically, the device required locking and unlocking
 of the internal water coil, and the older model tried to achieve this by adding angular torque
 calculations within the PTO-Sim block and used it as an additional torque exerted on the system.
 This meant the dynamic torque was being counted twice, once by the Simscape solver and then
 again analytically within the PTO-Sim block. This resulted in much higher power outputs, about
 roughly twice the amount of what the newer models were calculating. This was recognized by
 the TSR. Furthermore, the initial intention was to fix already existing models but creating new
 models from scratch was necessary because the models in RFTS-1 used WEC-Sim v4.0, and
 significant refactoring was done in WEC-Sim in the most recent versions, i.e., v6.1.

 While it is clear that the water coil as it pitches with the hull will impart an angular momentum
 to the internal fluid, it is unclear how the device will perform on a wave-to-wave basis. The
 working principle is analogous to an externally excited two-pendulum system, which implies that
 the working principle and the pertinent models show indications of an inherently chaotic system.
 An important challenge for numerical modeling was a lack of details on the physical parameters
 of the constituents of the hydraulic circuit—especially the accumulator parameters. Even a
 realistic range would have helped set expectations and numerical results.
