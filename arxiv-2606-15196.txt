Abstract
. We consider highness in the context of computable structure theory and, particularly, the Scott rank of a structure. We define highness for Scott rank and highness for computably defined Scott rank α \alpha and characterize them in terms of the ability to compute Δ β 0 \Delta^{0}_{\beta} sets for appropriate β \beta . We close with a discussion of the index sets of structures with a given Scott rank or computably defined Scott rank and a few words about highness for noncomputable Scott ranks.

Highness allows us to describe an oracle as maximally powerful in some context. The notion was first introduced by Soare in
[
Soa72
]
in the context of degree theory; here, the context was that of the Turing jump and the
\Delta^{0}_{2}
degrees. Since then, highness has been considered in the context of randomness
[
FSY11
]
and computable structure theory
[
CFT23
,
CFT25
]
. In this paper, we study highness not in the general context of computable structure theory but in that of structures of particular Scott ranks.

We refer the reader to the text by Ash and Knight
[
AK00
]
for background on both effective and noneffective formula complexity.

We will use the following definition of Scott rank due to Montalbán
[
Mon15
]
.

Definition 1
.

A structure
\mathcal{A}
has
Scott rank
\alpha
for
\alpha<\omega_{1}
if
\alpha
is least so that the automorphism orbit of every tuple in
\mathcal{A}
is
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\alpha}
-definable.
It has
computably defined Scott rank
\alpha
if
\alpha
is least so that every automorphism orbit is
\Sigma^{\mathrm{c}}_{\beta}
-definable for
\beta\leq\alpha
.

A computable structure
\mathcal{A}
has
computable Scott rank
if it has Scott rank
\alpha<\omega_{1}^{\mathrm{ck}}
.

Remark
.

Suppose
\mathcal{A}
has Scott rank
\lambda
, where
\lambda
is a limit ordinal. If
\bar{a}\in\mathcal{A}
, then by definition
\bar{a}
’s orbit is definable by a
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\lambda}
formula. But a
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\lambda}
formula is a disjunction of formulas which are each
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\beta}
for some
\beta<\lambda
. So by passing to the disjunct satisfied by
\bar{a}
, we may assume that the formula is
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\beta}
for some
\beta<\lambda
.
The same reasoning applies for computably defined Scott rank
\lambda
.

Note that by this remark there are structures of computably defined Scott rank
\omega_{1}^{\mathrm{ck}}
even though there are no
\Sigma^{\mathrm{c}}_{\omega_{1}^{\mathrm{ck}}}
-formulas. In fact, we will see (
Theorem

17
) that every computable structure with Scott rank
\omega_{1}^{\mathrm{ck}}
has computably defined Scott rank
\omega_{1}^{\mathrm{ck}}
.

Now we can define highness in this context. Calvert, Franklin, and Turetsky defined a set to be
high for isomorphism
if it can compute an isomorphism between any two isomorphic computably presented structures
[
CFT23
]
. In this case, we define highness for structures of a particular Scott rank.

Definition 2
.

Let
\mathbf{d}
be a Turing degree and
\alpha<\omega_{1}
.

(1)

\mathbf{d}
is
high for Scott rank
\alpha
if for every computable structure
\mathcal{A}
of Scott rank
\alpha
and for all computable
\mathcal{B}\cong\mathcal{A}
,
\mathbf{d}
computes an isomorphism
\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{A}
.

(2)

\mathbf{d}
is
high for computably defined Scott rank
\alpha
if for every structure
\mathcal{A}
of computably defined Scott rank
\alpha
and for all computable
\mathcal{B}\cong\mathcal{A}
,
\mathbf{d}
computes an isomorphism
\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{A}
.

Suppose
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
are computable structures of Scott rank at most
\alpha
, and
\mathcal{C}
is any computable structure of Scott rank
\alpha
. Then if
\mathcal{A}\oplus\mathcal{C}
is the disjoint union, correctly defined, it has Scott rank exactly
\alpha
. Further, any isomorphism
\mathcal{B}\oplus\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{A}\oplus C
gives an isomorphism
\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{A}
. Thus we could have equivalently defined highness for Scott rank
\alpha
in terms of structures of Scott rank at most
\alpha
, rather than in terms of structures of Scott rank exactly
\alpha
.

It follows that for
\alpha\leq\beta
, highness for Scott rank
\beta
implies highness for Scott rank
\alpha
. The same holds for highness for computably defined Scott rank.

The Scott rank of a structure is a structural measure of complexity, giving upper bounds on the quantifier complexity of definable relations on the structure. Meanwhile, the least Turing degree computing isomorphisms between any two computable copies of a structure—commonly referred to as its
degree of categoricity

[
FKM10
]
—gives a computational bound: If two computable structures
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
are computably isomorphic and
R\subseteq\omega
is a definable relation, then the Turing degrees of
R^{\mathcal{A}}
and
R^{\mathcal{B}}
are the same, and we can say that
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
possess the same computational properties. However, it is well known that this does not hold if
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
are not computably isomorphic. On the other hand, if the degree of categoricity of
\mathcal{A}
is
\mathbf{d}
, then modulo
\mathbf{d}
,
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
are computationally indistinguishable and hence degrees of categoricity provide a computational complexity measure.
Under these considerations, the immediate corollary of our main result that any
\Delta^{0}_{2\alpha+1}
-complete set is high for Scott rank
\alpha
shows that structural simplicity limits computational complexity: No structure of Scott rank
\alpha
can have degree of categoricity greater than
\mathbf{0}^{(2\alpha+1)}
(or, for finite
\alpha
,
\mathbf{0}^{(2\alpha)}
).

1.
Highness for computable Scott ranks

The main goal of this section is to prove the following theorem.

Theorem 1
.

For
0<\alpha<\omega_{1}^{\mathrm{ck}}
,
\mathbf{d}
is high for Scott rank
\alpha
if and only if
\mathbf{d}
computes every
\Delta^{0}_{2\alpha+1}
set.
1
1

1

We are stating the theorem in this fashion to unify the finite and infinite cases. If we were to write the criteria in terms of computing jumps, we would need to write
0^{(2n)}
for
n<\omega

0^{(2\alpha+1)}

The easy direction is taken care of by the following lemma.

Lemma 2

For
0<\alpha<\omega_{1}^{\mathrm{ck}}

\mathbf{d}
computes every
\Delta^{0}_{2\alpha+1}

\mathbf{d}
is high for Scott rank
\alpha

Proof.

Recall from Karp
[
Kar65
]
that
(\mathcal{A},\bar{a})\leq_{\alpha}(\mathcal{B},\bar{b})
if and only if every
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\alpha}
formula true of
(\mathcal{B},\bar{b})
is true of
(\mathcal{A},\bar{a})
. Fix a computable structure
\mathcal{A}
of Scott rank
\alpha
and an isomorphic copy
\mathcal{B}
. Then

B=\{(\bar{a},\bar{b})\colon(\mathcal{A},\bar{a})\leq_{\alpha}(\mathcal{B},\bar{b})\}

is a back-and-forth set for
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
. By a straightforward induction on the back-and-forth relations
\leq_{\alpha}
we have that
B
is
\Delta_{2\alpha+1}^{0}
. Let us quickly illustrate the limit step. For
\alpha
limit, we have that
(\mathcal{A},\bar{a})\leq_{\alpha}(\mathcal{B},\bar{b})
if and only if
(\forall\beta<\alpha)(\mathcal{A},\bar{a})\leq_{\beta}(\mathcal{B},\bar{b})
. This is
\Pi^{0}_{\alpha}
, so any set computing all
\Delta_{2\alpha+1}^{0}=\Delta_{\alpha+1}^{0}
sets can compute it. Furthermore, given
B
, we can compute an isomorphism between
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
using a standard back-and-forth argument.
∎

1.2.
Jump inversions and the successor case

To prove the successor case we will combine two jump inversion techniques with a relativized version of
Lemma

5
. We will use
Lemma

5
relative to
\Delta^{0}_{2\alpha+1}
, building a
\Delta^{0}_{2\alpha+1}
-computable structure
\mathcal{A}
of Scott rank
1
so that every isomorphism between two
\Delta^{0}_{2\alpha+1}
-computable copies must compute every
\Delta^{0}_{2\alpha+3}
set. We then use two different jump inversion, or Marker extension, techniques to obtain the successor step of our induction. Both of these techniques use pairs of structures as pioneered by Ash and Knight, culminating in the material in
[
AK00
]
. For a detailed development of what we call friendly jump inversions, see for example
[
Mon25

[
Har25
]
. The two techniques work similarly for the most part, so let us introduce the general machinery.

Given a graph
\mathcal{H}
and two structures
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}
in vocabulary
\tau

\mathcal{H}^{*}
in the vocabulary

\{V/1,W/3,U/1\}\cup\tau

(1)

V^{\mathcal{H}^{*}}
is infinite and coinfinite,

(2)

\neg V^{\mathcal{H}^{*}}
is partitioned into infinitely many infinite sets

and
W(u,v,-)=S_{u,v}
for each
u,v\in V^{\mathcal{H}^{*}}
.

(3)

U^{\mathcal{H}^{*}}
partitions each
S_{u,v}
into infinite, coinfinite sets.

Then, using
E
to denote the edge relation, we define the substructures

S_{u,v}\cap U^{\mathcal{H}^{*}}\cong\begin{cases}\mathcal{A}&u\mathrel{E}^{\mathcal{H}}v\\
\mathcal{B}&\neg u\mathrel{E}^{\mathcal{H}}v\end{cases}\text{ and }S_{u,v}\cap\neg U^{\mathcal{H}^{*}}\cong\begin{cases}\mathcal{B}&u\mathrel{E}^{\mathcal{H}}v\\
\mathcal{A}&\neg u\mathrel{E}^{\mathcal{H}}v\end{cases},

i.e., the substructure on
W(u,v,-)
is either isomorphic to the pair of structures
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
or

depending of whether there was an edge between

and

in
\mathcal{H}

Lemma 6

Suppose that

Lemma 16
.

Suppose that
\beta<\omega_{1}^{\mathrm{ck}}
and that the automorphism orbit of
\bar{a}\in\mathcal{A}
is
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\beta}
-definable. Then it is
\Sigma^{\mathrm{c}}_{2\beta+1}
-definable.

Proof.

Suppose that
\bar{a}
has
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\beta}
definable automorphism orbit. Then the elements
\bar{b}
such that
\bar{b}\leq_{\beta}\bar{a}
are precisely the elements in
\bar{a}
’s automorphism orbit. So having fixed
\bar{a}
, consider the labeled well-founded tree
T
obtained from the
\beta
-back-and-forth game.

The root node is labeled with
\bar{a}
and the ordinal
\beta
. For readability, if
\nu
is a node on the tree, let
\nu_{i}=\nu\restrict i
. Then, nodes
\nu
at even levels are labeled with the plays of the
\forall
-player, i.e., decreasing ordinals
o(\nu)<\beta
and tuples
t(\nu)
, while nodes at odd levels are labeled with responses of the
\exists
-player, i.e., tuples
t(\nu)
such that

D(t(\nu_{1}),t(\nu_{3}),t(\nu_{5}),t(\nu_{7}),\dots)=D(t(\nu_{0})=\bar{a},t(\nu_{2}),t(\nu_{4}),t(\nu_{6}),\dots).

Here

denotes the atomic diagram of the tuple in
\mathcal{A}
.

Note that
T
has rank
2\beta
.
Now,
\bar{b}\in aut(\bar{a})
if and only if the
\exists
-player has a winning strategy in the
\beta
-back-and-forth game
\bar{b}\leq_{\beta}\bar{a}
if and only if for the unique node
\nu
at level
1
with
t(\nu)=\bar{b}
,
\nu\in\mathrm{WS}_{\beta}
where
\mathrm{WS}_{\beta}
is defined inductively as follows:

\displaystyle\mathrm{WS}_{0}

\displaystyle=\{\nu\in T:\forall x(\nu\concat x\in T\land o(\nu\concat x)=0)\implies\exists y\ \nu\concat x\concat y\in T\}

\displaystyle\mathrm{WS}_{\alpha+1}

\displaystyle=\{\nu\in T:\forall x(\nu\concat x\in T\land o(\nu\concat x)=\alpha)\implies\exists y\ \nu\concat x\concat y\in\mathrm{WS}_{\alpha}\}

\displaystyle\mathrm{WS}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\bigcap_{\gamma<\alpha}\mathrm{WS}_{\gamma}\text{ if $\alpha$ limit}

Intuitively,

is in the set

for some

if whenever at the current game state specified by

\forall
-player plays the ordinal

-player has a winning strategy.
A simple transfinite induction shows that

and that this set is
\Pi^{0}_{2\beta}

[

Theorem 17
.

Suppose
\lambda\leq\omega_{1}^{\mathrm{ck}}
is a limit ordinal. A computable structure
\mathcal{A}
has Scott rank
\lambda
if and only if
\mathcal{A}
has computably defined Scott rank
\lambda
. Furthermore, if
\lambda
is computable, then the equality also holds for
\lambda+1
. Finally, it also holds at 1.

Proof.

For the left-to-right direction, we first dispense with the case for Scott rank 1. Suppose that an automorphism orbit is defined by a
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{1}
formula. By passing to the relevant disjunct, we may assume this formula is finite, and thus
\Sigma^{\mathrm{c}}_{1}
.

Now if
\mathcal{A}
has Scott rank a limit ordinal
\lambda
, then every automorphism orbit is definable by a
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\beta}
formula for some
\beta<\lambda
. By
Lemma

16
it is also definable by a
\Sigma^{\mathrm{c}}_{2\beta+1}
-formula. As
2\beta+1<\lambda
for
\beta<\lambda
,
\mathcal{A}
has computably defined Scott rank
\lambda
.

For the case that
\mathcal{A}
has Scott rank
\lambda+1
, let
\bar{a}
have
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\lambda+1}
definable automorphism orbit. Then without loss of generality there is
\varphi\in\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{\lambda}
such that
\exists\bar{y}\varphi(\bar{x},\bar{y})
defines the automorphism orbit of
\bar{a}
. Pick
\bar{c}
such that
\varphi(\bar{a},\bar{c})
. Then for every
\bar{b}
,
\bar{b}\in aut(\bar{a})
if and only if there is a
\bar{d}
with
\bar{a},\bar{c}\leq_{\lambda}\bar{b},\bar{d}
. Constructing the tree and
\mathrm{WS}_{\lambda}
similar to the tree and
\mathrm{WS}_{\beta}
above, we get that

\bar{b}\in aut(\bar{a})\iff\exists\bar{d}\exists\nu\ t(\nu)=\bar{b}\bar{d}\land|\nu|=1\land\nu\in\mathrm{WS}_{\lambda}.

As argued previously,
\mathrm{WS}_{\lambda}
is
\Pi^{0}_{2\lambda}
, and

since
\lambda
is limit. It follows that

is relatively intrinsically
\Sigma^{0}_{\lambda+1}
and thus
\Sigma^{\mathrm{c}}_{\lambda+1}
-definable.

For the right-to-left direction, if
\mathcal{A}
has computably defined Scott rank
\lambda
, then it has Scott rank at most
\lambda
, so we must only show that it cannot have lower Scott rank. This is trivial for
\lambda=1
.
For the case where
\lambda
is a limit ordinal we prove the contraposition. If
\lambda
is a limit ordinal and
\mathcal{A}
has Scott rank
\beta<\lambda
, then by

16
all orbits are
\Sigma^{\mathrm{c}}_{2\beta+1}
definable. But
2\beta+1<\lambda
and thus
\mathcal{A}
has computably defined Scott rank

. The same argument works for the case where
\mathcal{A}
has computably defined Scott rank

∎

4.
Highness for noncomputable Scott ranks

Here we briefly discuss highness for Scott ranks
\omega_{1}^{\mathrm{ck}}
and
\omega_{1}^{\mathrm{ck}}+1
. By
Lemma

10
a computable structure has Scott rank
\omega_{1}^{\mathrm{ck}}
if and only if it has computably defined Scott rank
\omega_{1}^{\mathrm{ck}}
. On the other hand, structures of computably defined Scott rank
\omega_{1}^{\mathrm{ck}}+1
do not exist, since there are no
\Sigma^{\mathrm{c}}_{\omega_{1}^{\mathrm{ck}}}
or
\Sigma^{\mathrm{c}}_{\omega_{1}^{\mathrm{ck}}+1}
formulas.

As mentioned before, if
\mathbf{d}
is high for Scott rank
\omega_{1}^{\mathrm{ck}}+1
, then
\mathbf{d}
will compute an isomorphism between any two isomorphic computable structures of Scott rank at most
\omega_{1}^{\mathrm{ck}}+1
, i.e., all isomorphic computable structures. Thus highness for Scott rank
\omega_{1}^{\mathrm{ck}}+1
is simply highness for isomorphism as studied in
[
CFT23
]
.

The situation for Scott rank
\omega_{1}^{\mathrm{ck}}
is less clear. Since highness for Scott rank
\omega_{1}^{\mathrm{ck}}
implies highness for Scott rank
\alpha
for every
\alpha<\omega_{1}^{\mathrm{ck}}
, it follows by
Theorem

1
that a degree which is high for Scott rank
\omega_{1}^{\mathrm{ck}}
must compute all
\Delta^{1}_{1}
sets. We will show more.

Theorem 19
.

If
\mathbf{d}
is high for (computably defined) Scott rank
\omega_{1}^{\mathrm{ck}}
, then
\mathbf{d}
computes a descending sequence through any ill-founded computable linear order.

A degree with this property is called
high for descending sequences
in
[
CFT23
]
, where it was observed that such a degree
\mathbf{d}
must have
\mathbf{d}^{(3)}\geq_{T}\mathcal{O}
.

Proof.

Fix an ill-founded computable linear order
\mathcal{L}
. By replacing
\mathcal{L}
with
1+\omega\cdot\mathcal{L}+2
, we may assume that the set of limit points and the successor relation on
\mathcal{L}
are both computable, that
\mathcal{L}
has a distinguished least element, and that
\mathcal{L}
has last elements
a<b
. Note that a descending sequence through
1+\omega\cdot\mathcal{L}+2
computes a descending sequence through
\mathcal{L}
.

We mimic the construction of a thin tree from
[
KM10
]
. First we define an increasing sequence of c.e. sets,
(A_{n})_{n\in\{-1\}\cup\omega}
. Considering each
A_{n}\subseteq\mathcal{L}
, with the induced order, we will maintain the following property: if
x\in A_{n}\setminus A_{n-1}
, then either
x
is the least element of
A_{n}
or
x
has an immediate predecessor in
A_{n}
, and this predecessor relation is uniformly computable. Note that
x
’s predecessor in
A_{n}
need not be the same as
x
’s predecessor in
\mathcal{L}
, and indeed
x
might be a limit point in
\mathcal{L}
. We also maintain that
A_{n}\setminus A_{n-1}
contains an element of the ill-founded part of
\mathcal{L}
.

•

A_{0}=\{a,b\}
and
A_{-1}=\emptyset
. Here
a
is the least element of
A_{0}
and is
b
’s predecessor within
A_{0}
.

•

We enumerate all of
A_{n}
into
A_{n+1}
. Also, for
x\in A_{n}\setminus A_{n-1}
, we may enumerate additional elements depending on
x
’s role in
\mathcal{L}
:

–

If
x
is the least element of
\mathcal{L}
, then we do nothing for
x
.

–

If
x
is the successor of
y
in
\mathcal{L}
, then we enumerate
y
into
A_{n+1}
. If
x
was the least element of
A_{n}
, then
y
is the least element of
A_{n+1}
. If instead
x
had a predecessor
z
in
A_{n}
other than
y
, then
z
is
y
’s predecessor in
A_{n+1}
. (If
y\in A_{n}
, then we will not have
y\in A_{n+1}\setminus A_{n}
, and so we do not need to define its predecessor in
A_{n+1}
.)

–

If
x
is a limit point of
\mathcal{L}
, then we fix a computable increasing sequence
y_{0}<y_{1}<\dots
with limit
x
. If
x
had a predecessor
z
in
A_{n}
, then we require that
z<y_{0}
. For each
i
,
y_{i}
is
y_{i+1}
’s predecessor in
A_{n+1}
. If
z
exists, it is
y_{0}
’s predecessor in
A_{n+1}
; otherwise,
y_{0}
is the least element of
A_{n+1}
.

This completes the construction of the sequence
(A_{n})_{n\in\{-1\}\cup\omega}
. A straightforward induction verifies that our predecessors are correctly defined, that
A_{n}\setminus A_{n-1}
contains an element of the ill-founded part of
\mathcal{L}
, and that the order-type of
A_{n}\subset\mathcal{L}
is an ordinal at most
\omega^{n}\cdot 2
.

Now we form a tree
T
consisting of decreasing sequences through
\mathcal{L}
which meet each
A_{n}
in order, and such that all nodes occur with infinite multiplicity. More formally,
T
consists of sequences
\sigma\in(\mathcal{L}\times\omega)^{<\omega}
satisfying:

•

If
\sigma(n)=(x,k)
, then
x\in A_{n}
; and

•

If
\sigma(n)=(x,k)
and
\sigma(n+1)=(y,m)
, then
y<_{\mathcal{L}}x
.

Note that the purpose of the second coordinate is to give all children infinite multiplicity.

A simple induction on tree rank shows that for
\sigma\in T
non-empty with last element
x
, the tree rank of
\sigma
is the order-type of
\mathcal{L}(<x)
when
x
is in the well-founded part of
\mathcal{L}
, and is
\infty
when
x
is in the ill-founded part of
\mathcal{L}
. Thus
T
is
rank homogeneous
: if
\sigma\in T
with tree rank
\alpha
, and there is some
\tau\in T
with tree rank
\beta<\alpha
and
|\tau|=|\sigma|+1
, then
\sigma
has infinitely many children of tree rank
\beta
.

We make a computable structure out of
T
using a unary function which maps the root to itself, and which maps each non-root to its parent. We define
T_{a}
to be the subtree consisting of elements comparable with
\langle(a,0)\rangle
, and similarly
T_{b}
. Then
T_{a}
and
T_{b}
are isomorphic and have Scott rank at most
\omega_{1}^{\mathrm{ck}}
(Lemmas 3.1 and 3.2 of
[
CKM06
]
, respectively).

Now, suppose
f:T_{b}\to T_{a}
is an isomorphism. Then note that
f
must map the root to the root, and must send
\langle(b,0)\rangle
to
\langle(a,0)\rangle
. Also,
\langle(b,0),(a,0)\rangle\in T_{b}
. Consider now
f(\langle(b,0),(a,0)\rangle)=\langle(a,0),(c,k)\rangle
. It must be that
c<_{\mathcal{L}}a
and
c\in A_{1}\subset A_{2}
. So
\langle(b,0),(a,0),(c,0)\rangle\in T_{b}
. We continue in this fashion, defining a decreasing sequence
c_{0}>_{\mathcal{L}}c_{1}>_{\mathcal{L}}\dots
satisfying:

•

c_{0}=a
;

•

c_{n+1}
is such that
f(\langle(b,0),(c_{0},0),\dots,(c_{n},0)\rangle)=\langle(c_{0},k_{0}),\dots,(c_{n},k_{n}),(c_{n+1},k_{n+1})\rangle
for some
k_{0},\dots,k_{n+1}
.

In this way,
f
computes the sequence
c_{0},c_{1},\dots
, which completes the proof.
∎
