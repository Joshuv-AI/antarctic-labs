Comparison of four commercially available adhesion barriers after open thyroid surgery: a prospective observational study
Authors: Yoon Kong, Ja Kyung Lee, Sukmin Yun, Eunji Kim, Jae Bong Choi, Hyeong Won Yu, June Young Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposePostoperative cervical adhesions after thyroidectomy can lead to neck tightness, dysphagia, and cosmetic dissatisfaction, but the clinical benefits of antiadhesion agents remain unclear. This study compared the efficacy and safety of four commercially available antiadhesion agents applied after open thyroid surgery.MethodsThis singlecenter prospective observational study at a tertiary care hospital included included patients aged 20–70 years undergoing their first open thyroid surgery with intraoperative application of 1 of 4 agents: MegaShield (MS; L&C Bio Co., Ltd.), MegaDerm (MD; L&C Bio Co., Ltd.), CollaBarrier (CB; Dalim Tissen Co., Ltd.), or ACE COL (AC; Korea BNC Co., Ltd. and IreBio Co., Ltd.). Two units were applied at the tracheal bed and subcutaneous layer at the end of surgery. A 7item adhesion questionnaire and the Vancouver Scar Scale (VSS) were assessed at 2 and 12 weeks postoperatively by an independent evaluator. Adverse events were recorded at each visit. Group differences were analyzed using nonparametric tests.ResultsOf 200 enrolled patients, 180 completed followup (MS, 46; MD, 45; CB, 47; and AC, 42). Baseline characteristics were comparable. Adhesion scores increased at 2 weeks and improved by 12 weeks in all groups, with no significant betweengroup differences at any time point. VSS total scores showed a similar pattern, with only a transiently higher pigmentation score at 2 weeks in the MS group; no differences persisted at 12 weeks. Complication rates were low and similar across groups.ConclusionFour different antiadhesion agents demonstrated comparable shortterm efficacy for adhesionrelated symptoms and scar quality after open thyroid surgery, with no clinically meaningful superiority of any single agent. All products were well tolerated.

INTRODUCTION

Postoperative adhesions, a frequent and often underappreciated complication of surgical intervention, can cause chronic discomfort or functional impairment [1]. During thyroid surgery, cervical adhesions commonly manifest as anterior neck tightness, dysphagia, a foreign body sensation in the throat, and restricted neck mobility. Although these symptoms may be perceived as mild, they can persist for months and adversely affect patients’ postoperative quality of life [2].

Anti-adhesion agents mitigate adhesion formation by providing a temporary physical barrier between tissues during the early healing phase, thereby preventing fibrous bridging [3]. The efficacy of several antiadhesion agents has been explored in various surgical fields. A systematic review of barrier materials summarized that gels, membranes, and films comprising hyaluronic acid, oxidized regenerated cellulose, and other biomaterials have been widely studied; however, translational success in achieving consistent clinical benefits remains limited [1]. A recent randomized trial involving patients who underwent thyroid surgery compared oxidized regenerated cellulose and high-molecular-weight hyaluronic acid with no barriers. Therein, modest improvements in selected dysphagia indices were noted; however, the magnitude and durability of clinical benefit from cervical antiadhesion agents were unclear [2]. In head and neck surgery, and more specifically in thyroidectomy, the clinical application of antiadhesion agents remains poorly characterized compared with abdominal or pelvic surgery [124].

Among the commercially available antiadhesion agents used in thyroid surgery, sheet-type acellular dermal matrix (ADM) products, collagen-based agents, and other collagen gels are being increasingly adopted in routine endocrine neck procedures [5678910]. These products have been approved for clinical use, and their basic safety and efficacy have been established in prior studies and post-marketing experience [56711]. However, existing trials have typically compared a single agent with placebo or standard care, providing little information regarding the relative performance of different products that vary in composition, rheological profile, and application characteristics. These mixed findings highlight the lack of consensus and the scarcity of comparative data on multiple antiadhesion agents in the field of thyroid surgery [2567]. Furthermore, several pivotal trials were sponsored by device manufacturers and focused on individual products, underscoring the need for independent studies that reflect the real-world use of multiple agents [2711]. Given the narrow anatomical corridors and sensitivity of neck tissues, balancing the efficacy and safety of antiadhesion agents is essential [2567].

Therefore, this prospective observational study compared the clinical efficacy of 4 different antiadhesion agents—MegaShield (MS; L&C Bio Co., Ltd.), MegaDerm (MD; L&C Bio Co., Ltd.), CollaBarrier (CB; Dalim Tissen Co., Ltd.), and ACE COL (AC; Korea BNC Co., Ltd. and IreBio Co., Ltd.)—applied after open thyroid surgery, by assessing adhesion-related symptoms and scar quality, as well as their safety profiles, with the aim of providing evidence-based guidance for clinicians performing thyroidectomy [256712]. Detailed descriptions of the four antiadhesion agents are provided in Supplementary Material.

METHODS

Ethics statement

All participants provided written informed consent before enrollment; the study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Bundang Hospital (No. B-2308-847-302) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study design

This single-center prospective observational study, conducted at a tertiary care hospital enrolled patients who underwent open thyroid surgery for thyroid diseases. Adult patients aged 20–70 years who were administered an anti-adhesion agent during thyroidectomy and underwent their first thyroid-related neck surgery were eligible. Patients were excluded if they had a history of thyroid or neck surgery, neck irradiation, pregnancy, or lactation.

The study period spanned 24 months following institutional review board approval. Patient enrollment occurred from October 2023 to March 2025, and each patient was followed up for 12 weeks after surgery. Because this was an observational study reflecting routine clinical practice, all consecutive patients who met the inclusion criteria after study approval were enrolled. Herein, 200 patients were initially enrolled, with approximately 50 patients in each of the 4 anti-adhesion agent groups.

Anti-adhesion agents and application

All products were applied routinely at the end of surgery. After the completion of thyroid resection and hemostasis, 2 units of the assigned agent were administered per patient: one applied to the tracheal bed and the other to the subcutaneous layer over the strap muscles, covering the entire operative field as widely as possible.

Study schedule and assessments

From the day of surgery to postoperative day 3—during which written informed consent was obtained—patient eligibility was confirmed, and baseline demographic data, medical and medication histories, adhesion scores (Supplementary Table 1), and Vancouver Scar Scale (VSS) scores (Supplementary Table 2) were collected. At 2 and 12 weeks after surgery, an independent evaluator reassessed the adhesion and VSS scores and recorded concomitant medication use and adverse events. The overall efficacy of the anti-adhesion agents was evaluated both subjectively and objectively using a patient-reported adhesion questionnaire and the VSS, respectively.

Subjective adhesion score

An independent evaluator administered a standardized adhesion questionnaire 2 and 12 weeks after surgery. The questionnaire consisted of 7 items scored on a numeric rating scale ranging from 0 to 10, with higher scores indicating more severe adhesion-related symptoms. Patient-reported symptoms included difficulty in swallowing saliva, water, and solid food, as well as the patient’s subjective perception of abnormal neck wrinkles. Examiner-rated findings comprised the symmetry and natural appearance of neck wrinkles at rest and during neck extension—defined as a comfortable neck extension with the chin elevated approximately 30°–45° above the horizontal axis—and the surgeon’s assessment of inflammatory reaction and scar formation. The total adhesion scores and sub-scores were compared among the 4 groups at each time point.

Objective scar assessment

Objective scar quality was evaluated at 2 and 12 weeks after surgery by an independent evaluator using the VSS. The VSS assesses 4 parameters: pigmentation, vascularity, pliability, and height, with higher total scores indicating more pronounced scarring. At each visit, the total and subscale VSS scores were analyzed across the 4 antiadhesion agent groups to compare objective scar outcomes.

Safety assessment

At every visit, all post-procedural adverse events were recorded in the case report form, regardless of the cause. Postoperative complications were defined as any unintended medical events occurring after surgery that required additional observation, intervention, or treatment beyond routine postoperative care. Transient hypocalcemia was defined as hypocalcemia requiring calcium and/or vitamin D supplementation that resolved within 6 months after surgery, whereas permanent hypocalcemia was defined as persistent hypocalcemia requiring ongoing supplementation beyond 6 months. Transient vocal cord palsy was defined as unilateral or bilateral vocal cord immobility on laryngoscopy that recovered within 6 months postoperatively, and permanent vocal cord palsy as immobility persisting for more than 6 months. Wound seroma was defined as a clinically evident, localized fluid collection at the operative site requiring aspiration or specific clinical observation, and wound erythema as visible redness of the surgical wound considered by the surgeon to be inflammatory or infectious in nature and requiring additional observation or treatment. Frequencies and proportions of adverse events were summarized and compared among the 4 groups. Patients who withdrew their consent or for whom further participation was deemed inappropriate by the investigators were considered withdrawn.

Statistical analysis

Efficacy was evaluated for all anti-adhesion agent groups at each time point. For the adhesion questionnaire, adhesion scores at 2 and 12 weeks and their changes from baseline were summarized using descriptive statistics (mean and standard deviation or median with minimum and maximum, as appropriate) for each group and time point. Normality of data distribution was evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Between-group comparisons of adhesion scores and changes in score at each time point were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test when normality assumptions were not met. Within-group changes were evaluated using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Statistical significance was defined as a 2-sided P-value <0.05.

For the VSS, scores at 2 and 12 weeks were summarized similarly with descriptive statistics for each group. Between-group comparisons at each time point were conducted using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test according to the results of the normality assessment.

RESULTS

Patient enrollment and baseline characteristics

The distribution of the 200 initially enrolled patients was as follows: MS (n = 50), MD (n = 51), CB (n = 50), AC (n = 44), and combined anti-adhesive agents (n = 5). Patients were excluded if they had completion thyroidectomy, postoperative bleeding requiring intervention, refusal to participate, or use of combined anti-adhesion agent products. Because some patients met more than one of these exclusion criteria, the total number of excluded patients was 20. Ultimately, 180 patients were included in the final analyses and allocated to 4 groups: MS (n = 46), MD (n = 45), CB (n = 47), and AC (n = 42) (Fig. 1). All 180 patients completed the scheduled assessments at baseline, 2 weeks, and 12 weeks after surgery.

The baseline clinicopathological characteristics were generally well balanced among the 4 groups (Table 1). No significant differences were observed in sex distribution, age, body mass index, preoperative thyroid-stimulating hormone levels, or the proportion of patients who underwent total thyroidectomy vs. lobectomy (P > 0.05). The mean age ranged from 47.4 to 50.2 years across groups, and the majority of patients were women. Approximately 60%–70% patients underwent lobectomy in each group, and the remaining patients underwent total thyroidectomy.

Pathologic and perioperative outcomes

The pathological findings and perioperative outcomes are summarized in Table 2. The distribution of benign vs. malignant pathologies was comparable across the groups, with papillary thyroid carcinoma being the most common. Tumor size, extrathyroidal extension, and lymph node metastasis rates did not differ significantly across the 4 anti-adhesion agent groups (P > 0.05).

The operative time was comparable across the groups, ranging from approximately 90–100 minutes (P > 0.05). However, postoperative hospital stay duration showed a statistically significant difference between the groups (P = 0.020). The MS group had a mean hospital stay duration of 2.2 days, while the AC group had a mean hospital stay duration of 2.0 days. Although this difference was statistically significant, the clinical relevance of the 0.2-day difference is negligible.

Subjective adhesion scores

Patient-reported and examiner-rated adhesion scores assessed at baseline and at 2 and 12 weeks after surgery are presented in Table 3. At baseline and immediately after surgery, the adhesion scores were low and did not differ significantly among the groups (MS 3.0, MD 3.9, CB 4.2, AC 5.1; P > 0.05).

Two weeks after surgery, the adhesion scores increased in all 4 groups, reflecting early postoperative discomfort and tissue healing (MS 6.7, MD 7.1, CB 6.4 AC 5.5; P > 0.05). After 12 weeks, the adhesion scores decreased substantially in all groups (MS 3.2, MD 4.3, CB 3.1 AC 3.7; P > 0.05), indicating the resolution of adhesion-related symptoms over time. No statistically significant differences were observed in the total adhesion scores among the 4 groups at any time point (all P > 0.05).

Objective scar assessment

VSS scores assessed 2 and 12 weeks after surgery are presented in Table 3. At 2 weeks, the total VSS score was numerically highest in the MS group (5.5 ± 2.1) and lowest in the MD group (4.4 ± 1.9), without statistical significance (P = 0.053).

In Table 4, pigmentation scores differed significantly among the 4 groups at 2 weeks (P = 0.046). The MS group exhibited a higher mean pigmentation score (1.3 ± 0.6) compared with the other groups (MD, 0.9 ± 0.5; CB, 1.0 ± 0.6; and AC, 1.1 ± 0.5), indicating more pronounced hyper- or hypopigmentation in the MS group. The vascularity, pliability, and height scores at 2 weeks did not differ significantly between the groups (P > 0.05).

At 12 weeks, the total VSS decreased in all groups, reflecting scar maturation (MS, 3.2; MD, 2.8; CB, 3.0; and AC, 2.9; P > 0.05). At this time point, no significant differences were observed in the total or subscale VSS scores among the 4 groups (P > 0.05).

Safety outcomes

Comparison of adverse events recorded at each visit across the groups is provided in Table 2. The incidences of hypocalcemia, vocal cord palsy, wound swelling, wound erythema, and other postoperative complications did not differ significantly among the 4 anti-adhesion agent groups (all P > 0.05). No serious device-related adverse events were observed in any of the groups. Overall, all 4 anti-adhesion agents were well tolerated with comparable safety profiles.

DISCUSSION

This prospective observational study compared the efficacy and safety of 4 commercially available anti-adhesion agents (MS, MD, CB, and AC) after open thyroid surgery. Overall, the agents showed similar performances with respect to adhesion-related symptoms and scar quality over 12 weeks, with no clinically significant superiority of any individual product. All 4 agents were well tolerated, demonstrating low complication rates and no serious device-related adverse events.

To date, only a few randomized controlled trials have investigated adhesion prevention strategies in the context of thyroidectomy [2567]. Yu et al. [5] conducted a randomized placebo-controlled trial using a hyaluronic acid–based collagen membrane and employed a vision-based markermovement system to objectively quantify cervical adhesions. They demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in marker displacement in the barrier group compared with the control group. In contrast, Ahn et al. [6] evaluated a thermosensitive solgel agent in a doubleblind trial and demonstrated its safety; however, no statistically significant differences were observed in adhesionrelated symptoms or operative adhesion scores. A multicenter randomized controlled trial of a temperaturesensitive ADM barrier after thyroidectomy similarly reported that ADM was safe and free of serious devicerelated adverse events; however, it did not confer clinically significant advantages in swallowing impairment, voice handicap, or throat symptom scales compared with control [1112].

Several randomized controlled trials have examined anti-adhesion agent strategies in thyroid surgery; however, the overall findings remain inconsistent [256711]. Yu et al. [5] demonstrated that compared with a placebo, a hyaluronic acid-based collagen membrane reduced objective cervical adhesion, as quantified by a vision-based marker movement system. Previous trials comparing thermosensitive sol-gel agents and ADM barriers with no treatment confirmed acceptable safety but failed to demonstrate clear benefits in swallowing function, voice handicap, or throat symptom scores relative to the control [61113]. In contrast, a randomized non-inferiority trial demonstrated that a temperature-sensitive ADM had anti-adhesive efficacy comparable to that of an established active control agent (Guardix-SG; Hanmi Pharm. Co., Ltd.), thereby supporting its clinical utility [714]. In a separate randomized double-blind trial, human ADM applied after thyroidectomy was reported to improve scar appearance and reduce cervical adhesions to some extent, but the magnitude of benefit was still modest and primarily cosmetic [12]. More recently, oxidized regenerated cellulose and high-molecular-weight hyaluronic acid barriers have been reported to provide only modest and variable improvements in dysphagia indices compared with no barrier [2]. Collectively, previous reports and the present findings suggest that while some products may influence specific objective or early postoperative parameters, the overall clinical impact on patient-centered outcomes remains limited in routine thyroid practice [2671112].

The present study extends the existing literature by directly comparing 4 different barrier types—ADM-based sheets, collagen-based gel, and hyaluronic acid and poloxamer—within a single real-world cohort. Subjective adhesion scores increased at 2 weeks in all groups, likely reflecting the expected trajectory of early postoperative discomfort and tissue healing, and subsequently decreased by 12 weeks. The absence of significant between-group differences in total adhesion scores at any time point suggests that, with contemporary surgical techniques, the choice of barrier may not markedly alter the medium-term course of adhesion-related symptoms. The VSS scores showed a similar pattern of early increase, followed by improvement at 12 weeks. Only pigmentation at 2 weeks differed significantly between groups, with slightly higher scores in the MS group. However, this difference did not persist at 12 weeks and is unlikely to have substantial clinical relevance.

From a clinical standpoint, these findings imply that all 4 agents can be considered reasonable options when an anti-adhesion agent is deemed necessary after thyroidectomy. Given their comparable efficacy and safety profiles, surgeons may prioritize other factors, such as handling characteristics, ease of application, institutional familiarity, and cost, when selecting a product. The small but statistically significant difference in postoperative hospital stay among the groups is unlikely to translate into meaningful differences in resource utilization or patient recovery and should be interpreted cautiously.

This study has several strengths. The present report reflects real-world thyroidectomy practice, includes a relatively large sample size for this clinical question, and uses both patient-reported and examiner-rated measures to assess adhesion-related outcomes. The VSS, developed in 1990, remains one of the most commonly used clinician-reported tools for assessing scar appearance despite variable inter-rater reliability and multiple proposed modifications [15161718]. In addition, it provides head-to-head comparative data across multiple agents, addressing an important gap in the literature, where most previous trials have focused on a single product vs. placebo or standard care [256711].

However, this study has certain limitations. First, the observational, non-randomized design raises the possibility of selection bias and residual confounding, despite broadly similar baseline clinico-pathological characteristics across groups. In our institution, an anti-adhesion agent is routinely applied in all patients undergoing open thyroid surgery, and patients receive one of the commercially available agents according to surgeon preference or product availability. A no-treatment control group was therefore not included, both because it does not reflect our current standard practice and because omitting an adhesion barrier in selected patients could raise ethical concerns. Nevertheless, the present study provides clinically relevant comparative data among 4 different approved agents under routine care conditions. Second, a follow-up duration of 12 weeks, although sufficient to capture early and intermediate scar changes, may not fully reflect long-term scar maturation or late-onset adhesion-related complaints. Third, the primary and secondary endpoints relied on symptom questionnaires and VSS scoring, which, while standardized and widely used, remain partly subjective and susceptible to interobserver variability. As device-derived measures, such as elastography or other quantitative imaging modalities, were not used, subtle differences in tissue adhesion may not have been fully captured [19]. Finally, this study was conducted at a single tertiary center, which may limit its generalizability to other settings.

Future research should focus on randomized, adequately powered head-to-head comparisons of different barrier materials, with longer follow-up periods and incorporation of objective functional measures, such as quantitative neck range of motion and validated thyroid-specific qualityoflife instruments [122021]. Costeffectiveness analyses should also be considered, given that the observed differences among products were small and transient. Within the design constraints, the present study suggests that MS, MD, CB, and AC can be used safely after open thyroid surgery with broadly comparable short-term efficacy in terms of adhesion-related symptoms and scar quality, supporting a more individualized, context-dependent approach to barrier selection in endocrine surgery [256711].

In conclusion, this prospective observational study involving patients undergoing open thyroid surgery with 4 different anti-adhesion agents (MS, MD, CB, and AC) demonstrated comparable short-term efficacy of the agents regarding adhesion-related symptoms and VSS scores, with no clinically meaningful superiority of any single product. All agents were well tolerated, and no serious device-related adverse events were observed. These findings suggest that the choice among these commercially available adhesion barriers may be guided primarily by handling characteristics, availability, and cost rather than by expectations of major differences in clinical performance during thyroidectomy.
