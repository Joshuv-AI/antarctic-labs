Population Pharmacokinetics of Bosutinib in Children with Philadelphia Chromosome-Positive Chronic Myelogenous Leukemia Based on a Phase I Dose-Finding Trial (ITCC-054/COG AAML1921) and Pediatric Recommended Phase II Dosage Evaluation

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectivesBosutinib, an orally administered dual Src and Bcr-Abl tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI), is approved for the treatment of chronic phase Philadelphia chromosome-positive chronic myelogenous leukemia, newly diagnosed or resistant/intolerant to previous treatment of one or more TKIs in adults and children ≥ 1 years (by the US Food and Drug Administration) and ≥ 6 years (by the European Medicines Agency). Owing to the limitations of non-compartmental analysis in pharmacokinetic (PK) characterization, this study applies a prior population PK modeling approach to describe the pediatric PK of bosutinib on the basis of its phase I dose-finding trial and the available bosutinib adult PK knowledge.MethodsFrom 26 pediatric patients in the phase I part of the ITCC-054/COG AAML1921 trial, 235 plasma bosutinib concentration samples were analyzed. A published adult bosutinib population PK (popPK) model was used as the reference model; the frequentist prior modeling approach was used during model development to integrate adult PK information, especially for parameters with poor identifiability.ResultsBosutinib pediatric PK was well characterized by a two-compartment model with first-order absorption, an absorption lag time of 0.467 h, and allometric scaling with fixed exponents. The typical clearance was 74.9 L/h (normalized to 70 kg), higher than the reported adult value (56.3 L/h). Model-based simulations validated that the bosutinib recommended phase II dose (RP2D) could achieve the target exposure observed in adults and demonstrated the influence of higher clearance in pediatric patients.ConclusionsThis study presents the first pediatric bosutinib popPK model, integrating prior adult PK knowledge to enable robust PK characterization using a small pediatric dataset from an early trial stage. In addition, the model confirms a higher clearance in children and supports the RP2D concluded in the phase I dose-finding part of the ITCC-054/COG AAML1921 trial.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s40262-026-01672-y.

Key Points

This study developed the first pediatric population pharmacokinetic model of bosutinib using data from the phase I dose-finding part of the ITCC-054/COG AAML1921 trial, integrated with prior adult PK knowledge via a frequentist prior modeling approach, to better characterize bosutinib pharmacokinetics compared with the trial protocol stated non-compartmental analysis.Pediatric bosutinib pharmacokinetics was well described by a two-compartment model with first-order absorption and an absorption lag time, and the model identified a higher bosutinib clearance in children compared with adults.Model-based simulations demonstrated that the recommended phase II dose achieves target adult exposure in pediatric patients, supporting its appropriateness.

Introduction

Bosutinib is an orally administered dual Src and Bcr-Abl tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) [1, 2] approved for the treatment of chronic phase (CP) Philadelphia chromosome-positive chronic myelogenous leukemia (Ph+ CML), newly diagnosed (ND) or resistant/intolerant (R/I) to previous treatment of one or more TKIs. In adults, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and European Medicines Agency (EMA) have approved bosutinib at a once-daily dose of 400 mg and 500 mg for patients with ND and R/I Ph+ CML, respectively [3, 4].

To address the unmet medical need in children, the phase I/II ITCC-054/COG AAML1921 (NCT04258943) was initiated to evaluate the safety and efficacy of bosutinib in pediatric patients with chronic phase ND or R/I Ph+ CML. The study aimed at determining the recommended phase II dose (RP2D) on the basis of a combination of safety and exposure matching with predefined target levels derived from adults for R/I and ND patients, respectively [5]. The pharmacokinetics (PK) relevant criteria for selecting the pediatric RP2D was the steady-state exposure examination in pediatric trial participants, estimated by non-compartmental analysis (NCA). According to the results of the phase I part of study ITCC-054/COG AAML1921, an acceptable safety profile was documented, and RP2D for R/I patients was established as 400 mg/m2 once daily (maximum 600 mg/day) and 300 mg/m2 once daily (maximum 500 mg/day) for ND patients, both administered with food [5]. On the basis of the data generated in this study, in September 2023, the FDA extended bosutinib approval to pediatric patients aged ≥ 1 year with chronic phase ND or R/I Ph+ CML, using a body surface area (BSA)-banded oral dosing regimen [4]. In March 2025, the EMA also extended the BSA-banded dosing regimen for the above-mentioned indication to children above the age of 6 years [6].

In adults, bosutinib PK have been well characterized by a two-compartment model with first-order absorption, an absorption lag time of 0.442 h and first-order elimination. This model was developed from a dataset of 573 adult patients across ten clinical trials, with an estimated absolute bioavailability (F) of 33.1% [7]. In the adult popPK model, covariates affecting the PK of bosutinib include Asian race and baseline creatinine on drug clearance, as well as age, weight, and Asian race on the central volume of distribution [7]. In both FDA prescribing information and EMA summary of product characteristics (SmPC) a 6.0 h time to maximum concentration (Tmax) was described and no difference in PK between tablet and capsule under fed conditions was observed [3, 4]. Additionally, a significant food effect increasing bosutinib AUC 1.7-fold compared with fasting state and an absolute F of 34% in healthy volunteers when administered with food were reported [3, 4]. Similar PK analyses in the pediatric population are limited. Notably, in the ITCC-054/COG AAML1921 phase I study, both ND and R/I pediatric patients’ RP2D regimens required higher doses to achieve the target exposure as observed in adults under the approved adult dosage. The underlying reasons for this difference remain unclear. As stated in the trial protocol, NCA was applied to determine bosutinib PK information from pediatric trial participants. While the number of patients and timepoints of PK blood collection were evaluated in silico [8] to ensure NCA provide essential PK estimates, this approach eliminated the possibility of further examining the disparities between adults and children systematically. Additionally, considering the limitations of NCA [9–11] in PK analysis, especially with a relatively low participant number, it is arguably not the most suitable method to describe the bosutinib pediatric population PK.

This study aimed to develop a population PK (popPK) model to characterize bosutinib PK in pediatric patients with CML in an early stage of a clinical trial. The model was constructed in nonlinear mixed effects modeling (NONMEM [12, 13]) using data from the phase I dose-finding part of the ITCC-054 trial, with prior PK knowledge from the published adult bosutinib popPK model integrated through the “frequentist prior” framework ($PRIOR subroutine of NONMEM) [14, 15]. Utilizing the pediatric popPK model, we sought to better understand bosutinib PK in children, systematically assess differences in PK between pediatric and adult populations, and evaluate the validity of the bosutinib RP2D and the influence of potential PK differences through model-based simulations.

Methods

Study Design and Patients

The phase I part of the ITCC-054/COG AAML1921 (NCT04258943) trial was a single-arm, open-label study designed to evaluate the PK, safety, and tolerability of bosutinib in pediatric patients (aged ≥ 1 to < 18 years) with R/I Ph+ CML. The trial investigated oral daily dosing regimens of 300 mg/m2 (max 500 mg), 350 mg/m2 (max 600 mg), and 400 mg/m2 (max 600 mg), administered in 28-day treatment cycles. The available formulations were tablets, which could be dissolved in water and orally administered with a syringe or dissolved for nasogastric administration if necessary; and capsules, which could be dissolved in water and orally administered with a syringe or opened and administered by sprinkling the contents on food. Tablets and capsules could be used in combination to reach the BSA-adjusted dose. No significant differences were found in the PK of bosutinib following the administration of either the tablet or capsule form under the fed condition in adults, and the administration instructions were supported by data from in vitro or clinical trial experience [3, 4]. The PK blood sampling schedule (Table 1) occurred pre dose on cycle 1 day 14 and at hours 3, 4, 6, 8, and 24 post dose. Additional PK sampling occurred prior to dosing on cycle 2, 3, and 4, and at the time of dose escalation. Details on the ITCC-054 trial were published by Brivio et al. [5], and the full inclusion/exclusion criteria are reported in Supplementary Table 1. The RP2D was determined on the basis of a combination of safety and PK data. Safety was assessed by monitoring the incidence of dose-limiting toxicities (DLTs) during the first treatment cycle. The NCA PK evaluation focused on identifying the dose level (DL) that achieved steady-state exposure within one dosing interval (AUCtau,ss) equivalent to the target exposure established from approved adult dosing regimens. For ND CML patients, the target AUCtau,ss was 2.27 × 103 h ng/mL (±20%), following the 400 mg daily dose in adults. For R/I CML patients, the target AUCtau,ss was 3.15 × 103 h ng/mL (± 20%), following the 500 mg daily adult dose. These target AUCtau,ss values corresponded to the simulated geometric mean AUCtau,ss of bosutinib in adults receiving 500 mg QD and 400 mg QD, respectively. Not specified in the trial protocol, but added as a post hoc objective, we also used the trough concentration (Ctrough of 68 ng/mL and 91 ng/mL; ± 20%) observed in adult ND and R/I CML patients following the approved dosing regimens [3, 4] as target exposure values for pediatric patients. Comparing the bosutinib Ctrough is of relevance as relationship between efficacy and Ctrough has been observed in adults [7].

Pediatric Population Pharmacokinetics Model Development

The development of the pediatric popPK model for bosutinib was based on the previously established adult popPK model. The adult model was characterized by a two-compartment structure with first-order absorption and an estimated absorption lag time of 0.442 h. Covariate effects included race and creatinine clearance on drug clearance, as well as age, weight, and race on the central volume of distribution [7] (Fig. 1; Supplementary Table 2).Fig. 1Bosutinib pharmacokinetics model structure in adults [7] central compartment, KA absorption rate constant, LAG TIME absorption lag time, Q intercompartmental distributional clearance between the central and peripheral compartments, V2 central compartment volume of distribution, V3 volume of the peripheral compartment

For the pediatric model, the same structural model as the adult popPK model was applied, with fixed exponent allometric scaling incorporated to account for body size effect. Clearance and volume parameters were scaled by weight with a power exponent of 0.75 and a power exponent of 1.0, respectively [16, 17]. Preliminary reference model evaluation was performed with the NONMEM EONLY = 1 command to examine whether the adult model was a suitable reference model for pediatric model development. After confirming the suitable reference model, PK parameter estimation was performed. To solve potential parameter estimation instability and model misspecification issues in the pediatric population, the NONMEM $PRIOR subroutine was utilized to incorporate prior PK knowledge from the adult reference model. The NONMEM $PRIOR subroutine summarizes and leverages prior relevant PK information from the adult popPK model [7] to support parameter estimation, particularly useful in sparse data scenarios [14, 15]. Additionally, further exploration of potential sources of variability was conducted to refine the model.

During pediatric model development, the informativeness of the adult priors for both fixed and random effects were first set to be “uninformative,” ensuring that parameter estimation was mainly driven by the pediatric data. For parameters exhibiting estimation difficulties, such as implausible values (≥ 50-fold different from the reference value or physiologically implausible value), unstable estimates (relative standard error (RSE) ≥ 50%), shrinkage ≥ 40%, informative priors were subsequently introduced. No priors were applied to the residual unexplained variability.

Pediatric Population Pharmacokinetics Model Evaluation

Model performance was evaluated by goodness-of-fit diagnostic plots, and prediction-corrected visual predictive check (pcVPC) [18]. The PK parameter estimates precision (95% confidence interval, 95% CI) was assessed using the sampling importance resampling (SIR) procedure [19] and the variance–covariance matrix from the NONMEM covariance step.

Model-Based Recommended Phase II Dosage Exposure Simulation

The first simulations using the final bosutinib pediatric popPK model were conducted to evaluate whether the exposure (AUCtau,ss and Ctrough) for pediatric Ph+ CML patients receiving the RP2D would achieve the target exposures consistent with exposures observed in adults. Bosutinib exposure was simulated in a virtual population of pediatric patients with CML comprising 5000 ND patients and 5000 R/I patients, who followed the BSA-calculated recommended phase II dosing regimens (Table 2) over 28 days. The age, weight, and height distribution of the virtual population matched with the children (aged ≥ 1 to < 18 years) demographics reported by the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) August 2021–August 2023 database [20]. A second set of simulations was conducted to compare the simulated exposure (AUCtau,ss and Ctrough) between near-adult pediatric patients and adults. Specifically, the exposure of adolescent pediatric patients (age ≥ 15 to < 18 years) was compared with that of adults simulated from the established bosutinib adult popPK model [7]. Bosutinib exposure in adolescent pediatric patients was simulated in a virtual population of 1000 ND and 1000 R/I patients receiving the BSA-calculated recommended phase II dose (Table 2) and the approved adult dosage for 28 days, respectively. Adult exposures were simulated in 1000 ND and 1000 R/I virtual patients receiving the approved adult dosing regimens. The distribution of demographic characteristics (age, body weight, and height) of the virtual near-adult and adult (aged ≥ 18 years) population was generated to reflect those reported in the NHANES August 2021–August 2023 dataset [20].

Software

Nonlinear mixed-effects modeling was conducted using NONMEM (version 7.5.0; ICON Development Solutions, Ellicott City, MD, USA) with stochastic approximation expectation maximization (SAEM) and importance sampling assisted by mode a posteriori (IMPMAP) as the estimation method [12, 13]. The modeling process was facilitated through Pearl-speaks-NONMEM (PsN, version 5.3.0) [21], and Pirana (version 21.11.1) as the graphical user interface for NONMEM [22]. Data handling and visualization were performed using R (version 4.4.0).

Pediatric Population Pharmacokinetics Model Results

Firstly, a reference model evaluation of the adult bosutinib popPK model with fixed exponent allometric scaling was conducted. The goodness-of-fit plots indicated that the model structure was a suitable starting point for subsequent pediatric model development, while the performance in describing the pediatric PK data could be further improved (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). Secondly, covariate effects that were either unavailable or biologically implausible were removed. Removed covariates included Asian race on CL and V2 (unavailable in the pediatric dataset), age on V2 (objective function value (OFV) increased insignificantly after the exclusion), and creatinine clearance on CL (OFV increased insignificantly after the exclusion and because bosutinib is minimally cleared renally). Afterward, the PK parameters were re-estimated using the pediatric data (Supplementary Table 3). The results revealed several indications of parameters with unstable estimation or unable to be estimated (implausible parameter estimate values or failed/terminated estimation). To address these issues, we applied the NONMEM $PRIOR subroutine to support the parameter estimation of the pediatric model by incorporating the summarized prior PK information from the reference adult popPK model [7]. The stepwise model development process is detailed in Supplementary Table 3. Finally, the bosutinib pediatric PK was well characterized by a two-compartment model with first-order absorption, an absorption lag time of 0.467 h, and fixed exponent allometric scaling (exponent of 0.75 and 1.0 for clearance and volume parameters, respectively). The pediatric model estimated initial half-life (6.38 h), terminal half-life (58.2 h) and time to maximum concentration (4.55 h) were all shorter in comparison with the respective values in adults (8.77 h, 67.8 h, and 5.30 h). The apparent CL (CL/F) estimated by the pediatric popPK model (226 L/h; SIR 95% CI 197–263) was higher compared with the NCA-derived apparent CL (median: 164 L/h, IQR: 130–211). Pediatric Population Pharmacokinetics Model Evaluation

Goodness-of-fit diagnostic plots of the final model demonstrated no indication of model misspecification in the pediatric population (Fig. 2). The prediction-corrected visual predictive check (pcVPC) demonstrated that simulated predictions from the final model aligned closely with observed data (Fig. 3). PK parameter estimates were physiologically reasonable with precision confirmed through stable estimates and confidence intervals derived from both the NONMEM variance–covariance matrix and the SIR procedure (Table 5). Furthermore, individual predictions showed strong agreement with observations for all patients (Fig. 3). Lastly, the distribution of the empirical Bayes estimate of IIV (ηη) on PK parameters fits the assumed normal distribution that centered at 0 (Supplementary Fig. 3), and the ηη distribution over age and BSA (Fig. 4) exhibits no obvious trend, suggesting that the final pediatric model adequately addressed the variability associated with body size with allometric scaling. The same was observed in ηη distribution plots over other covariates (Supplementary Fig. 3). Discussion

In this study, we developed a pediatric popPK model for bosutinib using data from the phase I dose-finding part of the ITCC-054/COG AAML1921 trial, representing a crucial step in understanding bosutinib pharmacokinetics in children with Ph+ CML. By leveraging prior knowledge from a previous bosutinib adult popPK model [7] and sparse pediatric PK samples, this modeling approach enabled robust parameter estimation without an extensive sampling burden for pediatric oncology patients. The resulting model provides a valuable framework for characterizing differences in bosutinib disposition between pediatric and adult populations. Moreover, it offers key insights into the validation of the bosutinib dosage recommendation for further trials. Although this population PK model was not included in the regulatory submission, the further characterization of bosutinib pediatric PK deepens understanding of bosutinib behavior in pediatric patients and provides further support of the evaluated dose levels within the ITCC-054/COG AAML1921 study.

A key finding was that bosutinib clearance in pediatric patients is higher than that observed in adults. The typical clearance in adults is 56.3 L/h​ [7], whereas our pediatric model indicated a higher weight-normalized clearance of 74.9 L/h, normalized at 70 kg. Clinically, this manifests in lower drug exposure in children when given the BSA-adjusted dose equivalent to the adult dosage—a trend also noted in the phase I dose-finding trial, where pediatric patients required a higher dose to achieve the target adult exposure at the adult-approved dosage [5]. This higher clearance was also reported in the FDA bosutinib prescribing information [4]. The phenomenon of increased clearance in pediatric populations is not unique to bosutinib. Likewise in another TKI - brigatinib, pediatric dosing needs to be higher on a mg/m2 basis to achieve exposures comparable to adults [23]. And a similar pattern has been reported for nilotinib, where the BSA-adjusted clearance in pediatric patients was slightly higher than in adults [24]. These findings emphasize that developmental pharmacokinetic differences can noticeably affect drug exposure in children. While data availability limitations precluded comprehensive bosutinib PK characterization across the full age continuum, it was our aim to implement a population modeling approach with limited phase I dose-finding trial data and prior knowledge to provide PK insight in pediatric population as early and as accurately as possible. And in this case, our analysis quantified and identified the potential difference in bosutinib PK for younger patients.

The enhanced bosutinib clearance in pediatric patients can be attributed to several physiological and developmental factors. Bosutinib is primarily metabolized hepatically via CYP3A4 [25, 26], and developmental changes in enzyme activity play a major role in its clearance. CYP3A4 expression is low at birth but rises rapidly in infancy and childhood, often reaching or exceeding adult activity levels and with considerable interindividual variability in early childhood [27–29]. This temporary enhancement in metabolic capacity can accelerate the clearance of CYP3A4 substrates such as bosutinib in young patients; while others argue that the CYP3A4 activity in children stabilizes and approaches adult levels around 1–2 years of age [30]. Taken together, relevant studies with more patients below the age of 2 years are needed for clearer conclusion. Another important factor is the difference in plasma protein concentration between pediatric and adult patients. Bosutinib is highly protein-bound (~94% bound to plasma proteins [4, 25]), and younger children tend to have lower plasma protein concentrations than adults [27, 31, 32]. A lower protein binding capacity in children increases the free (unbound) fraction of bosutinib available for metabolism and excretion, effectively enhancing clearance. Additionally, age-related changes in body composition can influence the volume of distribution and elimination. Pediatric patients generally have a higher total body water percentage and lower body fat content relative to adults [27, 31–33]. Given that bosutinib is lipophilic with a logP of 4-4.5, reduced body fat and increased extracellular fluid in children may lead to faster drug elimination. The interplay of these developmental factors (hepatic enzyme maturation, elevated hepatic perfusion, reduced protein binding, distinct body composition), and the choice of the reference weight of 70 kg while applying allometric scaling, collectively may explain why children clear bosutinib more rapidly than adults.

The first set of simulations based on this pediatric popPK model provided important confirmation of the pediatric RP2D for bosutinib, demonstrating that the dosing regimen employed within the ITCC-054/COG AAML1921 pediatric study achieves the targeted drug exposures. Moreover, the fact that bosutinib pediatric PK was well described by our model that included allometric scaling with fixed exponent supports the proposed BSA-based dosing approach. This is because BSA is approximately proportional to body weight raised to the power of 0.667, which is reasonably consistent with the fixed allometric exponents. The other set of simulations exhibited a relatively notable difference in exposure following the approved adult dosage and the RP2D. These results demonstrate the influence of higher bosutinib clearance discovered in our model, even in near-adult pediatric patients. Admittedly, it was hard to identify or analyze the underlying reasons for the higher clearance in these pediatric patients with this phase I trial dataset. Our efforts emphasize the utility of this modeling approach in an early clinical trial settings as a means for providing as much preliminary pediatric PK knowledge as possible, with limited data availability to guide/inform further trial design. In essence, the pharmacometrics evidence supports and validates the bosutinib dosing method to address pediatric needs in a scientific manner. In future trial applications, this population modeling approach could be beneficial in providing more accurate PK results that inform effective pediatric dosage while also minimizing need for unnecessary trial-and-error dosing attempts and a burdensome PK data collection schedule in an early clinical trial phase.

Despite the strengths of our pediatric popPK model and its clinical utility, several limitations must be acknowledged. Firstly, the relatively small sample size and sparse sampling in this early-phase pediatric trial constrained the depth of covariate analysis. There may be additional covariates (e.g., age and organ functions) influencing bosutinib PK in children that we could not conclusively identify owing to the limited data. Overall, the model diagnostics supported the accuracy of this PK characterization from the popPK model in pediatric patients. Yet, only one patient under 4 years of age was included, resulting in more uncertainty regarding the PK of bosutinib in younger children. That only one younger patient was included reflects that CML is known to be very rare in very young children [34]. Across the approved children age range, the steady-state exposure (AUCtau,ss and Ctrough) following the pediatric RP2D were similar according to the model-based simulations (Supplementary Table 5; Supplementary Fig. 4). Secondly, while the use of adult data as priors greatly stabilized the model and prevented implausible parameter estimates, it also introduced potential bias. The $PRIOR approach inherently pulls parameter estimates toward adult values, which might mask true pediatric-specific pharmacokinetic behavior if it deviates from adults. Nevertheless, the use of uninformative priors on ETAs (empirical Bayes estimate of the interindividual random effect in a pharmacokinetic parameter), together with the favorable evaluation of the ETA distributions (Supplementary Fig. 3), suggests that potential concerns regarding the estimation of pediatric variability are likely minimal in our case. In our dataset from the dose-finding trial, owing to the limited number of patients below 2 years old and the trendless clearance interindividual variability distribution over age (Fig. 4), we did not incorporate an explicit maturation function in our model for metabolic clearance except for allometric scaling with fixed exponents to account for body size. However, it is possible that ontogeny-related changes (such as enzyme maturation curves) are not fully captured by allometry alone. In the bosutinib phase I study paper, Brivio et al. hypothesized that bosutinib PK might also be affected by different absorption in younger children with a higher gastric pH and less water in the gastrointestinal tract [5, 35]. However, we were not able to estimate the bioavailability in pediatric patients as only orally administered bosutinib PK data were available. Therefore, in this analysis, the bioavailability was fixed to the value reported in adults [7]. Lastly, different modes of pediatric administration, which were not accounted for in the model, might contribute to interindividual/occasional variability in children. However, negligible effect of administration mode on bosutinib PK was observed in adults under the fed condition [3, 4], which was the case for pediatric trial participants. An update and validation of the model, when more pediatric PK and safety data become available, is essential to refine pediatric PK characterization and dosage recommendations. In the interim, this current modeling approach is valuable for PK description and model-informed dose selection at the early clinical trial stage in a pediatric population.

Conclusions

This study presents the first pediatric bosutinib popPK model, integrating PK data from the bosutinib pediatric dose-finding trial with prior knowledge from an adult study [7]. This model provides crucial insights into bosutinib PK in children during an early stage of the drug’s clinical development, demonstrating a higher clearance of bosutinib in pediatric patients compared with adults. This finding aligns with trends observed for another TKI (brigatinib) and supports the phase I clinical trial observations of lower drug exposure in pediatric patients when administered at equivalent BSA-adjusted adult doses [5]. The increased clearance may be attributed to multiple physiological factors, including enhanced hepatic metabolism, reduced plasma protein binding capacity, differences in body composition, and potential differences in absorption, all of which emphasize the necessity for pediatric-specific dose finding. Our model simulations confirmed the validity of the dosing strategy proposed from the phase I dose-finding part of the ITCC-054/COG AAML1921 trial and outlined the influence in drug exposure of bosutinib in pediatric patients. This model-informed dosing strategy exemplifies the critical role of pharmacometrics approaches in optimizing pediatric dosing of bosutinib for children with Ph+ CML.
