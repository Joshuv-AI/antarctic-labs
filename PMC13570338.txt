Partial replacement of soybean meal with brown mushroom stem powder: Effects on laying performance, egg quality, and serum biochemical indices of lohmann LSL lite hens
Authors: Ahmed AA Abdel-Wareth, Md Salahuddin, Kayla G Stamps, Venkatesh Balan, Prantic Kumar Goswami, Trahmilla L Carr, Cassandra D Gray, Adrian MW Aviña, Alejandro Argueta, Abigail Osei-Akoto, Jayant Lohakare
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA total of 120 Lohmann LSL Lite laying hens (34 weeks of age) were used to evaluate the effects of brown mushroom stem (BMS) powder as a partial replacement for soybean meal (SBM) on laying performance, egg quality, egg composition, and serum biochemical parameters. After a 7-d adaptation period, hens were randomly assigned at 35 weeks of age to 1 of 3 dietary treatments: a control diet (0% BMS) and diets in which 10% or 20% of SBM was replaced with BMS. Each treatment had 8 replicates of 5 hens, and the experiment lasted 6 weeks. Data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA with orthogonal polynomial contrasts. Dietary BMS inclusion improved laying performance in an age-dependent manner. Hens fed 20% BMS showed higher egg production (linear P = 0.019) and egg mass (linear P = 0.006) during later stages, with increased egg weight at specific time points. Feed intake increased linearly (P < 0.05), whereas feed conversion ratio was not affected. Egg quality was enhanced by BMS inclusion, primarily through a consistent, dose-dependent linear increase in yolk color across most of the production period (P < 0.05); other egg-quality effects were sporadic and largely confined to single weeks, with albumen proportion increased and yolk proportion decreased at 36 weeks only (linear P = 0.002 and 0.004, respectively). Haugh unit showed a transient increase in 36 weeks only. Eggshell breaking force was unchanged, while shell thickness and shell proportion increased only at isolated later time points (38 and 40 weeks, respectively). Serum biochemical parameters remained within normal physiological ranges, indicating no adverse effects on metabolic health. A linear decrease in aspartate aminotransferase and increase in gamma-glutamyl transferase suggest adaptive metabolic responses rather than hepatic dysfunction. Other indicators of renal function, protein metabolism, electrolyte balance, and energy status were unaffected.In conclusion, replacing up to 20% of SBM with BMS supports performance, improves egg quality traits, and maintains physiological stability, indicating its potential as a sustainable feed ingredient for laying hens.Keywords: Brown mushroom stem, Laying hens, egg quality, Soybean meal replacement, Sustainability

Introduction

Global population is projected to reach approximately 9.7 billion by 2050, placing unprecedented pressure on animal-source foods and the sustainability of production systems (United Nations, 2017). Poultry is the fastest-growing livestock subsector and a cornerstone of global food security due to its short production cycle, efficient feed conversion, and the affordability of eggs as a high-quality protein source (Mottet and Tempio, 2017). However, feed remains the largest operating expense in layer systems, accounting for 60–75% of total production costs, which underscores the need for cost-effective feeding strategies that maintain performance while improving efficiency (Fosu, 2023; USDA APHIS, 2013). Soybean meal (SBM) is the primary plant protein source in poultry diets because of its favorable amino acid profile and digestibility. Yet reliance on SBM faces economic and environmental challenges, including price volatility, supply chain instability, and ecological concerns such as deforestation and greenhouse gas emissions associated with soybean cultivation (Poore and Nemecek, 2018). These issues have accelerated interest in affordable, locally available, and environmentally sustainable alternative feed ingredients. Mushroom byproducts represent a promising solution within circular economy frameworks. Edible and medicinal mushrooms are nutrient-dense and contain bioactive compounds such as β-glucans, phenolics, ergothioneine, sterols, vitamins, and minerals, which exhibit antioxidants, immunomodulatory, antimicrobial, and metabolic properties (Adesida et al., 2026; Duda et al., 2025; Kumar et al., 2021). Incorporating mushroom residues into poultry diets not only reduces waste but also offers functional benefits that may enhance bird health and egg quality. Evidence from previous studies supports the potential of mushroom-derived additives in layer nutrition. For example, Lentinula edodes (shiitake) supplementation has been associated with reduced Salmonella colonization without performance loss (Willis et al., 2008), induced molting with increased egg output but thinner shells (Willis et al., 2009), and improved albumen quality and yolk fatty acid profile with lower yolk cholesterol (Hwang et al., 2012), although some trials reported no significant effects (Mužić et al., 2005). Similarly, Pleurotus eryngii stalk residue reduced egg and blood cholesterol and enhanced antioxidant enzyme activity without impairing production (Lee et al., 2015), while Cordyceps militaris waste improved feed conversion ratio and lowered yolk cholesterol (Wang et al., 2015). Fermented Flammulina velutipes mycelium improved egg weight, Haugh units, shell thickness, and reduced pathogenic bacteria and ammonia emissions, whereas unfermented media byproduct showed no effect (Lee et al., 2015; Na et al., 2005). Oyster mushroom waste (Pleurotus ostreatus) supplementation improved yolk fatty acid profile, oxidative stability, and serum cholesterol without compromising performance (Karageorgou et al., 2024). Reviews further highlight that medicinal mushrooms can serve as antibiotic alternatives, supporting immunity and productivity in layers (Mahfuz and Piao, 2019; Rai et al., 2005), and a recent synthesis emphasizes their role in improving poultry health, welfare, and egg quality through bioactive compounds such as β-glucans, polyphenols, amino acids, and minerals (Duda et al., 2025). Recent research by our group has demonstrated the feasibility of utilizing mushroom stems as sustainable feed ingredients in poultry diets. In Lohmann LSL Lite chicks, partial replacement of SBM with brown mushroom stem (BMS) powder maintained growth performance and organ weights, improved blood biochemical markers, and reduced gas emissions, suggesting environmental benefits (Abdel-Wareth et al., 2025). Similarly, white mushroom stem (WMS) powder inclusion up to 6% had no adverse effect on growth or organ development, influenced serum mineral profiles, and significantly reduced CO₂, NH₃, and CH₄ emissions, reinforcing its potential as an eco-friendly alternative to SBM (Stamps et al., 2025). Furthermore, our recent review highlighted the functional potential of mushrooms and their byproducts in poultry nutrition, emphasizing their roles in enhancing immunity, productivity, and sustainability (Salahuddin et al., 2025). These findings provide a strong rationale for exploring mushroom stem powders in layer diets. Within this context, BMS from Agaricus bisporus offers a practical, locally available byproduct with moderate levels of protein, essential minerals, dietary fiber, and bioactive compounds, including β-glucans, phenolics, ergothioneine, and provitamin D precursors (Bederska-Łojewska et al., 2017). Its inclusion in layer diets could enhance antioxidant capacity, lipid metabolism, physiological function, and egg quality while reducing reliance on SBM and supporting sustainability goals. However, data on BMS as a partial SBM replacer in commercial layer diets remain scarce, particularly in Lohmann LSL Lite hens and at higher inclusion levels where matrix effects and bioactive density may influence nutrient partitioning and shell traits. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the effects of graded BMS inclusion on productive performance, internal and external egg quality, and compositional attributes and physiological response during early peak production (35 to 40 weeks of age), with attention to dose-response and timing effects.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

The experiment was conducted at the Poultry Research Center of Prairie View A&M University (PVAMU), Prairie View, TX, USA. All animal handling and experimental procedures complied with the Animal Welfare Act (AWA) and the Animal Welfare Regulations (AWRs) of the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA), as well as the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (National Research Council, 2011; USDA APHIS (United States Department of Agriculture, 2020). Ethical approval for this study was granted by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of PVAMU under protocol number AUP# 2023-055.

Birds, experimental design, and diets

A total of 120 Lohmann LSL Lite laying hens, 34 weeks of age, were used in this study. Prior to the initiation of experimental feeding, hens were fed a standard layer diet for a 7-day adaptation period during which all birds received the updated control diet. During this adaptation period, hens exhibited an average body weight of 1,650±2.01 g, egg production of 96.42±2.19%, average egg weight of 63.43±2.65 g, and a mean daily feed intake of 105±2.89 g per hen. After adaptation, birds (35 weeks of age) were randomly allocated to three dietary treatments: a control group (0% BMS) and two treatment groups in which 10% and 20% of soybean meal were replaced with brown mushroom stem powder (BMS). Each treatment consisted of eight replicates, with five hens per replicate pen. The experimental period lasted for 6 weeks.

The BMS was prepared exclusively from the stems of brown mushrooms obtained from a local farm (Monterey Mushrooms, Madisonville, TX, USA). The stems were thoroughly washed and then freeze-dried using a Harvest Right freeze dryer (North Salt Lake, UT, USA), followed by grinding into a fine powder. All experimental diets were formulated to be isonitrogenous and isocaloric, in accordance with the nutrient requirements recommended by Lohmann LSL Lite management guidelines (Lohmann Tierzucht, 2023).

Diets were prepared at the Texas A&M University Feed Mill (College Station, TX, USA) and provided in crumbling form. The chemical composition and nutritional profile of BMS used in the diets are presented in Table 1. Feed and water were offered ad libitum throughout the study. Weekly feed intake was monitored by subtracting residual feed from the amount initially provided. Birds were housed in a floor-pen system within a barn under standard management conditions.

Laying hen performance and egg production

Feed intake (FI) was calculated weekly by subtracting the remaining feed from the initial amount provided. The feed conversion ratio (FCR) was determined by dividing FI (g) by egg mass (g). Hen-day egg production (HDEP) was calculated by dividing the total number of eggs laid per hen per day by the number of hens and expressing the result as a percentage. Egg quality assessment

Egg quality parameters were measured weekly using an Egg Tester Ultimate™ (12-ETU-001 Version B; ORKA Food Technology Ltd., West Bountiful, UT, USA). The integrated system electronically determined egg weight (g), albumen height (mm), Haugh unit (calculated from albumen height and egg weight), yolk color (DSM scale, 1–16), eggshell strength (kgf), and eggshell thickness (mm). All measurements were performed with automated calibration to ensure accuracy and operator-independent consistency. Each egg was first weighed individually, then placed on the egg force reader to determine shell strength. Subsequently, eggs were cracked onto a flat surface for internal quality evaluation. The yolk was carefully separated from the albumen, and each component was weighed individually. The percentage composition of yolk, albumen, and shell was calculated relative to the whole egg weight by dividing the weight of each component by the total egg weight and multiplying by 100 at 36, 38, and 40 weeks of age. Shell thickness was measured using an eggshell thickness gauge after isolating a piece of the shell.

Blood biochemical analysis

Blood samples were obtained from the brachial vein of birds at week 40, immediately following slaughter, and collected into 10 mL tubes. Serum was isolated by centrifugation at 3,000 × g for 15 min at 4°C using a Sorvall ST Plus centrifuge (Thermo Electron LED GmbH, Langenselbold, Germany). The harvested serum was stored at −20°C until analysis to preserve sample quality.

Serum biochemical analyses were conducted at the Arkansas Veterinary Diagnostic Laboratory using the Avian/Reptile Chemistry Panel on a Dimension EXL 200 automated chemistry analyzer (Siemens Healthcare Diagnostics Inc., Newark, DE, USA). A broad range of biochemical parameters was evaluated to characterize the birds' physiological status. Electrolyte concentrations included sodium, potassium, and chloride, while metabolic indicators comprised glucose, cholesterol, uric acid, creatinine, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), total protein, and total bilirubin. Serum calcium and phosphorus concentrations were also determined.

Activities of enzymes associated with metabolic and tissue function, including aspartate aminotransferase (AST), gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and amylase, were measured. Serum osmolality was additionally assessed to evaluate osmotic balance. The albumin-to-globulin ratio was calculated from measured serum albumin and globulin concentrations.

All analyses were performed using commercial reagent kits according to the manufacturers’ specifications, with rigorous quality assurance and control procedures implemented to ensure analytical accuracy and repeatability. Serum osmolality was measured using a calibrated osmometer integrated into the analyzer system. All analytes were determined using Siemens Dimension Flex reagent cartridges (e.g., DF33B Creatinine Flex Reagent Cartridge, Newark, DE, USA) specifically designed for use with the Dimension EXL analyzer. Analytical procedures followed the validated operating protocols described in the Siemens Operator’s Guide. This standardized diagnostic platform ensured reliable quantification of electrolytes, metabolites, minerals, and enzyme activities, enabling robust assessment of physiological responses to dietary treatments

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using a completely randomized design with the GLM procedure of SAS software (SAS Institute, 2013). The experimental unit was the replicate cage. Dietary treatment was included as the fixed effect in the model. Orthogonal polynomial contrasts were used to evaluate linear and quadratic responses to increasing levels of brown mushroom stem powder (BMS) replacing soybean meal.

A repeated-measures mixed-model analysis is recommended for confirmatory evaluation of age-dependent responses in future studies.

Results

Egg production

Replacing soybean meal with BMS powder in laying hen diets resulted in distinct age-dependent linear effects on several egg production performance parameters (Table 2). All hens were fed the control diet at 34 weeks of age to establish baseline production performance prior to the initiation of dietary treatments; therefore, no dietary effects were found during this period. Egg weight was not affected by dietary treatment from 35 to 37 weeks of age (P > 0.05); however, a significant linear increase was observed at 38 and 39 weeks of age as BMS inclusion increased (P = 0.007 and P = 0.005, respectively). By contrast, no linear or quadratic effects on egg weight were detected at 40 weeks of age (P > 0.05). Hen-day egg production was generally unaffected by dietary BMS inclusion throughout most of the experimental period. No significant linear or quadratic responses were observed between 34 and 38 weeks of age (P > 0.05). At 39 weeks of age, egg production increased linearly with increasing replacement of soybean meal by BMS (P = 0.019), whereas no effects were evident at 40 weeks of age. Egg mass followed a similar pattern to egg weight. No dietary effects were observed from 34 to 37 weeks of age (P > 0.05). In contrast, egg mass increased linearly at 38 and 39 weeks of age in response to increasing BMS inclusion (P = 0.025 and P = 0.006, respectively), indicating improved overall egg output at higher inclusion levels during this period. No differences were detected at 40 weeks of age.

Egg quality

Replacing soybean meal with BMS powder in laying hen diets resulted in marked linear responses in several egg quality traits across the 34- to 40-week production period (Table 3). For all egg quality parameters measured at 34 weeks of age, no significant differences were observed among groups (P > 0.05), as all birds were fed the control diet during this baseline period. Egg yolk color exhibited a consistent linear increase with increasing dietary BMS inclusion from 35 to 40 weeks of age (P ≤ 0.035). The progressive enhancement of yolk pigmentation with higher BMS levels was evident throughout the later stages of the experimental period, with no significant quadratic responses detected at any age. Egg height was not affected by dietary treatment at any week evaluated, as no linear or quadratic effects were observed from 35 to 40 weeks of age (P > 0.05), indicating that albumen height remained stable regardless of BMS inclusion level. Egg weight measured for quality assessment was generally unaffected by dietary BMS inclusion. A tendency toward a linear increase was observed at 35 and 38 weeks of age (P = 0.056 and P = 0.090, respectively), while a significant linear increase was detected at 39 weeks of age (P = 0.029). No dietary effects were evident at other points (P > 0.05). Haugh unit values, an indicator of internal egg quality, were largely unaffected by dietary treatment. A significant linear increase was observed at 36 weeks of age in response to increasing BMS inclusion (P = 0.040), whereas no linear or quadratic effects were detected during the remaining weeks (P > 0.05), suggesting a transient improvement in albumen quality at mid-period.

Egg composition

Replacing soybean meal with BMS had limited, age-specific effects on egg composition parameters of laying hens evaluated at weeks 36, 38, and 40 of the experimental periods (Table 4). Whole egg weight was not affected by dietary treatment at any time point, as no significant linear or quadratic responses were observed at 36, 38, or 40 weeks of age (P > 0.05). Albumen proportion responded to dietary BMS inclusion in a time-dependent manner. At 36 weeks of age (week 2), albumen percentage increased linearly with increasing replacement of soybean meal by BMS (P = 0.002). A tendency toward a linear increase was observed at 38 weeks of age (P = 0.093), while no significant linear effect was detected at 40 weeks of age (P > 0.05). No quadratic responses were observed at any age. Conversely, yolk proportion exhibited an opposite response at the early stage of evaluation. At 36 weeks of age, yolk percentage decreased linearly with increasing BMS inclusion (P = 0.004). No significant dietary effects on yolk percentage were detected at 38 or 40 weeks of age (P > 0.05), indicating that this response was transient. Shell proportion was not influenced by dietary treatment at 36 or 38 weeks of age (P > 0.05). However, at 40 weeks of age, shell percentage increased linearly with increasing BMS inclusion (P = 0.001), with hens fed diets containing BMS exhibiting a higher proportion of shell compared with the control group. No significant quadratic effects were observed for shell percentage at any time point. Overall, partial replacement of soybean meal with BMS resulted in modest shifts in egg component distribution, characterized by increased albumen proportion and reduced yolk proportion at the early stage, and an increased shell proportion at the later stage, without affecting whole egg weight.

Discussion

The present study evaluated the effects of partially replacing soybean meal with brown mushroom stem (BMS) powder on laying hen performance, egg quality, egg composition, and selected blood biochemical indicators. Overall, dietary inclusion of BMS, particularly at the 20% replacement level, resulted in age-dependent improvements in productive performance, consistent enhancement of yolk color throughout the experimental period, and modulation of egg component distribution at specific stages of production. Importantly, these responses occurred without detrimental effects on feed efficiency, shell breaking strength, or systemic metabolic health, supporting the suitability of BMS as a functional and sustainable feed ingredient for laying hens.

The observed increases in egg weight, egg production, and egg mass during the later stages of the experiment are in agreement with previous studies demonstrating improved or maintained laying performance following dietary inclusion of mushroom-derived products (Duda et al., 2025; Mahfuz and Piao, 2019).

Mushrooms are recognized as sources of highly digestible protein, essential amino acids, and trace minerals, including selenium, zinc, and iron, which are essential for antioxidant defense, endocrine regulation, and reproductive function (Salahuddin et al., 2025; Venturella et al., 2021; Won and Park, 2005). Selenium plays a particularly important role in thyroid hormone metabolism and antioxidant enzyme activity, processes that directly influence ovarian activity, egg synthesis, and egg size (Haro et al., 2020; Podkowa et al., 2020; Udensi and Tchounwou, 2017). The age-dependent responses observed in the present study suggest that the beneficial effects of BMS may accumulate over time, leading to more pronounced improvements during later production stages.

Beyond their nutritional contribution, mushroom-derived bioactive compounds may enhance productive efficiency by improving nutrient utilization. Previous reports indicate that mushroom supplementation can improve feed utilization and metabolic efficiency, supporting higher egg output without adverse effects on feed conversion ratio (Karageorgou et al., 2024; Seid, 2024). These improvements are frequently attributed to reduced oxidative stress and improved physiological condition of birds, both of which are closely associated with sustained reproductive performance.

Polysaccharides and β-glucans present in mushroom stems have been shown to improve intestinal morphology, modulate gut microbiota, and enhance nutrient digestibility, thereby increasing absorptive capacity and nutrient utilization efficiency (Tang et al., 2016; Yan et al., 2014; Yin et al., 2012). Improved gut health likely contributed to the linear increases in egg mass and egg production observed during the later phases of the current study without increasing feed conversion ratio, suggesting more efficient partitioning of nutrients toward egg formation.

Egg yolk color consistently and linearly improved with increasing dietary BMS inclusion from 35 to 40 weeks of age, indicating a robust and reproducible response. Although mushrooms are not traditionally considered rich sources of carotenoids (Abdel-Wareth et al., 2025; Buwjoom et al., 2004; Salahuddin et al., 2025; Tang et al., 2016), they contain phenolic compounds and other natural pigments that may influence yolk coloration directly or indirectly. Additionally, antioxidant-related mechanisms could plausibly reduce oxidative degradation of lipid-soluble pigments deposited in the yolk, thereby contributing to yolk color; however, antioxidant status was not measured in this study, so this mechanism remains speculative. Similar enhancements in yolk pigmentation and egg quality have been reported in hens supplemented with Flammulina velutipes or Lentinula edodes, supporting the role of mushroom-derived bioactives in improving yolk characteristics (Hwang et al., 2012; Lee et al., 2015).

Changes in egg composition were evident during early production stages. The increase in albumen proportion concomitant with a reduction in yolk percentage suggests a temporary shift in nutrient partitioning toward protein deposition. This response has been hypothesized to relate to mushroom-derived peptides, sulfur-containing amino acids, and ergothioneine, which have been reported to support protein synthesis and cellular antioxidant protection in other systems. Similar changes in albumen characteristics have been reported with shiitake mushroom supplementation (Hwang et al., 2012). Importantly, these compositional changes did not compromise overall egg weight, indicating redistribution rather than a reduction of egg components.

Eggshell quality was largely unaffected by dietary BMS inclusion, as shell breaking force remained stable across all ages. However, increases in shell thickness and shell proportion at later production stages suggest improved mineral utilization rather than alterations in systemic mineral balance. Mushroom stems contain bioavailable minerals and ergosterol derivatives that may support calcium absorption and vitamin D metabolism, thereby enhancing shell mineralization during periods of increased laying demand (Lee et al., 2015).

Blood biochemical analysis indicated that dietary BMS inclusion did not disrupt metabolic or physiological homeostasis, as all measured parameters remained within normal physiological ranges (Giannenas et al., 2011; Toghyani et al., 2012). Instead, the observed linear and quadratic responses reflect adaptive metabolic adjustments rather than pathological changes.

Liver enzyme activity exhibited selective modulation rather than evidence of hepatic dysfunction. ALT and ALP were unaffected by dietary treatment, indicating preserved hepatocellular integrity and biliary function. In contrast, AST decreased linearly with increasing BMS inclusion, while GGT increased linearly. The reduction in AST is commonly associated with decreased cellular stress or improved metabolic efficiency, whereas elevated GGT activity may reflect increased glutathione turnover and antioxidant metabolism. This enzyme pattern is consistent with modulation of hepatic redox balance rather than liver injury and has been hypothesized to relate to the antioxidant properties of mushroom-derived phenolics and ergothioneine (Ahmad et al., 2024; Alam et al., 2009; Attarat and Phermthai, 2015; Barcan et al., 2023; Rai et al., 2005.); because antioxidant biomarkers (e.g., MDA, SOD, glutathione, or TAC) were not measured, this interpretation is hypothetical. Markers of nitrogen metabolism and renal function also indicated metabolic adaptation without impairment. Uric acid concentrations showed a numerical trend toward lower levels, suggesting improved nitrogen utilization efficiency, potentially mediated by mushroom phenolic compounds that inhibit xanthine oxidase activity (Salahuddin et al., 2025). Blood urea nitrogen exhibited linear and quadratic responses, likely reflecting altered amino acid metabolism or protein intake rather than renal dysfunction, as creatinine and bilirubin levels remained stable.

Serum cholesterol showed a non-significant numerical reduction with increasing BMS inclusion (P = 0.087), which may indicate a hypocholesterolemic tendency that requires confirmation. Mushroom-derived β-glucans and dietary fiber are known to bind bile acids, enhance fecal excretion, and reduce hepatic cholesterol synthesis, thereby potentially influencing lipid metabolism (Almjalawi et al., 2024; Alves et al., 2012; Ayimbila and Keawsompong, 2023). Carbohydrate metabolism and osmotic balance remained unaffected, as indicated by stable glucose, amylase, and osmolality levels.

Serum protein profiles, including total protein, albumin, globulin, and albumin-to-globulin ratio, were not altered by dietary treatment, indicating preserved hepatic protein synthesis and immune balance. Stability of globulin concentrations suggests that BMS supplementation did not induce systemic immune activation or inflammatory stress. Although mushroom-derived β-glucans and phenolic compounds have been reported to exert immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory effects in other systems, no inflammatory or immune biomarkers were measured here, so any such contribution to the observed globulin stability remains speculative (Flores et al., 2024; Navarro-Simarro et al., 2024).

Electrolyte and mineral parameters exhibited minor fluctuations but remained within physiological limits. Observed variations in sodium and chloride concentrations likely reflect subtle adjustments in fluid balance regulated by renal mechanisms rather than electrolyte imbalance (Ayalew et al., 2022). Calcium and phosphorus concentrations were similarly maintained, supporting efficient mineral homeostasis, potentially enhanced by bioavailable mushroom-derived micronutrients (Altop et al., 2022).

The present findings align with previous studies reporting antioxidant, immunomodulatory, and metabolic benefits of mushroom supplementation in poultry. Agaricus bisporus stem residue increased antioxidant enzyme activity while reducing lipid peroxidation in laying hens and egg yolk (Yang et al., 2022), while dried mushroom supplementation improved antioxidant defense systems in broilers (Giannenas et al., 2010). Although production responses to mushroom supplementation vary depending on species, inclusion rate, and production phase, improvements in egg quality traits appear more consistent across studies (Hwang et al., 2012; Lee et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2015; Yang et al., 2022; Yoshida et al., 2017) supporting the responses observed in the present study. From a practical standpoint, the incorporation of BMS into laying hen diets offers a sustainable approach to valorize agricultural byproducts while partially replacing conventional protein sources. This strategy aligns with industry efforts to enhance sustainability, reduce feed costs, and limit reliance on antibiotic growth promoters (Duda et al., 2025; Mahfuz et al., 2018; Mahfuz and Piao, 2019).

A limitation of the present study is that no direct antioxidant or oxidative-stress biomarkers (e.g., malondialdehyde (MDA), superoxide dismutase (SOD), glutathione, or total antioxidant capacity (TAC) were measured. Consequently, the antioxidant- and bioactive-related mechanisms discussed above are inferred from the literature rather than demonstrated in this trial and should be regarded as hypotheses; their direct measurement is a priority for future work. A further limitation is the relatively short trial duration (six weeks, 35 to 40 weeks of age), which corresponds to early peak lay; consequently, the findings apply to this production phase and should not be extrapolated to the full laying cycle, and longer studies spanning early, peak, and late lay are warranted to determine whether these responses persist with advancing hen age.

Conclusion

Brown mushroom stem (BMS) powder can effectively replace up to 20% of soybean meal in laying hen diets without compromising performance or health. This inclusion level improved egg production, egg mass, in the later stages (egg weight increased only at isolated time points), while maintaining feed efficiency. BMS also enhanced egg quality principally through a consistent, dose-dependent increase in yolk color across the production period; other egg-quality changes (increased albumen proportion and transient shell-trait effects) were confined to isolated weeks. Shell breaking force was unaffected, and the single week increases in shell thickness (38 and 40 weeks) and shell proportion (40 weeks) may reflect localized changes in mineral utilization but were not consistent across the trial. Physiologically, all serum biochemical parameters remained within normal ranges, confirming metabolic safety. Changes in liver enzymes occurred within normal physiological ranges and were not accompanied by markers of hepatic injury; however, because antioxidant status was not measured, improved antioxidant function cannot be confirmed, while renal, protein, and electrolyte markers remained stable. Overall, BMS is a sustainable and functional feed ingredient that enhances productivity, egg quality, and metabolic efficiency, offering a viable alternative to conventional protein sources. Future research should focus on its bioactive compounds, its effects on gut health, and its long-term application under commercial conditions.
