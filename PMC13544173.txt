Lung transplantation outcomes in candidates aged ≥ 70 years old: A 10-year single center experience1
Authors: Jason M Aubrey, Preetha Pamidighantam, Stephen D Thorp, Isabelle Pokora, Taylor Zimmer, Cameron Lawson, Scott Chicotka, Reda E Girgis, Tomasz Timek, Edward T Murphy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe establishment of an upper age limit for lung transplantation remains controversial. Although the International Society of Heart and Lung Transplantation designates age over 65 as high risk, contemporary evidence demonstrates septuagenarians can achieve acceptable outcomes. This study aims to elucidate the outcomes in this older demographic compared to younger patients.MethodsThis is a single-center, retrospective review of all patients who received lung transplants over a 10-year period. Recipients were divided into two age groups: those under 70 and those 70 or older. The primary outcome was one-year survival, with secondary outcomes including three- and five-year survival, primary graft dysfunction, and acute kidney injury.ResultsA total of 380 lung transplants were performed: 307 (81%) in patients < 70 and 73 (19%) in those ≥ 70 years old. The median age at transplant was 63 (59;67) years in the younger cohort and 72 (71;73) years in the older cohort. There were no differences between groups in gender (p = 0.08), BMI (p = 0.29), lung composite allocation subscore (p = 0.18), or lung allocation score (p = 0.08). A higher proportion of patients aged ≥ 70 years received single lung transplants (p = <0.001) and had a diagnosis of restrictive disease (p < 0.001). Postoperatively, there were no differences in length of stay (p = 0.43), primary graft dysfunction (p = 0.14), or acute kidney injury (p = 0.99) between cohorts. No differences were observed in the 1-year (0.96 [95% CI: 0.93 – 0.98] vs 0.94 [95% CI: 0.86 – 0.98]), 3-year (0.88 [95% CI: 0.83 – 0.91] vs 0.89 [95% CI: 0.79 – 0.95]), or 5-year (0.73 [95% CI: 0.66 – 0.78] vs 0.81 [95% CI: 0.67 – 0.90]) survival rates between patients under 70 and those 70 or older (Log rank: p = 0.62). Multivariable Cox regression identified higher creatinine (HR 4.56, 95% CI 1.74–11.95; p = 0.002) and pretransplant ECMO use (HR 3.85, 95% CI 1.27–11.70; p = 0.017) as independent predictors of mortality, while age ≥ 70 was not significantly associated with mortality (HR 0.67, 95% CI 0.36–1.25; p = 0.206).ConclusionLung transplantation in patients aged ≥70 yields short- and long-term outcomes comparable to younger recipients, supporting expanded eligibility for carefully selected older candidates.Keywords: lung transplantation, septuagenarians, transplant eligibility, survival outcomes, elderly transplant, patient selection, transplantation

Introduction

Lung transplantation is an essential treatment for patients with end-stage lung disease, offering both a mortality and quality of life benefit. As the global population ages, the incidence of chronic respiratory diseases requiring transplantation increases. Historically, advanced age was deemed a relative contraindication, with many centers reluctant to transplant patients older than 65 given historically high mortality rates.1, 2 In the last several decades, 1-, 3- and 5-year lung transplant survival rates have significantly increased for lung transplant recipients, including those in older cohorts.3, 4, 5, 6 Improvements in donor selection, organ preservation, peri-operative and long-term management has improved survival.

Establishment of an upper age limit for lung transplant recipients remains a controversial topic. Early iterations of lung transplant candidacy guidelines from the International Society of Health and Lung Transplantation (ISHLT) deemed Age > 65 with low physiologic reserve as a relative contraindication for lung transplant and stated patients older than 75 were unlikely to be candidates for lung transplant.2, 7 The most recent ISHLT guidelines on lung transplant candidate removed the relative contraindication on age and replaced it as a risk factor; specifically, age > 70 as a risk factor with high or substantially increased risk and age 65–70 age solely a risk factor for unfavorable outcomes.8

The existing evidence on survival outcomes following lung transplantation in older adults remains inconclusive. There is a body of literature that demonstrates patients aged > 555, 9, > 6010, > 7011, 12, 13, and 7514 years of age had poor short- and long-term outcomes compared to younger cohorts. However, there is a growing body of recent literature that demonstrates carefully selected candidates aged greater than 70 can have comparable short- and long-term outcomes to younger cohorts in the Lung Allocation Score era.15, 16, 17, 18 At our center, lung transplantation is routinely performed in carefully selected septuagenarians. This study evaluates their outcomes compared to younger recipients. We hypothesize that appropriately selected septuagenarians can achieve similar morbidity and mortality to younger recipients.

Methods

Study design and population

We conducted a retrospective analysis of a prospectively maintained lung transplant database at our institution. The study included all patients (≥ 18 years old) who underwent single or bilateral lung transplantation between 2013 and 2024. Recipients were stratified by age into two groups: those younger than 70 years and those 70 years or older. All data was obtained from the institutional Lung Transplant Registry or United Network for Organ Sharing database. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the institutional ethics board (IRB #2015–262).

Standard demographic data including gender, body mass index (BMI), and ethnicity were collected for each patient. Preoperative variables included primary diagnosis, lung allocation score (LAS) at transplant, lung composite allocation subscore (CAS) at transplant, and the need for pre-transplant ventilatory or extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) support. Donor characteristics and waitlist data were obtained from a combination of our institutional lung transplant registry and the United Network for Organ Sharing database. Intraoperative data included type of procedure (single vs. bilateral), the use of cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), intraoperative support with either veno-venous or veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), total ischemia time, and the number of blood products administered.

Postoperative outcomes included duration of mechanical ventilation or ECMO, reintubation rate, primary graft dysfunction (PGD), chronic lung allograft dysfunction (CLAD), acute kidney injury (AKI), length of stay in the intensive care unit (ICU), total hospital length of stay, discharge disposition, and in-hospital survival. Primary graft dysfunction was assessed at 72 h after transplantation using chest radiograph findings and the PaO2/FiO2 ratio.19 Acute kidney injury was defined according to kidney disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) criteria.20 CLAD was defined as a persistent decline in FEV1 of at least 20% from the post-transplant baseline, occurring more than 3 months after transplantation, after exclusion of potentially reversible causes.21 Longer-term survival outcomes were obtained through clinical follow up with the transplant team. The primary endpoint was 1-year survival; secondary endpoints included 3-year survival, 5-year survival, incidence of AKI, PGD at 72 h, and development of CLAD. A pre-specified subgroup analysis was conducted to compare outcomes between recipients of single and bilateral lung transplants among the younger and older cohorts.

The standard operative technique utilized involved an anterior thoracotomy for single lung transplants and bilateral sequential anterior thoracotomies for bilateral lung procedures. Extracorporeal lung support or cardiopulmonary bypasses were not routinely employed. All patients received either an epidural nerve block or cryoneurolysis.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SAS Software (version 9.4, SAS institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Recipients were stratified by age into two groups (<70 years versus ≥70 years). Descriptive statistics were calculated for all variables in each group. Comparisons of categorical variables between cohorts were performed using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests, whereas continuous variables were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Kaplan-Meier survival analyses were conducted to assess overall and subgroup (single versus bilateral lung transplant) survival to 5-years, with significance evaluated by the log-rank test. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards analysis was performed to evaluate factors associated with post-transplant survival. Hazard ratios were adjusted for underlying diagnosis and transplant type. Reported variables include age ≥70 years, sex, transplant era, pre-transplant albumin, bilirubin, creatinine, ventilator support, and ECMO support. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

A total of 380 lung transplants were performed, of which 307 (80.8%) were in recipients younger than 70 years and 73 (19.2%) were in recipients aged 70 years or older. The annual proportion of recipients aged 70 years or older reached as high as 31.7% (Graph 1). The median age in the younger cohort was 63.3 years (IQR, 58.5–66.8), whereas in the older cohort it was 71.7 years (IQR, 70.8–73.1). The oldest patient transplanted was 76. There were no significant differences between these age groups in terms of sex (male: 58.6% vs 69.9%, p=0.08), body mass index (26.4 vs 25.3 kg/m, p=0.29), or ethnicity (p=0.53). Among younger patients, obstructive diseases (Group A) accounted for a greater proportion of transplant indications compared with older patients (34.3% vs 15.1%), whereas restrictive diseases (Group D) were more commonly observed in older recipients (83.6% vs 57.4%) (p < 0.001). The lung allocation scores (39.2 vs 40.7, p=0.08), lung composite allocation subscores (23.4 vs 25.0, p=0.18), and rates of pre-transplant ventilatory support (3.9% vs 0.0%, p=0.13) or extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (3.6% vs 1.4%, p=0.13) did not differ significantly between the two cohorts (Table 1).

A significant difference in procedure type was observed between the cohorts, with recipients aged ≥70 years were more likely to undergo single than bilateral lung transplants compared to the cohort < 70 years old (Single: 38.4% vs 20.2%, p=0.0042). However, there were no significant differences in the use of cardiopulmonary bypass (p=0.39) or intraoperative VA extracorporeal membrane oxygenation initiation (p=0.99) between the age groups. Intra-operative transfusion requirements measured as mean units of packed red blood cells (3 vs 2, p=0.21), platelets (2 vs 2, p=0.25), and fresh frozen plasma (2 vs 1, p=0.43) were also similar between cohorts (Table 2).

Postoperatively, the median duration of initial mechanical ventilation was comparable between cohorts (1 vs 1 day, p=0.39). Reintubation rates (16.1% vs 16.4%, p=0.99) and the median duration of reintubation (10.5 vs 13.5 days, p=0.55) did not differ significantly between groups. The incidence of grade 3 primary graft dysfunction at 72 h was similar between cohorts (9.8% vs 4.1%, p = 0.14). This finding was consistent in subgroup analyses of single lung transplant (19.4 vs 7.1%, p = 0.13) or bilateral lung transplant (7.4% vs 2.2%, p = 0.53). There was no significant difference in the rates of acute kidney injury (39.9% vs 39.7%, p=0.99). Intensive care unit length of stay (4.3 vs 3.9 days, p=0.22) and overall hospital length of stay (18 vs 17.5 days, p=0.43) were comparable between cohorts. While the proportion of subjects requiring post-operative ECMO support was comparable, recipients aged ≥70 years experienced a longer duration of postoperative ECMO use (6.5 vs 2 days, p=0.04). A subgroup analysis revealed that patients over 70 who underwent single lung transplant required significantly longer post-operative ECMO (7 vs. 2 days, p = 0.04) compared with those under 70. In contrast, no difference in ECMO duration was observed among age groups who underwent bilateral lung transplantation (3 vs 3, p = 0.99). In-hospital mortality was similar (1.9% vs 1.4%, p=0.99), and discharge disposition did not differ significantly (p = 0.92), with most patients in both groups being discharged home (71.4% vs 71.0%) or to acute inpatient rehabilitation (27.6% vs 29.0%). The incidence of chronic lung allograft dysfunction was similar between groups (p = 0.46). (Table 3).

Waitlist and donor characteristics were also comparable between age cohorts. Candidates aged ≥70 years had a shorter median total waitlist time overall (38 vs 56 days, p=0.072) and in the LAS era (36 vs 54 days, p=0.096), although neither difference reached statistical significance. In the CAS era, waitlist times were similar between cohorts (46.5 vs 46 days, p=0.94). Waitlist deaths occurred in 13 candidates <70 years and 4 candidates ≥70 years, with respiratory failure representing the most common cause of death in both cohorts. (Table 4) Donor characteristics were similar between recipients <70 and ≥70 years, including donor age (32 vs 31 years, p=0.26), BMI (25.4 vs 26.5 kg/m², p=0.25), gender (p=0.12), ethnicity (p=0.52), recent cigarette use (12.4% vs 9.6%, p=0.69), recent cocaine use (8.1% vs 8.2%, p=1.0), diabetes (9.4% vs 5.5%, p=0.36), donation after circulatory death (9.1% vs 5.5%, p=0.48), PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio (426 vs 426, p=0.91), donor cause of death (p=0.12), and chest X-ray findings (p=0.17). Subgroup analyses among single and bilateral lung transplant recipients similarly demonstrated no significant age-based differences in donor characteristics. (Table 5).

Discussion

In this single-center, 10-year analysis of 380 lung transplant recipients, 19.2% were aged ≥70 years. Although older recipients were more likely to receive single lung rather than bilateral transplantation, pre-operative characteristics, donor characteristics, intra-operative management, and post-operative outcomes were comparable between age cohorts. Kaplan-Meier survival analyses revealed no significant differences in 1-, 3-, or 5-year survival between recipients aged <70 and those aged ≥70 years. A prespecified subgroup analysis similarly revealed no difference in survival between single- and bilateral-lung transplant recipients across the age cohorts. These findings suggest that carefully selected patients aged ≥70 years can achieve outcomes comparable to those in younger recipients.

Despite the increasing proportion of older candidates undergoing lung transplantation, establishing an absolute upper age limit remains controversial. The most recent guidelines from the International Society of Heart and Lung Transplant removed age as a relative contraindication and instead designated age over 65 years as a high-risk feature.8 Zhou et al. demonstrated that nationally 8.8% of transplant recipients between 2005 and 2020 were aged ≥70 years, with 1-year survival rates in this cohort improving from 79.7% in 2005–2009 to 85.4% in 2015–2020.13 Over the past several decades, lung transplant outcomes across all age groups have improved, reflecting advancements in candidate selection, donor management, and perioperative care.6 High volume centers have lower mortalities after transplantation and are often more comfortable transplanting patients with higher LAS scores and age than lower volume centers.22 Our center transplants a higher proportion of candidates aged ≥70 years compared with the national average (19.2% vs 8.8%). Despite this larger percentage, our survival rates for recipients aged ≥70 years are comparable to those of younger cohorts. These findings are consistent with contemporary literature demonstrating similar outcomes between younger and older lung transplant recipients. Reich et al. found no significant difference in 5-year survival among patients under 70 and those ≥70 years.18 Similarly, Hayanga et al. utilized the SRTR database and demonstrated no difference in short-term outcomes between younger and older recipients in the modern era.15 Kilic et al. noted that age over 70 was associated with reduced 1-year survival in the pre–lung allocation score era, but not in the lung allocation score era.17 Our group previously demonstrated that hospital-free days in the first year after lung transplantation were strongly associated with long-term outcomes, whereas recipient age showed no relationship with the number of hospital-free days.23 These results challenge the historical paradigm that septuagenarians, by virtue of age alone, are relatively contraindicated to receive lung transplantation.

Bilateral lung transplantation is generally associated with improved long-term survival compared with single lung transplantation, however, at the expense of higher perioperative morbidity.24, 25 Historically, single lung transplantation was preferred in older patients due to concerns regarding elevated early mortality in bilateral transplantation and the less extensive nature of the single lung operation. Consequently, early guidelines recommended upper age limits of 60 years for bilateral transplants and 65 years for single lung transplant.26 Early data indicated significantly worse 1-year survival among patients older than 60 years who underwent bilateral transplantation.27, 28 Recent evidence has challenged these historical assumptions. In a national registry analysis spanning 2005–2020, Zhou et al. observed no difference in 1-year or 3-year survival between single and bilateral lung transplant recipients in both younger (<70 years) and older (≥70 years) cohorts.13 However, a higher mortality rate was noted at 5 years among single lung transplant recipients. Similarly, Weingarten et al. reported that in patients aged ≥65 years, single-lung transplantation was associated with improved perioperative morbidity and comparable 30-day and 1-year survival relative to bilateral transplantation, although 5-year survival remained inferior.29 Mosher et al. identified single lung transplant operations to be an independent associated risk factor for mortality in age ≥ 65 cohorts.30 Our results are similar to these findings, demonstrating no significant differences in 1-, 3-, or 5-year survival between younger and older candidates who received either single or bilateral lung transplants. However, one notable difference in our cohort was the longer duration of postoperative ECMO support among recipients aged ≥70 years who underwent single lung transplantation, a finding that was not observed after bilateral lung transplantation. In our experience, the postoperative course after single lung transplantation can present unique challenges, including a higher rate of grade 3 primary graft dysfunction compared with bilateral lung transplantation in both age cohorts. Although the frequency of postoperative ECMO use after single lung transplantation was similar between older and younger recipients, older patients who required ECMO support appeared to have a longer recovery period, suggesting reduced physiologic reserve after early graft dysfunction or postoperative respiratory failure. This data suggests that in well-selected candidates aged 70 years and older, both single and bilateral lung transplantation have acceptable short and long-term outcomes, although post-operative recovery after single lung transplantation may be more prolonged when ECMO support is required.

Native lung diagnosis influences post-transplant survival and outcomes, with restrictive disease associated with worse short- and long-term outcomes than obstructive disease.31, 32 However, the relative survival advantage from transplantation is greater in restrictive diseases, as shown by Vock et al., who reported a one-year expected survival benefit of 90% for restrictive lung disease versus 22% for obstructive.33 Patients with restrictive disease who qualify for transplantation tend to have severe disease with a life expectancy of 2–3 years.7 In the Lung Allocation Score era, which prioritizes the sickest patients, restrictive diseases, particularly idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, have become the most common indication for transplantation.13 In our cohort, 50.2% of patients under 70% and 83.6% of those 70 or older had a restrictive diagnosis. Despite a higher proportion of restrictive lung disease in the older cohort, their survival and postoperative outcomes remained comparable to younger recipients.

Chronologic age does not capture the inter-individual variability in physiological reserve, comorbidities, and functional status among transplant candidates. Relying solely on age-based thresholds may unjustly exclude candidates who could significantly benefit from lung transplantation, while at the same time including patients whose physiological age exceeds their chronologic age, potentially leading to poorer outcomes. The degree of frailty has long been recognized as a predictor of adverse outcomes in surgical populations, and an expanding body of literature is now examining its role in lung transplant candidates and recipients.34 A systematic review by Montgomery et al. found frailty prevalence in lung transplant candidates to range from 0% to 58%, depending on the scoring system used.35 This review demonstrated that frailty was associated with higher rates of pre-transplant de-listing and mortality, as well as increased early mortality following transplantation.

Despite these findings, frailty assessments are not yet a routine part of lung transplant evaluations on a national scale. Chronic lung disease patients often exhibit baseline frailty; however, emerging studies suggest that lung transplantation may reverse some aspects of frailty.36 Future research should focus on delineating which components of frailty are reversible post-transplant, and which are predictive of increased morbidity and mortality. Mosher et al. in a Scientific Registry of Transplant Recipients analysis identified several predictors of increased mortality among patients aged ≥65 years, including advanced age, elevated creatinine and bilirubin levels, hospitalization at the time of transplantation, single-lung transplantation, steroid use at the time of transplantation, donor history of diabetes, and donor–recipient cytomegalovirus mismatch.30 We recommend shifting toward an evaluation of patients’ physiological and functional status, using tools such as frailty assessments, instead of relying solely on chronologic age as the cutoff for transplant candidacy.

The transition from the Lung Allocation Score (LAS) to the Lung Composite Allocation Score (CAS) represents a significant shift in lung transplant allocation policy, with important ethical and clinical implications. Under the LAS system, allocation was primarily determined by medical urgency rather than waiting time. This approach inadvertently resulted in a higher proportion of elderly patients receiving transplants, as demonstrated by the increase in recipients aged 70 or older from 2.2% in 2005 to 14.3% in 2022, and with 36.9% of candidates aged 65 or older in 2022.13 In contrast, the CAS model, implemented in 2023, emphasizes the optimization of long-term outcomes. Although this strategy seeks to maximize the overall benefit of scarce donor organs, it may inadvertently deprioritize older candidates despite emerging evidence that carefully selected septuagenarians can achieve outcomes comparable to those of younger cohorts. Although this difference did not reach statistical significance in our single-center cohort, recipients aged 70 or older had a trend for longer waitlist times in the CAS era compared with the LAS era, with median waitlist times of 46.5 vs. 36 days, respectively. This paradigm shift introduces a notable ethical dilemma: while prioritizing long-term graft survival is clinically advantageous, it risks marginalizing a subset of patients who, despite their advanced chronological age, may exhibit favorable physiological profiles and similar post-transplant outcomes compared to younger individuals. Given these considerations, further research is warranted to assess whether the CAS era will lead to reduced access to lung transplant in elderly patients.

At our center, lung transplant candidate selection and perioperative management follow a comprehensive, multidisciplinary approach aimed at optimizing outcomes. Preoperatively, all candidates undergo a frailty assessment conducted by a multidisciplinary team. Our institutional frailty assessment includes six components: hand grip strength, nutritional status, timed up and go (TUG) test, cognitive function, mood, and hemoglobin level (Table 7). We do not apply a strict cutoff score, however, the score is used to inform the overall evaluation of a candidate’s ability to tolerate transplantation and to identify potentially modifiable risk factors. In addition to these measures, we routinely evaluate 6-minute walk distance and nutritional markers, although this is not incorporated into our formal frailty scoring system. Comorbidities were similarly evaluated as part of a holistic assessment rather than through fixed exclusion criteria. Although no specific comorbidity-based exclusion criteria differed between younger and older candidates, comorbid conditions in older patients were assessed more stringently in the context of overall physiologic reserve, frailty, functional status, and anticipated ability to tolerate transplantation. As a general institutional approach, older candidates with more than one major comorbidity in addition to their underlying lung disease were unlikely to be considered suitable candidates for transplantation. Postoperatively, care is guided by enhanced recovery principles, including early extubation protocols, minimization of narcotic and sedative use, implementation of delirium prevention strategies, and early mobilization facilitated by physical and occupational therapy. Patients requiring additional support are transitioned to acute rehabilitation services, where care is closely coordinated through weekly multidisciplinary meetings between our team and the rehabilitation facility to review progress and optimize recovery. Longitudinal care is ensured through close follow up with our dedicated team, supported by a high coordinator to patient ratio, facilitating proactive management of post-transplant needs.

Limitations and future directions

Our study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design limits our ability to establish causal relationships. Secondly, the cohorts were relatively small and there was a discrepancy in the size of the cohorts, 81% of patients were under age 70% and 19% were 70 or older, which has potential to introduce bias by limiting the representativeness and statistical power of the smaller group. Third, as all transplants were conducted at a single center, the results may not be generalizable to other institutions or patient populations. Lastly, a selection bias arises from comparing older (≥70 years) candidates who underwent transplantation with those deemed unsuitable for listing, thereby limiting the broader applicability of our findings to all elderly patients. Further research should explore the impact of the lung composite allocation score era on waitlist times, transplant rates, and outcomes in the elderly population. In addition, efforts to refine selection criteria are imperative to better distinguish which older candidates stand to benefit most from transplantation versus those at higher risk of poor outcomes.

Conclusion

Our single-center experience over a 10-year period shows that carefully selected patients aged ≥70 years can successfully undergo lung transplantation with outcomes comparable to younger recipients. Despite older patients’ increased likelihood of receiving single-lung rather than bilateral transplants, their short- and long-term post-transplant survival, as well as key perioperative outcomes, were similar. These findings support the inclusion of well-selected older candidates in lung transplant programs.
