Surviving the Attack of the Clones
Denis S. Grebenkov

Abstract
We consider a population dynamics model in which each diffusing particle that hits a catalytic surface can split into two independent copies (clones). The particles of such a growing-in-size population search in parallel for a hidden partially reactive target to trigger a reaction event (e.g., a viral attack). We investigate the statistics of the fastest first-reaction time (FRT) among all the particles. We establish a nonlinear integral equation for the survival probability and then analyze the associated probability density of the FRT and its moments. Lower and upper bounds on the mean FRT are then deduced in terms of the system parameters (target reactivity, catalytic rate, diffusivity, etc.). Because autocatalytic replication can rapidly increase the number of searchers, it can substantially accelerate the diffusive search. We solve the nonlinear equations numerically in a basic geometric setting and reveal advantages and limitations on the autocatalytic search.

In this paper, we look at the multi-particle search problem from a
different perspective. Suppose that a single particle diffuses in a
medium that contains catalytic regions, on which the particle can be
replicated. To what extent can such a cloning process accelerate the
diffusive search? At first sight, one might expect that the optimal
strategy is for a single particle to travel directly to the target.
However, as the likelihood of following such direct trajectories is
very small, the mean FPT to the target is dominated by long
explorations of the medium. A more advantageous strategy may thus be
to spend some time near the catalytic region, thereby increasing the
number of searchers through cloning before resuming the search by a
large swarm.

To address this optimization problem quantitatively, we consider a
broad class of diffusion-driven autocatalytic reactions on surfaces

;
Grebenkov26b
;
Grebenkov26c

II.1
Long-time behavior

According to Eq. (
3
), both
P(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})
and

S_{\mathrm{a}}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
exhibit the exponential decay at long times that is
controlled by the principal Laplacian eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}
.
Consequently, the survival probability
S(t|\bm{x}_{0})
is expected to
exhibit the same exponential decay. This expectation can be confirmed
by taking the Laplace transform of Eq. (
6
):

\tilde{S}(p|\bm{x}_{0})=\tilde{S}_{\mathrm{a}}(p|\bm{x}_{0})+{q_{\rm c}}D\int\limits_{\Gamma_{\rm c}}d\bm{x}\,\tilde{P}(\bm{x},p|\bm{x}_{0})\,\mathcal{L}\{S^{2}(t|\bm{x})\}(p),

(18)

where both
\mathcal{L}
and tilde denote the Laplace transform. Both

\tilde{S}_{\mathrm{a}}(p|\bm{x}_{0})
and
\tilde{P}(\bm{x},p|\bm{x}_{0})
as functions of

p
have the largest pole at
-D\lambda_{0}
. Assuming the long-time
behavior
S(t|\bm{x}_{0})\propto e^{-\nu t}
with some rate
\nu>0
, the
largest pole of
\mathcal{L}\{S^{2}(t|\bm{x})\}(p)
is then
-2\nu
. Matching the
dominant poles on both sides of the equation yields
\nu=D\lambda_{0}
.
Moreover, since
\mathcal{L}\{S^{2}(t|\bm{x})\}(p)
has no pole at
-\nu
, we
conclude that

S(t|\bm{x}_{0})\simeq A\,u_{0}(\bm{x}_{0})\,e^{-Dt\lambda_{0}}\qquad(t\to\infty),

(19)

with

A=\int\limits_{\Omega}d\bm{x}\,u_{0}(\bm{x})+{q_{\rm c}}D\int\limits_{\Gamma_{\rm c}}d\bm{x}\,u_{0}(\bm{x})\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}dt\,e^{Dt\lambda_{0}}\,S^{2}(t|\bm{x}),

(20)

where the last factor in the second term is the evaluation of the
residue at
p=-\nu=-D\lambda_{0}
. While the asymptotic form of

S(t|\bm{x}_{0})
is fully determined by the principal eigenmode of the
Laplace operator, the prefactor
A
depends on
S^{2}(t|\bm{x})
, integrated
over the entire range of times.

Interestingly, at long times the catalytic region
\Gamma_{\rm c}
affects
the decay rate exactly as if it were an additional partially reactive
target (with reaction rate
{q_{\rm c}}
). According to the standard
variational principle
Levitin
, one has

\lambda_{0}=\inf\limits_{v\in L^{2}(\Omega)\atop\|v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=1}\left\{\int\limits_{\Omega}|\nabla v|^{2}+{q_{\rm c}}\int\limits_{\Gamma_{\rm c}}|v|^{2}+{q_{\rm a}}\int\limits_{\Gamma_{\rm a}}|v|^{2}\right\}.

(21)

This relation immediately implies that
\lambda_{0}
monotonously
increases with both
{q_{\rm a}}
and
{q_{\rm c}}
. Equation (
21
)
provides a simple variational interpretation of the search
acceleration: increasing either the target reactivity or the catalytic
rate raises the principal eigenvalue, thereby shortening the
characteristic decay time of the survival probability.

Dual representation

As discussed in

\displaystyle\partial_{t}P_{0}

\displaystyle=D\Delta P_{0}\quad(\bm{x}\in\Omega),

(22a)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}P_{0}

\displaystyle=0\quad(\bm{x}\in\Gamma_{\rm c}),

(22b)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}P_{0}+{q_{\rm a}}P_{0}

\displaystyle\partial_{n}P_{0}

\displaystyle=0\quad(\bm{x}\in\Gamma_{\rm r}),

Following the same argument as in

From Eq. (

\displaystyle=J_{0}(t|\bm{x}_{0})+{q_{\rm c}}D\int\limits_{\Gamma_{\rm c}}d\bm{x}\int\limits_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,P_{0}(\bm{x},t^{\prime}|\bm{x}_{0})

(28)

The same bound holds for the MFRT (and higher-order moments):

T_{\mathrm{a}}(\bm{x}_{0})\leq T(\bm{x}_{0})\leq T_{0}(\bm{x}_{0}).

(29)

The upper bound has a simple physical interpretation: autocatalytic
replication can only create additional searchers and therefore cannot
make the search less efficient. In fact, if a particle has reached
the catalytic region, an eventual splitting event may increase the
number of particles and thus speed up the search, as compared to the
case
{q_{\rm c}}=0
when the same particle is just reflected from
\Gamma_{\rm c}

and continues searching alone. In turn, the lower bound means that if
the catalytic region
\Gamma_{\rm c}
was partially reactive (with the rate

{q_{\rm c}}
), then a reaction event on
\Gamma_{\rm c}
would mean the completion
of the search process; however, when
\Gamma_{\rm c}
is catalytic, such a
reaction event on
\Gamma_{\rm c}
leads to splitting of the particle, and
the search process continues.

The lower bound may appear counterintuitive, particularly for very
large catalytic rates: despite the fact that the population of
particles grows extremely fast (see
Grebenkov26a
and
Sec.
II.4
), the MFRT
T(\bm{x}_{0})
remains limited by

T_{\mathrm{a}}(\bm{x}_{0})
and cannot thus significantly decrease, especially if the
starting point
\bm{x}_{0}
is not located on the catalytic region. In
fact, if
\bm{x}_{0}\notin\Gamma_{\rm c}
, the lower bound
T_{\mathrm{a}}(\bm{x}_{0})

approaches a finite limit as
{q_{\rm c}}\to\infty
. The rationale for this
limited improvement is elementary: in order to initiate such a rapid
growth, the first particle needs to reach the catalytic region, and
the required mean time is related to
T_{\mathrm{a}}(\bm{x}_{0})
. In other words,
the initial journey toward the catalytic region constitutes an
irreducible bottleneck that limits the achievable speed-up.
We will illustrate this behavior by numerical examples in
Sec.
III
.

II.3
Regimes of low and high catalytic rates

To determine the dependence of the survival probability and the MFRT
on the catalytic rate
{q_{\rm c}}
, one needs to solve the nonlinear integral
equation (
6
) or the PDE problem (
8
).
While its numerical study for a simple geometric setting is postponed
to Sec.
III
, we briefly sketch some basic properties and
highlight the eventual difficulties.

In the limit of small
{q_{\rm c}}
, the boundary condition (
8b
) is
close to the Neumann condition, and the survival probability

S(t|\bm{x}_{0})
should be close to
S_{0}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
. In fact, as cloning
events are rare, the catalytic region mainly reflects the particles.
More formally, Eq. (
23
) implies immediately that

S_{0}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
is the leading-order approximation for
S(t|\bm{x}_{0})
as

{q_{\rm c}}\to 0
. Moreover, one can apply a perturbation approach to get
higher-order corrections in terms of
S_{0}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
and

P_{0}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})
. Similarly, Eq. (
26
) implies a
two-term approximation for the MFRT:

\displaystyle T(\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=T_{0}(\bm{x}_{0})-{q_{\rm c}}D\int\limits_{\Gamma_{\rm c}}d\bm{x}\,P_{0}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle\times\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}dt\bigl(S_{0}(t|\bm{x})-S_{0}^{2}(t|\bm{x})\bigr)+O(q_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}).

(30)

The opposite limit of large
{q_{\rm c}}
is considerably more subtle; in
fact, even for the conventional survival probability
S_{\mathrm{a}}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
,
the limit
{q_{\rm c}}\to\infty
of the Robin boundary condition is known to
be singular. Dividing the boundary condition (
8b
) by
{q_{\rm c}}

and taking
{q_{\rm c}}\to\infty
lead to the limiting boundary condition

S(t|\bm{x}_{0})=S^{2}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
on
\Gamma_{\rm c}
. Since
S(t|\bm{x}_{0})
is bounded
above by
S_{0}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
, which is strictly less than
1
for any
t>0
, one can discard the solution
S(t|\bm{x}_{0})=1
on
\Gamma_{\rm c}
and thus
get the Dirichlet boundary condition
S(t|\bm{x}_{0})=0
on
\Gamma_{\rm c}
in
the limit. In other words,
S(t|\bm{x}_{0})
is expected to become closer
and closer to
S_{\mathrm{a}}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
as
{q_{\rm c}}\to\infty
. Similarly, the
MFRT
T(\bm{x}_{0})
is expected to be close to
T_{\mathrm{a}}(\bm{x}_{0})
, which
satisfies the conventional Poisson equation:

\displaystyle D\Delta T_{\mathrm{a}}

\displaystyle=-1\quad(\bm{x}_{0}\in\Omega),

(31a)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}T_{\mathrm{a}}+{q_{\rm c}}T_{\mathrm{a}}

\displaystyle=0\quad(\bm{x}_{0}\in\Gamma_{\rm c}),

(31b)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}T_{\mathrm{a}}+{q_{\rm a}}T_{\mathrm{a}}

\displaystyle=0\quad(\bm{x}_{0}\in\Gamma_{\rm a}),

(31c)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}T_{\mathrm{a}}

\displaystyle=0\quad(\bm{x}_{0}\in\Gamma_{\rm r}).

(31d)

The latter admits a standard perturbation analysis. In fact,
substituting
T_{\mathrm{a}}(\bm{x}_{0})=v_{0}(\bm{x}_{0})+v_{1}(\bm{x}_{0})/{q_{\rm c}}+\cdots
into
this equation, one sees that
v_{0}
is the MFRT with the Dirichlet
boundary condition
v_{0}=0
on
\Gamma_{\rm c}
, whereas the first-order
correction
v_{1}
satisfies

\displaystyle\Delta v_{1}

\displaystyle=0\quad(\bm{x}_{0}\in\Omega),

(32a)

\displaystyle v_{1}

\displaystyle=-\partial_{n}v_{0}\quad(\bm{x}_{0}\in\Gamma_{\rm c}),

(32b)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}v_{1}+{q_{\rm a}}v_{1}

\displaystyle=0\quad(\bm{x}_{0}\in\Gamma_{\rm a}),

(32c)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}v_{1}

\displaystyle=0\quad(\bm{x}_{0}\in\Gamma_{\rm r}).

(32d)

In particular, this perturbative analysis implies that if the starting
point is located at
\Gamma_{\rm c}
, one has
T_{\mathrm{a}}|_{\Gamma_{\rm c}}\approx q_{\mathrm{c}}^{-1}(-\partial_{n}v_{0})|_{\Gamma_{\rm c}}
in the leading order. A
similar asymptotic behavior is expected for the MFRT
T(\bm{x}_{0})
. We
emphasize that the above heuristic arguments remain formal and require
rigorous mathematical justification, which lies beyond the scope of
the present work.

II.4
Moderate efficiency of autocatalytic reactions

The competition between cloning events on
\Gamma_{\rm c}
and reaction
events on
\Gamma_{\rm a}
and the consequent long-time behavior of the mean
population size
N(t|\bm{x}_{0})
was investigated in
Grebenkov26a
.
In particular, it was shown the existence of a critical catalytic rate

{q_{\rm c}^{\rm crit}}
that distinguished three scenarios: (i) the subcritical
regime
{q_{\rm c}}<{q_{\rm c}^{\rm crit}}
, in which
N(t|\bm{x}_{0})
vanishes at long times;
(ii) the critical regime
{q_{\rm c}}={q_{\rm c}^{\rm crit}}
when
N(t|\bm{x}_{0})
reaches a
nonzero steady-state; and (iii) the supercritical regime
{q_{\rm c}}>{q_{\rm c}^{\rm crit}}
when
N(t|\bm{x}_{0})
grows exponentially fast as
t\to\infty
. A
mathematical framework to determine the critical catalytic rate

{q_{\rm c}^{\rm crit}}
and its dependence on
{q_{\rm a}}
and on the geometric
configuration was established.

At first glance, it may be surprising that the critical catalytic rate
has not yet appeared in our former analysis. For instance, the
survival probability
S(t|\bm{x}_{0})
exhibits the long-time exponential
decay (
19
) with the rate
\lambda_{0}
, which smoothly
depends on the catalytic rate
{q_{\rm c}}
(as it follows from the
variational principle (
21
)). In other words, the
properties of the FRT do not almost change when
{q_{\rm c}}
crosses the
critical value
{q_{\rm c}^{\rm crit}}
. This apparent paradox has two complementary
explanations. (i) In mathematical terms, the long-time behavior of
the mean population size is controlled by another eigenvalue,

\lambda_{0}^{-}
, that corresponds to the Laplacian eigenvalue problem,
in which
+{q_{\rm c}}
in the Robin boundary condition (
4b
) is
replaced by the
negative
parameter
-{q_{\rm c}}
. As discussed in

Grebenkov26a
, the long-time behavior
N(t|\bm{x}_{0})\propto e^{-Dt\lambda_{0}^{-}}
is very sensitive to the sign of

\lambda_{0}^{-}
, which vanishes at
{q_{\rm c}}={q_{\rm c}^{\rm crit}}
and thus
distinguishes the above three regimes. In contrast, the long-time
behavior of the survival probability
S(t|\bm{x}_{0})
is controlled by the
eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}
, which smoothly depends on
{q_{\rm c}}
. (ii) In the
analysis of the population dynamics, the stochastic process was not
stopped upon the first-reaction time, and the long-time behavior of

N(t|\bm{x}_{0})
results from the competition between (usually many)
cloning and reaction events. In turn, the analysis of the FRT imposes
to stop the stochastic process after the first reaction event so that
the effect of the catalytic rate
{q_{\rm c}}
on the FRT is substantially
weaker.

III
Example of an interval

To illustrate the effect of autocatalytic dynamics onto the
first-reaction time distribution, we consider a basic setting of an
interval,
\Omega=(0,L)
, with the catalytic endpoint
\Gamma_{\rm c}=\{0\}
and the target
\Gamma_{\rm a}=\{L\}
. Although we focus on the
interval for clarity, the analysis extends straightforwardly to
concentric annuli and spherical shells. In fact, the rotational
invariance of these domains makes all computations effectively
one-dimensional so that only technical details would change (see,
e.g.,
Grebenkov26c
for a concentric annulus). In this paper,
we focus on the interval to keep the presentation as transparent as
possible.

In this case, the critical catalytic rate is
Grebenkov26a

{q_{\rm c}^{\rm crit}}=\frac{{q_{\rm a}}}{1+{q_{\rm a}}L}\,.

(33)

As discussed in Sec.
II.4
, this threshold distinguishes
three asymptotic regimes for the mean population size but it is
expected to play no role in the analysis of the FRT distribution. We
will confirm this statement by inspecting different values of the
catalytic rate
{q_{\rm c}}
.
In the following numerical illustrations, we set
L=1
and
D=1

to fix length and time units.

III.1
Survival probability and PDF

Since the catalytic boundary
\Gamma_{\rm c}
of an interval is reduced to a

23

and

\displaystyle S(t|x_{0})

\displaystyle=S_{0}(t|x_{0})-{q_{\rm c}}D\int\limits_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,P_{0}(0,t^{\prime}|x_{0})

III.2
Mean FRT

After the visual inspection of the survival probabilities in
Sec.
III.1
, we focus on the analysis of the MFRT. To
check the quality of the two-sided bound (
29
), we
recall that the MFRT
T_{\mathrm{a}}(x_{0})
has a simple quadratic form:

T_{\mathrm{a}}(x_{0})=-\frac{x_{0}^{2}}{2D}+\frac{L(2+{q_{\rm a}}L)(1+{q_{\rm c}}x_{0})}{2D({q_{\rm a}}+{q_{\rm c}}+{q_{\rm a}}{q_{\rm c}}L)}\,.

(36)

Expectedly,
T_{\mathrm{a}}(x_{0})
is a monotonously decreasing function of
both
{q_{\rm a}}
and
{q_{\rm c}}
. In particular, its infimum with respect to

{q_{\rm c}}
corresponds to the limit
{q_{\rm c}}\to\infty
:

\lim\limits_{{q_{\rm c}}\to\infty}T_{\mathrm{a}}(x_{0})=T_{\rm min}(x_{0})=\frac{x_{0}}{2D}\biggl(L-x_{0}+\frac{L}{1+{q_{\rm a}}L}\biggr).

(37)

As a consequence, if
x_{0}>0
, even an extremely fast growth of the
population in the limit
{q_{\rm c}}\to\infty
cannot diminish the MFRT

T(x_{0})
below this value. This is expected: in order to trigger
cloning events, the first particle has to reach the catalytic region.

Let us now determine the dependence of
T(x_{0})
on the starting point

x_{0}
. For this purpose, we first note that setting
{q_{\rm c}}=0
to
Eq. (
36
) yields

T_{0}(x_{0})=\frac{L^{2}-x_{0}^{2}}{2D}+\frac{L}{D{q_{\rm a}}}\,.

(38)

In addition, the Green’s function for the interval
(0,L)
is

\displaystyle D\tilde{P}(x,0|x_{0})=\frac{1}{{q_{\rm c}}+{q_{\rm a}}+{q_{\rm c}}{q_{\rm a}}L}

(39)

\displaystyle\times\begin{cases}(1+{q_{\rm c}}x)(1+{q_{\rm a}}(L-x_{0}))\quad(0\leq x\leq x_{0}\leq L),\cr(1+{q_{\rm c}}x_{0})(1+{q_{\rm a}}(L-x))\quad(0\leq x_{0}\leq x\leq L).\end{cases}

Setting
x=0
yields

D\tilde{P}(0,0|x_{0})=\frac{1+{q_{\rm a}}(L-x_{0})}{{q_{\rm c}}+{q_{\rm a}}+{q_{\rm a}}{q_{\rm c}}L}\,,

(40)

whereas
{q_{\rm c}}=0
implies

D\tilde{P}_{0}(0,0|x_{0})=L-x_{0}+\frac{1}{{q_{\rm a}}}\,.

(41)

As a consequence, Eq. (
26
) for the MFRT becomes

T(x_{0})=T_{0}(x_{0})-{q_{\rm c}}(L-x_{0}+1/{q_{\rm a}})\bigl[T(0)-T^{(2)}(0)\bigr],

(42)

where
T^{(2)}(0)
was defined in Eq. (
15
). In
particular, one has at
x_{0}=0
:

T(0)=\frac{T_{0}(0)+{q_{\rm c}}(L+1/{q_{\rm a}})T^{(2)}(0)}{1+{q_{\rm c}}(L+1/{q_{\rm a}})}\,,

(43)

where
T_{0}(0)=L^{2}/(2D)+L/(D{q_{\rm a}})
. Expressing the last term via

T(0)
, one gets

T(x_{0})=T(0)\biggl(1-\frac{x_{0}}{L+1/{q_{\rm a}}}\biggr)+\frac{x_{0}T_{0}(0)}{L+1/{q_{\rm a}}}-\frac{x_{0}^{2}}{2D}\,.

(44)

We observe that the MFRT at any point
x_{0}
can be easily determined
from
T(0)
.

The explicit quadratic dependence of the MFRT on
x_{0}
has immediate
consequences on the search optimality. Since
d^{2}T(x_{0})/dx_{0}^{2}=-1/D<0
, the function
T(x_{0})
reaches a maximum at
x_{0}^{*}=D(T_{0}(0)-T(0))/(L+1/{q_{\rm a}})
. Using the explicit form of
T_{0}(0)
, it
is easy to check that
x_{0}
lies in the interval
(0,L)
, i.e.,
starting the particle from
x_{0}^{*}
indeed maximizes the MFRT. In
turn, the minimum is reached at either of the endpoints:
0
or
L
.
Intuitively, one may expect that placing the starting point on the
target would always be an optimal solution. However, this is not true
for weakly reactive targets. Indeed, setting
x_{0}=L
, we find

T(L)=\frac{1}{1+{q_{\rm a}}L}\biggl(T(0)+\frac{L^{2}}{2D}\biggr).

(45)

The condition
T(L)\geq T(0)
is equivalent to
T(0)\leq L/(2{q_{\rm a}}D)
. When the catalytic rate
{q_{\rm c}}
grows to infinity, the MFRT
T(0)

vanishes (see Sec.
II.3
). As a consequence, for any
partially reactive target (i.e.,
{q_{\rm a}}<\infty
), the above inequality
can be satisfied for a sufficiently large
{q_{\rm c}}
. In other words,
starting on the catalytic region is more advantageous than starting on
the target, if the catalytic rate is large enough. This explicit
result illustrates the optimality dilemma formulated in
Sec.
I
: if the target is partially reactive, the
particle may need to return to it a number of times before the
successful reaction; in this situation, it may be more efficient to
cross the interval, to spend some time to produce many clones, and
then to trigger the reaction on the target by a large swarm. This
conclusion evidently fails for a perfectly reactive target with
{q_{\rm a}}=\infty
.

In the following, we focus on
T(0)
that incorporates the dependence
on the catalytic rate
{q_{\rm c}}
. This MFRT is computed numerically by
integrating
S(t|0)
over
t
(see Eq. (
12
) and
Appendix
A
).
When the catalytic rate
{q_{\rm c}}
is small, one can use the perturbative
approach described in Sec.
II.3
; in particular,
Eq. (
30
) gives the two-term approximation:

T(0)\approx T_{0}(0)-{q_{\rm c}}(L+1/{q_{\rm a}})\biggl\{T_{0}(0)-\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}dt\,S_{0}^{2}(t|0)\biggr\}+O(q_{\rm c}^{2}),

(46)

where we used Eq. (
41
). Higher-order corrections can also
be evaluated in a systematic way.
In turn, an accurate evaluation of the large-
{q_{\rm c}}
asymptotic behavior
is much more challenging and presents an open problem even for the
interval (see Sec.
IV
).

Figure
5
presents the MFRT
T(0)
as a function of the
catalytic rate
{q_{\rm c}}
. When the target is perfectly reactive (panel
(a),
{q_{\rm a}}=\infty
), the autocatalytic dynamics with moderate
{q_{\rm c}}

provides only a minor reduction of the MFRT as compared to the
conventional search (
{q_{\rm c}}=0
): e.g., even at
{q_{\rm c}}=1
, we have

T(0)\approx 0.37
, whereas
T_{0}(0)=0.5
. In turn, the search
efficiency is improved as
{q_{\rm c}}
increases; for instance, at
{q_{\rm c}}=10
, one achieves a four-fold reduction of the MFRT, from
T_{0}(0)=0.5
to
T(0)\approx 0.13
.

The situation changes dramatically for weakly reactive targets (panel
(b),
{q_{\rm a}}=0.1
). Because successful reaction typically requires
multiple returns to the target, the MFRT
T_{0}(0)
in the conventional
search is large:
T_{0}(0)=10.5
according to
Eq. (
38
). In this scenario, cloning of particles on

\Gamma_{\rm c}
can significantly improve the search. For instance, at
{q_{\rm c}}=1
, we find
T(0)\approx 2.23
, i.e., almost a five-fold reduction
as compared to
T_{0}(0)
. The improvement becomes even more pronounced
as the catalytic rate increases. We conclude that the autocatalytic
dynamics is more advantageous for finding targets with low reactivity.
This result highlights that autocatalytic replication is particularly
effective when repeated unsuccessful encounters dominate the search
process.

IV
Discussion and conclusion

The present work extends classical first-passage theory by
incorporating autocatalytic branching events localized on the
boundary.
Branching processes have many applications in disciplines as diverse
as physics, chemistry, biology, social sciences, and risk management

Harris
;
Athreya
;
Williams
;
Kimmel
;
Murrey
. Branching events in the
bulk
Kolmogorov47
;
Sevastyanov58
;
Skorohod64
naturally lead to
nonlinear PDEs, including the seminal Fisher-KKP equation

Fisher37
;
Kolmogorov37
and many other nonlinear
reaction-diffusion equations
Grindrod
. Over the past three
decades, significant efforts were dedicated to develop a rigorous
mathematical description of branching events on lower-dimensional
subsets in the continuous setting

LeGall
;
Dynkin
;
DelGross76
;
Dawson99
;
Klenke00
;
Englander04
;
Delmas05
;
Morters05
;
Englander07
;
Bocharov14

and on a subset of catalytic sites for lattice random walks

Redner84
;
Albeverio98
;
Bogachev98
;
Kesten03
;
Vatutin04
;
Vatutin07
;
Hu12
;
Topchi13
;
Bulinskaya18
;
Bauer21
.
In particular, the opposite effects of branching and annihilation
events onto the population dynamics were thoroughly analyzed (see,
e.g,
Canet04a
;
Canet04b
;
Odor04
;
Grebenkov26a
and reference
therein). In turn, the first-passage statistics within the realm of
autocatalytic dynamics were not studied in depth, to our knowledge.

We investigated diffusion inside a bounded domain whose boundary
contains (up to) three distinct regions: a partially reactive target

\Gamma_{\rm a}
, a catalytic region
\Gamma_{\rm c}
, and the remaining reflecting
part
\Gamma_{\rm r}
. When there is no catalytic region (i.e.,
\Gamma_{\rm c}=\emptyset
), finding the survival probability and the distribution of
the associated first-reaction time is the classical problem in the
theory of diffusion-controlled reactions

Redner
;
Schuss
;
Metzler
;
Masoliver
;
Lindenberg
;
Grebenkov
;
Grebenkov23n
.
Because the catalytic region generates additional searchers through
replication, its presence is expected to accelerate the search
process. The central quantity of our study was the first-reaction
time
{\mathcal{T}}
on the target by any of the particles in the population. We
derived two equivalent representations – the integral equation
(
6
) and the PDE (
8
) – for the survival
probability
S(t|\bm{x}_{0})={\mathbb{P}}_{\bm{x}_{0}}\{{\mathcal{T}}>t\}
that fully
characterizes the random variable
{\mathcal{T}}
. Accounting for cloning events
on the catalytic region
\Gamma_{\rm c}
led to a nonlinear term in the
integral equation and to a nonlinear Robin-type boundary condition in
PDE. In particular, even though the mean first-reaction time

T(\bm{x}_{0})
satisfies the Poisson equation (
17
), as in
the conventional setting, this PDE problem is not self-contained,
given that the boundary condition (
17b
) includes the
integral of the squared survival probability. These nonlinear
features were the major challenges in the analysis of the
first-reaction time. Nevertheless, we managed to obtain the lower and
the upper bounds for the survival probability
S(t|\bm{x}_{0})
and,
consequently, for all the positive-order moments of
{\mathcal{T}}
. We also
deduced the long-time behavior of the survival probability and
explained that the critical catalytic rate governs the long-time
population dynamics but not the statistics of the first successful
reaction. The regimes of both low and high catalytic rates were
briefly discussed. Our results therefore demonstrate that localized
autocatalytic replication fundamentally alters first-passage
statistics without changing the underlying diffusion operator.

In order to get a deeper insight onto the dependence of the FRT on the
autocatalytic dynamics, we focused on a simple yet representative
example of the interval
(0,L)
, whose two endpoints were the
catalytic region and the target. We developed a numerical scheme for
solving the integral equation to access the survival probability

S(t|x_{0})
, the probability density of the FRT, and its mean value

T(x_{0})
. In particular, we further showed how
T(x_{0})
can be
expressed in terms of
T(0)
and then analyzed the optimality of the
MFRT; in particular, we argued that starting from the target is not
necessarily the optimal strategy when the reaction rate
{q_{\rm a}}
is
finite. This observation may be particularly relevant for
diffusion-controlled biochemical reactions, where reaction
probabilities are often much smaller than unity.

Our main focus was on the dependence of
T(0)
on two parameters: the
catalytic rate
{q_{\rm c}}
and the reaction rate
{q_{\rm a}}
. For perfectly (or
highly) reactive targets (with infinite or large
{q_{\rm a}}
), the effect of
autocatalytic dynamics with small or moderate
{q_{\rm c}}
was minor; indeed,
the MFRT
T(0)
could be significantly reduced only at large
{q_{\rm c}}
.
The situation was drastically different for weakly reactive targets
(small
{q_{\rm a}}
). Here, even a weak autocatalytic dynamics could
significantly decrease
T(0)
. We anticipate that our conclusions
that were obtained for the interval, remain qualitatively valid in
more general settings. For instance, an extension to concentric
circular annuli and spherical shells is rather straightforward due to
their rotational invariance. Moreover, as most chemical and
biophysical applications deal with small targets, the cloning
mechanism can present a considerable advantage, as in our setting of a
weakly reactive target. Further investigation of this small-target
limit presents a promising perspective of this work.

Another important open problem concerns the asymptotic behavior of the
survival probability and the MFRT at large catalytic rate
{q_{\rm c}}
. Even
in the case of an interval, for which the “ingredients”
S_{0}(t|0)

and
P_{0}(0,t|0)
determining the integral equation
(
35
) are explicitly known, this problem turned out
to be surprisingly difficult. (i) From the population dynamics
perspective, the mean number of particles grows exponentially fast,
approximately as
e^{q_{\rm c}^{2}Dt}
, that prohibits Monte Carlo
simulations. In this regime, it may be instructive to compare the
autocatalytic search to the large-
N
asymptotic behavior of the
extremal statistics for a fixed-size population (with
N
particles).
(ii) As we argued in Appendix
B
,
S(t|0)\approx S_{0}(t|0)=1+O(e^{-C/t})
at very short times; as a consequence, one
can linearize the integral equation for
u(t)=1-S(t|0)
but its
solution exhibits a steep exponential growth,
\propto e^{q_{\rm c}^{2}Dt}
, until the nonlinear term becomes relevant. As a consequence, an
accurate numerical solution of the nonlinear equation
(
35
) with large
{q_{\rm c}}
via standard quadratures is
computationally demanding due to the need of using extremely small
timesteps, whereas the numerical results are sensitive to round-off
errors.
(iii) At short times, one has
P_{0}(0,t|0)\approx 1/\sqrt{\pi Dt}
, so
that the integral term is close to the fractional integral operator of
order
1/2
. Its formal inversion allows one to recast the original
problem at short times as a forced fractional logistic problem:

{\mathcal{D}}_{t}^{\frac{1}{2}}u=f+qu(1-u),

(48)

where
q\propto{q_{\rm c}}
, the forcing term
f(t)
is related to

S_{0}(t|0)
, and
{\mathcal{D}}_{t}^{\frac{1}{2}}
is the fractional
derivative of order
1/2
. Equation (
48
) reveals
an unexpected connection between autocatalytic search and nonlinear
fractional dynamical systems. In contrast to the classical setting of
forced logistic equation, which is integrable, the solution of the
fractional logistic equation (
48
) depends on the
entire history, i.e., it is effectively an infinite-dimensional
dynamical system, which may exhibit rich dynamical behavior,
especially for large
q
. Further analysis of the asymptotic behavior
at large
{q_{\rm c}}
is an interesting open problem.

Finally, we stress that the considered model of the autocatalytic
dynamics can naturally be generalized in different ways: (i) If each
branching event produces
m
particles, the term
S^{2}(t|\bm{x})
in the
integral equation (
6
) is replaced by
S^{m}(t|\bm{x})
;
moreover, one can randomly pick the branching order
\hat{m}
from a
given distribution, in which case one deals with the expectation
{\mathbb{E}}\{S^{\hat{m}}(t|\bm{x})\}
. (ii) In many applications, the particles have a
finite lifetime (e.g., radioactive decay), or diffuse in a reactive
medium that can spontaneously destroy the particle

Yuste13
;
Meerson15
;
Grebenkov17
; such “mortal” particles may
fail to find the target due to their death or passivation; the
inclusion of a finite lifetime with a rate
\nu
can be realized by
adding the term
-\nu S
to the right-hand side of the diffusion
equation (
8a
). (iii) Throughout this work, we assumed
that the search was initiated with a single particle; as the
conventional diffusion-controlled reactions are described by linear
equations, a passage from a single initial particle to a configuration
with many particles is straightforward; for instance, the distribution
of the fastest FRT
{\mathcal{T}}_{N}
for
N
independent particles started from

\bm{x}_{0}
is fully determined as
{\mathbb{P}}_{\bm{x}_{0}}\{{\mathcal{T}}_{N}>t\}=[S_{0}(t|\bm{x}_{0})]^{N}
, in terms of the survival probability
S_{0}(t|\bm{x}_{0})

for a single particle; in sharp contrast, the nonlinear nature of
branching events makes the impact of multiple initial particles highly
sophisticated. (iv) Finally, the ordinary diffusion with constant
diffusivity
D
can be replaced by a more general diffusion process in
a heterogeneous medium or in the presence of an external potential, as
well as anomalous diffusions (like the fractional diffusion equation).
All these extensions open several promising research directions.
More broadly, the present work establishes a framework for
incorporating localized branching mechanisms into first-passage
theory. We expect that similar ideas will prove useful in stochastic
population dynamics, intracellular transport, catalytic reactions, and
search processes involving self-replicating agents.

Numerical solution

This appendix summarizes the mathematical ingredients used in the
numerical analysis of autocatalytic reactions and related FRTs on the
interval
(0,L)
with the catalytic endpoint
\Gamma_{\rm c}=\{0\}
and the
reactive endpoint
\Gamma_{\rm a}=\{L\}
: the Laplacian eigenbasis and the
propagator (Sec.
A.1
), a numerical method for
solving integral equations (Sec.
A.2
), and an
independent Monte Carlo technique (Sec.
A.3
).

A.1
Spectral representations

The Laplacian eigenvalues and eigenfunctions are

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{k}^{2}/L^{2},

(49a)

\displaystyle u_{k}(x)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2}{L}}\beta_{k}\biggl(\cos(\alpha_{k}x/L)+\frac{h_{1}}{\alpha_{k}}\sin(\alpha_{k}x/L)\biggr),

(49b)

where
h_{1}={q_{\rm c}}L
,
h_{2}={q_{\rm a}}L
,

\beta_{k}=\biggl(\frac{\alpha_{k}^{2}}{\alpha_{k}^{2}+h_{1}+h_{1}^{2}+h_{2}(\alpha_{k}^{2}+h_{1}^{2})/(\alpha_{k}^{2}+h_{2}^{2})}\biggr)^{1/2}

(50)

are the normalization constants, and
\alpha_{k}
are the positive
solutions of the equation:

\frac{\tan(\alpha_{k})}{\alpha_{k}}=\frac{h_{1}+h_{2}}{\alpha_{k}^{2}-h_{1}h_{2}}\qquad(k=0,1,2,\ldots).

(51)

The integral of an eigenfunction over the interval reads

\int\limits_{0}^{L}dx\,u_{k}(x)=\sqrt{2L}\frac{\beta_{k}}{\alpha_{k}^{2}}\left(h_{1}+(-1)^{k}h_{2}\sqrt{\frac{\alpha_{k}^{2}+h_{1}^{2}}{\alpha_{k}^{2}+h_{2}^{2}}}\right).

(52)

In the special case of a perfectly reactive target (
{q_{\rm a}}=\infty
),
Eqs. (
50
,
51
) are reduced to

\beta_{k}=\biggl(\frac{\alpha_{k}^{2}}{\alpha_{k}^{2}+h_{1}+h_{1}^{2}}\biggr)^{1/2}

(53)

and

\frac{\tan(\alpha_{k})}{\alpha_{k}}=-\frac{1}{h_{1}}\,.

(54)

These eigenmodes provide spectral representations of the propagator

P(x,t|x_{0})
and the survival probability
S_{\mathrm{a}}(t|x_{0})
via their
spectral expansions; for instance,

\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{a}}(t|x_{0})=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}e^{-Dt\lambda_{k}}u_{k}(x_{0})\int\limits_{0}^{L}dx\,u_{k}(x).

Truncating these expansions after
K
terms allows one to accurately
compute them for any
t>\delta
with
\delta
such that

D\delta\lambda_{K}\gg 1
. For
x=x_{0}=0
, we have the following
short-time behavior, which follows from the exact propagator on the
half-line:

P(0,t|0)\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi Dt}}-{q_{\rm c}}\mathrm{erfcx}\bigl({q_{\rm c}}\sqrt{Dt}\bigr)\quad(t\to 0),

(55)

with exponentially small corrections, where the last term is actually
the next-order correction, given that
\mathrm{erfcx}(z)\approx 1+O(z)
as

z\to 0
(here
\mathrm{erfcx}(z)=e^{z^{2}}\mathrm{erfc}(z)
is the scaled
complementary error function).

The Laplace transforms of
P(x,t|x_{0})
and
S_{\mathrm{a}}(t|x_{0})
admit a
fully explicit form. In particular, one has

D\tilde{P}(0,p|0)=\frac{{q_{\rm a}}\sinh(\alpha L)+\alpha\cosh(\alpha L)}{(\alpha^{2}+{q_{\rm a}}{q_{\rm c}})\sinh(\alpha L)+\alpha({q_{\rm a}}+{q_{\rm c}})\cosh(\alpha L)}

(56)

and

\tilde{S}_{\mathrm{a}}(p|0)=\frac{[\alpha\sinh(\alpha L)+{q_{\rm a}}(\cosh(\alpha L)-1)]/(D\alpha)}{(\alpha^{2}+{q_{\rm a}}{q_{\rm c}})\sinh(\alpha L)+\alpha({q_{\rm a}}+{q_{\rm c}})\cosh(\alpha L)}\,,

(57)

where
\alpha=\sqrt{p/D}
. In turn,
D\tilde{P}_{0}(0,p|0)
and

\tilde{S}_{0}(p|0)
follow from these expressions by setting
{q_{\rm c}}=0
.
In the limit
p\to\infty
, we have then

D\tilde{P}_{0}(0,p|0)\simeq\frac{1}{\alpha}+\frac{2}{\alpha}\,\frac{\alpha-{q_{\rm a}}}{\alpha+{q_{\rm a}}}e^{-2\alpha L}+O(e^{-4\alpha L}).

(58)

For a finite
{q_{\rm a}}
, the ratio
(\alpha-{q_{\rm a}})/(\alpha+{q_{\rm a}})
is close
to
1
as
p\to\infty
, from which the short-time behavior follows:

P_{0}(0,t|0)\simeq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi Dt}}\bigl(1+2e^{-L^{2}/(Dt)}+\cdots\bigr)

(59)

(note that these two leading-order terms are independent of
{q_{\rm a}}
).
Similarly, for a finite
{q_{\rm a}}
, one has

\tilde{S}_{0}(p|0)\simeq\frac{1-2e^{-\alpha L}}{p}+O(e^{-2\alpha L}),

(60)

and the Laplace transform inversion yields the short-time behavior

S_{0}(t|0)\approx 1-\frac{4\sqrt{Dt}}{\sqrt{\pi}L}e^{-L^{2}/(4Dt)}.

(61)

In the special case
{q_{\rm c}}=0
and
{q_{\rm a}}=\infty
, the spectrum is
fully explicit:

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}

\displaystyle=\pi^{2}(k+1/2)^{2}/L^{2},

(62a)

\displaystyle u_{k}(x)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2/L}\cos(\pi(k+1/2)x/L),

(62b)

so that

\displaystyle P_{0}(0,t|0)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{L}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}e^{-\pi^{2}(k+1/2)^{2}Dt/L^{2}},

(63a)

\displaystyle S_{0}(t|0)

\displaystyle=2\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}\frac{e^{-\pi^{2}(k+1/2)^{2}Dt/L^{2}}}{\pi(k+1/2)}\,.

(63b)

Using the Poisson summation formula, one also gets equivalent
representations, which are more suitable at short times:

\displaystyle P_{0}(t|0)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi Dt}}\biggl(1+2\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}e^{-k^{2}L^{2}/(Dt)}\biggr),

(64a)

\displaystyle S_{0}(t|0)

\displaystyle=1-2\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}\mathrm{erfc}((k+1/2)L/\sqrt{Dt}).

(64b)

In particular, one recovers the short-time behavior
(
61
).

A.2
Quadrature solver

We need to solve numerically the nonlinear equation
(
35
) with
x_{0}=0
. Following

Grebenkov26c
, we introduce

p(t)={q_{\rm c}}DP_{0}(0,t|0)\,\sqrt{t}

(65)

to remove the weak square-root singularity of the propagator

P_{0}(0,t|0)
at short times. In fact, according to
Eq. (
55
), we have
p(0)={q_{\rm c}}\sqrt{D}/\sqrt{\pi}
.
Discretizing the time interval
(0,t)
into
k
subintervals of
duration
\delta=t/k
, we get for
x_{0}=0

S(k\delta|0)\approx S_{0}(k\delta|0)+\sum\limits_{j=0}^{k}w_{j}\bigl[S^{2}((k-j)\delta|0)-S((k-j)\delta|0)\bigr],

(66)

with the quadrature weights
w_{j}

\displaystyle w_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{a_{j}+a_{j-1}}{2}\qquad(j=0,1,\ldots,k),

(67a)

\displaystyle a_{j}

\displaystyle=\bigl(\sqrt{t_{j+1}}-\sqrt{t_{j}}\bigr)\bigl[p(j\delta)+p((j+1)\delta)\bigr],

(67b)

and
a_{-1}=a_{k}=0
(see more details in
Grebenkov26c
). In
the shown numerical examples, the time step was set to
\delta=10^{-3}
.
Isolating the
j=0
contribution yields the quadratic equation,
whose solution is

\displaystyle S(k\delta|0)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{2f_{k}}{1+w_{0}+\sqrt{(1+w_{0})^{2}-4w_{0}f_{k}}}\,,

(68a)

\displaystyle f_{k}

\displaystyle=S_{0}(k\delta|0)+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{k}w_{j}S^{2}((k-j)\delta|0).

(68b)

Once the survival probability
S(t|0)
has been computed, the solution
for any starting position
x_{0}>0
follows directly from
Eq. (
35
). In addition, the mean FRT can be
approximated as

T(0)\approx\delta\sum\limits_{k=0}^{K}S(k\delta|0),

(69)

with a large enough
K
. Numerically, the summation is continued
until the survival probability falls below a prescribed tolerance.
(e.g.,
10^{-4}
). For the computation of
T(0)
shown in
Figs.
5
and
6
, we first determined

such that

for

,
and then set
\delta=t_{\rm max}/10^{4}
. This adaptive procedure
naturally employs smaller timesteps for larger catalytic rates.
Numerical convergence was verified by repeating the calculations with
a timestep reduced by a factor of two (results shown by crosses).

In turn, the integral equation (
25
) is
linear

with respect to
J(t|x_{0})
and can thus be solved by standard
deconvolution techniques (e.g., via a discrete FFT transformation).
However, we keep using the above direct scheme even for this equation.
Here, we simply write

\displaystyle J(k\delta|0)

\displaystyle\approx J_{0}(k\delta|0)

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{j=0}^{k}w_{j}\bigl[2S((k-j)\delta|0)-1\bigr]J((k-j)\delta|0)),

from which

\displaystyle J(k\delta|0)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{1+w_{0}(1-2S(k\delta|0))}\biggl[J_{0}(k\delta|0)

(70)

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{k}w_{j}(2S((k-j)\delta|0)-1)J((k-j)\delta|0))\biggr].

In practice, we use this scheme at short times; in turn, at longer

from a

J(k\delta|0)\approx\frac{S(k\delta|0)-S((k-1)\delta|0)}{\delta}\,.

(71)

A.3
Monte Carlo simulations

For validation purposes, we also perform independent Monte Carlo
simulations of a random walk on the interval
(0,L)
. For this
purpose, we discretize the interval with a step
a
and introduce the
associated timestep
\delta=a^{2}/(2D)
(we set
a=0.005
). When
the particle is on the catalytic site (
x=0
), it can either jump to
the neighboring site
a
with probability
1-p_{\mathrm{c}}
, or be split
into two particles with the probability
p_{\mathrm{c}}=a{q_{\rm c}}/(1+a{q_{\rm c}})
.
This corresponds to a standard probabilistic interpretation of partial
reactivity
{q_{\rm c}}
with the Robin boundary condition (see, e.g.,

Grebenkov03
). Simulations are advanced with the timestep

\delta
for all present particles, until at least one particle
reaches the reactive endpoint at
x=L
. Here, the particle either
reacts with the probability
p_{\mathrm{a}}=a{q_{\rm a}}/(1+a{q_{\rm a}})
and the
simulation stops, or it moves to the neighboring site
L-a
to
continue diffusion. Although more efficient algorithms have been
developed for modeling autocatalytic dynamics (see, e.g.,

Grebenkov26c
), this basic scheme was sufficient for our
validation purposes. To get adequate statistical accuracy, the
simulation is repeated
M=10^{4}
times.

A.4
Spectral ODE solver

To verify that the numerical solution of the nonlinear integral
equation is independent of the discretization strategy, we also
implemented an alternative solver based on spectral decomposition.
Since both functions
S_{\mathrm{a}}(t|0)
and
P(0,t|0)
admit spectral
representations, one can reduce the nonlinear integral equation to a
system of nonlinear ordinary differential equations (ODEs). Let us
consider a nonlinear Volterra equation

u(t)=f(t)+\int\limits_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,g(t-t^{\prime})\,F(u(t^{\prime})),

(72)

where
F(z)
is a prescribed function, and
f(t)
and
g(t)
can be
accurately approximated via truncated exponential expansions:

f(t)=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{K}a_{k}e^{-b_{k}t},\quad g(t)=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{K}c_{k}e^{-b_{k}t}.

(73)

Setting

y_{k}(t)=a_{k}e^{-b_{k}t}+c_{k}\int\limits_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,e^{-b_{k}(t-t^{\prime})}\,F(u(t^{\prime})),

(74)

we have

u(t)=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{K}y_{k}(t).

(75)

Evaluating the time derivatives of
y_{k}(t)
, one gets the system of
nonlinear ODEs:

y_{k}^{\prime}(t)=-b_{k}y_{k}+c_{k}F(y_{0}(t)+\cdots+y_{K}(t)).

(76)

The resulting ODE system can be integrated using standard adaptive
Runge-Kutta methods. Here, we solved these equations iteratively with
a small timestep
\delta
by using Eq. (
74
):

\displaystyle y_{k}(t+\delta)

\displaystyle=a_{k}e^{-b_{k}(t+\delta)}+c_{k}\int\limits_{0}^{t+\delta}dt^{\prime}\,e^{-b_{k}(t+\delta-t^{\prime})}\,F(u(t^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=e^{-b_{k}\delta}y_{k}(t)+c_{k}\int\limits_{t}^{t+\delta}dt^{\prime}\,e^{-b_{k}(t+\delta-t^{\prime})}\,F(u(t^{\prime}))

\displaystyle\approx e^{-b_{k}\delta}y_{k}(t)+c_{k}\frac{1-e^{-b_{k}\delta}}{b_{k}}F(u(t)).

This numerical scheme was efficient for solving
Eq. (
35
), in which
S_{0}(t|0)
and
P_{0}(0,t|0)

admit easily accessible spectral expansions. It was accurate for
moderate
{q_{\rm c}}
but it required very small time steps for large
{q_{\rm c}}
.
This scheme was only used for validation purposes (results not shown).

Appendix B
Short-time behavior

In this Appendix, we sketch some preliminary results on the short-time
behavior. Setting
\bar{S}(t)=1-S(t|0)
, we first rewrite
Eq. (
35
) as

\bar{S}(t)=1-S_{0}(t|0)+{q_{\rm c}}D\int\limits_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,P_{0}(0,t-t^{\prime}|0)\,\bar{S}(t^{\prime})\bigl(1-\bar{S}(t^{\prime})\bigr).

(77)

According to the asymptotic relation (
59
,

61
), one has
P_{0}(0,t|0)\approx 1/\sqrt{\pi Dt}
,
whereas
1-S_{0}(t|0)
is exponentially small at short times. At short
times,
\bar{S}(t)
is thus small, so that the nonlinear term can be
neglected, and the approximate linearized equation reads

\bar{S}(t)\approx f(t)+{q_{\rm c}}\sqrt{D/\pi}\int\limits_{0}^{t}\frac{dt^{\prime}}{\sqrt{t-t^{\prime}}}\,\bar{S}(t^{\prime}),

(78)

with

f(t)=\frac{4\sqrt{Dt}}{\sqrt{\pi}L}e^{-L^{2}/(4Dt)}

(79)

representing
1-S_{0}(t|0)
. The above linearized equation can be
solved by applying the Laplace transform. Using
\mathcal{L}\{1/\sqrt{\pi t}\}=1/\sqrt{p}
and

\tilde{f}(p)=2\frac{e^{-L\sqrt{p/D}}}{p}\biggl(1+\frac{1}{L\sqrt{p/D}}\biggr),

(80)

we get

\tilde{\bar{S}}(p)\approx\frac{2e^{-L\sqrt{p/D}}(1+L\sqrt{p/D})}{p^{3/2}L/\sqrt{D}(1-{q_{\rm c}}\sqrt{D/p})}\,.

(81)

Using the identities

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{-1}\biggl\{\frac{e^{-a\sqrt{p}}}{p(b+\sqrt{p})}\biggr\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{b}\mathrm{erfc}\bigl(a/\sqrt{4t})

(82)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{b}e^{-a^{2}/(4t)}\mathrm{erfcx}\bigl(a/\sqrt{4t}+b\sqrt{t}\bigr)

and

\mathcal{L}^{-1}\biggl\{\frac{e^{-a\sqrt{p}}}{b+\sqrt{p}}\biggr\}=e^{-a^{2}/(4t)}\biggl(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi t}}-b\,\mathrm{erfcx}\bigl(a/\sqrt{4t}+b\sqrt{t}\bigr)\biggr),

(83)

we find

\displaystyle\bar{S}(t)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{2e^{-L^{2}/(4Dt)}}{{q_{\rm c}}L}\biggl\{(1+{q_{\rm c}}L)\,\mathrm{erfcx}\biggl(\frac{L}{\sqrt{4Dt}}-{q_{\rm c}}\sqrt{Dt}\biggr)

\displaystyle-\mathrm{erfcx}\bigl(L/\sqrt{4Dt}\bigr)\biggr\}.

(84)

We emphasize that this expression is meaningful only when
\bar{S}(t)\ll 1
, i.e., at short times. At very short times, one can use

\mathrm{erfcx}(z)\approx 1/(\sqrt{\pi}z)
as
z\to 0
in order to get

\bar{S}(t)\simeq f(t)\approx 1-S_{0}(t|0)

(85)

to the leading order. In other words,
S(t|0)\approx S_{0}(t|0)
at
very short times. However, at moderately short times, the argument of

\mathrm{erfcx}(z)
in the first term in Eq. (
84
) is
not necessary large. In fact, this term vanishes at
t_{*}=L/(2{q_{\rm c}}D)
that gives a natural timescale for the analysis of this
approximation. Note that the argument of
\mathrm{erfcx}(z)
is positive for

t<t_{*}
and negative for
t>t_{*}
. Moreover, if
t\gg t_{*}
, one
can use
\mathrm{erfcx}(-z)\approx 2e^{z^{2}}
for large
z
, so that

\bar{S}(t)\sim 4e^{-{q_{\rm c}}L+q_{\rm c}^{2}Dt}
, which is
exponentially large and thus is not applicable.

Let us summarize the above analysis. When
{q_{\rm c}}L
takes small or
moderate values, the short-time behavior of
\bar{S}(t)
is
essentially inherited from
1-S_{0}(t|0)
at
t\ll t_{L}=L^{2}/(4D)
, see
Eq. (
85
). When
{q_{\rm c}}L
increases, the second
timescale
t_{*}\ll t_{L}
emerges. If
t\ll t_{*}
, the leading-order
term of Eq. (
84
) gives precisely
Eq. (
85
), as expected. However, the asymptotic
values in this regime are extremely small (and thus of limited
practical interest). In turn, if
t_{*}\ll t\ll t_{L}
,
Eq. (
84
) is not applicable, even though this
regime is still formally qualified as “short times”. It simply
means that the quadratic term
\bar{S}^{2}(t)
cannot be neglected in
this regime. A thorough asymptotic analysis at large
{q_{\rm c}}
presents
an interesting open problem.
