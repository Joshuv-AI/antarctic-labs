Oncologists’ insights and solutions on navigating financial toxicity: an exploratory study in Pakistan
Authors: Wajiha Ahmad, Shahid Muhammad Iqbal, Muhammad Nauman Jamil, Anees Ur Rehman, Qurratul Ain Jamil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveFinancial toxicity (FT) poses a major challenge in cancer treatment. This study aimed to explore oncologists’ attitudes towards FT, the barriers they face in managing it and potential solutions.DesignThe study employed an exploratory qualitative design using inductive thematic analysis.SettingThe study was conducted at two tertiary care hospitals in Bahawalpur, Pakistan.ParticipantsThirteen oncologists were approached; 12 participated. Purposive sampling was used to select the participants. Eligible participants were all licensed oncologists who signed a written consent form and possessed at least 1 year of oncology experience.ResultFour themes emerged: the oncologist’s role in addressing FT, the impact of FT on patient outcomes, barriers in discussing FT and proposed solutions. Participants acknowledged that oncologists have a critical role in addressing FT in patients through cost-effective treatment planning and counselling. However, several challenges make it difficult to discuss FT. The oncologists advocate including financial counselling services in treatment, expanding coverage of the Sehat Sahulat Program, improving healthcare infrastructure and regulating cancer drug prices to mitigate FT.ConclusionsFT is a multidimensional problem that requires systemic reforms rather than relying solely on oncologists. Future studies should involve patients and various stakeholders across different areas to explore FT and develop context-specific interventions for cancer care more comprehensively.

STRENGTHS AND LIMITATIONS OF THIS STUDY

Purposive sampling was used to include oncologists with varied clinical experience, qualifications and professional roles.

Introduction

Cancer is one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality globally.1–3 It is estimated that in 2050, there will be over 35 million new diagnoses of cancer, a 77% increase over the estimated 20 million cases in 2022.4 All kinds of health systems in different countries are witnessing an increase in the financial burden of cancer.5 6 A multinational research team stated that cancer will cost the global economy 25.2 trillion dollars between 2020 and 2050.7

The negative impact of the high cost of cancer treatment on patients can be termed financial toxicity (FT).8 9 FT reflects the cumulative effect of direct and indirect costs, together with the changes in the financial situation of patients and their households.10 11 FT can cause physical and psychological harm and may influence treatment decisions, leading to suboptimal outcomes.10 12 13

Pakistan provides an important context for examining FT because it has a mixed public–private healthcare system, and out-of-pocket expenditure accounts for approximately 47.3% of total health spending.14 15 Although the Sehat Sahulat Program (SSP), a government-backed health insurance initiative in Pakistan, provides financial support for selected treatments, it does not systematically cover all out-of-pocket costs faced by patients with cancer.15 Affordability concerns may be further intensified by Pakistan’s 2024 deregulation of prices for non-essential medicines, which allows market-based pricing for medicines outside the national essential medicines list, creating an additional burden on patients with cancer.14 16

Oncologists need to understand the impact of FT on patients and their treatment decisions, including discussions about treatment costs, health insurance coverage and affordable care options.17–20 A study in Australia reports that patients appreciate the high-quality communication during treatment.21 Additionally, various professional bodies recommend that discussions between patients and oncologists regarding the cost of cancer treatment should be conducted.22 The American Society of Clinical Oncology, the National Academies of Medicine and the Canadian Association of Psycho-Oncology have recommended that patients be informed of the costs associated with quality cancer treatment.23 24

A substantial amount of research has examined oncologist and patient interactions regarding cancer-related healthcare expenses. Several studies, primarily conducted in the USA, focused on oncologists discussing treatment options influenced by unsubsidised high-cost cancer treatment and the financial impact this had on patients.19 25 26 These studies broadly address issues of cost and communication,24 the challenges experienced and approaches currently being used17 22 and roles in mitigating financial hardship.22

Oncologists are vital to patients’ lives, helping them express and resolve their concerns. Previous literature indicates that some oncologists feel uncomfortable discussing treatment costs with patients for several reasons.27 The literature from developed countries suggests that considering FT has become crucial for designing a care plan for patients with newly diagnosed cancer. As a result, there are growing discussions and expectations about the critical role of oncologists in financially preparing patients for cancer care.28 29 To our knowledge, and based on a literature review, no similar research on oncologists’ roles in managing FT has been conducted in Pakistan. Therefore, this study aims to investigate oncologists’ perspectives and practices in managing FT, identify barriers to effective intervention and propose recommendations to enhance support for patients with cancer. By understanding oncologists’ current practices and challenges, we can develop targeted interventions to reduce FT and improve patient outcomes.

Methods

Study design

An exploratory qualitative approach was used. This study follows the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist.

Study setting

Data was obtained from two leading hospitals in Bahawalpur. They were selected because they are major public-sector oncology care centres in the region and serve a large population.30 Bahawalpur Institute of Nuclear Oncology, a specialised cancer care hospital under the Pakistan Atomic Energy Commission (PAEC) and Bahawal Victoria Hospital, a tertiary care teaching hospital31 32 run by the Punjab government.

Participant recruitment

The following were the inclusion criteria: (1) Oncologist holding a licence from the respective regulatory body of Pakistan to practice; (2) at least 1 year of experience working in the cancer department; and (3) giving written consent to participate in interviews. The study excluded oncologists undergoing oncology training at the hospital or who did not provide written consent to participate.

Data collection

From April 2024 to June 2024, data was gathered through individual semistructured interviews using purposive sampling. The aim was to include oncologists with varied experience, gender, qualifications and professional roles to capture diverse perspectives on FT. The interview guide was developed following an extensive literature review to minimise bias. Three experts with experience in qualitative research and patient-care-related studies reviewed the interview guide. Their feedback focused on the relevance, clarity, wording, sequencing, cultural appropriateness and content coverage of the questions. Based on their suggestions, repetitive items were removed, wording was simplified and follow-up probes were refined to improve the depth and clarity of the interviews.

Authors WA and QAJ thoroughly discussed and finalised the interview guide. Interview guide with probes are placed as online supplemental table 1. The researcher, WA, is a female PhD student in pharmacy practice, trained in qualitative studies and QAJ is her supervisor. The interview guide and follow-up questions underwent pilot testing and were refined with minor edits. Participants were selected based on the most diverse demographic factors (age, gender, qualification, work experience and professional rank) to assess the variety of themes under investigation. WA initially contacted the participants in their hospital offices and explained that the survey was part of WA’s PhD research. By outlining its objectives, WA provided them with a general overview of the study’s importance.

After participants showed interest in the study, WA arranged the interview time. On the day of the interview, WA again described the project’s aim and assured them of the data’s privacy and confidentiality. These two meetings helped to build rapport. All interviews took place in a private room within the hospital. No third person was present in the interview room to maintain participant confidentiality and comfort. WA’s role as the primary researcher stems from its relevance to her PhD research. WA conducted and audio-recorded interviews after obtaining written consent from participants on-site. Several interview techniques (questioning, summarising and responding) were used during the face-to-face interviews. All interviews were conducted in a single session, with no follow-up interviews. Right after each interview, reflection notes were promptly recorded.

After reviewing the interview material and analysing the data, interviews were stopped if no new themes emerged. Sample size is determined by data saturation, that is, the point at which interviews yield repetitive information and no new themes arise. Following this, two further interviews were conducted to validate the saturation point.33 The interviews lasted from 13 to 34 min, with an average duration of 19 min. To preserve the participants’ original viewpoints, the transcripts were not sent back to them for review and comment.

Data analysis

The researchers studied the outline and content of the recording following the interview. A computer was used to import audio recordings from the smartphone. First, the interviews were transcribed by the first author, WA. Transcripts were translated into English and checked through a forward–backward translation of approximately 20% of the transcripts to ensure accuracy. To become fully familiar with the data, transcripts were read repeatedly. All coauthors listened to the audio files again, edited and compiled the transcripts. To anonymise the data, numbers were used instead of names (eg, O1, O2). Researchers downloaded the transcripts into OneDrive for storage after uploading the audio and making any necessary changes.

The approach to data analysis was thematic analysis. We employed this technique to identify key themes. An inductive approach was used to identify themes, thereby reducing analytical bias and highlighting participants’ perspectives.34 The authors, WA, MNJ and SMI, familiarised themselves with the data and began the analysis by conducting open coding to identify relevant words, phrases and sentences aligned with the research objectives. These elements were inductively organised into meaningful segments. Patterns observed from initial interviews helped refine and guide the coding of subsequent transcripts, ensuring depth and consistency. The process began with open coding and progressed to focused coding to explore relationships among codes based on frequency, similarity, relevance and difference. Codes were grouped into categories and then refined into themes and subthemes through discussions with other members of the research group. All coauthors independently reviewed the entire coding tree and the final list of themes. If initial disagreements between the two writers remained unresolved, all authors met weekly to discuss the issues and challenges identified during theme analysis until a consensus was reached. The themes were further reviewed and refined until a final, concise version was established. Participants were not asked to provide feedback on the findings to ensure the authenticity of their initial viewpoints.

To enhance rigour, various strategies were employed. Credibility was supported through verbatim transcription, team-based coding, participant quotations and confirmation of saturation. Detailed descriptions of the study setting and purposive sampling supported transferability. Confirmability was improved through independent coding and by maintaining an audit trail of analytical decisions and discussions among researchers. Dependability and trustworthiness were strengthened by adhering to COREQ guidelines, documenting the analysis steps and involving multiple researchers in the review of the coding process. We plan to disseminate the results of study in conferences so that policy makers may modify strategies according to evidences.

Result

Of the 13 potential respondents, 12 oncologists were willing to participate. Data saturation was reached after 10 interviews, after which two additional interviews were conducted to confirm the saturation point. Of all clinicians who have been part of this research, 10 were attending consultant oncologists, with the remainder being haematologist and radiation oncologist. Role in managing financial toxicity

Cost-effective treatment planning

Most respondents (7 out of 12) stated that oncologists have an important role in addressing FT. The oncologists emphasised the importance of creating cost-effective treatment plans and highlighting their efforts to avoid unnecessary, costly drugs.

”I suggest cost-effective therapy according to their conditions. I don’t push the patient towards expensive treatment if he is not willing to or if he is not affording it.” (O6)

Moreover, respondents reported that they discuss overall treatment costs with their patients under favourable conditions, so that patients are aware of any expenses that may be incurred.

"Yes, I tell the patient the approximate treatment cost according to the favorable circumstances if they persist.” (O2)

Financial counselling and referral to support schemes

Furthermore, a few oncologists (3 out of 12) reported that we actively counsel the patients on financial matters and guiding them in accessing financial assistance programmes.

Furthermore,

"I also assist patients in finding different pharmaceutical schemes, like buy one get one free medicine." (O1)

"We inform the patient about various options, such as Zakat, Bait-ul-Mal, and our Bahawalpur Cancer Society Fund (BACAS), which the patient can utilize. Nowadays, SSPs are widely used. We support patients through these options." (O4)

Oncologists also reported explaining the financial limits of available support schemes. This included educating patients on how they would need to pay for the remainder of their treatment if they were not fully covered by SSP or Pakistan Bait-ul-Mal, and that they would need to seek other sources of financial assistance. They also reported obtaining informed consent after discussing patients’ financial obligations beyond SSP coverage limits. This counselling can assist patients in understanding the financial consequences of treatment and in considering other support options.

"I informed patients about their therapy costs, ensuring they understood the financial aspects. We obtain their written consent regarding the costs and their responsibility for further treatment beyond the Baitul Maal and SSP financial limits." (O8)

Perceived as beyond the oncologist’s domain

Although some oncologists manage FT, a small number (2 of 12) believe it is outside their scope of practice. Financial problems were not their main concern; respondents indicated that social services should address them.

"This is a problem of social security. This is not in the domain of a consultant clinical oncologist. As a doctor, I have to offer my treatment." (O5)

However, oncologists often fill this gap due to the lack of dedicated financial support professionals.

"This is not our work. It is the work of social services, social workers, and financial navigators to cover these issues. However, we do not have such professional workers. So, usually, everything is done by the doctors." (O4)

Barriers to discussing financial toxicity with patients

Limited patient health literacy

The main factors identified as obstacles to discussing FT with patients were limited patient health literacy, language barriers, limited doctor knowledge, time constraints and lack of trust. All of this contributes to a lack of communication and understanding between oncologists and patients regarding the financial aspects of cancer care.

Limited health literacy is a significant barrier, and many patients have difficulty understanding information about their treatment and related costs, even when the information is repeated. The challenge is not only to explain but also to ensure patients grasp the information presented.

"Illiteracy is a big issue, especially health literacy, because some patients are not very educated, but they follow what you advise them." (O4)

"The problem is how much the patient understands and complies with what is being said to them. This is more important." (O12)

Language differences

Oncologists (2 out of 12) also report that language barriers pose a significant challenge. They often encounter patients who speak different dialects or languages, making effective communication difficult. For instance,

"Yes, language; I am from Lahore, and I learned a little Saraiki after coming here. So, at the start, it was difficult for me to understand their complaints." (O1)

Limited clinician knowledge of financial assistance programmes

A few respondents (4 out of 12) observed that doctors sometimes lack sufficient knowledge of the financial aspects of treatment. No awareness seminars have been organised by the healthcare system for oncologists to inform them of the SSP or other financial assistance programmes. Consequently, oncologists might feel ill-equipped to advise patients on these matters.

"At the start of the SSP, no meetups with doctors were held so that we could know how much benefit the patient was getting and how the patient could get the benefit." (O3)

"I do not know much about financial assistance programs and procedures, so how could I help them?" (O7)

Unpredictable treatment costs

Because of the dynamic nature of patient conditions, treatment plans and currency fluctuations, it is difficult to provide accurate information on treatment costs in advance.

"We cannot provide the patient with an accurate cost because everything is poorly defined. First, you investigate the patient; then, you can explain the treatment plan. The treatment plan you describe is also initially limited, as the patient’s condition and your treatment plan can change over time. However, if there is something defined, we tell the patient." (O4)

Time constraints

The time constraint is a major challenge for financial discussions, as many respondents (9 out of 12) indicated that this is one of the main challenges.

"Normally, I see 60–70 patients in a 6-hour shift, which gives me about 5 minutes with each patient, which is not enough to have detailed financial discussions." (O1)

Lack of trust in financial assistance schemes

Few respondents (3 out of 12) identified a lack of trust between patients and the healthcare system as a barrier. One of the oncologists explained that some patients mistrust financial assistance schemes due to prior experiences with fraud.

"Some people took PKR 60,000 from these patients in the name of jobs and disappeared. Therefore, when we told them that in SSP you have PKR360000 for your treatment, they do not trust you because of their past bitter experience." (O1)

This scepticism can prevent patients from believing in and accepting the financial support options available to them, further complicating efforts to address FT.

Impact of financial toxicity on patient outcome

Psychological distress

It is noted that FT has a profound impact on a patient’s mental health, treatment adherence and disease progression. The mental stress, as reported by several oncologists (8 out of 12), often leads to a loss of motivation and hope and a decreasing quality of life.

"The significant impact would be a toll on mental health, and the patient cannot feel enough motivation, and when you are not motivated enough, your quality of life and commitment are decreased." (O6)

Missed follow-up and poor adherence

Moreover, it is observed that FT frequently results in missed follow-up doctor appointments, which prolong treatment duration and diminish treatment response.

"FT affects adherence to the therapy, leading to increased treatment duration and unsatisfactory treatment outcomes." (O7)

Disease progression and poorer outcomes

All oncologists have a consensus that incomplete treatments due to financial limitations lead to more aggressive disease progression, negatively impacting overall cancer treatment success rates. This issue is particularly severe in Pakistan, where treatment success rates are already lower than international standards.

"FT affects treatment success, leading to relatively more aggressive disease. So, it did have a harmful effect." (O4)

Every respondent agreed that having FT affects treatment effectiveness.

Proposed solutions

Specialised cancer-care infrastructure

Oncologists suggested several solutions, including specialised healthcare infrastructure and changes to financial policies. Some respondents (3 out of 12) indicated that it is necessary to set up rural health clinics. The first steps are to be at government-funded cancer hospitals. One oncologist stated that such centres are needed, saying

"It is high time the government should start some free-of-cost cancer hospitals because the cancer incidence rate is increasing nowadays." (O12)

Sehat Sahulat Program coverage reform

Most oncologists (10 out of 12) feel that the current health system, especially the SSP, should be changed. They say it should be used only for certain life-saving anticancer drugs, to prevent funds from being depleted prematurely or replaced by full coverage from other financial assistance programmes, such as Baitul Maal.

"For cancer patients, medicines are required for symptomatic relief also and it is also cheap medicine as compared to anticancer drugs so mainly patient consume that drug therefore large amount of funds are spent on symptomatic relief medicine rather than anticancer medicine this is why the SSP funds should be increased." (O3)

Updated clinical guidance

Moreover, oncologists highlighted the importance of updated clinical guidelines. They mentioned ongoing efforts to modify international treatment guidelines to better suit local patients, particularly in addressing patient follow-up.

"Proper doctor training should be based on our guidelines. Even this year, 2024, we are following international guidelines mindlessly despite their inapplicability here. The race, financial conditions, and healthcare setup are different, so it is high time to develop our national guidelines." (O8)

Financial counsellors and psychologists

Another important suggestion, supported by oncologists, was to bring in a financial counsellor and include psychologists as part of the treatment team. They emphasised that it could provide much-needed support and guidance to patients, assisting them to deal with the financial and emotional burden of cancer treatment. One of the oncologists explained,

"We need a qualified counselor, such as a psychologist and financial counsellor for cancer patients. It would be helpful for both doctors and patients." (O6)

Cancer medicine price regulation

Finally, there was a constant demand for price control of cancer drugs, with all oncologists supporting immediate regulatory intervention. They stated that some patients find it difficult to afford their full treatment due to repeated price hikes of medications. Consequently, they face higher treatment costs. This was mentioned by one of the respondents:

"There should be proper checks and balances on the prices of cancer medicines. If prices continue to increase at this rate, how can patients complete their treatment?" (012)

These findings underscore the importance of an integrated strategy that encompasses regulatory reforms and direct patient support networks to reduce the financial burden on patients with cancer.

Discussion

The principal finding of this study is that FT is a complex issue arising from interactions among multiple factors at the patient, provider and health system levels. Participants describe their role in reducing FT a0000by using cost-effective treatment planning. However, they were unable to fully manage FT due to low patient health literacy, language barriers, limited time for patient consultations and unpredictable price increases in treatment costs. All participants recognised that FT contributes to poor treatment adherence, which ultimately worsens disease progression. They recommend financial counselling services, increased coverage of SSP, stronger oncology infrastructure and regulation of medicine prices.

At the provider level, our findings align with other international literature, which reports that oncologists recognise discussing treatment costs as part of cancer care and that many of them adjust their treatment plans based on the financial capacity of patients.13 35 36 This role remains challenging and is not always undertaken. Some oncologists believe that discussing costs is not their responsibility. Similar findings are reported in other studies, where oncologists cite avoiding financial conversations due to time constraints, limited resources, discomfort or lack of knowledge about financial assistance options.37 38 This issue is especially evident in low-resource settings, where oncologists are expected to act as financial counsellors but may lack appropriate training.38 This study extends this evidence by demonstrating that oncologists not only make clinical decisions but also engage in informal negotiations about treatment affordability within weak support systems. This places an unfair burden on clinicians and leads to variable financial counselling, depending on their knowledge, available time and institutional resources.

At the patient level, limited health literacy is a significant barrier to effective cost communication. This is not to be confused with simple illiteracy. The issue is more specific: many patients might not be aware of the length of cancer treatment, the cumulative costs, their insurance coverage, how to access assistance with treatment costs or the consequences of missing or stopping treatment. Sheridan noted that patients may lack the skills to evaluate the medical information they are given.39 This is especially true in Pakistan, where patients may begin treatment without knowing the financial burden or how much support is available. Low health literacy has previously been linked to suboptimal treatment outcomes and increased healthcare costs.40 41 In this study, oncologists noted that a lack of knowledge about treatment costs and support programmes made counselling more challenging and could lead to delayed decision-making, poor adherence and reduced follow-up.

At the health-system level, participants proposed several reforms, including improving cancer care infrastructure by adding financial counsellors and psychologists, updating clinical guidelines and regulating medicine prices. While the SSP provides financial aid to underprivileged families and ensures access to essential healthcare services.42 Participants noted that it is sometimes insufficient for treating cancer. In other low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), the lack of insurance coverage and high out-of-pocket expenses also present significant barriers to cancer treatment.43 44 This indicates that SSP should not only be increased, but also that cancer coverage requires cancer-specific redesign, as cancer care is prolonged, recurrent and expensive. The inclusion of financial counsellors and psychologists within oncology teams can assist patients in understanding treatment costs, insurance limitations, available support, medication options and emotional distress linked to FT.5 45–47 Furthermore, strengthening rural cancer clinics, establishing referral systems and decentralising follow-up services could help reduce both direct and indirect costs, particularly given the limited availability of specialised cancer care in Pakistan, which is largely concentrated in major urban centres. This aligns with global efforts to enhance healthcare infrastructure in LMICs to address inequalities in access to care, as part of Sustainable Development Goals (SDG) 10.48–50

The implications of this study for the future are significant for clinical practice, policy and research. Financial navigation services and multidisciplinary teams, including financial counsellors and psychologists, should be implemented in institutions. The issue of cost-related non-adherence should not be solely blamed on overworked oncologists; it should be recognised as a structural, ethical and clinical problem that needs to be addressed across the entire system.51 Potential reforms in Pakistan include public negotiation of cancer medicine prices based on clinical value,52 caps on price increases when there is no substantial new clinical evidence53 and a greater focus on affordable generic and biosimilar drugs.54 Guidelines for local oncology services should also emphasise high-value cancer care, such as WHO-recommended treatments55 and those with demonstrable clinical benefit, including through European Society for Medical Oncology – Magnitude of Clinical Benefit Scale (ESMO-MCBS) and SMO Scale for Clinical Actionability of Molecular Targets – European Society for Medical Oncology Magnitude of Clinical Benefit Scale (ESCAT-ESMO-MCBS).56 57

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it only considers oncologists’ perspectives, so it might not reflect a broader stakeholder view of FT in cancer care. Second, the research covers a limited geographical area. Third, like all qualitative research, it may be subject to recall bias. Nevertheless, it provides valuable insights into how oncologists perceive and respond to FT in a resource-constrained oncology setting.

Conclusions

Overall, this study shows that FT is not just a private financial matter between the patient and the oncologist. Oncologists recognised their role in planning cost-effective treatments and providing financial counselling but faced limited capacity due to barriers at patient, provider and health-system levels. To tackle FT, coordinated efforts are necessary across oncology practice, patient education, financial navigation, medicine pricing, insurance design and cancer care infrastructure.
