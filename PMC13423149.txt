Importance of patient-centered research for hospital-based violence intervention programs
Authors: James Dodington, Christopher Morrison
Published: 2026

We welcome Johnson et al’s contribution, which describes individual treatment plan goal completion among 263 participants in the Life Outside Violence (LOV) Intervention Program.1 This Hospital-based Violence Intervention Program (HVIP) provides services for predominantly firearm-injured patients. Whereas most studies of HVIPs examine reinjury outcomes among participants, this research underscores the importance of adopting a person-centered view and defining intervention success within an individual’s full social-ecological context.

HVIPs are part of a comprehensive strategy to reduce injury and death from intentional violence and to support patient recovery. During the past decade, these programs have spread across the USA, focusing on intensive case management for patients after injury.2 Importantly, HVIPs predominantly serve black and Hispanic patients in the USA, and systemic inequities and significant barriers persist in the promotion and implementation of these programs to serve patient-centered needs.3 Furthermore, research to investigate the mechanisms and impacts of HVIPs is sparse, which is consistent with broad disinvestment in treatment and prevention for firearm injuries compared with other leading causes of death and disability in this country.4

Johnson et al help address this gap, building on prior research and expanding their focus beyond reinjury outcomes within a multi-institutional HVIP.5 They report that a significant majority of LOV program participants, 85%, identified a program-related goal. Among the goals, health and healthcare were most common, and the mental health subcategory was highlighted.1 This finding on mental health support aligns with a core set of outcomes reported in a Delphi study of HVIP service providers and leaders and is a point of focus for the Health Alliance for Violence Intervention, the leading training and technical assistance provider for HVIPs.6 7

The descriptive study by Johnson et al is an essential step in setting the stage for future etiologic studies of HVIPs. Priorities for the field include understanding how participants achieve their self-identified goals compared with standard care, and how specific attributes of the HVIP—such as facilitating connections with community partners—contribute to goal achievement, and, in turn, how goal achievement affects long-term health outcomes for individuals and communities. Unlike other public health interventions, HVIPs have been built to address a clear need prior to significant investment in program research, and now we must be dedicated to advancing the research to match the magnitude of the problem and the urgency of the moment.
