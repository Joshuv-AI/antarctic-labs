Filling Surfaces with very few systoles
Olivier Mathieu

Abstract
. In the paper we describe hyperbolic surfaces filled by their systoles, where the total number of systoles is in O ⁡ ( g ln ⁡ g ) O(\frac{g}{\ln\,g}) , that is equivalent to the lower bound of Anderson, Parlier and Pittet [ 2 ] . Various papers [ 17 ] [ 7 ] [ 16 ] [ 9 ] [ 13 ] have investigated the same question, and the best previously known upper bounds where in o ⁡ ( g ln ⁡ g ) o(\frac{g}{{\sqrt{\ln\,g}}}) . Surprizingly the present approach is, in our opinion, much simpler than the methods of earlier papers.



Introduction

We start with definitions. For
g\geq 2
, let
\mathcal{T}_{g}
be the Teichmüller space, that is the space of all marked closed oriented hyperbolic surfaces of genus
g
.

Let
C
be a finite set of geodesics on some surface
\Sigma\in\mathcal{T}_{g}
,
where by
geodesic
we mean a closed geodesic.
We say that
C

fills
the surface if
C
cuts
\Sigma
into polygons.
We denote as
{\mathrm{Syst}}(\Sigma)
the set of all

systoles
of
\Sigma
, that is the geodesic of shortest length.
Since the spectrum length is discrete
[
5
]
,
{\mathrm{Syst}}(\Sigma)
is always finite.

A generic surface
\Sigma\in\mathcal{T}_{g}
admits a unique systole,
which does not fill. However, W. Thurston had shown that the subspace
\mathcal{P}_{g}\subset\mathcal{T}_{g}
of all surfaces
\Sigma
which are filled by their systoles is nonempty
[
19
]
. Thus, we define two
filling numbers

\mathrm{Fill}(g)\geq\mathrm{fill}(g)
, as follows:

(a)

\mathrm{Fill}(g)=\mathrm{Min}_{\Sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{g}}\,\mathrm{Card\,}\,{\mathrm{Syst}}(\Sigma),\text{ and}

(b)

\mathrm{fill}(g)
is the smallest integer
n
for which there is
a hyperbolic surface
\Sigma\in\mathcal{T}_{g}
filled by a set of
n
systoles.

In
[
2
]
, Anderson, Pittet and Parlier found the lower bound

(1)

\displaystyle\mathrm{fill}(g)\geq(1-o(1))\pi\frac{g}{\ln g}

for some explicit function
o(1)
. In this
paper, we provide an upper bound which is equivalent to the lower bound:

Theorem 1
.

We have

\mathrm{Fill}(g)<9\ln(2+\sqrt{3})\frac{g}{\ln g}=11.8526\ldots\frac{g}{\ln g},

for infinitely many genera
g\geq 2
.

Previous related works

It has been known for some time that
\mathrm{Fill}(g)\leq 2g
for all
g
,
see
[
17
]
,
[
2
]
and
[
16
]
. Indeed, some of these references concerns the inequality

\mathrm{fill}(g)\leq 2g
.

There are no better upper bound known for all genera g, but there are
bounds valid for infinitely many
g
in
[
7
]
[
9
]
[
13
]
.
Even the best bound

(2)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Fill}(g)\leq o(1)\frac{g}{\sqrt{\ln\,g}}

for infinitely many
g
is not equivalent to
Anderson, Parlier and Pittet lower bound (
1
).

In fact, these upper bounds are not efficient: Fortier-Bourque bound

[
7
]
is not explicit and even
the explicit function
o(1)
in Inequality (
2
) has a very slow decay.
For example, in
[
7
]
Fortier-Bourque had shown that

(3)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Fill}(g)<2g\text{ for infinitely many }g.

This remarkable counterintuive result means that
that the systoles can fill a surface although
the corresponding cycles do not generate
H_{1}(\Sigma,\mathbb{Q})
.
However, finding an explicit
g
satisfying (
3
) has been challenging: the paper

[
9
]
only predicts the existence of such genus of size around
2^{1000}
. However the analysis of an explicit example in
[
13
]
shows
that
\mathrm{Fill}(17)\leq 32
and it was hinted that
\mathrm{Fill}(17)\leq 25
.

Idea of the proof

We start with the unique genus two surface
S
which admits a tessalation by
four regular rigth-angled hexagons. Although no name is attached to
S
, this surface has been widely studied since a long time,
see e.g.
[
4
]
[
17
]
[
8
]
[
10
]
.

The one-skeleton of the tessalation of
S
consists of six geodesics

c_{1},\ldots,c_{6}
. It has been proved by P. Schmutz Schaller
[
17
]
that
these geodesics are the six systoles of
S
. Their length is

L=2\mathrm{arcosh}(2)=\ln(2+\sqrt{3}).

It has been observed in
[
10
]
that
S
is an arithmetic surface, see
also
[
18
]
.
We explicitly compute the corresponding quaternion ring.
Let
\mathcal{H}
be the ring defined by

\langle I,J\mid I^{2}=3,J^{2}=3,IJ+JI=0\rangle.

We show that
\pi_{1}(S)
is
isomorphic to an explicit subgroup
\Pi\subset\mathcal{H}^{*}
such that

\mathcal{H}^{*}=\Pi\times\{\pm 1\}
. It follows that

S\simeq\mathbb{H}/\Pi
.

For
n\geq 2
, we define integers
p_{n}
and
q_{n}
by the equation

(2+\sqrt{3})^{n}=p_{n}+q_{n}\sqrt{3},

and we consider the surfaces

S(n)=\mathbb{H}/\Pi(n)
where

\Pi(n):=\Pi\cap\big(\mathbb{Z}\oplus q_{n}\mathcal{H})

is a congruence subgroup of
\Pi
. Let
\pi_{n}:S(n)\to S
be the
Galois covering with Galois group
\Pi/\Pi(n)
.

The key Lemma
21
shows that the systoles of
S(n)
are the connected components
of
\pi_{n}^{-1}(c_{1})\cup\ldots\cup\pi_{n}^{-1}(c_{6})
and their lengths are

nL
. We conclude that

\mathrm{Card\,}{\mathrm{Syst}}(S(n))=\frac{6}{n}[\Pi:\Pi(n)],

which easily implies that

(4)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Card\,}{\mathrm{Syst}}(S(n))\leq 18\ln(2+\sqrt{3})\frac{g_{S(n)}}{\ln g_{S(n)}},

where
g_{S(n)}
is the genus of
S(n)
.

To finish the proof, we need to replace the constant
18
by
9
.
We first observe that
S(2)
is the Galois covering
of
S
with Galois groups
(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{4}
, that is the surface of genus
17
investigated in
[
13
]
. When
n
is even,
we have
\Pi(n)\subset\Pi(2)
, thus there is a Galois covering
\theta_{n}:S(n)\to S(2)
.

For any filling subset
A\subset{\mathrm{Syst}}(S(2))
, the set

B:=\{c\in{\mathrm{Syst}}(S(n))\mid\theta(c)\in A\}

obviously fills
S(n)
, and its cardinality is

\frac{\mathrm{Card\,}A}{\mathrm{Card\,}{\mathrm{Syst}}(S(2))}\mathrm{Card\,}{\mathrm{Syst}}(S(n))

It follows from a result on Penner systems
proved in
[
13
]
, that
{\mathrm{Syst}}(\Sigma)=B

for some hyperbolic surface

\Sigma
near
S(n)
in
\mathcal{T}_{g}
.

Therefore, we are looking at a filling set
A\subset{\mathrm{Syst}}(S(2))

of minimal cardinality. In
[
13
]
, we described
a filling set
A
with
32
systoles, and it was
hinted that
A
can be further reduced to
25
systoles:
this would allow to replace
18
by

9.375=\frac{25}{48}18
.
Recently N. An, F. Ihringer and I. Irmer
got a better result in
[
1
]
:
{\mathrm{Syst}}(S(2))
contains
a filling subset with only
24
elements. This allows to obtain the desired constant
9
.

1.
Conventions

We denote as
\mathbb{H}
the Poincaré half-plane.
Its isometry group is isomorphic to

{\mathrm{PGL}}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Indeed an element

g=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in{\mathrm{GL}}_{2}(\mathbb{R})

acts on
\mathbb{H}
as

g(z)=\frac{az+b}{cz+d}

if
\det g>0
and as

g(z)=\frac{a\overline{z}+b}{c\overline{z}+d}

otherwise. The subgroup
\mathrm{Isom}^{+}(\mathbb{H})
of preserving orientation isometries is isomorphic to

{\mathrm{PSL}}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\simeq\{g\in{\mathrm{GL}}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\mid\det\,g>0\}/\mathbb{R}^{*}.

Computations and explanations are based on the Poincaré half-plane. The Poincaré disk model is only used for pictures.
The paper contains repeated fastidious computations involving products of two-by-two matrices, which have been checked with Python. Claude was used to draw the pictures.

Given a toplogical surface
\mathcal{S}
, a
faithfull representation

\rho:\pi_{1}(\mathcal{S})\to{\mathrm{PSL}}_{2}(\mathbb{R})

is called
loxodromic
if its
image is a discrete subgroup of
{\mathrm{PSL}}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Identifying

\mathcal{S}
with
\mathbb{H}/\rho(\pi_{1}(\mathcal{S})
provides a hyperbolic metric
on
\mathcal{S}
.

Any loxodromic representation can be lifted
to
{\mathrm{SL}}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, as proved by L. Bers
[
3
]
.
However we should notice a strange phenomenon, already observed in
[
3
]
. To keep track of the

\mathbb{Z}/3
-symmetry of the
3
-holed sphere, it is pleasant to present its fundamental group as

\langle x,y,z\mid xyz=1\rangle.

In a loxodromic representation, representing
x,y,z
by matrices

X,Y,Z\in{\mathrm{SL}}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
with negative traces is the only way to maintains the relation

XYZ=1
together with the
\mathbb{Z}/3Z
-symmetry. This explains the
annoying minus signs in the formulas of the paper.
