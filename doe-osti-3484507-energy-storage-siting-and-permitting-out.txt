 Abstract
 On March 24th, 2026, under the sponsorship of the U.S. Department of Energy’s Office of
 Electricity, Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL) staff hosted the Energy Storage Siting
 & Permitting Outreach Workshop at PNNL’s Grid Storage Launchpad (GSL) facility in Richland,
 Washington. The workshop convened a cohort of state and regional stakeholders from across
 the country to build a shared understanding of energy storage technologies, regulatory
 frameworks, and best practices for engaging in the permitting process. Participants left with a
 deeper understanding of energy storage technologies, grid uses and benefits, interconnection
 and regulatory processes, battery safety standards and risk management, and local
 engagement strategies and approaches. The workshop concluded with a guided tour of the GSL
 for hands-on exposure to energy storage research and development. The workshop had 17
 external participants. The attendees represented a range of backgrounds, including state and
 local governments, nonprofits or other local organizations, project developers, and utility
 stakeholders.

 1.0 Introduction and Overview
 On March 24th, 2026, PNNL staff members hosted the one-day Energy Storage Siting and
 Permitting Outreach Workshop at the Grid Storage Launchpad (GSL) at PNNL’s campus in
 Richland, Washington. The workshop, which was funded by the U.S. Department of Energy
 (DOE)’s Office of Electricity (OE), brought together 17 invited participants representing a cross-
 section of organizations working on local and regional energy issues, local and state
 government planning officials, energy storage developers, and utilities. Its primary goal was to
 offer accurate and informed perspectives on benefits, impacts, and risk mitigation strategies for
 energy storage technologies to key stakeholders to help them address common concerns and
 barriers to energy storage deployment that arise in siting, permitting, and regulatory processes.1
 The workshop was free and PNNL provided travel funding to reduce barriers to participation.

 The workshop and associated materials were developed as a response to permitting challenges
 faced by many proposed grid-scale energy storage projects, with local constituent concerns and
 opposition often a significant factor driving permitting delays and, in some cases, leading to
 project cancellations.2 The workshop offered participants an opportunity to discuss siting and
 permitting challenges specific to energy storage projects and build a shared understanding of
 energy storage technologies, regulatory frameworks, and best practices for engaging in the
 permitting process. The event also included a tour of GSL’s laboratory facilities, providing
 participants with hands-on exposure to PNNL’s cutting-edge work on energy storage research
 and development.

 PNNL was well-positioned to lead this event as a national laboratory with established expertise
 in energy storage testing and validation, grid integration, and regulatory analysis. The workshop
 was also aligned with OE’s strategic priorities, including enhancing the grid’s ability to reliably
 and affordably meet growing demand and maintaining grid reliability and security, given the role
 of energy storage as a timely and effective tool to support achieving these goals.3 Energy
 storage’s contributions to reliability, affordability, and accommodation of rapid demand growth
 were among the topics emphasized throughout the event.

 2.0 Workshop participants and outreach
 The event was attended by 17 invited participants. Notably, this represented 100% attendance
 by everyone who RSVP’d to the event. Initial outreach was focused on representatives from
 organizations who participate in, or support participation in, siting and permitting processes for
 energy storage projects. Outreach began in 2025 to organizations with existing relationships
 with PNNL staff from previous work and groups identified as relevant potential attendees or
 partners based on the focus of their work. Additional organizations were identified for outreach
 via these initial conversations.

 These outreach efforts were intended both to gauge interest in the workshop and to gather input
 on topics for the workshop and related materials. Echoing discussion themes at the workshop
 itself, these conversations emphasized the importance of accessible, plain-language resources
 describing battery safety and risk mitigation, support for stakeholders navigating siting and
 permitting processes, and clear explanations of the grid benefits of energy storage. Based on
 input from these initial conversations, the PNNL team developed a collection of factsheets that
 were shared with workshop participants and published online.1 The factsheets are also included
 as Appendix B of this report.

 All attendees were either directly invited to the event by PNNL or reached out about the event
 after learning about it from colleagues or partners. As expected, not all groups who were part of
 PNNL’s original outreach efforts were able to send representatives to the workshop.

Energy Storage Siting and Permitting Outreach Workshop Report 5

 3.0 Workshop sessions
 The workshop was organized into seven session topics, each presented by a PNNL
 representative with subject matter expertise.

 1. Energy storage fundamentals: The workshop began with an overview of the
 fundamentals of energy storage technologies and some of their grid benefits, led by
 Jessica Kerby. This presentation included descriptions of different battery chemistries
 and their common applications, with a focus on market-dominant lithium-ion batteries. It
 also described energy storage applications and their impact on project economic
 potential, including how storage can contribute to energy arbitrage, ancillary services,
 transmission and distribution deferral, and demand management.

 2. Regulatory and permitting processes: The subsequent session focused on some key
 considerations and challenges for permitting, siting, and regulation of energy storage,
 led by Devyn Powell. This presentation described permitting pathways and challenges,
 addressed the rise of local moratoria against energy storage projects, and summarized
 PNNL research on best practices for including energy storage in local zoning
 regulations.

 3. Interconnection and grid integration: The third session was led by Karyn Boenker and
 introduced the interconnection process for energy storage solutions, including grid
 integration challenges and emerging solutions. The presentation outlined unique
 interconnection challenges faced by energy storage projects navigating queues that
 were designed for generation technologies and discussed grid services that energy
 storage can provide in additional detail.

 4. Case studies and working lunch: The working lunch session was a guided discussion
 of a selection of energy storage case studies, followed by a Mentimeter exercise asking
 participants what they believed were the most important benefits of energy storage, what
 concerns and challenges they have seen in their experiences, and what they perceive
 are the biggest barriers to energy storage deployment. Themes from this and other
 participant discussions are described more detail in Section 4.0 below.

 5. Safety and risk mitigation: After lunch, Vilayanur Viswanathan walked through trends
 in energy storage safety incidents and risk mitigation, highlighting the critical role of
 energy storage safety standards development and timely standards adoption.

 6. Local engagement strategies: With safety in mind, the next session, led by Kendall
 Parker, discussed the value of intentional community engagement strategies to support
 project success and community acceptance.

 7. Energy storage in siting proceedings: Finally, best practices in supporting energy
 storage siting proceedings were presented, also by Kendall Parker, with discussion
 highlighting the difference between public comments and expert testimony, and the
 importance of each in informed proceedings.

 Vincent Sprenkle, director of the Grid Storage Launchpad, then joined the workshop to provide
 a brief overview of PNNL’s energy storage portfolio and the value of the GSL facility in
 advancing the energy storage landscape and accelerating the pace of technological
 advancement. He and Adam Jivelekas then led a guided tour of the GSL, where workshop

 4.0 Emergent Discussion Themes
 Over the course of the workshop, through both open discussion and responses to structured
 discussion questions presented via Mentimeter, some common themes emerged. These
 included topics that participants were eager to learn more about, common challenges and
 concerns, and suggestions for future materials or outreach work. The outcomes of these
 discussions can offer valuable insight to help inform future PNNL and OE work on energy
 storage, and are summarized below.

 4.1 Topics of Interest
 • Operational considerations for specific climates: Participants were interested in learning
 more about the additional requirements or specific considerations to keep in mind when
 siting energy storage in high temperature climates. Specific questions were raised regarding
 the impact of temperature on battery safety and incidence of failure as well as the impacts
 on battery operational lifetimes when located in high-temperature climates, such as southern
 California or the Nevada desert.
 • Battery chemistries: The energy storage fundamentals session of the workshop introduced
 basic functions of grid-scale batteries and briefly mentioned a range of different battery
 technologies. Given the prevalence of lithium-ion batteries, the presentation largely focused
 on lithium-ion. The session explained that lithium-ion batteries hold the largest market share
 of commercially available batteries, and provided a brief overview of the various lithium-ion
 battery chemistries that are commonly used and their typical use cases. Participants sought
 further detail on how to decide which chemistry is best suited for their project, and greater
 insight into market trends for each chemistry. Some participants expressed interest in non-
 lithium-ion chemistries, including practical applications, economic feasibility, and safety
 tradeoffs as compared to lithium-ion technologies.
 • Safety statistics: Batter safety was emphasized as the most common concern voiced by
 community members that workshop attendees encountered in their work. Participants
 expressed a strong desire to dive deeper into this topic, including interest in learning more
 about industry trends, the impacts of updated codes and standards adoption, and variation
 in safety risk between battery chemistries. Specific questions were raised concerning the
 failure mechanisms that lead to battery fires and trends in failure rates and drivers over time.
 Participants also expressed interest in additional information and resources to help
 colleagues, neighbors, and constituents contextualize the scale and severity of safety
 incidents, such as by comparing storage fires to other, more familiar events, like a house
 fire.
 • Demand charge reduction and peak load shaving: Realizing the benefits of energy storage
 projects at scale often depends on the effective communication of their potential use cases.
 When participants considered that residential demand charge reduction and peak load
 shaving on the grid were effectively the same energy storage use case, just at a different
 scale, the workshop cohort realized that this relationship could be leveraged for effective
 communication of the benefits of energy storage, specifically in how it can be used to
 address both modern and emerging grid and customer challenges.

 4.2 Common Challenges and Concerns
 • Air and water impacts: Participants were eager to learn more about the potential impacts to
 watershed and local air quality in the event of a battery fire. This was highlighted as the

 primary concern and reason for opposition to local siting of energy storage projects. Key
 questions were how to safely divert and dispose of water runoff from fighting battery fires
 and how the smoke and emissions from a battery fire compares to that from other fire
 sources, like a house fire or wildfire.
 • Addressing opposition from trusted local voices: While much of the community engagement
 discussion highlighted the value of partnering with trusted local voices to facilitate effective
 dialogue with community members, participants presented a challenge they were facing
 where their local fire chief was the strongest oppositional voice to a project. This topic was
 discussed at length, with a few strategies presented both in how to respond to this scenario
 and how to avoid it in the future.
 • Identifying local partners: Both project developers and advocates noted that it can be difficult
 to identify potential local partners to lend support to a proposed project. The “spectrum of
 support” analogy was presented as a tool to identify and communicate the range of voices
 that might be encountered during the project development cycle. Participants requested
 lessons learned on what “good” project development practices looked like, and how to
 emulate those successful pathways.

 4.3 Participant Suggestions for Future Work
 • Communicating the benefits of siting energy storage locally: There was much discussion on
 how and which local benefits of an energy storage project are realized based on where it is
 sited on the grid. Participants raised concerns that some siting decisions may not maximize
 storage benefits. For example, zoning restrictions that require energy storage projects to be
 sited in industrial zones may not maximize grid benefits compared to locating the project
 closer to a substation or on a distribution feeder closer to residents. Conversely, another
 concern was that community members are often unsure of how the benefits of a grid-scale
 energy storage project will practically benefit them. Suggestions were offered for resources
 that effectively communicate local benefits of energy storage, such as lowering energy bills,
 improving grid reliability, or providing blackstart capabilities.
 • Project success stories: Workshop participants repeatedly came back to the need for more
 case studies and success stories and responded favorably to the lunchtime presentation
 describing case studies of energy projects from previous PNNL publications. The
 importance of building emotional connection to by illustrating tangible project benefits was
 raised as one strategy to help navigate local skepticism about energy storage safety or other
 potential downsides. Participants were interested in more case studies and other resources
 that could be used to hold productive discussions to help people to see themselves as
 benefiting from a proposed project as opposed to only fearing potential harms.
 • Contextualize risks against alternatives: Energy storage projects are generally proposed to
 solve a real or perceived challenge within a local or regional energy system, such as a need
 for additional capacity to meet growing peak demand. A recurring conversation at the
 workshop brought up alternatives to energy storage that would otherwise meet those needs,
 which led to an interest in comparative resources to help community members evaluate the
 risks, benefits, and costs of energy storage against other infrastructure. These alternatives
 may include substation upgrades, new transmission projects, or new generation plants.
 Some of these alternatives may encounter more local opposition than an energy storage
 project, or may have other downsides, such as a higher increase in electricity rates to pay
 for more expensive infrastructure investments.

 • Regulatory mechanisms to maximize affordability benefits of energy storage: Participants
 highlighted the uncertainty of available market mechanisms for energy storage
 compensation, and this uncertainty was named as a potential barrier to energy storage
 deployment. Some participants also expressed that it was unclear what inputs are used to
 determine rate tariffs available to energy storage. Where specific rates or enabling
 regulatory mechanisms do not already exist to maximize the affordability benefits of energy
 storage, pilot programs were suggested as both a pathway for potential compensation and a
 source of data to inform regulatory and ratemaking proceedings. Recent PNNL work on the
 affordability benefits of energy storage1 was mentioned during these conversations, but
 additional research exploring various use cases in more detail or resources directed toward
 specific stakeholders may be of value.
 • End of life considerations: Some participants requested more information about battery end
 of life considerations that would be helpful for local planners and their constituents. A
 factsheet with high-level information about end of life considerations was shared with
 participants during and after the workshop, but the subject was not explored in detail in the
 workshop sessions. Questions were also raised about options for battery repurposing or
 second life applications, including ongoing interest in innovations to help maximize batteries’
 second-life potential.
 • Additional safety and risk mitigation topics: Safety remained a topic of high interest to all
 participants. There was a strong appetite for additional resources that described safety
 considerations and risk mitigation activities in more detail for a range of audiences, including
 and especially members of the public with limited technical backgrounds. Participants
 expressed interest in additional factsheets or white papers, videos, webinars, and case
 studies exploring safety topics that could be used for public education and other purposes.

 5.0 Summary and Conclusions
 The Energy Storage Siting and Permitting Outreach Workshop hosted by PNNL in March 2026
 highlighted PNNL’s wide-ranging expertise in energy storage, including regulation, valuation,
 safety, and research and development. Attendees were largely practitioners working on different
 aspects of energy storage deployment, including project development, grid planning, and local
 regulation. The workshop content was focused on addressing topics of interest identified
 through previous research and initial outreach conversations, which emphasized a growing
 need for objective, accessible information about the benefits, risks, and general characteristics
 of energy storage.

 Participants offered positive feedback about the workshop, both at the event and in a brief post-
 event survey.1 Attendees said they appreciated the knowledge and clarity demonstrated by the
 PNNL presenters, the scope of topics, and the opportunity for discussion with the presenters
 and each other. The positive feedback and high levels of engagement with the content
 throughout the event emphasize the value of continued PNNL investment in educational
 materials designed to be accessible for non-technical stakeholders, whether through additional
 workshops, webinars or virtual trainings, factsheets, or other resources.

 As energy storage deployment continues to increase nationwide in response to demand growth,
 economic factors, and needs for investment in grid reliability and resilience, local opposition and
 permitting delays are likely to also increase without proactive investment in effective training and
 resources. Building on the success of the workshop and participant suggestions, PNNL is likely
 well-positioned to continue to develop these resources. Topics of interest include expanded
 work on battery safety and risk mitigation, affordability and economic benefits, and opportunities
 to maximize both grid and community benefits of energy storage, among others.

FUNDAMENTALS
This factsheet presents an overview of the fundamentals of energy storage and
best practices for energy storage systems, or large stationary batteries installed in
residential, commercial, and industrial settings.

 Value Stacking involves designing an energy storage system to provide multiple services, such as resilience or backup
 power, improved grid reliability, utility bill optimization and cost savings, and the opportunity to participate in the energy
 market. Maximizing the utilization of a battery ensures that the potential benefits of the project are maximized.
  Value Stacking

 Configuring the battery’s operating profile, or charge/discharge strategy, to provide multiple services requires
 operational flexibility and forecasting of extreme weather events, utility rate tariffs, and market conditions.
 Operating Profile

 Identifying priority or critical loads can help extend the battery’s ability to survive an extended outage by switching off,
 or “shedding”, non-critical loads. This can be achieved manually or via a critical loads panel installed with the battery
 system.
  Critical Loads

 The ability for a battery to participate in local or regional energy markets depends on state and federal regulations,
 where the battery is located, and whether it is in a residential, commercial, or industrial setting.

 and a cycle life. The calendar life refers to the expected lifetime of the battery due to the aging of its components over
 time. The cycle life is the number of charge/discharge cycles the battery is expected to achieve before its capacity falls
  Calendar and below usable levels.

BENEFITS OF
ENERGY STORAGE
This factsheet presents an overview of the benefits of energy storage systems, or
large stationary batteries installed in residential, commercial, and industrial settings.

 Energy storage can provide access to on-demand electricity. When paired with locally generated
 electricity, energy storage can support energy independence.
 Access

 Energy storage can reduce the cost of electricity by storing low-cost, off-peak or locally generated
 electricity for use during high-cost, peak electricity price periods, supporting time-of-use bill
 management and reducing demand charges.
 Affordability

 Energy storage can reduce the need for backup diesel generators, improving local air quality during
 an outage. Energy storage paired with generators that do not require purchased fuel can reduce the
 emissions impact of local electricity demand without sacrificing grid reliability.
 Improved Air Quality

 Energy storage provides resilience to grid outages, whether the result of local grid conditions, extreme
 weather events, or utility-initiated shutoffs by providing uninterrupted access to electricity for critical
 infrastructure, such as hospitals and community gathering spaces.
 Resilience

 Energy storage paired with local generation sources can serve as a community asset, not only providing
 energy independence, but community ownership and wealth creation.
 Social Impact

SAFETY
This factsheet presents an overview of battery safety considerations for energy
storage systems, or large stationary batteries installed in residential, commercial,
and industrial settings.

 Battery Management System (BMS) is a required component in all multi-celled batteries that controls
 and monitors the operation of the battery to ensure its safe operation. The BMS is designed to shut off
 the battery if it detects early signs of battery failure to prevent safety incidents.
 Battery Management System

 Battery fires can occur due to internal malfunction or proximity to extreme heat or external fire. Thermal
 runaway occurs when a battery heats up faster than the heat can be dissipated, resulting in the venting
 of hazardous gases and the potential for ignition or explosion.

 battery manufacturing and the safe operations of battery systems for every application.

process of receiving all necessary approvals from government bodies.

for BESS can reduce uncertainty and enable more efficient project review.

MICROGRIDS
This factsheet presents an overview of the benefits and use cases of microgrid
systems, implementation challenges and potential solutions, planning considerations,
and project timelines for their development.

KEY BENEFITS

 Microgrids provide access to locally generated energy for remote communities who may not be
 grid-connected. Microgrids can also provide energy for communities whose utility generation mix
 comes at a high cost or adversely impacts local air quality.
 Access

 Microgrids can reduce the cost of electricity by using local generators, providing an alternative to
 utility-purchased electricity and avoiding transmission and distribution costs. Local generators that do not
 rely on purchased fuel can also provide additional cost savings, especially in remote locations with limited
 accessibility. These savings can reduce local energy burden and prevent shutoff notices for utility non-
 Affordability payment.

 Microgrids that do not rely on purchased fuel can replace the need for backup diesel powered generators or
 utility-purchased electricity, providing local air quality improvements and health benefits.
 Improved Air Quality

 Microgrids provide resilience to grid outages, whether the result of local grid conditions, extreme weather
 events, or public safety power shutoffs. By automatically disconnecting from the main grid during these
 events, a microgrid can provide uninterrupted access to electricity—this is especially important for critical
 infrastructure, such as hospitals and community gathering spaces.
 Resilience

 Microgrids provide an alternative to traditional, utility-purchased electricity. This can allow microgrids
 to serve as a community asset, not only providing energy independence, but allowing for wealth creation,
 community ownership, and community building.
 Social Impact

BEFORE PURSUING A MICROGRID
While a microgrid can provide numerous community benefits, it is important to consider simpler, less expensive
energy measures as precursors to microgrid development. Improving energy efficiency can reduce electricity
demand, lower energy costs, and potentially decrease the size and cost of a future microgrid system. Some
example measures are described below.

 Weatherization is the process of improving a building’s physical condition to more efficiently use electricity. An energy auditor
 inspects the building to identify the most cost-effective measures to implement. The most common energy-saving measures
 are usually related to improving insulation and window and door sealing to reduce conditioned air loss. More information can
 be found at: https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/weatherization.
  Weatherization

 Energy efficiency measures are any steps that can reduce the energy required to perform the same task; this can be as
 simple as replacing old, inefficient appliances with newer, high efficiency models. Energy efficiency can also be utilized as
 a design principle during a building’s design and construction by using strategies such as maximizing solar heat gain and
 applying passive cooling techniques to make the most of solar heat gain and passive cooling techniques. More information
 Energy Efficiency can be found at: https://betterbuildingssolutioncenter.energy.gov/efficiency/energy-efficiency/tech.
