The DSR-PRISMA protocol: Embedding systematic knowledge mapping into design science research for applied technology studies
Authors: Thanh-Cong Truong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDesign Science Research (DSR) studies in applied technology fields often rest on literature reviews that cannot be traced and stop at artifacts that are never abstracted into design knowledge. Both habits damage reproducibility: readers cannot retrace the path from evidence to requirements to artifact, and evaluations drift away from the claims they should test. The DSR-PRISMA Protocol addresses this gap by embedding PRISMA 2020 into the rigor cycle of DSR. The protocol runs in seven phases: problem framing, systematic knowledge mapping, design requirement derivation, artifact design and development, demonstration, evaluation, and design knowledge abstraction. Six worksheet instruments (T1 to T6) document each phase, and three traceability rules connect them: every requirement traces to evidence, every component traces to a requirement, and every claim traces to an evaluation episode. A worked example in AI solution selection for small and medium-sized enterprises runs all seven phases and fills every template. Two mini-scenarios show that the instruments transfer to cybersecurity training and learning analytics, and a qualitative comparison contrasts the protocol with unstructured DSR practice on four reproducibility criteria. The protocol gives authors a documented chain from evidence to design knowledge, and gives reviewers an instrument to audit that chain.−Seven phases take a study from problem framing to design knowledge abstraction, with PRISMA 2020 embedded at the systematic knowledge mapping phase.−A finding-gap-requirement table converts review findings into traceable design requirements, the bridge missing from both DSRM and PRISMA.−Six worksheet templates and an eleven-item reporting checklist let authors and reviewers audit every phase of a DSR study.Keywords: Design Science Research, PRISMA 2020, Systematic literature review, Design requirements, Artifact evaluation

Background

Design Science Research (DSR) is expected to deliver two outputs: an artifact that addresses a practical problem class, and design knowledge that explains why the artifact works [3]. Published DSR studies in applied technology fields often fall short of this standard in two recurring ways. The first concerns the knowledge base. Many studies treat the literature review as a narrative summary: sources appear without a documented search strategy, screening records, or selection criteria. When design requirements later surface in the paper, readers cannot trace which finding, theory, or stakeholder need produced them.

The second failure occurs at the other end of the process. Studies stop once the artifact is built, reporting construction and perhaps a usage scenario but no abstraction into reusable design knowledge. Engström et al. [4] report the same pattern in software engineering: few studies present their contributions through a design science lens, so the design knowledge in applied work stays implicit. Gregor and Hevner [5] label work of this kind routine design, the application of known solutions to known problems, and exclude it from the space of research contributions. A study that neither grounds its requirements in traceable evidence base nor abstracts its results beyond the local artifact is a development report, whatever methodology label it carries.

Both failures damage reproducibility. A reader cannot retrace the path from literature to requirements to artifact components, and cannot check whether the evaluation tests the claims the artifact makes. Reviewers face the same problem: with no audit trail, judgments about novelty and rigor rest on the authors' assertions. This gap between what DSR promises and what its reporting allows others to verify motivates the protocol presented here.

The canonical DSR frameworks describe what a study must contain but not how to produce it in a checkable form. The Design Science Research Methodology (DSRM) of Peffers et al. [1] specifies six activities, from problem identification through communication, and remains the dominant process model. Hevner [3] adds the three-cycle view, in which a rigor cycle connects the project to the knowledge base, a relevance cycle connects it to the application environment, and a central design cycle iterates building and evaluating. Neither framework standardizes the rigor cycle's core task: building the knowledge base and converting it into design requirements. The step is named, not operationalized.

PRISMA 2020 solves the opposite half of the problem. Its 27-item checklist and flow diagram standardize how a systematic literature review (SLR) is reported, recording how many sources were identified, screened, excluded, and retained [2]. PRISMA, however, was built for evidence synthesis, not for design. It offers no mechanism for converting review findings into design requirements, and its outputs do not connect to artifact design, demonstration, or evaluation. An SLR can satisfy every PRISMA item and still leave the design cycle without usable input.

No existing protocol operationalizes the full chain from systematic review to design requirements, artifact components, evaluation claims, and design knowledge as a sequence of documented, checkable steps.

The DSR-PRISMA Protocol closes this gap by embedding PRISMA 2020 into the rigor cycle of DSR as a seven-phase procedure: problem framing, systematic knowledge mapping, design requirement derivation, artifact design and development, demonstration, evaluation, and design knowledge abstraction. The protocol makes three contributions. First, it integrates PRISMA into DSR so that the knowledge base feeding artifact design is transparent and auditable. Second, it supplies an instrument set (Templates T1 to T6) covering problem framing, DSR-oriented data extraction, a finding-to-gap-to-requirement table, a requirement-to-component traceability matrix, and a claim-to-evaluation matrix. Third, it provides an eleven-item reporting checklist that lets authors and reviewers audit a DSR study phase by phase.

In the contribution framework of Gregor and Hevner [5], the protocol is an improvement: a customization of DSRM [1] that adds a standardized evidence mechanism to a known problem in research practice. Section Method details the seven phases and their instruments. Section Validation validates the method through a worked example in AI solution selection for small and medium-sized enterprises, two cross-domain mini-scenarios, and a qualitative comparison with DSR practice that does not use the protocol.

Method details

Protocol overview

Seven phases and overall logic

The DSR-PRISMA Protocol organizes a design science study into seven phases (Fig. 1): problem framing, systematic knowledge mapping, design requirement derivation, artifact design and development, demonstration, evaluation, and design knowledge abstraction. The division of labor between the two parent methods is fixed. The systematic review, reported under PRISMA 2020, builds the evidence base and feeds the derivation of design requirements; DSR designs, demonstrates, evaluates, and abstracts the artifact into reusable design knowledge. Each phase produces a defined output captured in one of six instruments (Templates T1 to T6, supplementary material), and each output serves as input to a later phase. This chain of instruments is the protocol's audit trail.

The seven phases of the DSR-PRISMA Protocol with the instrument documenting each phase. PRISMA 2020 is embedded at Phase 2; the dashed arrow marks the iteration path from Phase 6 back to Phases 3 and 4 after a negative summative result.

The protocol operationalizes existing frameworks rather than replacing them. Table 1 maps each phase to the six DSRM activities of Peffers et al. PRISMA 2020 enters at Phase 2 and nowhere else: it governs how the assembly of the knowledge base is documented and reported, while the surrounding phases follow DSR logic.

Scope and entry conditions

The protocol targets applied technology studies, including AI, data science, information systems, cybersecurity, digital transformation, and educational technology. It applies when the intended artifact belongs to the four types of March and Smith [6] (constructs, models, methods, and instantiations) or to derived forms such as frameworks, process models, dashboards, architectures, and prototypes. Two entry conditions apply. First, the study must address a problem class rather than a single implementation task. Second, the literature on that class must be mature enough for a systematic review to return a usable evidence base; the Limitations discusses the consequences when this condition fails.

Phase 1: Problem framing

Purpose and core question

Phase 1 defines the problem class the artifact will address. The core question is: what practical problem class requires a new or improved artifact? A problem class is shared by a category of stakeholders across settings; one organization's implementation task is not a problem class. Framing at class level is what later allows design knowledge, rather than a local product, to be the study's contribution.:

Procedure

The researcher identifies the stakeholders who experience the problem, the context in which it occurs, its consequences, and the limits of existing solutions. The researcher then hypothesizes a provisional artifact type and selects the entry point of the project: problem-centered, objective-centered, design-and-development-centered, or client-initiated [1]. Each statement should rest on at least one practice-based source, such as an industry report, stakeholder input, or an observed failure.

Output and completion criteria (Template T1)

Template T1 records six fields: practical problem, preliminary research gap, design opportunity, initial artifact type, stakeholders, and entry point. Phase 1 is complete when (a) the problem class is verifiable against at least one practice-based source, (b) the problem statement names stakeholders and consequences rather than a technology, and (c) the artifact type is provisional but explicit. The preliminary gap remains a hypothesis until Phase 2 confirms or revises it.

Phase 2: systematic knowledge mapping with PRISMA

Review questions oriented to design

Phase 2 differs from a conventional SLR in its purpose: the review supplies inputs to a design process, not a descriptive synthesis. Review questions therefore target four extraction goals: prior artifacts for the problem class, candidate design requirements, kernel theories, and evaluation methods used in comparable studies. A usable question pattern is: what criteria, methods, and frameworks have been used to guide [problem class] in [context]? Questions of the form "what is known about X" are too loose to feed Phase 3.

Search, screening, and PRISMA 2020 reporting

Search and screening are conducted following established SLR guidance [7] and reported according to PRISMA 2020 [2]. The researcher selects databases appropriate to the domain (for applied technology, candidates include Scopus, Web of Science, IEEE Xplore, ACM Digital Library, and ScienceDirect), constructs search strings, defines inclusion and exclusion criteria, and screens records by title, abstract, and full text. The flow diagram records counts of records identified, screened, excluded, and retained. The 27-item checklist governs reporting; restating it here would add nothing, so the protocol incorporates it by reference.

For CS/IS domains, the systematic search and thematic mapping steps can be operationalized using a bibliometric-content analysis workflow [8], which produces structured evidence clusters directly compatible with Template T2.

DSR-oriented data extraction (Template T2)

Extraction is where the protocol departs from a pure SLR. Template T2 assigns one row per included study and six columns: problem themes, existing artifacts, potential design requirements, kernel theories, evaluation methods used, and stated or observed gaps. Each column feeds a defined later phase (Table 2). Extraction decisions should be traceable to specific passages, with page or section references recommended.

Phase 3: design requirement derivation

From finding to gap to requirement

Phase 3 is the central bridge of the protocol. It converts review findings into design requirements (DRs) through an auditable chain: finding, then gap, then requirement. The core question is: what should the artifact do because the literature shows this need? One traceability rule governs the phase: every DR must trace to at least one literature finding (a T2 row) or one documented stakeholder need (T1). Requirements without a source are flagged, then either grounded or removed.

Template T3 and worked entries

Template T3 records the chain in five columns: DR code, literature finding with source study IDs, gap, design requirement, and origin. Table 3 shows three entries from the worked example, where the artifact is an AI solution selection protocol for small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs).

Requirement quality check

Before Phase 4, each DR passes five tests: it is specific (states what the artifact must do, not an aspiration), verifiable (a reader could check whether the final artifact satisfies it), solution-neutral (describes a capability, not a technology), grounded (traces to T2 findings or T1 needs), and consistent (conflicts with other DRs are identified and the resolution documented). DRs failing any test are revised before design begins.

Artifact design and development

Selecting the artifact type

A recurring misunderstanding treats the DSR artifact as a working software system. The canonical view is broader: valid artifacts include the four March and Smith [6] types and derived forms (Table 4). The artifact type chosen here may revise the provisional type recorded in T1; the revision is documented, not silent.

Design rationale and traceability (Template T4)

Template T4 has two parts. Part A is a traceability matrix: each artifact component declares which DR(s) it satisfies (primary or secondary) and whether its design logic comes from the literature, from the practical context, or both. The matrix exposes two defects at a glance: components serving no DR, and DRs served by no component. Part B requires prose answers to six design rationale questions: what the artifact is, what components it consists of, which components derive from the literature, which derive from the practical context, what overall design logic connects components to the problem class, and which gaps the artifact addresses.

Design cycle iterations

Building and refining proceed in iterations of Hevner's [3] design cycle: build, run an internal check, revise. The internal check at this stage is informal (walkthroughs, self-checks against the DRs) and does not substitute for Phase 6. Every iteration is logged with date, change, and trigger. The log preserves auditability when the final artifact differs from the first design, which it almost always does.

Phase 5: demonstration

Demonstration is not evaluation

Demonstration shows that the artifact can be applied to at least one relevant instance of the problem class [1]. It establishes applicability, not value. Conflating the two is a common defect in DSR reporting: a single successful application is presented as proof of merit. The protocol keeps the phases separate so that the evaluation in Phase 6 can be matched to claims rather than to convenience.

Selecting a demonstration form

Four forms cover most artifact types (Table 5). The choice follows the artifact, not the other way around.

Required outputs

The demonstration record contains three elements: a described use case or scenario, the application steps showing the artifact in operation, and initial feedback. The feedback is input for refinement, not evidence of value; it feeds the design cycle log (see Design cycle iterations above) and may sharpen the claims to be tested in Phase 6.

Phase 6: evaluation

Matching claims to evaluation methods (Template T5)

The governing principle is that evaluation must match the claims the artifact makes. Template T5 operationalizes this as a claim-evaluation matrix: each claim receives an identifier, an evaluation method capable of supporting it, explicit criteria, and the participants or data involved. Each claim is also positioned within Framework for Evaluation in Design Science Research (FEDS) [9] as formative or summative and artificial or naturalistic. Reference pairings guide the choice: claims of usefulness to experts point to expert review or Delphi; claims of technical performance point to benchmarking or controlled experiments; claims of organizational fit point to field studies or case deployment. Claims without matching evidence, and evaluations that test nothing claimed, are exposed by the matrix itself.

Two evaluation layers as minimum

The protocol requires at least one formative episode, whose purpose is to refine the artifact, and one summative episode, whose purpose is to conclude on its value. For each episode the researcher specifies participants or data, criteria (candidates include completeness, clarity, usefulness, feasibility, and risk awareness), and how results are recorded. A single thin evaluation is acceptable only when the claims are narrow; broad claims demand layered evidence [9].

Stop criteria and negative results

The refine loop needs an explicit stop criterion, stated before evaluation begins, such as no critical issues raised in the latest formative round. Negative summative results are findings, not failures of the protocol: they send the researcher back to Phase 3 if the requirements were wrong, or to Phase 4 if the design did not satisfy them. The return path is documented in the design cycle log.

Phase 7: design knowledge abstraction

From artifact to design principles

Phase 7 separates DSR from a product report. The researcher abstracts the evaluated artifact into design principles, design propositions, and boundary conditions. A workable format states each principle as an imperative with a scope: for example, data readiness must precede solution selection (DP2 in the worked example below). Propositions link a design choice to an expected effect; boundary conditions state where the knowledge applies and where it does not.

Contribution statement and novelty positioning

The contribution statement declares the quadrant the study occupies in the framework of Gregor and Hevner [5]: invention, improvement, or exaptation. Routine design is not a research quadrant; a study that cannot place itself in one of the three should not claim a DSR contribution. The statement covers three levels: the artifact itself, the design knowledge abstracted from it, and the implications for practice.

Reporting checklist (Template T6)

Template T6 closes the protocol with eleven yes/no questions, each linked to a phase and an instrument. The items cover problem class definition, artifact explicitness, requirement grounding, contribution beyond routine design, novelty positioning, design rationale, demonstration, claim-matched evaluation, stated contributions, abstraction, and communication to scholarly and practitioner audiences. Authors use it for self-audit before submission; reviewers can use the same instrument to audit a submitted study. Every "no" requires either a fix or a limitation statement.

Instrument set and access

Six instruments accompany the protocol: T1 (problem framing worksheet), T2 (DSR-oriented extraction table), T3 (finding-gap-requirement table), T4 (requirement-component traceability matrix), T5 (claim-evaluation matrix), and T6 (reporting checklist). All six are provided as supplementary material in worksheet form. Column labels may be renamed to fit domain vocabulary; "kernel theories" may become "pedagogical theories" in educational technology. Three rules must survive any adaptation (Fig. 2): every DR traces to evidence, every component traces to a DR, and every claim traces to an evaluation episode. These three chains are the protocol's reproducibility mechanism.

The instrument chain T1 to T6 and the three traceability rules. Each rule binds a later instrument back to an earlier one; the T6 checklist audits the complete chain.

Method validation

Validation proceeds in three steps. A worked example runs all seven phases on one project and fills every template. Two mini-scenarios test whether the templates transfer to other applied technology domains. A qualitative comparison contrasts the protocol with unstructured DSR practice on four reproducibility criteria. The full worked example, with all six templates filled, is provided as supplementary material; this section reports the condensed version.

Worked example: an AI solution selection protocol for small or medium-sized enterprises (SMEs)

Context and problem framing (Phase 1)

The worked example develops a selection protocol that takes a manager in a small or medium-sized enterprise from a business problem to a justified AI adoption decision. SMEs want to apply AI to customer service, marketing, demand forecasting, and analytics, but their managers cannot judge which solution class fits the problem, whether the data are ready, what risks apply, or how to measure effectiveness. The documented consequences are stalled pilots, sunk cost, and loss of trust in AI initiatives. Template T1 records this problem class, the stakeholders (owners and department managers without an AI team), a preliminary gap, and a provisional artifact type: a method-class protocol, not a software system. The entry point is problem-centered [1].

Phases 2 to 4: from systematic review to a six-module artifact

Phase 2 posed the review question what criteria, methods, and frameworks have been used to guide AI solution selection and adoption in organizational contexts? and searched Scopus, Web of Science, IEEE Xplore, ACM Digital Library, and ScienceDirect. Fig. 3 reports the PRISMA 2020 flow diagram for the example. Extraction with Template T2 organized the evidence into six clusters: AI adoption, AI readiness, AI governance, model selection, decision-support systems, and SME digital transformation. The existing-artifact column showed that no integrated selection protocol exists for this stakeholder group, which positions the contribution as improvement rather than routine design.

PRISMA 2020 flow diagram for the worked example. Counts are illustrative, consistent with the hypothetical character of the example (see Ethics statements); the diagram shows the record-keeping the protocol requires at Phase 2.

Phase 3 derived six design requirements (DR1 to DR6) through the finding-gap-requirement chain in Template T3; Table 3, reproduced in Template T3 and worked entries above, shows three of them. Each DR traces to at least one cluster, and DR6 (usability without AI expertise) was logged as a cross-cutting constraint on the form of the other instruments.

Phase 4 produced the artifact: a six-module protocol covering business problem framing (M1), data readiness assessment (M2), solution mapping (M3), risk and governance check (M4), evaluation criteria selection (M5), and implementation decision (M6), plus a one-page worksheet layer (W). The T4 traceability matrix is complete in both directions: every DR has one primary component and every component serves at least one DR (Fig. 4). The design cycle log records three internal iterations; the main change merged an original seven-module draft into six after a walkthrough exposed redundancy.

Phases 5 to 7: demonstration, evaluation design, and design principles

The demonstration applied the protocol end to end in a retail SME receiving about 8000 customer messages per month, of which around 45% are repeated questions. Walking through M1 to M6 produced a traceable decision: a four-week pilot of an FAQ chatbot, with sensitive-information filtering required because the risk check in M4 surfaced personal data in the chat history. The case establishes applicability, not value; demonstration and evaluation serve distinct purposes in the protocol.

Phase 6 filled Template T5 with two claims. CL1 (the protocol is complete, clear, and feasible for SME use) is paired with a formative expert review involving eight experts across three groups; in FEDS terms [9], this episode is formative and artificial. CL2 (the protocol improves decision quality over unstructured selection) is paired with a summative case study or controlled two-group task, positioned as summative and naturalistic. Both episodes carry explicit criteria, a stop rule for the formative loop, and documented return paths to Phase 3 or Phase 4 on negative results. Within this article the two episodes are designed, not executed; the T6 checklist makes this visible at item 8, and the Limitations section reports it as a limitation.

Phase 7 abstracted five design principles (DP1 to DP5), each with a rationale anchored in the demonstration, for example data readiness must precede solution selection (DP2) and risk screening belongs before the implementation decision (DP3). Boundary conditions restrict the knowledge to literature-mature domains and exclude vendor negotiation and post-deployment monitoring. The contribution statement places the study in the improvement quadrant of Gregor and Hevner [5].

Transferability check: two cross-domain mini-scenarios

Two mini-scenarios test whether the templates operate outside the AI/SME domain. Each scenario ran Phases 1 to 3 only, filling condensed versions of T1, T2, and T3. The purpose is narrow: to show that the instruments and traceability rules function in other applied technology domains, not to claim validated artifacts there.

Scenario A addresses cybersecurity training. Employees pass annual security training yet keep failing phishing simulations, because the training is generic slide-based content disconnected from job roles. Phase 1 framed the problem class and proposed a scenario-based training framework; Phase 2 mapped three literature clusters (security awareness training, simulation-based learning, phishing susceptibility); Phase 3 derived three requirements (Table 6).

The difference the protocol makes is auditability at each link of the chain: from source to finding (T2), finding to requirement (T3), requirement to component (T4), and claim to evidence (T5). In unstructured practice each link exists, if at all, only in the authors' heads.

Limitations

The protocol inherits the preconditions of a systematic review. When the target domain is young and the published evidence is thin, Phase 2 returns few usable findings, the finding-gap-requirement chain in Phase 3 starves, and requirements must lean on stakeholder input instead. The protocol remains usable in such cases, but its central promise, a literature-grounded requirement base, weakens in proportion to the evidence available.

A full systematic review with PRISMA 2020 documentation is also expensive. Searching five databases, screening in two stages, and documenting the flow diagram can take months that a rapid review would avoid. Projects under time pressure can run a scoped variant with fewer databases and a narrower publication window. What cannot be cut is the documentation of search, screening, and extraction, because the audit chain depends on it.

The vocabulary IS-centered. The protocol builds on DSRM [1], the three-cycle view [3], and the contribution framework of Gregor and Hevner [5], all rooted in the information systems tradition of DSR. Design traditions in engineering, human-computer interaction, or architecture name artifacts, requirements, and evaluation in other terms and may order them in other ways. Transfer to those traditions needs a vocabulary mapping that this article does not provide.

Validation stops at the worked example and the two mini-scenarios. Within the worked example itself, the two evaluation episodes are designed rather than executed, which the T6 checklist records at item 8. No expert panel has reviewed the protocol, and no multi-group comparison has tested whether teams using it produce more reproducible studies than teams that do not. Both are next steps: an expert review of the instrument set, followed by a controlled comparison across research teams.
