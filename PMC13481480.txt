Adjunctive Pharmacotherapy for Major Depressive Disorder with Anxiety Symptoms: from Monoamines to Recent Clinical Evidence
Authors: Roger S. McIntyre, Masaki Kato, Michael E. Thase, François Therrien, A. Marilise Marrache, Kenta Ikehara, Ferhat Ardic, Matthieu Boucher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Anxiety symptoms are common in patients with major depressive disorder (MDD). The presence of anxiety symptoms can indicate greater depression severity, and is associated with a higher comorbidity burden, suicidality, lower levels of functioning, and poorer quality of life. The presence of anxiety identifies patients who are likely to experience significant burden from MDD despite standard treatment. Although clinical guidelines provide recommendations for adjunctive treatment in patients who have not responded to an antidepressant, there are currently limited recommendations specifically for patients with MDD and anxiety symptoms. Dysregulation of the monoamine system, notably noradrenaline (norepinephrine), serotonin, and dopamine signalling, has been implicated in the pathophysiology of MDD and of anxiety disorders, based mainly on preclinical, mechanistic and translational evidence. This review has two aims: (1) to explore the hypothetical role of monoamine signalling in patients with MDD with anxiety symptoms, and (2) to provide a comprehensive overview of clinical data for pharmacotherapies recommended for the first- and second-line adjunctive treatment of difficult-to-treat MDD, focusing on recent studies and analyses of patients with MDD with anxiety symptoms. Reviewed treatments included atypical antipsychotics (aripiprazole, brexpiprazole, olanzapine, quetiapine XR, risperidone, cariprazine), bupropion, ketamine/esketamine, lithium, mirtazapine/mianserin, modafinil, and triiodothyronine. Clinical data in patients with MDD with anxiety symptoms were scarce or lacking for olanzapine, risperidone, cariprazine, lithium, modafinil, and triiodothyronine. Aripiprazole, brexpiprazole, quetiapine XR, and ketamine/esketamine have been shown to improve depression and/or anxiety symptoms in patients with MDD with anxiety symptoms, although evidence is mainly short-term and derived from exploratory post hoc analyses of trials not designed to assess anxiety outcomes. Treatment decisions for patients with MDD with anxiety symptoms should be based on robust clinical data, and there is an unmet need for prospective clinical trials with anxiety-specific endpoints in this patient population.

Key Points

People with major depressive disorder (MDD) commonly experience anxiety symptoms, the presence of which is associated with poorer treatment outcomes, yet clinical guidelines provide limited treatment recommendations for such patients.Various adjunctive treatments are available for patients with MDD who have not responded to antidepressant treatment, which have different binding profiles for noradrenaline, serotonin, and dopamine receptors.Studies of adjunctive treatment options in patients with MDD and anxiety symptoms are scarce, short-term, and mostly post hoc, but provide preliminary evidence that some treatments may be beneficial for this patient group; additionally, preclinical, translational, and mechanistic data support hypotheses that receptor binding profiles may contribute to their efficacy and safety profiles.

Introduction

Patients with major depressive disorder (MDD) commonly experience anxiety symptoms [1–3]. Amongst patients with MDD who respond to antidepressant treatment (ADT), 78% experience anxiety as a residual symptom [4]. Residual symptoms in depression are a strong predictor of relapse [5]. Among patients with MDD, anxiety symptoms can indicate greater illness severity, and are associated with a higher comorbidity burden, functioning impairments, suicidality, lower quality of life, higher number of hospitalisations, and greater likelihood of inadequate responses to conventional ADTs [2, 3, 6–8]. The presence of anxiety may also have implications for safety. In patients with MDD, in addition to anxiety-related adverse events (AEs), the incidence of cardiac, neurological, and gastrointestinal AEs during ADT is increased in patients with versus without anxiety symptoms [9–11]. From the patient’s perspective, feeling less anxious, agitated, or irritable are the most common subjective signs of treatment effectiveness in MDD [12].

The presence of anxiety symptoms in MDD has been associated with reduced treatment response and remission: those with anxious distress are less likely to respond to ADT [13]; the presence of an anxiety disorder is associated with treatment-resistant depression [14]; and in patients with treatment-resistant depression, anxiety symptoms are associated with more severe illness and a lower probability of remission [8].

In patients with MDD who have a poor response to initial ADT, adjunctive treatment is a recommended option [15–18]. However, many clinical practice guidelines for MDD can be considered outdated: while clinical practice guidelines are typically reviewed every 3–5 years, this timeframe is based on limited evidence, and more regular updates may be required in fields with a high research turnover [19, 20]. Older guidelines for MDD may not reflect more recent regulatory approvals or advances of newer therapies [20]. Clinical guidelines for MDD either have no or limited recommendations specifically for patients with MDD with anxiety symptoms [15–18, 21, 22]. It can be hypothesised that these limitations may result in suboptimal treatment of patients with MDD with anxiety symptoms, highlighting the need for a review of clinical data in this patient group.

Defining MDD with Anxiety Symptoms

The presence of anxiety symptoms can be defined in several ways. This review focuses on three common approaches used in clinical studies: (1) MDD with anxious distress, (2) anxious depression, and (3) other definitions.

The terms ‘anxious distress’ and ‘anxious depression’ are not interchangeable [23]. Both include psychiatric and somatic symptoms; however, the anxious distress specifier focuses more on psychiatric symptoms, whereas the anxious depression classification encompasses a wider range of somatic symptoms including gastrointestinal, respiratory, and genitourinary [24, 25]. Anxious distress is a specifier in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders fifth edition (DSM-5) [24], whereas anxious depression is based on criteria used in the Sequenced Treatment Alternatives to Relieve Depression (STAR*D) trial [2].

MDD with Anxious Distress

The DSM-5 anxious distress specifier requires the presence of two or more of the following five symptoms during a depressive episode: “feeling keyed up or tense,” “feeling unusually restless,” “difficulty concentrating because of worry,” “fear that something awful may happen,” and “feeling that the individual might lose control of himself or herself”. It is considered a simple method of assessing anxiety in MDD in clinical practice and facilitates treatment planning and assessment of treatment outcomes [11, 26, 27]. In clinical studies, patients with anxious distress can be identified using proxies for the DSM-5 criteria from the Montgomery–Åsberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS), Inventory for Depressive Symptomatology Self Report (IDS-SR), and Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAM-D) [3, 13, 28, 29]. The prevalence of anxious distress in patients with MDD is reported as 56–79% across studies [3, 6, 30–34].

Anxious Depression

Anxious depression was defined in the STAR*D trial as a score ≥7 on the HAM-D anxiety/somatisation factor [2], which includes six items from the HAM-D: anxiety (psychic), anxiety (somatic), somatic symptoms (gastrointestinal), somatic symptoms (general), hypochondriasis, and insight [2].

In STAR*D (N = 1500), 46% of outpatients with MDD met the criteria for anxious depression [2].

Other Definitions of MDD with Anxiety Symptoms

Numerous other definitions for MDD with anxiety symptoms have been used in clinical trials and post hoc analyses, including Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (HAM-A) score ≥20, ≥16, or >14; Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7-item (GAD-7) score ≥10; a requirement for anxiety medication; presence of comorbid anxiety disorders; or presence of anxiety symptoms judged by physician assessment [35–40]. It has been shown that 45–67% of individuals with MDD have one or more comorbid anxiety disorders [1].

Rationale, Aims, and Scope of Review

Depression and anxiety may share a similar basis, both symptomatically and genetically [41], although mechanisms underlying depression and anxiety together are not fully understood [42, 43]. For example, anxiety and depression may be linked through anhedonia: for some individuals, anxiety leads to anhedonia, which then leads to depression [42]; in others, anhedonia may be a common substrate contributing to the development of both depression and anxiety [43]. It is likely that a number of physiological processes contribute to the presentation of MDD with anxiety symptoms: previous publications have summarized the potential roles of neurobiology, inflammation, working memory, and cognitive control activation [1, 27, 44–48]. Monoamine dysregulation has been implicated in the pathophysiology of MDD [49–52] and in the pathophysiology of anxiety disorders [53, 54]; however, reviews of monoamine system involvement in MDD together with anxiety symptoms are rare. Recent reviews explored the role of serotonin (5-HT) or dopamine (DA) in depression and anxiety as separate conditions [55–57], but there are (to the authors’ knowledge) no corresponding recent reviews of the role of noradrenaline (also called norepinephrine; NE).

This narrative review has two aims. First, to summarize the hypothetical role of NE, 5-HT, and DA systems in MDD with anxiety symptoms. Second, to review recent clinical data supporting pharmacotherapies recommended for the first- and second-line adjunctive treatment of MDD with anxiety symptoms. The focus on adjunctive treatments is to recognise the poor treatment outcomes for patients with MDD with anxiety symptoms [2, 8, 13, 14, 58], and therefore the likelihood that many of these patients will require treatment beyond initial ADT.

The first aim was addressed by examining published data relating to the role of monoamines in MDD. Due to the nature of this topic, much of the literature is based on preclinical, mechanistic, and pharmacological data, rather than prespecified clinical trials. The authors did not identify any direct data on the role of monoamines in MDD with anxiety symptoms. As such, literature relating to depression, anxiety, and other indications (such as post-traumatic stress disorder) and more general neuropharmacological studies (for example, those investigating wakefulness, cognition, hyperarousal, and hedonic tone) were considered when relevant. Given this, the discussions on monoamine dysregulation are exploratory and hypothetical. Direct connections between monoamine signalling and MDD with anxiety symptoms, and between receptor binding profiles and efficacy and safety outcomes, have not been established or evaluated in clinical trials.

For the second aim, the scope was defined to ensure that reviewed data were specifically relevant to patients with MDD with anxiety symptoms. Other populations, such as patients with bipolar depression with anxiety, or those with anxiety alone, were excluded. To identify clinically relevant adjunctive treatments, the authors assessed several clinical practice guidelines for the treatment of MDD [15–18, 21, 22]. Of these, the 2023 Canadian Network for Mood and Anxiety Treatments (CANMAT) guidelines were ultimately chosen to guide the treatments reviewed [15]. The CANMAT guidelines were chosen as: (1) they represent the most up-to-date recommendations at the time of writing, so may be considered most reflective of the latest clinical data; (2) CANMAT is specifically dedicated to the topics of mood and anxiety; (3) while developed in Canada, the guidelines are used globally; and (4) the guidelines were considered to be the most comprehensive regarding the adjunctive treatment of MDD. The treatments reviewed in this article are recommended for adjunctive treatment of difficult-to-treat MDD (defined as “persistent depression that has failed numerous standard treatments”) [15]. By focusing on first- and second-line adjunctive pharmacotherapies, some treatment modalities were excluded. Examples include: vortioxetine monotherapy, for which clinical data are available in MDD with anxiety symptoms [59, 60] but is not recommended in the CANMAT guidelines for adjunctive treatment of MDD [15]; benzodiazepines, which are used clinically for the treatment of MDD with anxiety symptoms [61] despite a lack of adequate clinical data [62]; and non-pharmacological therapies, which are a cornerstone of initial MDD treatment and are relevant throughout all treatment stages [15].

Literature Search Strategy

Publications relating to the role of monoamines in MDD were identified through a PubMed search using the following terms in the title or abstract, published from January 2014 to July 2024: (major depressive disorder OR MDD) AND (anxiety OR anxious) AND (monoamine OR dopamine OR dopaminergic OR serotonin OR serotonergic OR noradrenaline OR norepinephrine OR noradrenergic). The search results were manually screened for relevance. Reference lists of review articles were also screened, and additional targeted searches were conducted in PubMed for topics that required further research.

Following the identification of relevant adjunctive treatments from the CANMAT guidelines [15], publications of recent clinical data were identified through a PubMed search using the following terms in any field, published from January 2004 to July 2024: [drug name] AND (major depressive disorder OR MDD) AND anxi*. The search results were manually screened for relevance. Publication types were limited to those that provide the most robust clinical data: randomised controlled trials (RCTs); post hoc subgroup, secondary, and pooled analyses of RCTs; meta-analyses; non-randomised, open-label, and/or single-arm trials; retrospective studies; non-interventional studies; and chart reviews. The following publication types were excluded: case studies; opinion articles; narrative reviews; non-peer-reviewed evidence such as books and conference proceedings.

For completeness, the searches were re-run up to October 2025, prior to manuscript submission.

Monoamine Dysregulation in MDD with Anxiety Symptoms

The information in this section is hypothesis-generating and based on interpretation of preclinical, mechanistic, and translational data across relevant indications. No published clinical trials were identified that directly explored links between monoamine signalling and the clinical presentation of MDD with anxiety symptoms. Although this review focused on NE, 5-HT, and DA, other systems are likely to have a role in the pathophysiology of MDD with anxiety symptoms, and not all manifestations of depression and anxiety are solely due to dysregulation of NE, 5-HT, and DA.

Noradrenaline

Noradrenaline regulates wakefulness, energy levels, attention, and motivation [63–67]. A hyperactive NE system leads to increased sympathetic activity and arousal, which can result in anxiety, agitation, or irritability [63–65, 68]. A hypoactive NE system leads to decreased sympathetic activity and arousal, which can result in blunted affect, reduced energy, lassitude, anhedonia, and apathy [63, 65, 68, 69]. The effects of NE are mediated via three classes of adrenergic receptors (ARs): α1-, α2-, and β-AR. These are further divided into nine subtypes (α1A, α1B, α1D, α2A, α2B, α2C, β1, β2, and β3) [70, 71]. Depending on the subtype, ARs can be located pre-synaptically or post-synaptically [72], and their activity may be inhibitory or excitatory [68]. Furthermore, the density of each receptor subtype varies across brain regions, and ARs vary in their affinity for NE [68]. For example, α2C-ARs inhibit NE release when the concentration of NE is low, whereas α2A-ARs inhibit NE release at higher NE concentrations [73]. As such, the physiologic effect of NE in psychological disorders is a complex subject, which is covered in detail by Jain et al [68].

Serotonin

The 5-HT system has long been the target of ADT, and has a role in mood regulation, anxiety, irritability, and sleep [63, 74, 75]. The role of 5-HT in depression and anxiety has been extensively reviewed elsewhere [55, 56].

The effects of 5-HT are mediated via 5-HT receptors: there are seven groups of receptors (5-HT1 to 5-HT7), which can be further divided into subgroups [76]. The 5-HT1A receptors are an important regulator of 5-HT activity [55, 76], and are expressed both pre-synaptically and post-synaptically [56]. The 5-HT2A receptors are postsynaptic, and their activation affects synaptic plasticity and memory formation [55]. The 5-HT2B receptors can be found in several brain regions including the medial amygdala, cerebellum, lateral septum, and dorsal hypothalamic nucleus [77, 78]. Activation of 5-HT2B receptors can influence arousal, mood, and cognitive systems [77, 78].

Dopamine

Dysfunction in the DA system results in pessimistic thoughts, avoidant behaviour, and loss of interest [63, 79, 80]. An example of evidence for the role of DA in MDD with anxiety symptoms is found in a positron emission tomography study, in which increased D2 and D3 receptor availability was observed in patients with MDD compared with healthy controls [81]. This increase in D2 and D3 receptor availability in patients with MDD was also associated with higher levels of anxiety [81]. In addition, patients with MDD who did not achieve remission after ADT had higher anxiety levels, and greater D2 and D3 receptor availability, compared with patients who did achieve remission [81]. This study provides preliminary and exploratory evidence for a DA-mediated process in patients with MDD and anxiety [81].

There are five classes of DA receptors (D1 to D5), of which the D2 and D3 receptors have been implicated in the pathophysiology of depressive symptoms [82, 83]. The dopaminergic system is complex: D2 and D3 receptors can be located pre-synaptically or post-synaptically, and receptor activity is mediated by several factors across different brain regions (reviewed in depth by Zhao et al [83]).

Signalling Pathway Interactions

Although NE, 5-HT, and DA pathways are often discussed separately, interactions exist between the three systems. For example, an increase in 5-HT not only increases serotonergic neurotransmission but can also lower NE and DA levels [84, 85]. The interplay between monoamine systems highlights the importance of a careful and informed approach to balancing monoamine levels, and that consideration of only one system in isolation is not necessarily the optimal approach to treatment.

None of the discussed mechanisms have been shown to predict clinical response or safety in MDD with anxiety symptoms.

Levels of evidence in the CANMAT guidelines are: Level 1, “High-quality meta-analysis with narrow confidence intervals and/or 2 or more RCTs (randomised controlled trials) with adequate sample size, preferably placebo-controlled”; Level 2, “Lower-quality meta-analysis with wide confidence intervals and/or 1 or more RCTs with adequate sample size”; Level 3: “Small-sample RCTs or non-randomised, controlled prospective studies or high-quality retrospective studies”; Level 4: “Expert opinion/consensus” [15]. The levels of evidence relate to the treatment of difficult-to-treat MDD, not to the treatment of MDD with anxiety symptoms.

For some treatments, no or only limited clinical data were identified, and so the corresponding subsections are short. The authors acknowledge these treatments may be used clinically for MDD with anxiety symptoms, but this review includes only published clinical data.

Atypical Antipsychotics

The presence of anxiety symptoms is a key factor influencing physician decisions when prescribing an adjunctive antipsychotic in MDD [106]. The efficacy of adjunctive atypical antipsychotics in patients with MDD and an inadequate response to ADT has typically been evaluated in short-term studies, and longer-term efficacy is less clear [117]. Longer-term studies have been conducted (investigating symptom control or relapse-prevention) [118–122], but none of these included an anxiety component. As such, the present review includes only short-term data.

Aripiprazole

Aripiprazole is recommended as a first-line adjunctive treatment for difficult-to-treat MDD (Level 1 evidence) in the CANMAT guidelines [15]. Aripiprazole acts as a partial agonist at D2, D3, and 5-HT1A receptors, and an antagonist at 5-HT2A receptors [108, 123]. Aripiprazole also acts as an antagonist at α1- and α2-ARs, with lower binding affinity relative to its activity at DA and 5-HT receptors (Fig. 2).

A post hoc pooled analysis from two 6-week RCTs (N = 742) evaluated adjunctive aripiprazole in patients with anxious depression according to the STAR*D criteria [86]. In this analysis, depression symptoms (MADRS total score) improved with adjunctive aripiprazole versus adjunctive placebo in patients with anxious depression, and in patients with non-anxious depression [86].

Two small studies assessed aripiprazole in patients with MDD with anxiety symptoms: a single-centre retrospective chart review of patients with MDD who required additional treatment for reduction of anxiety symptoms (N = 41) [35], and an open-label, single-arm study in patients with MDD and comorbid anxiety disorders (N = 10) [36]. With adjunctive aripiprazole, depression symptoms (HAM-D) and anxiety symptoms (HAM-A) significantly improved from baseline to Week 8 in the chart review, and depression symptoms (MADRS) and anxiety symptoms (HAM-A) significantly improved from baseline to Week 9 in the open-label study.

Safety data were reported in two of the above analyses. In the post hoc analysis (N = 742), AEs reported in ≥10% of patients receiving ADT + aripiprazole versus ADT + placebo were akathisia (25.5% vs 5.2% in anxious depression; 23.4% vs 2.9% in non-anxious depression) and restlessness (15.3% vs 1.7% in anxious depression; 7.1% vs 2.2% in non-anxious depression). Antidepressant treatment + aripiprazole was associated with greater weight gain (1.6–1.8 kg) than ADT + placebo (0.2–0.5 kg), which did not differ between anxious and non-anxious subgroups [86]. In the open-label, single-arm study (N = 10), ~50% of participants reported restlessness or stiffness when increasing aripiprazole dose from 5 to 10 mg, although the small sample size limits strong conclusions [36].

Brexpiprazole

Brexpiprazole is recommended as a first-line adjunctive treatment for MDD (Level 1 evidence) in the CANMAT guidelines [15]. Brexpiprazole exhibits partial agonism at D2, D3, and 5-HT1A receptors, and antagonism at 5-HT2A, α1, and α2C receptors [124] (Fig. 2).

Three post hoc pooled analyses were identified for adjunctive brexpiprazole in patients with MDD with or without anxious distress (using proxy criteria for the DSM-5 specifier). Two analyses included data from the same three 6-week RCTs (Pyxis, Polaris, and Sirius; N = 1908 [29] and N = 1171 [28]), and an earlier analysis included data from two of the three RCTs (Pyxis and Polaris; N = 989) [87]. Overall, these analyses showed that, compared with adjunctive placebo, adjunctive brexpiprazole improved depression symptoms (MADRS total score and a number of individual MADRS item scores) and anxiety symptoms (HAM-D anxiety/somatisation factor; assessed in one analysis only) in patients with and without anxious distress [28, 29, 87].

One of these post hoc analyses also evaluated patients with MDD and anxious depression (N = 574), defined by STAR*D criteria [28]. In this analysis, depression symptoms (MADRS total score) and anxiety symptoms (HAM-D anxiety/somatisation factor) improved with adjunctive brexpiprazole versus adjunctive placebo in patients with anxious depression, and in patients with non-anxious depression [28].

In addition, in an exploratory, open-label, single-arm 6-week study in participants with MDD and a HAM-A total score ≥20 (N = 37), depression (MADRS total score) and anxiety (HAM-A total score) symptoms improved significantly from baseline with adjunctive brexpiprazole [37].

In the post hoc analyses of three RCTs by Thase et al (N = 1171) [28], akathisia was the only AE with an incidence ≥10% with ADT + brexpiprazole in any subgroup: 10.3% and 8.5% in patients with and without anxious distress, respectively (3.6% and 2.0% with ADT + placebo), and 9.0% and 10.3% in patients with and without anxious depression, respectively (3.2% and 2.6% with ADT + placebo). Weight increase was reported as an AE with ADT + brexpiprazole in 6.3% of patients with anxious distress and 5.9% of patients without anxious distress (1.8% and 1.2% for placebo + ADT, respectively) [28]. In the exploratory, single-arm, open-label study (N = 37), AEs reported in ≥10% of patients receiving ADT + brexpiprazole were increased appetite (13.5%), and diarrhoea, dizziness, and dry mouth (each 10.8%). Treatment-emergent AEs related to extrapyramidal symptoms were reported by 8.1% of participants, including mild akathisia in 5.4% of participants. The mean change in body weight from baseline to Week 6 was +1.6 kg [37].

Olanzapine

Olanzapine is recommended as a second-line adjunctive treatment for MDD (Level 1 evidence) in the CANMAT guidelines [15]. Olanzapine has moderate-to-low affinity as an antagonist for 5-HT2A, 5-HT2C, D1-4, α1, and α2c receptors (Fig. 2) [110, 114].

One publication was identified that assessed olanzapine + venlafaxine (initiated together as combination therapy) and venlafaxine alone in patients with MDD and anxiety symptoms (based on clinical observation and psychiatrist interview based on DSM-5 criteria). In this 4-week single-centre naturalistic study (N = 57), both treatment arms showed an improvement in symptoms of depression (MADRS and Beck Depression scale) and anxiety (HAM-A total score) over time, but there was no significant difference between treatment groups at the end of the study [40]. Adverse events were reported by week rather than overall; those that occurred in ≥10% of patients during Week 1 with olanzapine + venlafaxine versus venlafaxine alone were drowsiness (35.3% vs 0%), dry mouth (32.4% vs 0%), collapse states (29.4% vs 26.1%), increased sweating (23.5% vs 30.4%), palpitations (23.5% vs 26.1%), obstipation (20.6% vs 0%), accommodation disorders (20.6% vs 0%), dizziness (17.6% vs 8.7%), dyspeptic difficulties (11.8% vs 30.4%), and muscle weakness (11.8% vs 0%); AEs were mostly resolved by the end of Week 4 [40].

Quetiapine XR

Quetiapine XR (extended release) is recommended as a second-line adjunctive treatment for MDD (Level 1 evidence) in the CANMAT guidelines [15]. Quetiapine is an antagonist at D2 and 5-HT2A receptors [107], with a lower overall binding affinity for monoamine receptors compared with other atypical antipsychotics (Fig. 2).

In a post hoc pooled analysis of patients with anxious depression (STAR*D criteria) from two 8-week RCTs assessing quetiapine XR monotherapy (DIAMOND and MOONSTONE; N = 968) [97], depression (MADRS total score) and anxiety (HAM-A score) symptoms showed greater improvement with quetiapine XR versus placebo in those with anxious or non-anxious depression [97]. This analysis also included a subgroup of patients with MDD and anxiety symptoms defined as HAM-A total score ≥20 [97]. This same criterion was used in a post hoc analysis of the 6-week RCTs, PEARL and ONYX, assessing adjunctive quetiapine XR in MDD (N = 919) [98], and a post hoc analysis of a 9-week RCT of quetiapine XR monotherapy in older patients (aged >65 years) with MDD (N = 335) [99]. All three analyses showed greater improvements with quetiapine XR versus placebo in depression (MADRS total score) and anxiety symptoms (HAM-A score) [97–99]. These post hoc analyses do not report the incidence of individual AEs.

Three small RCTs of quetiapine XR in patients with MDD with anxiety symptoms as adjunctive treatment (total N = 143) or monotherapy (total N = 14) (Table 1) also showed improvements in depression and anxiety symptoms with quetiapine XR compared with placebo, but the effects were not always significant [100–102]. Adverse events relating to somnolence, sedation, and lethargy were reported differently across the three RCTs (i.e., grouped terms vs individual terms), which precludes presentation of event rates for these AEs. However, in the three RCTs (total N = 143), these events as grouped terms or individual AEs were common and reported in ≥10% of patients receiving quetiapine XR, with an incidence greater than placebo [100–102]. Other AEs reported in ≥10% of patients with quetiapine XR versus placebo in any of the three RCTs (and with incidence greater than placebo) were increased weight (34.5% vs 10.3%), dry mouth (39.6–54.5% vs 8.3–21.4%), fatigue (14.6–45.5% vs 16.7–21.4%), dizziness (14.6–20.7% vs 7.1–24.1%), increased dreaming/nightmares (13.8% vs 0.0%), constipation (13.8% vs 0.0%), appetite disturbance (12.5% vs 10.7%), dysuria (10.3% vs 3.4%), gastrointestinal AEs (22.9–27.3% vs 14.3–16.7%), and headache (13.8–27.3% vs 0.0–27.6%) [100–102]. Weight change was +2.8 kg with adjunctive quetiapine and +0.3 kg with adjunctive placebo in the 12-week RCT [100], and +2.4 kg with adjunctive quetiapine and −0.3 kg with adjunctive placebo in the 8-week RCT [102].

Risperidone

Risperidone, a 5-HT2A, D2, α1A, and α2C antagonist (Fig. 2) [114], is recommended as a second-line adjunctive treatment for MDD in the CANMAT guidelines (Level 1 evidence) [15]. No studies were identified that assessed risperidone in patients with MDD with anxiety symptoms.

Cariprazine

Cariprazine is recommended as a second-line adjunctive treatment for MDD (Level 2 evidence) in the CANMAT guidelines [15]. The primary mechanism of action for cariprazine is thought to be via the dopaminergic pathway [82], with high binding affinity as a D2 and D3 partial agonist (Fig. 2).

A post hoc analysis of a 6-week RCT (N = 751) assessed the efficacy of adjunctive cariprazine in patients with anxious depression (STAR*D criteria), and in patients with MDD and moderate/severe anxiety symptoms (HAM-A score >14) [38]. Depression (MADRS total score) and anxiety symptoms (HAM-A total score) improved with adjunctive cariprazine versus adjunctive placebo in patients with anxious depression, and in patients with MDD and moderate/severe anxiety [38]. However, the improvement versus adjunctive placebo was not significant in patients with non-anxious depression, nor in patients with MDD and mild anxiety symptoms [38]. No AEs were reported in ≥10% of patients in the ADT + cariprazine treatment groups, although only AEs occurring at twice the rate of ADT + placebo were presented. Akathisia was reported in 5.2% and 7.9% of patients treated with cariprazine at doses of 1.5 mg and 3.0 mg, respectively, and in 0.8% of patients in the placebo group [38]. While not reported in the above publication, weight gain with cariprazine is considered to be lower than with other D2 partial agonists (aripiprazole and brexpiprazole) [82].

Other Pharmacotherapies

Bupropion

Recommended as second-line adjunctive treatment with Level 1 evidence in the CANMAT guidelines [15], bupropion is a dual inhibitor of NE and DA reuptake, with functional potency for the presynaptic NE and DA transporters [125]. It has no clinically significant effects on the 5-HT system, or direct effects on postsynaptic receptors [125].

Ten RCTs of bupropion monotherapy versus selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SSRI) monotherapy were included in a post hoc pooled analysis (N = 2122) in patients with or without anxious depression (STAR*D criteria) [89]. Bupropion showed worse outcomes compared with SSRIs across depression and anxiety symptoms (HAM-D and HAM-A total scores) in patients with anxious depression, with no difference between treatment arms in non-anxious depression [88]. In a meta-analysis of pooled individual patient data from the same trials and sample (N = 2122), there was no difference between bupropion and SSRI monotherapy in improvement in anxiety symptoms (HAM-D anxiety/somatisation factor and HAM-A total score) in patients with MDD [89].

Safety data were not reported in these analyses.

Ketamine/Esketamine

In the CANMAT guidelines, intranasal esketamine and intravenous (IV) racemic ketamine are recommended as second-line adjunctive treatment for MDD with Level 1 evidence [15]. The primary mechanism of action of ketamine and esketamine is via the glutamatergic system as a non-selective antagonist of the N-methyl-d-aspartate receptor [126]. Ketamine and esketamine may have some activity on the 5-HT and NE systems, but the mechanisms are not well studied [127].

One post hoc analysis has been conducted in patients with treatment-resistant MDD with anxious distress, defined using proxy criteria for the DSM-5 specifier, and based on data from a retrospective single-centre study of adjunctive IV ketamine (N = 209) [90]. An improvement from baseline in depressive symptoms (Quick IDS-SR16 [QIDS-SR16] total score) and anxiety symptoms (GAD-7 score) was observed with IV ketamine, which was more pronounced in patients with anxious distress than those without [90].

Five post hoc or secondary analyses of small RCTs assessed IV ketamine or esketamine as adjunctive treatment (total N = 274) or monotherapy (N = 26) in patients with and without anxious depression using STAR*D criteria (Table 1). The magnitude of effect varied across studies but, overall, the publications indicated improvements from baseline in depression or anxiety with ketamine/esketamine treatment in anxious and non-anxious depression [91–95]. One study included a placebo comparator arm: greater improvements in depressive symptoms (HAM-D total score) were reported with ketamine versus placebo in patients with non-anxious depression, but there was no treatment difference in patients with anxious depression [92]. Overall, there was no consensus across studies regarding whether the treatment effect was more pronounced in patients with anxious or non-anxious depression [91–95].

One post hoc analysis of a 4-week RCT of esketamine nasal spray plus a newly initiated ADT (N = 223) [39], and one retrospective analysis of adjunctive subcutaneous esketamine (N = 70) [96], were conducted using other definitions of anxiety symptoms in patients with treatment-resistant MDD. Both studies identified a reduction in depressive symptoms (MADRS total score) in patients with and without anxiety at baseline. In the post hoc analysis, there was no apparent difference between patients with anxiety symptoms (baseline GAD-7 >10) or patients without anxiety symptoms (baseline GAD-7 ≤10) [39], whereas the retrospective analysis found that anxiety disorder comorbidity predicted a better outcome [96]. The efficacy of ketamine and esketamine on depression in adults with MDD, in the presence of anxiety symptoms, has been supported by real-world evidence [128, 129].

Of the analyses above, AEs were reported only in the post hoc analysis of a 4-week RCT in patients with MDD and comorbid anxiety (N = 223) [39]. In patients with comorbid anxiety (n = 162), AEs reported in ≥10% of patients with esketamine nasal spray versus placebo were nausea (28.9% vs 7.6%), dissociation (26.5% vs 2.5%), vertigo (24.1% vs 3.8%), dysgeusia (21.1% vs 12.7%), dizziness (19.3% vs 3.8%), headache (18.1% vs 20.3%), somnolence (16.9% vs 3.8%), anxiety (13.3% vs 5.1%), paraesthesia (13.3% vs 1.3%), insomnia (12.1% vs 6.3%), vomiting (12.1% vs 1.3%), paraesthesia oral (10.8% vs 1.3%), and blurred vision (10.8% vs 2.5%). In patients without comorbid anxiety (n = 61), AEs reported in ≥10% of patients with esketamine versus placebo: vertigo (32.3% vs 0%), dysgeusia (32.3% vs 10.0%), dissociation (25.8% vs 6.7%), headache (25.8% vs 10.0%), dizziness (22.6% vs 6.7%), nausea (19.4% vs 3.3%), blurred vision (16.1% vs 3.3%), increased blood pressure (12.9% vs 2.1%), and postural dizziness (12.9% vs 0%). The placebo‐adjusted incidence of anxiety, dissociation, nausea, and paraesthesia was higher for esketamine‐treated patients with versus without comorbid anxiety [39].

Lithium

Lithium is recommended in the CANMAT guidelines as a second-line adjunctive treatment with Level 1 evidence [15]. It has broad effects on the central nervous system, including modulation of DA, glutamate, and GABA neurotransmission [130]. No studies were identified that assessed lithium in patients with MDD with anxiety symptoms.

Mirtazapine/Mianserin

Mirtazapine and mianserin are recommended in the CANMAT guidelines as second-line adjunctive treatment with Level 2 evidence [15]. They are antagonists of the presynaptic α2-AR [15], which causes an increased release of NE and may underlie their activating effect on the sympathetic nervous system [131, 132]. Mirtazapine and mianserin are also potent antagonists of the histamine H1 receptor and the 5-HT2A and 5-HT2C receptors [131, 132].

A post hoc analysis of an RCT assessed adjunctive mirtazapine or adjunctive placebo in patients with treatment-resistant MDD (N = 477). Patients were stratified by baseline anxiety level according to GAD-7 score [104]. Patients with MDD and severe anxiety (GAD-7 ≥16) showed improvement in depression (Beck Depression Inventory II) and anxiety (GAD-7) symptoms with mirtazapine compared with placebo, but no benefit was observed in patients with no/mild generalised anxiety (GAD-7 ≤10) [104].

In an open-label 8-week trial (N = 60), patients with MDD with anxiety symptoms (HAM-A score >18) were randomised to mirtazapine or paroxetine monotherapy. Both treatments showed a similar and significant reduction from baseline in depression and anxiety symptoms (HAM-D and HAM-A score) [105].

A preliminary RCT assessed various treatment regimens based on mirtazapine and/or serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) in patients with MDD with anxiety symptoms (assessed by items of the IDS and HAM-D) (N = 107) [103]. The treatment arms were mirtazapine or SNRI (with or without cognitive behavioural therapy), and mirtazapine + SNRI (with or without brain stimulation therapies) [103]. All treatment groups showed significant improvement from baseline in depressive symptoms (HAM-D and QIDS-SR scores) [103].

Two of these studies reported safety outcomes. In the open-label 8-week trial (N = 60), AEs reported in ≥10% of patients in the mirtazapine versus paroxetine monotherapy groups were somnolence (26.7% vs 10.0%), dizziness (23.3% vs 10.0%), increased appetite (23.3% vs 0%), dry mouth (13.3% vs 16.7%), and weight gain (10.0% vs 0%) [105]. In the preliminary RCT of regimens based on mirtazapine and/or SNRIs (N = 107), there was no difference in AE rates between treatment groups. Tremor was the only AE reported in ≥10% of patients in any treatment group (11.1% with mirtazapine or SNRIs; 3.6% with mirtazapine or SNRIs + cognitive behavioural therapy; 0% with mirtazapine + SNRI; 0% with mirtazapine + SNRI + brain-stimulation therapies) [103].

Modafinil

Recommended in the CANMAT guidelines as a second-line adjunctive treatment with Level 2 evidence [15], modafinil inhibits DA reuptake via binding to the DA transporter, resulting in increased extracellular DA levels [133]. No studies were identified that assessed modafinil in patients with MDD with anxiety symptoms.

Triiodothyronine

Triiodothyronine is a thyroid hormone and is recommended in the CANMAT guidelines as a second-line adjunctive treatment with Level 2 evidence [15]. Modulation of the 5-HT system and, to a lesser extent, the NE system is thought to be involved in the antidepressant effects of triiodothyronine [134]. No studies were identified that assessed triiodothyronine in patients with MDD with anxiety symptoms.

Discussion

Based on preclinical, mechanistic, and pharmacological data, it has been hypothesised that dysregulation of the monoamine system may play a role in the pathophysiology of MDD with anxiety symptoms, including complex interplay between hyperactive and hypoactive NE signalling [65, 68], serotonin receptor activity [56, 135, 136], and DA receptor activity [79, 81]. Many of the pharmacological treatments recommended for the adjunctive treatment of MDD [15] act on monoamine systems, and it has been proposed that the monoamine binding profile of a treatment may influence the clinical efficacy and safety profile [82, 124, 137], although there is no direct clinical evidence for this link.

It is widely recognised that the presence of anxiety symptoms in MDD is associated with poorer outcomes [2, 8, 13, 14, 58]. However, currently available treatment guidelines provide either no or limited recommendations for treatments specific to this patient population [15–18, 21, 22]. Therefore, there was a need for a review of available clinical data that may assist clinicians in their treatment decisions.

For the adjunctive treatment of difficult-to-treat MDD, the CANMAT guidelines recommend aripiprazole or brexpiprazole as first-line treatment, with a larger number of options for second-line treatment (listed in Table 1) [15]. Real-world data indicate that adjunctive treatment regimens are most commonly received in the form of an additional ADT (69–81% of patients) followed by an atypical antipsychotic adjunctive to ADT (17–25% of patients) [138].

Both aripiprazole and brexpiprazole showed preliminary signals of efficacy in patients with MDD with anxiety symptoms, although the majority of the studies were post hoc and therefore exploratory in nature. The evidence base for adjunctive brexpiprazole encompasses data for a range of definitions of MDD with anxiety symptoms, and the analyses generally utilised large sample sizes including post hoc analyses of RCTs (up to N = 1908), and a small exploratory open-label study (N = 37) [28, 29, 37]. The data for aripiprazole included fewer patients, with a post hoc analysis of two RCTs in anxious depression (N = 742) [86], a small single-centre chart review study (N = 41) [35], and a small open-label trial (N = 10) [36].

Of the atypical antipsychotics recommended in the CANMAT guidelines for second-line adjunctive treatment of difficult-to-treat MDD, quetiapine XR has the largest dataset for the treatment of MDD with anxiety symptoms, with three post hoc analyses (N = 968, N = 919, and N = 335) and three small clinical trials (N = 76, N = 23, and N = 58) [97–102]. In the three post hoc analyses, quetiapine XR showed a greater improvement in depression and anxiety symptoms than placebo, and this was generally supported by clinical trials. Only one study/analysis in MDD with anxiety symptoms was identified for olanzapine (N = 57) [40] and one for cariprazine (N = 751) [38], and no data were available for risperidone.

Most other treatments recommended in the CANMAT guidelines for the second-line adjunctive treatment of difficult-to-treat MDD (bupropion, lithium, mirtazapine/mianserin, modafinil, triiodothyronine) had limited or no clinical data in MDD with anxiety symptoms. Although they are potentially used by some clinicians in this patient population, the lack of published clinical data precludes wider discussion in this review. Several post hoc and secondary analyses were available for ketamine/esketamine in the treatment of MDD with anxiety symptoms (N = 26–223) [39, 90–93, 95, 96]. Overall, the data suggests a positive effect with ketamine/esketamine on depression and anxiety symptoms for patients with MDD and anxiety symptoms.

Discussion of safety was restricted by inconsistent reporting between studies and analyses. As with any treatment, the balance of benefit and safety should be considered on an individual basis according to a patient’s presentation as well as their needs and preferences.

Akathisia and weight gain are common safety concerns for physicians prescribing adjunctive antipsychotics in MDD [106]. Where data were reported, akathisia rates were 5.2–7.9% for ADT + cariprazine [38], 5.4–10.3% for ADT + brexpiprazole [28, 37, 87], and 23.4–25.5% for ADT + aripiprazole [86]. This is broadly in line with comparative analyses of clinical trial and product label data, which indicated lower rates of akathisia with brexpiprazole compared with aripiprazole in patients with MDD [139, 140], although rates of akathisia were higher with cariprazine across approved indications [82]. Where reported, weight gain from baseline throughout short-term analyses was 1.6–1.8 kg with ADT + aripiprazole [86] and 2.4–2.8 kg with ADT + quetiapine XR [100, 102].

Sedation is also an important consideration for physicians when treating MDD [106]. Although sedation may sometimes be considered beneficial, it is not always a desired effect [141], and it may be particularly problematic for functioning and well-being in the context of use of antipsychotics in the treatment of psychological disorders [142]. In MDD, treatments with a sedating effect are associated with an increased likelihood for treatment discontinuation and reduced functioning [12, 141], and may contribute to weight gain via reduced physical activity [143]. Where safety data were reported in the summarised articles, AEs of sedation, somnolence, drowsiness, and/or lethargy were reported in ≥10% of patients receiving olanzapine + venlafaxine (drowsiness 35.3%) [40], esketamine (somnolence 16.9%) [39], mirtazapine monotherapy (somnolence 26.7%) [105], and was notably common for quetiapine XR [100–102]. Antagonism of the H1 receptor can cause sedating effects [82, 114, 143], and quetiapine is a potent H1 receptor antagonist; quetiapine’s sedative effects are also noted in a Cochrane review of five atypical antipsychotics in MDD and dysthymia [144].

It is important to note that the term “MDD with anxiety symptoms” encompasses a heterogeneous patient group, and the summaries of clinical data should be interpreted with this in mind. Treatment effects in an individual may not necessarily be reflected in group effects or captured in a clinical trial, and vice versa. Of the definitions for MDD with anxiety symptoms used in this review, the DSM-5 specifier for anxious distress may be of most importance as it is considered clinically relevant in the acute and maintenance phases of treatment and can be used to plan treatment and monitor anxiety symptoms over time [34, 145, 146]. However, this definition was used in only four of the reviewed publications, all of which were post hoc analyses, and therefore defined using proxies rather than via clinical consultation based on the DSM-5 [28, 29, 87, 90]. Prospective studies of treatments in patients with MDD with anxious distress per DSM-5 criteria using anxiety-specific endpoints would be valuable.

Given the different monoamine receptor binding profiles across atypical antipsychotics, and the hypothetical role of monoamine signalling in MDD with anxiety symptoms, it is relevant to consider how monoamine receptor activity may impact the efficacy and safety profiles of these treatments. However, there is no direct clinical evidence supporting a link between receptor binding profile and efficacy or safety in MDD with anxiety symptoms, and exploration of the topic is therefore based on interpretation of preclinical, mechanistic, and pharmacological data across indications.

A previous review article suggested that α1B-AR and α2C-AR antagonism may reduce symptoms of NE hyperactivity, such as anxiety and irritability [68]. In addition, data from preclinical and clinical studies indicate that α1A-AR and α2C-AR antagonism can have an antidepressant effect [73, 147–150]. Animal studies have indicated that targeting 5-HT receptors may be useful in the treatment of depression and anxiety: modulation of presynaptic 5-HT1A receptors altered anxiety-like behaviour [136]; activation of postsynaptic 5-HT1A receptors reduced neuronal hyperactivity and may alleviate depression and anxiety symptoms [56, 135]; and agonism of postsynaptic 5-HT1A receptors demonstrated dose-dependent anxiolytic effects [151]. In a review article assessing agents targeting 5-HT1A, both full and partial agonism had anxiolytic and antidepressant effects [55]. Animal studies have also indicated that 5-HT2A receptor antagonists may have antidepressant effects through inhibition of 5-HT reuptake, and clinical trials suggest that 5-HT2A receptor antagonists can enhance the antidepressant and anxiolytic effects of SSRIs [152, 153], as well as improving sleep patterns [154]. Antagonism of 5-HT2 receptors in the amygdala attenuated fear and anxiety behaviours in guinea pigs [155]. In murine studies, partial deletion of 5-HT2B receptors was associated with improvements in cognition, and antagonism of 5-HT2B receptors was associated with increased motor activity and wakefulness [77, 78]. Therapies with partial agonism of D2 and D3 receptors – as opposed to full agonism or antagonism – can act to rebalance a dysregulated dopaminergic system by acting as both a functional agonist and antagonist [79, 156]. Given the hypothesised role of DA in the pathophysiology of MDD with anxiety symptoms [81], the modulatory action of D2 and D3 receptor partial agonism may be an appropriate treatment approach in MDD with anxiety symptoms.

Regarding safety profiles, D2 receptor antagonism has been associated with weight gain and increased food intake, extrapyramidal symptoms, tardive dyskinesia, hyperprolactinaemia, and chronic insulin depletion [82, 114, 143, 157]. Conversely, partial agonism at the D2 receptor may regulate excess DA activity and reduce the potential for antidopaminergic-related side effects, while also contributing to a low risk for D2 receptor agonist-mediated AEs (such as akathisia, restlessness, insomnia, and nausea) [112]. It may be hypothesised that 5-HT1A receptor partial agonism could reduce side effects associated with modulation of D2 receptors, including extrapyramidal symptoms, weight gain through increased food intake, and hyperprolactinaemia [114, 158], and 5-HT2A receptor antagonism reduces the potential for akathisia that can result from D2 receptor antagonism [124, 159]. Finally, α1B-AR antagonism may mitigate the risk of akathisia and extrapyramidal symptoms associated with D2 receptor antagonism [140].

While beyond the scope of this review, benzodiazepines are frequently prescribed in patients with MDD, often for symptoms of anxiety [61, 160–162]. The perceived benefit of benzodiazepines may be mediated in part by indirect action on monoamine signalling through positive allosteric modulation of synaptic GABA receptors [61, 163]. However, there is a lack of adequate and well-controlled studies in MDD [62, 164], and more recent clinical guidelines for the treatment of MDD do not routinely recommend use of benzodiazepines [15, 17, 18, 21, 22]. Common AEs include sedation, slowed reactions, and cognitive impairment [61]. Although some clinicians remain proponents of benzodiazepine use [61], careful consideration of the potential benefits versus the possible harms is required [62]. The frequent use of benzodiazepines highlights an implementation gap in the treatment of MDD with anxiety symptoms, wherein patients are receiving benzodiazepines and other treatments rather than approved, evidence-based therapeutics.

The strengths of this review include the presentation of a robust summary of a highly clinically relevant topic, given that a substantial proportion of patients with MDD also experience anxiety in some form. The review methodology encompassed three different definitions of ‘anxiety’, which aimed to ensure that the available data were captured, regardless of the descriptor or definition. In addition, the treatment focus was guided by a widely used clinical practice guideline. Lastly, the 20-year timeframe of the literature search enabled relevant data to be captured for all reviewed treatments, regardless of the recency of trial conduct or regulatory approval.

This review has several limitations. By defining MDD as the initial focus, studies primarily in anxiety could have been overlooked. However, the authors believe that identification of depression as the initial focus was the most clinically relevant approach. The literature search was performed in July 2024 and re-run up to the point of submission (October 2025); however, the authors believe no recent data have been excluded (up to time of publication). Some authors are employees of the pharmaceutical developers of brexpiprazole and aripiprazole, but potential bias was minimised by defining a clear and clinically relevant scope for the review, aligning with up-to-date treatment guidelines for MDD, and reviewing a large number of other pharmacotherapies beyond these treatments. The selected treatments reviewed in this work were based on the CANMAT guidelines, which were considered the most relevant due to their global reach and comprehensive recommendations for the adjunctive treatment of MDD; they are also the most recent. As the CANMAT guidelines provide minimal recommendations specific to MDD with anxiety symptoms (recommendations for anxious distress are the same as for standard MDD), the selected pharmacotherapies reviewed here are those recommended for first- and second-line adjunctive treatment in difficult-to-treat MDD. The authors focused on adjunctive options in recognition of the fact that patients with MDD and anxiety symptoms typically have poor treatment outcomes [2, 8, 13, 14, 58], and are therefore likely to require treatment beyond initial ADT. It is possible that relevant anxiety-based data could exist for third-line recommendations; however, based on preliminary research, the authors believe this is not the case. The scope of the review resulted in a focus on atypical antipsychotics and ketamine/esketamine, as these are the agents with the most clinical data in MDD with anxiety symptoms; other treatments were discussed more briefly due to the scarcity of relevant clinical data. Additionally, due to the scope of the review, monotherapy treatments that have shown effectiveness in MDD with anxiety symptoms, such as vortioxetine [59, 60], were not included. Lastly, many of the reviewed sources report post hoc data rather than prospective investigations. Evidence should therefore be interpreted in line with the limitations of post hoc analyses, such as lack of prospective endpoints, limited statistical power, and the exploratory nature. In addition, the wide variety of study designs and the heterogeneity in patient populations precluded cross-trial comparisons of efficacy and safety, and comparison of treatment effect between different definitions of MDD with anxiety was not possible. A gap remains in the evidence base due to the lack of primary studies in this population.

Conclusions

Dysregulation of the monoamine system may partly underlie the pathophysiology of MDD with anxiety symptoms. It has been hypothesised, based on preclinical, mechanistic, and translational evidence, that the specific binding profiles of pharmacotherapies for NE, 5-HT, and DA receptors, and whether they act as an antagonist, agonist, or partial agonist, may influence a treatment’s efficacy profile as well as the safety profile. The atypical antipsychotics aripiprazole, brexpiprazole, and quetiapine XR, alongside ketamine/esketamine, have shown improvements in depression and/or anxiety symptoms in patients with MDD with anxiety. However, the evidence is mainly short-term and derived from exploratory post hoc analyses of trials not designed to assess anxiety outcomes, highlighting the need for prospective clinical trials to assess treatments in this patient population. Ultimately, when evaluating adjunctive treatment options for patients with MDD with anxiety symptoms, it is important to consider the underlying pathophysiology of the disease, and treatments that are supported by robust clinical data.
