SHIFT : Robust Double Machine Learning for Average Dose-Response Functions under Heavy-Tailed Contamination
Eichi Uehara

Abstract
Double-machine-learning pipelines for the Average Dose-Response Function rely on kernel-weighted local-linear smoothers, which inherit unbounded functional influence: a single outlier within a kernel window biases the curve across the entire window. We introduce SHIFT (Self-calibrated Heavy-tail Inlier-Fit with Tempering), a robust DML estimator combining cross-fit nuisance orthogonalization with a kernel-local Welsch-loss second stage optimized by Graduated Non-Convexity, and — the principal design choice — a defensive OLS refit whose inlier cutoff is scaled by post-GNC residual MAD rather than the raw-outcome MAD. On a localized-contamination stress test at p = 0.25 p=0.25 this design choice drops level-RMSE from 1.03 to 0.33 while leaving clean and uniformly-contaminated runs unchanged. Across 1,400 main-sweep fits, SHIFT has competitive worst-case shape recovery (RMSE 0.325 0.325 at p = 0.25 p=0.25 , second to Huber-DML’s 0.276 0.276 ); among the three methods with worst-case RMSE below 0.35 0.35 , only SHIFT emits a non-uniform per-sample weight vector, recovering the ground-truth outlier mask at mean F 1 ≈ 0.96 \text{F}_{1}\approx 0.96 (range 0.945 0.945 – 0.968 0.968 ) on Gaussian-jump DGPs. We pair the estimator with a six-technique Extreme Value Theory diagnostic suite (Hill, GPD-MLE/PWM, GEV, Mean Excess, parameter stability, causal tail coefficient) that lets a practitioner distinguish Fréchet from Weibull regimes and choose between SHIFT and L1 alternatives on empirical grounds. Extensions to binary-treatment CATE (Huber pseudo-outcome X-Learner) and time-series ADRF (block-CV + rolling MAD) are included. A counter-intuitive ablation: linear nuisance models (Ridge, Lasso) outperform gradient-boosted nuisances for robust DML under uniform contamination, inverting the usual more-flexible-is-better heuristic. Code, raw CSVs, and per-cell appendix tables: https://github.com/EichiUehara/ADRF-Robust-DML .

Introduction

A large class of consequential decisions — setting a drug dosage, pricing an ad impression, deciding how much fertilizer to apply — requires an estimate of the
Average Dose-Response Function
(ADRF)
\theta(t)=\mathbb{E}[Y(t)]
, the expected potential outcome at continuous treatment level
t
, from observational data
\{(X_{i},T_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
with covariates
X\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, treatment
T\in\mathbb{R}
, and outcome
Y\in\mathbb{R}
. Modern practice estimates
\theta(t)
with double machine learning
[
9
]
: nuisance functions
\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X]
and
\mathbb{E}[T\mid X]
are learned with cross-fitted flexible models, and a lower-dimensional second-stage regressor (often a kernel-weighted local-linear smoother) recovers
\theta(t)
from the orthogonalized residuals.

The received wisdom in industrial and social data is that the generating mechanism is well-described by a mixture
[
23
,
21
]
: a stable
core
structural component plus a volatile
tail
of idiosyncratic outliers — “whales” in marketing, adverse drug events in medicine, flash crashes in finance. For the
core
estimand — the structural dose-response curve that an intervention policy should follow — these tail events are contamination: they do not represent the mechanism one wants to act on, and they exist in the support space where intervention may be costly or unethical. The statistical problem is that the MSE-based smoothers at the heart of DML pipelines have unbounded
functional
influence. A single large residual at the local point
t_{0}
does not only bias
\hat{\theta}(t_{0})
; the kernel window around
t_{0}
smears its effect across the curve, producing a globally-distorted
\hat{\theta}
whose derivative
\hat{\theta}^{\prime}
(marginal effect) is the quantity that downstream policy optimizers actually differentiate. We call this pathology
functional smearing
(Figure
1
).

This paper addresses functional smearing head-on with a robust DML estimator we call

. Four contributions:

1.

0.325
(sinusoidal_region), second only to

0.276

2
Background and Landscape

2.1
ADRF estimation and Double Machine Learning

Let
Y(t)
be the potential outcome under treatment level
t
and assume unconfoundedness
Y(t)\perp\!\!\!\perp T\mid X
and positivity
p(T=t\mid X)>0
on a set of interest. The ADRF is
\theta(t)=\mathbb{E}_{X}[\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X,T=t]]
. Direct outcome-modelling estimates
\theta
by fitting
\hat{m}(x,t)=\hat{\mathbb{E}}[Y\mid X=x,T=t]
and averaging over the empirical covariate distribution; this is efficient when
\hat{m}
is well specified but inherits its bias when not.

Double machine learning
[
9
,
40
]
orthogonalizes the target by cross-fitting nuisances
\hat{m}_{Y}(x)=\hat{\mathbb{E}}[Y\mid X=x]
and
\hat{m}_{T}(x)=\hat{\mathbb{E}}[T\mid X=x]
and forming residualized observations
\tilde{Y}_{i}=Y_{i}-\hat{m}_{Y}(X_{i})
,
\tilde{T}_{i}=T_{i}-\hat{m}_{T}(X_{i})
. Under Neyman orthogonality, a second-stage regressor applied to
(\tilde{T}_{i},\tilde{Y}_{i})
is
\sqrt{n}
-consistent for the partially-linear coefficient. For a non-parametric ADRF, the second stage is typically a kernel-weighted local-linear smoother
[
18
]
, which retains Neyman-orthogonality pointwise.

2.2
Functional smearing: why MSE smoothers are the problem

Let
K_{h}(u)=\exp(-u^{2}/(2h^{2}))
be a Gaussian kernel of bandwidth
h
. The pointwise local-linear estimator at
t_{0}
solves

(\hat{\alpha}(t_{0}),\hat{\theta}(t_{0}))=\argmin_{\alpha,\theta}\sum_{i}K_{h}(T_{i}-t_{0})\bigl[\tilde{Y}_{i}-\alpha-\theta(\tilde{T}_{i}-t_{0})\bigr]^{2}.

(1)

The influence of a single observation
(T_{i},Y_{i})
on
(\hat{\alpha},\hat{\theta})
scales linearly in the residual
r_{i}=\tilde{Y}_{i}-\hat{\alpha}-\hat{\theta}(\tilde{T}_{i}-t_{0})
and in the kernel weight
K_{h}
. For
every

t_{0}
within
\approx 3h
of
T_{i}
, observation
i
contributes — a kernel outlier at
T_{i}\approx 0
therefore biases the entire window
\{t_{0}:|T_{i}-t_{0}|\lesssim 3h\}
. The failure mode is global, not pointwise. A sufficient condition for unbounded functional influence is that (
1
) uses any loss whose derivative is unbounded in
r
; MSE, Huber (beyond its threshold), and Winsorizing all fall short of this.

2.3
Redescending M-estimators and GNC

A bounded-influence fix is a
redescending
loss
\rho
such that its derivative
\psi=\rho^{\prime}
satisfies
\psi(r)\to 0
as
|r|\to\infty

[
2
,
1
]
. Tukey’s biweight and the Welsch (Leclerc) loss are standard choices. We use the Welsch parametrization

\rho_{\sigma,\gamma}(r)=1-\exp\!\bigl(-\tfrac{\gamma}{2}(r/\sigma)^{2}\bigr),\qquad\psi_{\sigma,\gamma}(r)=\rho_{\sigma,\gamma}^{\prime}(r)=\tfrac{\gamma}{\sigma^{2}}\cdot r\cdot\exp\!\bigl(-\tfrac{\gamma}{2}(r/\sigma)^{2}\bigr),

(2)

where
\sigma
is the
scale
(inferred from the data, e.g.
\operatorname{MAD}(\tilde{Y})
) and
\gamma>0
is a
fixed sharpness hyperparameter
(default
\gamma=0.2
in our experiments). Some references write the Welsch loss as
1-\exp(-r^{2}/(2\sigma_{\text{eff}}^{2}))
, identifying
\sigma_{\text{eff}}^{2}=\sigma^{2}/\gamma
; we keep
\gamma
and
\sigma
separate to make the GNC schedule (which scales
\sigma
, not
\gamma
) explicit. The
\gamma
-divergence connection of
Fujisawa and Eguchi 2008
corresponds to the choice
\gamma=1/\sigma^{2}
, which we do not adopt — our
\gamma
is fixed across the schedule. These losses are non-convex: local minima can form around clusters of outliers (“whales”), and naive IRLS will land in one if the initial scale
\sigma
is too tight.

Graduated Non-Convexity
[
5
,
34
]
anneals
\sigma
from large to small. With
\sigma\gg\operatorname{MAD}(r)
,
\rho
is nearly quadratic and the global trend is recovered; as
\sigma
shrinks the loss becomes progressively more aggressive at rejecting the tail. GNC has strong empirical success in robust estimation from computer vision to SLAM
[
44
]
and in certified robust point-cloud registration
[
45
]
. We apply it inside each local kernel window.

2.4
Extreme Value Theory for the discarded tail

A robust estimator that produces a weight vector implicitly defines a soft partition of the sample into
core
and
tail
. The tail is not noise to be thrown away — its distribution determines how aggressive the rejection rule should be, and whether the problem is well-posed at all. Extreme Value Theory
[
13
,
3
]
provides a principled vocabulary. A tail of independent excesses above a threshold
u
is asymptotically Generalized Pareto,
G_{\xi,\sigma}(x)=1-(1+\xi x/\sigma)^{-1/\xi}
; block maxima are asymptotically Generalized Extreme Value,
H_{\xi,\mu,\sigma}
. The shape parameter
\xi
classifies the domain:
\xi<0
(Weibull, bounded tail),
\xi=0
(Gumbel, exponential tail),
\xi>0
(Fréchet, heavy-tailed, power-law).
\xi>0
implies finite moments only up to order
1/\xi
;
\xi\geq 1/2
means the second moment does not exist and
any
MSE-based estimator is asymptotically inconsistent for the population mean.

This is the hook into the method: under Fréchet contamination with
\xi\geq 1
the population mean does not exist and any kernel-DML method is asymptotically inconsistent for the ADRF (in our experiments
\xi\approx 0.26<1
, so the mean is still defined); under Weibull contamination, Welsch/Huber dominate because they retain efficiency while rejecting large excursions.
The practitioner therefore needs to know the tail shape
. The Hill index
[
22
]
estimates
\alpha=1/\xi
, the Pareto tail-index parameter; it is most diagnostic when
\xi>0
(Fréchet domain) where
\alpha
has a clean interpretation as the order of the highest finite moment. Under bounded (
\xi<0
) tails the Hill estimator
still produces a number
, but that number is not a moment-existence threshold — it is dominated by a finite-tail-length artifact and saturates at large values (e.g.,
\hat{\alpha}\approx 9
–
10
on our Gaussian-jump DGPs). We use Hill in conjunction with GPD-MLE/PWM (which estimate
\xi
directly) and treat large
\hat{\alpha}
as a Weibull/Gumbel signal rather than a moment-existence claim. We include six estimators total and use their agreement (or disagreement) as a diagnostic signal.

The
causal tail coefficient
of
Gnecco et al. 2021
ties tail behavior back to the design. Their definition: for two random variables
X,Y
with continuous marginals and copula CDFs
F_{X},F_{Y}
,

\Gamma(X\to Y)\;:=\;\lim_{u\uparrow 1}\Pr\bigl(F_{Y}(Y)>u\,\big|\,F_{X}(X)>u\bigr),

(3)

a tail-conditional probability that an exceedance in
X
co-occurs with an exceedance in
Y
.
\Gamma=0.5
is asymptotic tail-independence (no asymmetric coupling);
\Gamma>0.5
signals that an extreme in
X
predicts an extreme in
Y
. The empirical estimator is a count of joint exceedances above the
1-k/n
-th quantile of each marginal:

\hat{\Gamma}_{n}(X\to Y)\;=\;\frac{\#\{i:F_{n,X}(X_{i})>1-k/n,\;F_{n,Y}(Y_{i})>1-k/n\}}{k},

(4)

where
F_{n,\cdot}
is the empirical CDF and
k
is the tail-sample size (we use
k=\lfloor 0.1n\rfloor
).
Gnecco et al. 2021
establish
\sqrt{k}
-asymptotic-normality of
\hat{\Gamma}_{n}
around the population
\Gamma
under regular variation; we report bootstrap CIs (10-fold subsampling) in the EVT diagnostic suite. We use
\hat{\Gamma}(T\to|y_{\text{res}}|)
as the diagnostic: a value near
0.5
signals
T
-independent contamination (uniform), while
\hat{\Gamma}\gtrsim 0.6
flags
T
-dependent contamination where the tail and the treatment co-occur. In the latter case the tail may be part of the structural response, and the robust estimator’s rejection rule is taking a stance the analyst should be aware of.

Related Work

Continuous-treatment DML.

The DML framework
[
9
]
was originally formulated for a low-dimensional causal parameter;
Semenova and Chernozhukov 2021
and
Colangelo and Lee 2020
extend it to heterogeneous and continuous effects using Neyman-orthogonal moment conditions combined with local polynomial regression. The
econml

[
33
]
and
causalml

[
7
]
libraries provide mature implementations. None of these libraries, to our knowledge, wrap the second-stage smoother in a redescending M-estimator or expose per-sample outlier weights as a first-class artifact.

Robust regression.

The robust-statistics canon
[
23
,
21
,
32
]
distinguishes (i) M-estimators with convex bounded-influence functions (Huber), (ii) L-estimators (quantile / median), and (iii) redescending M-estimators (Tukey biweight, Welsch). The distinction matters in our setting: sklearn’s
HuberRegressor

[
37
]
produces a single bounded residual weight per sample but does not re-weight by
1/(1+r^{2})
-type decay, so its influence is bounded but does not vanish.
QuantileRegressor
has maximum linear breakdown
[
39
]
and is the L1 choice in our benchmark.
\gamma
-divergence minimization
[
19
,
26
]
motivates the Welsch loss and has density-ratio and Bayesian-estimator ties, but we use it only as a pointwise loss.

Graduated non-convexity in estimation.

GNC is an old idea
[
5
]
that has recently returned in computer-vision registration
[
44
,
45
,
30
]
, SLAM, and pose estimation with redescending losses. Our GNC application is a simple IRLS ladder over
\sigma
, inside each kernel window; it does not use the recent theoretical variants (e.g., convex envelope tracking, certified recovery conditions), because the per-window problem is small and well-conditioned once the annealing schedule reaches the target scale.

Robust causal inference.

The literature on causal inference under contamination is comparatively small but growing.
Nie and Wager 2021
note the need for robust pseudo-outcome regression in the X-Learner but do not empirically evaluate.
Kennedy 2023
propose debiased doubly-robust CATE estimation under minimax rates but focus on asymptotic guarantees; the form of the second-stage regressor is left flexible.
Dorn et al. 2024
study heavy-tailed outcomes in IV settings. Three recent 2024 contributions are particularly relevant:

Wager 2024
gives a general account of causal inference under heavy-tailed responses and argues that the choice of second-stage loss should be tied to the tail index;

Zhou and Song 2024
propose a doubly-robust estimator under heavy tails that relies on truncation of influence functions, orthogonal in spirit to our Welsch-loss approach;

Kallus et al. 2024
study robust heterogeneous treatment effects under an adversarial
\epsilon
-contamination of the outcome and give minimax rates.

Dukes et al. 2024
specifically address the continuous-exposure case and propose doubly-robust inference strategies — complementary to our smoother-level robustness.

Jin et al. 2023
frame the problem as Wasserstein distributional robustness.
Finally,
Chernozhukov et al. 2024
introduce “Riesz regression” as an automatic-debiasing tool compatible with flexible second-stage estimators; combining Riesz regression with
SHIFT
’s redescending second stage is a natural follow-up.

Our contribution is a complementary empirical head-to-head that pits convex-robust (Huber, Quantile) against non-convex-robust (Welsch+GNC) second stages on a controlled contamination sweep, with a controlled architectural ablation (pre-fit vs post-GNC MAD) that the theoretical papers above do not diagnose.

Continuous-treatment ADRF estimators we did not benchmark.

Three recent estimator families are particularly relevant but were not included as full baselines: (i)
Highly-Adaptive-Lasso plug-in dose-response

[
42
]
uses HAL with undersmoothing to obtain valid pointwise inference; (ii)
higher-order influence functions / DR ADRF

[
6
,
27
]
propose efficient doubly-robust estimators with provable rates; (iii)
entropy balancing for continuous exposures

[
41
]
extends Hainmueller-style balancing to a continuous treatment by matching empirical moments of
T\cdot X
. We implement a simplified Bonvini-Kennedy-style DR baseline in §

, which behaves competitively on clean data (
\approx 0.10
RMSE) but degrades sharply under contamination because the DR pseudo-outcome amplifies outliers. A robustified DR variant — e.g., HOIF with influence-function truncation, or HAL combined with a Welsch second stage — is a natural extension we identify as future work.

Representation-learning approaches.

Nie et al. 2021
(VCNet) and follow-up work on continuous-treatment heterogeneous response surfaces (CRNet and variants) target conditional dose-response; our focus is the marginal ADRF and its associated diagnostic suite. The robust second-stage losses studied here could in principle be applied on top of a learned
T
-conditional representation, but the empirical comparison would require harmonizing different estimands.

Robust local-polynomial regression.

The closest classical relatives of
SHIFT
are robust local-polynomial regression methods that pre-date the GNC literature: robust LOESS
[
11
]
with bisquare reweighting iterated to convergence, MM-LPR
[
46
]
with Tukey biweight initialization-then-refit, and trimmed local-linear regression. We benchmark all three in App.
H.1
;
SHIFT
, MM-LPR-Tukey, and Robust-LOESS are within

SHIFT
retains stable behavior on the localized-contamination DGP (where MM-LPR-Tukey diverges to RMSE

). The post-GNC MAD architectural fix differentiates

from these classical methods empirically; theoretically, it amounts to a principled scale estimation step that the LOESS / MM literature handled less systematically.

The robust-causal landscape, organized by paradigm.

§
6.10
situates this work against five worldviews on what to do about heavy outcome tails.

(i) Robust M-estimation
(median-of-means, Catoni, Huber-DML, Robust X-Learner) keeps the ATE estimand and replaces the empirical mean inside the estimator with a concentration-friendly alternative; sub-Gaussian CIs under finite variance.

(ii) Weight stabilization
(overlap weights, trimming, SNIPS, CBPS, entropy balancing) attacks the propensity-side tail by capping/shrinking weights or redefining the target population; recovers finite IPW variance at the cost of changing the estimand.

(iii) Functional replacement
(QTE, distributional TE, CVaR-CATE) changes the estimand from mean-ATE to a tail-sensitive functional — the tail
is
the answer.

(iv) Distribution-free / worst-case
(conformal ITE, KL-DRO, Wasserstein-DRPL) gives up point estimation in exchange for coverage guarantees that survive heavy tails without a tail model.

(v) Partial identification
(Manski, Yadlowsky, Dorn-Guo, Rosenbaum sensitivity) concedes that the target is not point-identified and reports sharp bounds.

SHIFT
belongs to (i): keep the ADRF target, robustify the estimator. The post-GNC MAD architectural fix is what makes this practical at finite
n
under localized contamination. Section
6.10
runs representatives from (i)–(v) on the same DGPs and reports the qualitative differences predicted by the paradigm framing.

X-Learner.

The X-Learner
[
29
]
is a two-stage CATE estimator for binary treatments: (i) fit outcome models on each arm, (ii) regress counterfactual pseudo-outcomes on covariates, (iii) combine using a propensity-weighted average. Each of the three stages involves regression on potentially contaminated outcomes. We adapt the second stage to use Huber loss via scikit-learn’s
HuberRegressor
; Section
4.5
and Appendix
C.11
show this gives a large empirical robustness margin over vanilla X-Learner.

Time-series causal inference.

Block cross-validation
[
4
,
38
]
and buffered splits mitigate leakage in autoregressive data. We follow that convention — a
buffer
of
b
timestamps separates each fold’s train/test boundary — and add a rolling-MAD scale to the GNC annealing schedule so that
\sigma_{t}
adapts to local variance. This is closest in spirit to
Dettling and Bühlmann 2003
, who adapt boosting to time series; we adapt GNC.

Extreme Value diagnostics for residuals.

Applying EVT to regression residuals is standard in hydrology
[
13
]
and actuarial science. The contribution here is pairing an EVT suite with a robust causal estimator so that the two systems inform each other: the estimator produces the rank-ordering of the tail; the EVT suite tells the analyst whether that rank ordering is trustworthy (heavy tails imply rank-indistinguishability at small excursions).

4
Method: ADRF-Robust-DML

The estimator decomposes into three stages: (1) cross-fit nuisance orthogonalization, (2) pointwise kernel-weighted GNC with a redescending Welsch loss, and (3) a defensive refit whose inlier cutoff is rescaled using post-GNC residuals. The global ADRF is assembled by integrating the pointwise slopes and anchoring the integration constant against the local intercepts. We describe each stage, then list the three variants used in our benchmark (
GNC-Fixed
,
SHIFT
, and the global-MAD control that our ablation finds no support for).

— Gaussian kernel.

4.1
Stage 1 — Nuisance orthogonalization

Given

(5)

Cross-fitting guarantees

[

. For continuous treatments this is the local-linear analogue; for binary treatments we use the X-Learner decomposition (Section

4.2
Stage 2 — Kernel-weighted GNC

For each target
t_{0}
in a grid
\mathcal{T}_{\text{grid}}
of size 40, define kernel weights

4.3
Stage 3 — Defensive refit (the principal design choice)

The GNC solution is robust but inefficient at the Gaussian. We restore efficiency with a defensive kernel-weighted OLS on the
inliers
, defined by a
3\sigma
cutoff on the residuals using a cutoff scale
\sigma_{\text{cut}}
. The choice of
\sigma_{\text{cut}}
is where the two main variants diverge:

GNC-Fixed
.

\sigma_{\text{cut}}=\sigma_{\text{anchor}}=\operatorname{MAD}(\tilde{Y})
. The anchor is computed once, before the GNC fit; it is
pre-fit
.

SHIFT
(recommended).

\sigma_{\text{cut}}=\operatorname{MAD}(\{r^{\star}_{i}\}_{i\in S(t_{0})})
, the MAD of the
post-GNC residuals
inside the current kernel window.

The difference matters exactly when contamination is spatially localized. When
\geq 50\%
of
S(t_{0})
are outliers,
\operatorname{MAD}(\tilde{Y})
is inflated by them (MAD’s breakdown is
50\%
); the
3\sigma
cutoff is then too wide and the defensive refit reinstates the outliers. GNC-pushed post-GNC residuals have the inverse property: outliers have large
|r^{\star}|
, inliers have small
|r^{\star}|
, and
\operatorname{MAD}(r^{\star})
is controlled by the inlier mass. Section
6.4
shows empirically that on the
sinusoidal_region
DGP at
p=0.25
the
GNC-Fixed
refit admits the outlier mass back, producing RMSE
=1.03
; swapping in the post-GNC MAD produces RMSE
=0.33
with every other line unchanged.

We also test an alternative hypothesis and find no support for it. A natural guess is that the fix is
MAD scope
— that
SHIFT
wins because it uses local-window MAD while
GNC-Fixed
uses the global MAD. We test this separately: swapping
GNC-Fixed
’s anchor from global to local-window
\operatorname{MAD}
without touching the pre-fit vs post-fit distinction moves RMSE from 1.15 to 1.06, still an order of magnitude from
SHIFT
’s 0.33. The fix is
timing
(pre- vs post-GNC), not scope.

4.4
Global ADRF assembly

Boundary handling.

Local-linear smoothers have well-known boundary
bias on the left- and right-most
h
-width of the support, where the kernel
window is one-sided rather than two-sided. We mitigate this in two standard
ways: (i) the evaluation grid
\mathcal{T}_{\text{grid}}
is restricted to the

5
th–
95
th percentile of
T
, leaving a
\sim 5\%
buffer at each end of
the support; (ii) at boundary grid points where fewer than 8 samples enter
the kernel window,
\hat{\theta}(t_{0})
is marked NaN and linearly interpolated
from neighboring grid points before integration. Boundary-bias-corrected
local-linear estimators (e.g., the Cheng-Fan-Marron 2001 boundary kernels)
would replace step (ii) with a principled correction; we do not implement
this, leaving boundary inference as future work.

Integration and anchor.

The pointwise slopes
\hat{\theta}(t)
for
t\in\mathcal{T}_{\text{grid}}
are integrated via trapezoidal quadrature to give an unidentified-constant ADRF shape
\hat{S}(t)=\int_{t_{\min}}^{t}\hat{\theta}(u)\,du
. We anchor the integration constant by aligning the mean of
\hat{S}
to the mean of the local intercepts
\hat{\alpha}(t)
:

\hat{g}(t)=\hat{S}(t)-\bar{\hat{S}}+\bar{\hat{\alpha}}.

(7)

Both
\hat{S}
and
\hat{\alpha}
are averaged over the grid, giving a stable anchor. Grid points with undefined
\hat{\theta}
(sparsity) are linearly interpolated before integration. The “non-uniform-weight” output is the matrix
W^{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{T}_{\text{grid}}|\times n}
of final robust weights; its row-average
\bar{w}^{r}_{i}
is our per-sample outlier score.

4.5
Binary-treatment extension: Robust X-Learner

For
T\in\{0,1\}
, we inherit the X-Learner decomposition
[
29
]
. Let
\hat{m}_{0},\hat{m}_{1}
be cross-fitted outcome models on the control and treated subsamples, and
\hat{e}(X)=\hat{\Pr}(T=1\mid X)
the propensity. Define pseudo-outcomes

\displaystyle D_{0}

\displaystyle=\hat{m}_{1}(X)-Y,\quad\text{(on control subsample)}

(8)

\displaystyle D_{1}

\displaystyle=Y-\hat{m}_{0}(X).\quad\text{(on treated subsample)}

(9)

The X-Learner then regresses
D_{0}
on
X
(call this
\hat{\tau}_{0}
) and

on
X
(call this
\hat{\tau}_{1}
), and returns
\hat{\tau}(x)=\hat{e}(x)\hat{\tau}_{0}(x)+(1-\hat{e}(x))\hat{\tau}_{1}(x)
. We replace both
\hat{\tau}_{0},\hat{\tau}_{1}
with Huber-loss regressors (scikit-learn
HuberRegressor
with default
\epsilon=1.35
, bounded-influence). This surface change bounds the per-observation influence on
\hat{\tau}_{k}
at the second stage — exactly where contamination bites hardest, because the pseudo-outcomes
D_{0},D_{1}
are
amplified
by the outcome-model error on contaminated folds. Results at Appendix
C.11
show a
5\times
CATE RMSE reduction at
10\%
contamination.

4.6
Multi-treatment extension

For
\vec{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

4.7
Time-series extension

For serially correlated data with possibly non-stationary variance, we replace Silverman’s rule with a block time split of

4.8
Rate inheritance under Neyman orthogonality

This subsection shows that the pointwise convergence rate of the convex-M
kernel-DML estimator
inherits
to
SHIFT
under standard Neyman-orthogonality
and local-linear regularity conditions. It is
not
a self-contained
proof; it is an informal composition of three standard results that identifies
the assumptions under which the empirical §
6
rates match
theory. A fully rigorous treatment requires the certified-recovery machinery
of
Yang et al. 2020

Yang et al. 2021
adapted to the DML setting, which is

Setup.

Let the observational data
\mathcal{D}_{n}=\{(X_{i},T_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
be i.i.d. from
a mixture distribution

(X,T,Y)\sim(1-p)\,P_{\text{core}}+p\,P_{\text{out}},

(10)

where
P_{\text{core}}
is the uncontaminated joint and
P_{\text{out}}

contaminates
Y
only (so
(X,T)\sim P_{\text{core}}
is preserved).
Under
P_{\text{core}}
,
Y=m_{Y}(X)+\theta(T)-\theta_{0}+\epsilon
with

\mathbb{E}[\epsilon\mid X,T]=0
, where

is the
unidentified integration constant. Define the partial residual

r_{i}(\alpha,\theta)\;:=\;\tilde{Y}_{i}\;-\;\alpha\;-\;\theta\cdot(\tilde{T}_{i}-t_{0}),\qquad\tilde{Y}_{i}=Y_{i}-\hat{m}_{Y}(X_{i}),\;\tilde{T}_{i}=T_{i}-\hat{m}_{T}(X_{i}),

(11)

where
(\alpha,\theta)
are the local-linear intercept and slope. The kernel-DML

\alpha
and
\theta

\displaystyle g_{\alpha}(t_{0};\eta,\alpha,\theta)(W_{i})

\displaystyle\;=\;K_{h}(\tilde{T}_{i}-t_{0})\,\psi_{\sigma_{\text{eff}},\gamma}\!\bigl(r_{i}(\alpha,\theta)\bigr),

(12)

\displaystyle g_{\theta}(t_{0};\eta,\alpha,\theta)(W_{i})

\displaystyle\;=\;(\tilde{T}_{i}-t_{0})\,K_{h}(\tilde{T}_{i}-t_{0})\,\psi_{\sigma_{\text{eff}},\gamma}\!\bigl(r_{i}(\alpha,\theta)\bigr),

(13)

where
W_{i}=(X_{i},T_{i},Y_{i})
, the nuisance is
\eta=(m_{Y},m_{T},\sigma_{\text{eff}})

[
9

a known function of the score, leaving the

3.

GNC contractivity.

Local IRLS contractivity at fixed scale.

Proposition 1

(Local IRLS contractivity, finite sample)
.

Fix the kernel window
S(t_{0})
with
|S(t_{0})|=N
, the effective scale

\sigma>0
, and the sharpness
\gamma>0
. Let
\bm{\theta}=(\alpha,\theta)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
X_{i}=(1,\tilde{T}_{i}-t_{0})^{\top}
, design matrix

\mathbf{X}=(X_{i})_{i\in S(t_{0})}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 2}
, and weight matrix

\mathbf{W}(\bm{\theta})=\mathrm{diag}\bigl(K_{h}(\tilde{T}_{i}-t_{0})\cdot w^{r}_{i}(\bm{\theta})\bigr)\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
with
w^{r}_{i}
from
(
6
). The IRLS map is

\mathcal{T}(\bm{\theta})\;=\;\bigl(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{W}(\bm{\theta})\mathbf{X}\bigr)^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{W}(\bm{\theta})\,\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}.

(15)

Assume:

(B1)

\mathcal{T}
has a fixed point
\bm{\theta}^{\star}\in\mathcal{N}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
where
\mathcal{N}
is an open ball.

(B2)

\mathbf{M}^{\star}:=\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{W}(\bm{\theta}^{\star})\mathbf{X}
is non-singular with smallest eigenvalue
\lambda_{\min}^{\star}>0
(well-conditioned local Hessian).

(B3)

w^{r}_{i}
is
C^{1}
on
\mathcal{N}
with
\partial_{r}w^{r}_{i}(\bm{\theta})=-(\gamma/\sigma^{2})\,r_{i}(\bm{\theta})\cdot w^{r}_{i}(\bm{\theta})
.

Then for
\bm{\theta}\in\mathcal{N}
in a neighborhood of
\bm{\theta}^{\star}
,

\|\mathcal{T}(\bm{\theta})-\bm{\theta}^{\star}\|_{2}\;\leq\;\rho_{N}(\sigma)\cdot\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{\star}\|_{2}\;+\;o(\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{\star}\|_{2}),

(16)

with the (sample) Lipschitz constant

\rho_{N}(\sigma)\;\leq\;\frac{\gamma}{\sigma^{2}}\cdot\kappa(\mathbf{M}^{\star})\cdot\frac{\sum_{i\in S(t_{0})}K_{h}(\tilde{T}_{i}-t_{0})\,w^{r}_{i}(\bm{\theta}^{\star})\,r_{i}(\bm{\theta}^{\star})^{2}\,\|X_{i}\|_{2}^{2}}{\sum_{i\in S(t_{0})}K_{h}(\tilde{T}_{i}-t_{0})\,w^{r}_{i}(\bm{\theta}^{\star})\,\|X_{i}\|_{2}^{2}},

(17)

where
\kappa(\mathbf{M}^{\star})=\lambda_{\max}^{\star}/\lambda_{\min}^{\star}

is the condition number of
\mathbf{M}^{\star}
.

In the population limit (under (A1) and provided the kernel-window
sub-population satisfies a uniform convergence condition), the sample
ratio in (
17
) converges to the conditional
expectation
\mathbb{E}_{P_{\text{core}}}[r^{2}(\bm{\theta}^{\star})w^{r}(\bm{\theta}^{\star})\mid T=t_{0}]/\mathbb{E}_{P_{\text{core}}}[w^{r}(\bm{\theta}^{\star})\mid T=t_{0}]

and
\kappa(\mathbf{M}^{\star})\to\kappa^{\star}_{\infty}
, a fixed constant
depending on the local design.

In particular,
\rho_{N}(\sigma)\to 0
as
\sigma\to\infty
(loss
becomes quadratic) and
\rho_{N}(\sigma)\leq 1
when the empirical inlier
moment ratio in the numerator of (
17
) is bounded
by
\sigma^{2}/(\gamma\cdot\kappa(\mathbf{M}^{\star}))
.

Proof.

Linearize
\mathcal{T}
at
\bm{\theta}^{\star}
. Write

\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{W}(\bm{\theta})
,
\mathbf{W}^{\star}=\mathbf{W}(\bm{\theta}^{\star})
,

\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{X}
,
\mathbf{M}^{\star}=\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{W}^{\star}\mathbf{X}
. Then

\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{T}(\bm{\theta}^{\star})\;=\;\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\!\left[(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{W}\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}\right]\bigg|_{\bm{\theta}^{\star}}.

By the product rule,

\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{T}(\bm{\theta}^{\star})=(\mathbf{M}^{\star})^{-1}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}[\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{W}\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}]-(\mathbf{M}^{\star})^{-1}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}[\mathbf{M}]\cdot\bm{\theta}^{\star}
.
At the stationary point,
\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{W}^{\star}(\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}-\mathbf{X}\bm{\theta}^{\star})=\mathbf{0}
(the IRLS first-order condition),
so the second-difference structure simplifies. By assumption (B3),

\partial_{\bm{\theta}}W_{ii}(\bm{\theta}^{\star})=\partial_{r}w^{r}_{i}\cdot(-X_{i}^{\top})=(\gamma/\sigma^{2})r_{i}(\bm{\theta}^{\star})w^{r}_{i}(\bm{\theta}^{\star})X_{i}^{\top}
.
Substituting:

\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{T}(\bm{\theta}^{\star})=-\frac{\gamma}{\sigma^{2}}(\mathbf{M}^{\star})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathrm{diag}\!\left(K_{h}(\tilde{T}_{i}-t_{0})\,r_{i}(\bm{\theta}^{\star})\,w^{r}_{i}(\bm{\theta}^{\star})X_{i}^{\top}\right)\mathbf{X}.

Bounding the operator-2 norm of this Jacobian:
\|\nabla\mathcal{T}(\bm{\theta}^{\star})\|_{2}\leq(\gamma/\sigma^{2})\cdot\|(\mathbf{M}^{\star})^{-1}\|_{2}\cdot\|\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathrm{diag}(\cdots)\mathbf{X}\|_{2}
.
The first factor is
1/\lambda_{\min}^{\star}
. For the second, using the bound

\|\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathrm{diag}(a_{i})\mathbf{X}\|_{2}\leq\max_{i}|a_{i}|\cdot\|\mathbf{X}\|_{2}^{2}=\max_{i}|a_{i}|\cdot\lambda_{\max}^{\star}

is too loose; instead, the trace bound gives

\|\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathrm{diag}(K_{h}rw^{r}X^{\top})\mathbf{X}\|_{2}\leq\sum_{i}K_{h}\cdot|r_{i}|\cdot w^{r}_{i}\cdot\|X_{i}\|_{2}^{2}
in operator
norm, which combined with Cauchy-Schwarz on
|r_{i}|
gives the ratio in
(
17
). Identifying
\kappa(\mathbf{M}^{\star})=\lambda_{\max}^{\star}/\lambda_{\min}^{\star}
collects the conditioning
factor. The
o(\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{\star}\|)
remainder collects the
second-order Taylor term, which is
O(\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{\star}\|^{2})

under (B3).
∎

Population-limit interpretation.

Under (A1) and the standard kernel-smoothing assumptions (bandwidth

h\to 0
,
nh\to\infty
, design density
f_{T}
continuous at
t_{0}
),
the sample ratio in (
17
) converges to

\mathcal{R}(\sigma):=\mathbb{E}[r^{2}(\bm{\theta}^{\star})w^{r}(\bm{\theta}^{\star})\,\|X\|^{2}\mid T=t_{0}]/\mathbb{E}[w^{r}(\bm{\theta}^{\star})\,\|X\|^{2}\mid T=t_{0}]
, and the
contractivity condition becomes

\mathcal{R}(\sigma)\;\leq\;\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\gamma\cdot\kappa^{\star}_{\infty}}.

(18)

This is a clean condition on the kernel-conditional second moment of
the inlier residual relative to the schedule’s effective scale. The
Jacobian-norm-vs-condition-number argument means an ill-conditioned
local design (e.g., highly collinear
\tilde{T}_{i}-t_{0}
values, or a
kernel window that nearly degenerates) reduces the contractive margin;
this connects directly to the (A5) violation regime, where windows shrink
to the inlier minority and conditioning degrades.

Discussion of the bound.

\rho(\sigma)\leq 1
is the contraction condition. Two regimes:

•

Inlier-dominated window
(A5 holds):
\mathbb{E}[r^{2}w^{r}|T=t_{0}]\approx\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}[w^{r}|T=t_{0}]
with
\sigma_{\epsilon}\ll\sigma
and
w^{r}
near 1 on inliers. The bound is
\rho\lesssim\gamma\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}/\sigma^{2}
, which is small for the schedule’s intermediate
\sigma
values and approaches
\gamma
at
\sigma=\sigma_{\text{anchor}}\approx\sigma_{\epsilon}
— still
\leq 1
since our
\gamma=0.2<1
.

•

Outlier-dominated window
(A5 fails):
w^{r}\approx 0
on the outliers but the conditional second moment
\mathbb{E}[r^{2}w^{r}]
is large because surviving inliers have small
w^{r}
relative to the kernel. The bound can exceed
1
, and IRLS may not converge to the inlier solution from an arbitrary start. This is exactly the regime documented in Table
1
.

The empirical contraction-ratio measurements in Appendix
G.1

(
\rho\leq 0.5
at every schedule step on every DGP, including

sinusoidal_region
) are consistent with the bound on the inlier-dominated
side and indicate that the GNC schedule’s gradual annealing keeps the IRLS
trajectory close to the contractive regime even on the cells where (A5) fails
in realization — because the previous schedule step’s solution provides a
warm start in the contractive basin.

Selection-induced bias of the defensive refit.

Reviewers correctly noted that the defensive OLS refit operates on a
data-dependent inlier set
\hat{\mathcal{I}}(t_{0}):=\{i\in S(t_{0}):|r^{\star}_{i}|/\operatorname{MAD}(r^{\star})\leq 3\}
, where
r^{\star}_{i}
is the post-GNC
residual. Standard kernel-DML rate arguments (
Chernozhukov et al. 2018
,
Fan and Gijbels 1996
)
assume the second-stage moment is differentiable in the nuisance and does
not depend on the data through hidden selection. Selection invalidates this
in general; we make the conditions for it to be benign in our setting
explicit.

Let
\mathcal{I}^{\star}(t_{0}):=\{i\in S(t_{0}):(X_{i},T_{i},Y_{i})\in\mathrm{supp}(P_{\text{core}})\}
be the (latent) true inlier set, and

\hat{\theta}^{\mathrm{ref}}(t_{0})
the kernel-weighted OLS slope on

\hat{\mathcal{I}}(t_{0})
. Define
\hat{\theta}^{\mathrm{or}}(t_{0})
as the
oracle estimator: kernel-OLS on
\mathcal{I}^{\star}(t_{0})
.

Proposition 2

(Selection consistency
\Rightarrow
refit
oracle-equivalence)
.

Suppose (B1)–(B3), (A1)–(A4), and additionally:

(C1)

(Selection consistency)
The symmetric difference
of the estimated and true inlier sets vanishes:

|\hat{\mathcal{I}}(t_{0})\,\triangle\,\mathcal{I}^{\star}(t_{0})|\,/\,|S(t_{0})|=o_{p}(1).

(C2)

(Inlier-conditional regularity)
The conditional
distribution of
Y
given
\mathcal{I}^{\star}(t_{0})
matches the core
population
P_{\text{core}}|_{T=t_{0}}
, with finite second moment.

Then
\hat{\theta}^{\mathrm{ref}}(t_{0})-\hat{\theta}^{\mathrm{or}}(t_{0})=o_{p}(n^{-2/5})
, so the defensive refit inherits the oracle’s pointwise

O_{p}(n^{-2/5})
rate (
14
) and its asymptotic
variance.

Proof sketch.

The kernel-OLS slope is
\hat{\theta}(t_{0})=(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{K}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{K}\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}
for diagonal

\mathbf{K}
of kernel weights, restricted to a sample subset. By (C1) the
sample subset
\hat{\mathcal{I}}(t_{0})
differs from
\mathcal{I}^{\star}(t_{0})

by
o_{p}(1)
fraction of samples; by (C2), the contribution of any single
sample to the slope is
O_{p}(1/(nh))
. The total selection-induced
perturbation is therefore
|\hat{\mathcal{I}}(t_{0})\triangle\mathcal{I}^{\star}(t_{0})|\cdot O_{p}(1/(nh))=o_{p}(1)\cdot O_{p}((nh)^{-1})
, which is

o_{p}(n^{-2/5})
at the MSE-optimal
h\sim n^{-1/5}
.
∎

When (C1) holds.

Selection consistency requires the post-GNC inlier set to recover the
true inliers asymptotically. This is implied by:

•

Separation
between
P_{\text{core}}
and
P_{\text{out}}
residual distributions: their supports overlap in a
o(1)
-mass region. On Gaussian-jump DGPs (mean
\pm 12
vs noise
\sigma\approx 0.5
), separation is essentially complete and (C1) holds with the empirical false-rate at the
3\sigma
cutoff being
<1\%
.

•

(A5) holds
so that
\operatorname{MAD}(r^{\star})
is dominated by the inlier mass and the cutoff
3\cdot\operatorname{MAD}(r^{\star})
correctly classifies a
1-o(1)
fraction.

On heavy-tail DGPs (
sinusoidal_heavytail
), the residual
distributions overlap (a small
t_{3}
jump is rank-indistinguishable from
clean noise), and (C1) only holds in a probabilistic sense: the

symmetric-difference fraction
is bounded by the rank-overlap
probability, which we have shown is
\approx 0.30
at
p=0.25

(complement of detection-AUCPR
\approx 0.70
, App.

The refit then inherits an
O_{p}(0.3)
-fraction selection bias, which
explains why

does not match
Quantile-DML
on heavy-tail DGPs even though
the rate is the same: the constant in

When (C1) fails strongly (
sinusoidal_region
A5-violating

\hat{\mathcal{I}}(t_{0})
may include outliers

5.3
Metrics

Level-metrics are computed after centering both
\hat{\theta}
and
\theta
by their grid-means (the integration constant is not identified):

\displaystyle\textsc{RMSE}_{\text{level}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_{\text{grid}}|}\sum_{t}(\hat{\theta}_{c}(t)-\theta_{c}(t))^{2}},\quad\hat{\theta}_{c}(t)=\hat{\theta}(t)-\bar{\hat{\theta}},

\displaystyle\text{MAE}_{\text{level}}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_{\text{grid}}|}\sum_{t}|\hat{\theta}_{c}(t)-\theta_{c}(t)|,

\displaystyle\text{SupErr}_{\text{level}}

\displaystyle=\max_{t}|\hat{\theta}_{c}(t)-\theta_{c}(t)|.

The derivative metric is MASE-normalized:

\textsc{MASE}_{\text{deriv}}=\frac{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_{\text{grid}}|-1}\sum_{t}|\hat{\theta}^{\prime}(t)-\theta^{\prime}(t)|}{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_{\text{grid}}|-1}\sum_{t}|\theta^{\prime}(t)-\theta^{\prime}(t-1)|}.

(19)

Outlier detection is evaluated at matched predicted-positive rate: label the lowest-weight
k=\lfloor pn\rfloor
samples as predicted-outlier, compute precision, recall, and
\text{F}_{1}
against the ground-truth contamination mask. Methods with uniform sample weights (Huber, Quantile, Standard, Naive) thus receive
\text{F}_{1}=p
(the base rate) as a floor; only non-uniform-weight methods (Winsor, GNC-Fixed,
SHIFT
) can exceed this floor.

w_{i}=\min(1,\epsilon\sigma/|r_{i}|)
as part of its inner loop, and these weights are bounded but generally not uniform. Two observations that justify our reported convention. First, sklearn’s
HuberRegressor
does not expose the final weights through a documented attribute; using them would require a custom wrapper. Second, and more substantively, those weights are computed
per kernel window
, so a sample that lies in the kernel windows around 10 different grid points receives 10 different weights. Aggregating them into a single per-sample score for ranking requires a choice of rule (mean, max, kernel-weighted-mean), and the resulting ranking depends on that choice.
SHIFT
’s grid-averaged
\bar{w}^{r}_{i}
(§
4
) is the natural single-number outlier score the architecture produces, with the kernel-mass weighting baked in via the GNC-IRLS structure. We have implemented and informally tested a kernel-mass-weighted aggregation of
Huber-DML
’s per-window weights; on Gaussian-jump DGPs it produces an outlier ranking with
\text{F}_{1}\approx 0.93
at
p=0.25
— below
SHIFT
’s
\approx 0.96
but well above the base rate. We did not include this as a main-table comparison because (i) it is not part of any stock library and (ii) it would obscure the cleaner architectural narrative. Practitioners who need an outlier mask alongside
Huber-DML
can implement the aggregation in a few lines.

7
Discussion and Limitations

7.1
What the evidence supports

1.

Competitive — but not leading — shape recovery.
At
p=0.25
,
Huber-DML
is the shape-recovery leader on worst-case RMSE (0.276) and on the mean across DGPs (0.202);
SHIFT
is second on both (0.325, 0.224). The legitimate framing is that
SHIFT

combines
competitive shape recovery (within 0.05 RMSE of
Huber-DML
at 19 of 20 main-sweep cells; the single exception is
sinusoidal_region

p=0.15
at
+0.085
) with a non-uniform weight output; none of the uniform-weight methods like
Huber-DML
and
Quantile-DML
can produce the latter.

2.

A single architectural fix closes an 11
\times
gap.
The pre-fit vs post-GNC MAD distinction is a small code change with a large empirical impact in exactly one regime (localized contamination) and no cost in others.

3.

Combined shape + outlier-mask output.

Winsor-DML
is an equally-strong or better outlier detector on Gaussian-jump DGPs, but it loses shape recovery because winsorizing a kernel-local jump mass shifts the local OLS slope.
Huber-DML
and
Quantile-DML
are strong shape estimators but cannot rank outliers at all. Only
SHIFT
is simultaneously competitive on both axes.

4.

EVT is the principal tool for choosing between robust losses.
Three shape estimators (GPD MLE, GPD PWM, GEV) all flip sign between contamination laws; Hill
\hat{\alpha}
recovers
\nu
to within sampling bias. This gives an analyst grounds to switch between
SHIFT
,
Huber-DML
, and
Quantile-DML
, rather than choosing a robust loss by habit.

7.2
Where
SHIFT
is dominated

Negative results we report:

1.

Heavy-tailed contamination (
sinusoidal_heavytail
).

Quantile-DML
leads on the main DGP: RMSE 0.132 at
p=0.25
vs
SHIFT
’s 0.152. The Student-
t_{\nu}
family sweep (App. B.10) shows that the bounded-influence estimators are essentially tied across
\nu\in\{2,3,5\}
(per-row gaps
\leq 0.003
, within seed noise):
Huber-DML
,
Quantile-DML
, and
SHIFT
all land in
[0.124,0.155]
. The clearest separation is at
\nu=10
(near-Gaussian), where
SHIFT
wins outright (0.122 vs Huber 0.135). The recommendation is to switch to
Quantile-DML
when Hill
\hat{\alpha}\leq 3
or GPD
\hat{\xi}>0
, and to retain
SHIFT
for the per-sample weight output and for
\nu\geq 10
.

2.

Asymmetric contamination.
Every
symmetric
robust loss under-corrects one-sided mean shifts. At
p=0.25
Naive-LL (0.264) narrowly beats
SHIFT
(0.219) on
sinusoidal_asymmetric
: the uncontrolled DML bias happens to cancel in one direction. When
\Gamma(T\to|y_{\text{res}}|)>0.6
,
Huber-DML
is a safer shape choice than
SHIFT
;
SHIFT
is still needed for the detection mask.

3.

Very low contamination (
p=0.05
) on uniform DGPs.

SHIFT
is within
0.002
RMSE of
Quantile-DML
on
parabola
,
sinusoidal
, and
sinusoidal_heavytail
. A reviewer could reasonably pick
Quantile-DML
on efficiency grounds at
p=0.05
; the cost of switching methods across contamination regimes is an operational burden that
SHIFT
avoids by being close enough everywhere.

4.

Detection under heavy tails.
Every robust method’s
\text{F}_{1}
plateaus at
0.68
–
0.71
on
sinusoidal_heavytail
. This is an
identifiability limit
:
t_{3}
draws span the full magnitude range, so small jumps are rank-indistinguishable from clean noise. The EVT suite documents the problem (Hill
\alpha<3
); no rank-based mask can solve it.

5.

95% CI under-coverage on clean kernel-DML.
The percentile bootstrap CI under-covers at
\approx 80\%
on clean data. This is a general problem with percentile-bootstrap CIs on non-parametric regressions, not a
SHIFT
bug; we recommend BC
a
or studentized bootstrap (
arch
,
scipy
) for applications that need calibrated coverage.

7.3
Computational cost

A single
SHIFT
fit on
n=800
takes
0.94\pm 0.07
seconds wall-time on a single CPU thread —
\approx 3\times

Standard-DML
(
0.30
s) and
\approx 0.75\times

Quantile-DML
(
1.03
s). The 1,400-fit main sweep finishes in
\approx 18
minutes. Appendix
C.8
gives per-method timings. Every method is embarrassingly parallel across grid points; a full ADRF on
n=10^{5}
fits comfortably on an 8-core laptop in under a minute.

7.4
Threats to external validity

The DGPs are synthetic.

Every stress test here is constructed — we chose the contamination distributions, localized supports, and tail families to expose specific failure modes. A single semi-synthetic IHDP-like benchmark (E15, Appendix
C.12
) is the closest we get to real data, and
SHIFT
wins there too, but it is not a substitute for deployment on industrial pricing or dosing data. We are not in a position to report such results in this paper; we invite practitioners to run
SHIFT
on their data and submit back findings.

Cross-fit nuisance choice.

We use HistGradientBoostingRegressor by default but §
6.7
shows that the choice substantively affects shape recovery: on uniform contamination, linear nuisances (Ridge, Lasso) cut
SHIFT
RMSE by 60% relative to HistGBM, inverting the conventional DML more-flexible-is-better heuristic. A misspecified nuisance model will bias every DML method, but the robust variants are more resistant to
Y
-contamination because the second stage is the only place the robust loss is applied.

Contamination is fixed per-run.

The contamination fraction
p
is a hyperparameter of the DGP, not adapted within a run. In real data
p
is unknown; the intended workflow is to run
SHIFT
, inspect the non-uniform-weight weights and the EVT diagnostics, and iterate. We do not claim
SHIFT
is self-calibrating.

7.5
When not to use
SHIFT

Explicitly: if (i) the data are known to be clean (no contamination) and efficiency is critical, use
Standard-DML
— the clean-data tax is nearly zero but not exactly zero; (ii) the contamination is known to be heavy-tailed (Hill
\alpha<3
), use
Quantile-DML
; (iii) the contamination is known to be one-sided, consider
Huber-DML
or a non-symmetric loss; (iv) the analyst needs calibrated confidence intervals, the percentile bootstrap is not the right CI for any kernel-DML method including
SHIFT
.

7.6
Choosing among the five paradigms

The landscape comparison in §
6.10
concretely illustrates a more general decision tree:

•

If you want a point estimate of the mean ADRF and outliers are a nuisance to suppress:
stay in the robust M-estimation paradigm. Use
SHIFT
when you also need an outlier-detection mask,
Huber-DML
when you do not,
Quantile-DML
when the tail is heavy (
\hat{\alpha}\leq 3
).

•

If your scientific question is about the tail:
switch to the functional-replacement paradigm. QTE / distributional TE / CVaR-CATE answer a different question — the gap between QTE and ADRF is large under contamination (Table
11
,
1.6
on
sinusoidal_region

p=0.15
for QTE
0.25
) precisely
because
the tail is what you asked about.

•

If you need coverage guarantees and the data may be adversarially distributed:
use a distribution-free method (Conformal-ADRF or DRO). The cost is wide, sometimes uninformative, intervals — our Conformal-ADRF achieves
1.0
coverage with
5
–
30
-RMSE-unit half-widths.

•

If unconfoundedness is doubtful:
report partial-identification bounds (Manski / Yadlowsky / Dorn-Guo) alongside the point estimate. Manski bounds in our sweep also achieve
1.0
coverage with similarly wide intervals; the value is the honest worst-case story rather than the band itself.

•

If propensity scores are extreme:
weight stabilization (overlap weights, trimming, CBPS) attacks the right failure mode but redefines the target population. Useful when the propensity tail
is
the source of variance, not the outcome tail.

The empirical observation from §
6.10
that no robust point estimator strictly dominates across all DGPs justifies the framework’s plurality: each paradigm answers a different question, and a practitioner should pick by question, not by RMSE.

8
Conclusion

We introduced
SHIFT
, a robust DML estimator for ADRF on continuous treatments that pairs GNC-optimized redescending Welsch loss with a defensive refit whose inlier cutoff is scaled by the post-GNC residual MAD. The post-GNC MAD choice closes an 11
\times
RMSE gap on localized-contamination stress tests relative to the natural pre-fit-MAD ablation, at no cost on uniform contamination. Across
5
DGPs,
4
contamination levels, and
10
seeds,
SHIFT
has competitive worst-case shape recovery (RMSE 0.325 at
p=0.25
, second only to Huber-DML’s 0.276) and is the sole method among the top-three shape estimators that simultaneously produces a non-uniform outlier-detection mask with mean
\text{F}_{1}\approx 0.96
(range
0.945
–
0.968
) on Gaussian-jump DGPs.

We pair the estimator with a six-technique EVT suite — Hill, GPD (MLE and PWM), GEV block-maxima, Mean Excess Function, parameter stability, return-level CIs, and the Gnecco–Meinshausen causal tail coefficient — which gives an analyst a principled basis for choosing between
SHIFT
and L1-type alternatives (
Quantile-DML
) on their specific data. This pairing is novel: the robust estimator produces the rank ordering of the tail, and the EVT suite tells the analyst whether that rank ordering is trustworthy. We also extend the framework to binary-treatment CATE — the Huber-pseudo-outcome X-Learner delivers a
5
–
7\times
CATE RMSE reduction under
10
–
20\%
contamination on a synthetic benchmark — and to time-series ADRF via block-CV and rolling-MAD; the time-series result is honest but modest, with the three methods compared essentially tied at
p=0
and
p=0.25
and
SHIFT
taking a
\approx 10\%
RMSE lead only at intermediate contamination
p=0.1
. We flag the time-series setting as an area where more work is needed.

We also surface a surprise: linear nuisance models (Ridge, Lasso)
outperform
gradient-boosted nuisances for robust DML under uniform contamination (§
6.7
); this inverts the more-flexible-is-better heuristic and has operational consequences for deployment.

We report negative results: under heavy-tailed contamination the bounded-influence estimators (
Huber-DML
,
Quantile-DML
,
SHIFT
) are essentially tied across
\nu\in\{2,3,5\}
(gaps
\leq 0.003
, within seed noise), with
SHIFT
only winning outright at
\nu=10
;
Huber-DML
leads under asymmetric contamination and on the IHDP-like semi-synthetic. We describe the EVT diagnostic signals that flag these regimes. Detection
\text{F}_{1}
plateaus at
0.68
–
0.71
under
t_{3}
contamination, which we argue is a fundamental identifiability limit rather than an estimator bug.

Future work.

(i)
Certified GNC recovery.
Section
4.8
gives a pointwise-rate argument conditional on IRLS contractivity; a certified-recovery extension of the computer-vision GNC literature
[
44
,
45
]
to our DML setting would close this. (ii)
Adaptive loss selection.
Using the EVT output to pick between Welsch, Huber, and Quantile within a single pipeline, with provable coverage of the meta-selector’s error. (iii)
Real-data deployment.
A controlled study on an industrial ADRF problem (pricing, dosing, or ad-spend response) would turn this evaluation from semi-synthetic to real-world and is the most important next step. (iv)
Honest inference.
Appendix
E.3
shows that BCa alone is not enough to fix the kernel-DML CI under-coverage; undersmoothed-bandwidth plus per-draw studentized bootstrap is the right combination and has not yet been evaluated here.

Reproducibility.

Source code is Python (NumPy, scikit-learn, pandas, matplotlib) with no exotic dependencies, available at
https://github.com/EichiUehara/ADRF-Robust-DML
. The full main sweep plus all sensitivities plus the EVT suite takes
\approx 30
minutes on a single laptop core. Per-cell mean-
\pm
-std statistics for every experiment are in the
DETAILED_RESULTS.md
companion document in the repository. All commands are wrapped in
reproduce.sh
; running it end-to-end regenerates every number and figure in this paper.

Appendix D
Extended discussion

D.1
Why post-GNC MAD works: a partitioned view

The core intuition is a partitioned view of the contaminated sample. Let
\mathcal{I}\subset\{1,\ldots,n\}
be the unknown inlier index set and
\mathcal{O}
the outlier set. Inside a kernel window
S(t_{0})
around a target point
t_{0}
:

•

Pre-fit MAD.

\operatorname{MAD}(\tilde{Y}_{S(t_{0})})
is an estimate of scale on a
mixture
of inliers and outliers. If the outlier mass fraction in
S(t_{0})
is
\geq 50\%
, MAD’s breakdown is exceeded and the estimate is dominated by the outlier spread — which is typically
10\times
larger than the inlier spread. A
3\sigma
cutoff built on this MAD admits the outliers back in.

•

Post-GNC MAD.
After the GNC annealing loop, the estimator
(\hat{\alpha},\hat{\theta})
is the majority trend in
S(t_{0})

as filtered by the redescending loss
. Post-GNC residuals partition cleanly:
|r^{\star}_{i}|
is small for
i\in\mathcal{I}
and large for
i\in\mathcal{O}
.
\operatorname{MAD}(\{r^{\star}_{i}\})
is therefore controlled by the inlier mass, regardless of the outlier mass fraction in
S(t_{0})
.

The asymptotics of this argument are inherited from the GNC literature
[
5
,
44
]
: under standard regularity and an appropriate schedule, GNC converges to the global minimum of the redescending loss with high probability. The Section
6.4
ablation shows the finite-sample behavior matches: the
3\sigma(r^{\star})
cutoff recovers
\approx 95\%
of the true outliers on
sinusoidal_region
at
p=0.25
, versus
\approx 50\%
for the pre-fit MAD cutoff.

D.2
Relationship to other redescending-loss pipelines

The Welsch loss is also known as Leclerc’s formulation
[
31
]
and is used in the ROBOSLAM / TEASER certified-registration literature
[
45
,
44
]
. Our usage is comparatively simple: a one-dimensional (slope) problem per kernel window, with a short IRLS ladder that converges in
\sim 3
–
7
iterations per schedule step. We do not use the certified-recovery machinery (convex envelope tracking, inlier-set convergence proofs); the per-window problem is small enough that empirical convergence is uniform across our experiments.

D.3
On the choice of breakdown point

An alternative to the Welsch loss is a truly high-breakdown M-estimator like Tukey’s biweight (same shape as Welsch, compact support) or the MM-estimator
[
46
]
. We chose Welsch for its smoothness (the density-power
\gamma
-divergence basis) and for the computational reason that its IRLS iteration is numerically stable at every scale; Tukey’s biweight has a hard cutoff that can produce empty kernel windows during the ladder. An MM-estimator would add one more stage but we do not see evidence (in our DGPs) that such an increment would be worth the added pipeline depth.

D.4
Extending to multiple continuous treatments

The current method extends to
\vec{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
via a
d
-dimensional product kernel and a
(d+1)
-parameter WLS fit (§
4.6
); the GNC loop is unchanged because the redescending weight is still scalar per sample. A practical concern is that the “majority of kernel window is outlier” regime becomes easier to enter in higher dimensions (curse of dimensionality for kernel smoothers), so the post-GNC MAD fix is expected to become
more
important, not less. This is empirically confirmed in §
6.8
: the
SHIFT
-vs-OLS gap at
p=0.15
widens from
0.37
at
d=2
to
0.45
at
d=3
, while the clean-data tax stays
\leq 0.003
RMSE at both.

D.5
Why detection F1 plateaus under Student-
t_{3}

A
t_{3}
draw has tails
P(|X|>x)\sim x^{-3}
, so small jumps — on the order of a few
\sigma_{\epsilon}
— are
rank-indistinguishable
from the Gaussian noise floor. A rank-based classifier (which is what our outlier mask reduces to at matched predicted-positive rate
k
) cannot separate two distributions whose
k
-th order statistics overlap: the best it can do is a probabilistic assignment. The plateau at
\text{F}_{1}\approx 0.70
corresponds to the rate at which
|t_{3}|
draws exceed the Gaussian
95\%
-quantile — a property of the data, not the estimator.

The EVT suite documents this. On
sinusoidal_heavytail
, Hill
\hat{\alpha}=2.27,2.52,2.68
at
p=0.05,0.15,0.25
respectively — a clean monotone estimate of the generating
\nu=3
, corrected for bias-from-finite-tail-
n
. The MEF flattens as expected; the parameter-stability plot shows
\xi(u)\approx 0.2
–
0.3
stable over a threshold range. These diagnostics tell the analyst:
your detection F1 will plateau; no amount of robust loss tuning will fix it; switch to quantile regression for point estimation and use EVT for tail quantification
.

D.6
Why Winsor wins detection but loses shape

A Winsor cutoff at
\pm 3\operatorname{MAD}(Y)
is a
global
classifier: it labels any
|y_{i}|>3\operatorname{MAD}(Y)
as an outlier. Under uniform Gaussian-jump contamination (mean
\mu
, std
\sigma_{\mu}
), the two modes separate cleanly at
3\operatorname{MAD}
if
\mu>3\operatorname{MAD}
— which it is by construction in our DGPs — so Winsor’s
\text{F}_{1}
is
>0.97
by counting. But the same cutoff applied to
Y
before local-linear fitting
replaces the outlier
with
\pm 3\operatorname{MAD}
; in a kernel window concentrated in the outlier cluster, this shifts the OLS slope estimate away from its value on clean data. Hence high detection, poor shape. The two problems (identify and estimate) are orthogonal and our main-sweep tables make this visible.

D.7
Fundamental limits: when no method can help

Three regimes where
no
method in our comparison recovers the clean-data error:

1.

Majority-outlier kernel window on bounded
T
support.
If
p>0.5
and every kernel window near some
t_{0}
has
>50\%
outliers, every robust method has breakdown exceeded. Practitioners should widen the kernel or collect more clean data.

2.

Rank-indistinguishable tail.
Student-
t_{\nu}
with
\nu\leq 3
and small-magnitude contamination:
\text{F}_{1}
plateaus.

3.

Asymmetric +
p>0.3
.
A one-sided mean shift with
>30\%
contamination pushes the robust estimators’ median-based initial estimate off the structural trend, and GNC cannot recover. In our main sweep,
p\leq 0.25
, so this is out of scope but worth flagging for deployment.

D.8
A practitioner workflow

We suggest the following:

1.

Run
SHIFT
as the default. Inspect the per-sample weight vector for a candidate outlier mask and the ADRF shape.

2.

Run the EVT diagnostic on the
GNC-Fixed
residuals (or on
(1-w^{r})\cdot r
). If Hill
\hat{\alpha}<3
or GPD
\hat{\xi}>0
:
switch to
Quantile-DML
for the point estimate
; retain
SHIFT
’s mask for the detection report.

3.

If the causal tail coefficient
\Gamma(T\to|y_{\text{res}}|)>0.6
: the contamination is
T
-dependent. Consider
Huber-DML
for the point estimate;
SHIFT
’s mask can identify which
T
-regions are affected.

4.

If the percentile-bootstrap CI width is
>0.5\cdot
(signal scale), switch to BC
a
or studentized bootstrap, or use a block-wise resampling scheme if the data are dependent.

This workflow gives the analyst defensible choice points rather than an opaque pipeline. The tradeoff is human-in-the-loop effort; the payoff is that the final estimate is calibrated to the actual tail of the data rather than to an estimator’s implicit assumption about it.

F.8
Hill index interpretation under bounded tails

It is worth flagging that “Hill
\hat{\alpha}\approx 9
–
10
” on Gaussian-jump DGPs is a non-standard diagnostic interpretation. The correction (now in §
2
): the Hill estimator converges to
\alpha=1/\xi
when
\xi>0
(Fréchet domain) but
still produces a number
under bounded tails (
\xi<0

\hat{\alpha}=9

as a moment-existence claim; we interpret it as “
\alpha
is so large that the tail behaves as if exponentially bounded.” GPD-MLE/PWM remain the principal
\xi
estimators; Hill is a sanity check that distinguishes Fréchet from non-Fréchet but should not be over-interpreted in the bounded-tail case.

It is worth flagging that the manuscript previously stated

EVT independence assumption and kernel-local dependence.

One observation is that classical EVT assumes approximate independence of
exceedances. Cross-fitting and kernel weighting create mild dependence:
neighboring grid points share kernel-window samples, and cross-fit folds
produce residuals that are conditionally independent given
X
but
marginally weakly dependent. We mitigate this in two ways: (i) the EVT fit
is on the
global
sample of post-fit absolute residuals, not on the
40 grid-point fits separately, so within-window dependence is averaged out;
(ii) we apply a simple declustering rule when computing the GPD MLE
threshold: consecutive runs of exceedances within a kernel-window-radius
are collapsed to a single exceedance representative (the maximum). On
our DGPs the declustered and undeclustered
\hat{\xi}
estimates agree to
within
\pm 0.02
everywhere we have tested; the dependence-induced bias
is dominated by the much larger sampling variance (
\pm 0.10
–
0.15

seed-bootstrap CI on
\hat{\xi}
at

I.1
Notation reconciliation:
\gamma
vs
\sigma_{\text{eff}}

The previous draft used the Welsch parametrization

\rho_{\sigma}(r)=1-\exp(-r^{2}/(2\sigma^{2}))
in §

(citing

•

We use the Welsch loss in the form
\rho_{\sigma,\gamma}(r)=1-\exp(-\gamma r^{2}/(2\sigma^{2}))

This is equivalent to the one-parameter form

\sigma_{\text{eff}}^{2}=\sigma^{2}/\gamma

\sigma

•

The
\gamma
-divergence connection
\gamma=1/\sigma^{2}
from
Fujisawa and Eguchi 2008
is a different convention that we do not adopt.

The corresponding implementation is in
src/adrf_robust_dml/gnc.py

K.3
Local bandwidth adaptation in A5-failing windows

A natural alternative is local-bandwidth adaptation as a remedy for windows
that fail (A5). The natural recipe:

•

For each grid point
t_{0}
, compute the post-GNC inlier fraction
\rho(t_{0}):=\#\{i\in S(t_{0}):w^{r}_{i}\geq 0.5\}/|S(t_{0})|
.

•

If
\rho(t_{0})<0.5
(A5 violated),
widen
the bandwidth:
h(t_{0})\leftarrow h\cdot\min(2,0.5/\rho(t_{0}))
.

•

Re-run the GNC on the widened window; the additional samples are statistically less informative for
\theta(t_{0})
but reduce the chance of majority-outlier dominance.

We have not implemented this for the main sweep, but the analytical
prediction is: on
sinusoidal_region
at
p=0.25
where

\approx 11\%
of grid points violate (A5) (Tab.
1
),
adaptive bandwidth would expand the kernel for those grid points by a
factor up to
2
, decreasing the local outlier fraction by approximately
the same factor and likely restoring (A5). The cost is increased bias on
the already-difficult points; the trade-off is empirically testable but
not within scope. We flag this as the most promising future-work
extension.

Data-driven bandwidth selection (LOOCV/GCV) under contamination.
The classical LOOCV bandwidth selector minimizes squared prediction error, which under contamination is biased. A robust variant would use a Huber or trimmed prediction loss. App.
I.4
addresses one bandwidth issue (under-coverage); a fully data-adaptive bandwidth across DGPs is a separate and largely orthogonal project.
