 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Fusion energy represents a transformative opportunity to deliver a safe, plentiful, and carbon-free source
of reliable primary power. In recent years, fusion R&D has accelerated significantly, particularly within
the US, driven by decades of foundational public investment. Notably, in 2024 the US Department of
Energy (DOE) established a comprehensive Fusion Energy Strategy aimed at collaborating with industry
partners to enable the deployment of fusion power plants and achieve grid integration by the 2030s. This
strategy is chiefly implemented through the DOE Office of Science’s Fusion Energy Sciences program
and was updated in 2026.

This project was initiated to identify potential approaches for the Office of Environment, Safety, and
Health’s Nuclear Materials Integration Division (NA-ESH-12) within DOE’s National Nuclear Security
Administration to begin engaging with the fusion community on future accountable material needs. The
goal of the project is to inform and influence the supply of and demand for accountable nuclear materials
as fusion energy is developed and commercialized. NA-ESH-12 must proactively engage with the fusion
community regarding the production and management of accountable nuclear materials. Given the
complexity and scale of the materials required for research, pilot projects, and eventual commercial
reactors, early coordination is vital. The project’s objective is to provide insights that will shape the
supply and demand landscape for critical nuclear materials, ensuring that DOE is prepared to effectively
support fusion energy development and commercialization.

In FY 2025 and 2026, an initial review was conducted to assess the fusion energy community’s progress
toward full-scale energy production and the need for accountable nuclear material. To date, the review
indicates that the amounts of tritium, lithium-6, and deuterium required by the fusion industry will be
dependent on fuel types, breeding technology, blankets, and R&D improvements. One concern is that the
commercial sector does not have a sufficient supply chain to meet the forthcoming demand for the
development and commercialization of fusion energy production. The supply and demand estimates for
these materials should be routinely reviewed as fusion technologies mature.

 1. BACKGROUND

Fusion can potentially provide a safe, abundant, zero-carbon source of reliable primary energy. Over the
past decade, the fusion energy R&D landscape has evolved significantly, especially in the US. Building
on decades of public investments in fusion science and technology, major advances are being achieved
domestically and globally by public and private sector entities. In 2024, the US Department of Energy
(DOE) developed a Fusion Energy Strategy to work with industry to support the deployment of fusion
energy and supply it to the grid by the mid-to-late 2030s (Figure 1) [DOE 2024].

 Figure 1. Timeline for demonstration and deployment of fusion energy.

According to an industry survey, an estimated 35 fusion companies anticipate operating a commercially
viable plant capable of generating net energy by 2030 to 2035; some are targeting commercial readiness
before 2030 [Fusion Industry Association 2025].

Even before fusion energy is commercialized, significant quantities of accountable nuclear materials will
be needed to support R&D and technology demonstration facilities. The scale-up of the production of
these materials is so significant that years of advanced planning are needed to ensure that materials are
available to support the commercialization of the technology. An understanding of consumption,
production, and material interactions during R&D, pilot plant, and full-scale operations will be essential
to managing accountable material inventories in the major streams in a full fusion nuclear environment.
The goal of this project is to identify potential approaches for the Office of Environment, Safety, and
Health’s Nuclear Materials Integration Division (NA-ESH-12) within DOE’s National Nuclear Security
Administration (NNSA) to begin engaging with the fusion community on future accountable material
needs.

The goal of the project is to inform and influence the supply of and demand for accountable nuclear
materials as fusion energy is developed and commercialized. The scope of the project includes the
following:

• Develop an initial assessment of the accountable materials needed to support fusion development and
 implementation.
• Identify any potential effects on demand and supply for these materials.
• Assess the international supply of these materials.
• Identify potential approaches for NA-ESH-12 to use in engaging with the fusion community.
• Provide NA-ESH-12 with contact information for initiating engagement with the fusion community
 to consider DOE Order 410.2A requirements in a full fusion environment.

 2

The approach for accomplishing these goals is to begin engaging with the fusion energy community via
direct communications with organizations within DOE that participate in fusion energy programs and via
workshops and conferences with the private sector and international communities. In FY 2025 and
FY 2026, an initial limited review was conducted to identify the status of the fusion energy community’s
progress toward full-scale energy production and the need for accountable nuclear material. This task
included a literature review, attending sessions at various conferences, and attending fusion industry
workshops—for example, the Rutgers University–sponsored Supply Chain Workshop: Scaling the Fusion
Industry; the International Atomic Energy Agency’s Ninth DEMO Programme Workshop; DIII-D
National Fusion Facility industry meeting; Fusion Byproduct Materials Workshop; and the University of
Nevada–sponsored workshop Digging Deeper into Lithium.

 2. STATUS OF FUSION NUCLEAR ENERGY DEVELOPMENT EFFORTS

The fusion industry is growing rapidly; the US has the largest number of fusion energy companies
(Figure 2) [Fusion Industry Association 2025]. A list of investors is provided in Appendix A, and
company profiles are available in the Fusion Industry Association’s reports. DOE estimates that the US
private sector has attracted more than $10 billion in cumulative private investments to commercialize
fusion technology and deliver fusion energy to the grid [DOE 2026].

An estimated 172 fusion devices were operational, under construction, or planned in 2025 [IAEA 2025].
Of these devices, 45% were tokamaks, 17% were stellarators, and 10% were lasers or inertial devices.
Various other technologies made up the remaining 28%.

The majority of the companies surveyed by the Fusion Energy Association indicated that they are
developing deuterium–tritium (D-T) fuel sources (Figure 3) [Fusion Energy Association 2025]. This is
because D-T is regarded as an easier route to achieving fusion because its reactivity is significantly higher
at lower plasma temperatures than that of other fuels [IAEA 2026]. The roles that tritium, deuterium, and
lithium play in the fusion reaction are shown in Figure 4 [Pearson 2022].

Both the fusion industry and DOE agree that development of the fuel supply is critical for fusion energy
commercialization. The Fusion Industry Association’s 2024 survey of the global fusion industry indicated
that tritium self-sufficiency is the second greatest major challenge between now and 2030. After 2030,
tritium self-sufficiency is projected to become the greatest major challenge, along with full life cycle
issues such as waste management. The 2025 survey indicated that tritium self-sufficiency and full life
cycle issues (e.g., maintenance, waste, recycling, decommissioning) are two of the most significant
challenges for fusion energy implementation. The US fusion roadmap indicates that securing sufficient
tritium supplies and producing excess tritium is critical to the future growth of fusion energy [DOE 2026].

 3. ACCOUNTABLE NUCLEAR MATERIAL NEEDS

Fusion uses light isotopes as its fuel, such as deuterium and tritium (isotopes of hydrogen), helium-3, and
lithium-6. The required isotopic purity for deuterium, tritium, and helium-3 is estimated to be ≥99%
[Neuhoff 2026], The required purity for lithium-6 is ≥90%. None of these isotopes are found in nature
with the isotopic purities that fusion technologies require. They require enrichment to be useful to the
fusion industry.

Because tritium is not naturally available in meaningful quantities, sustainable commercial fusion energy
based on D-T fuel requires generating (i.e., breeding) tritium from lithium-6, which is therefore also
required for D-T fusion plants. Although it is envisioned that each D-T fusion plant will breed tritium
continuously to sustain its own tritium supply, each plant also requires a certain amount of startup tritium
and lithium-6. A sustainable supply of helium-3 would be required for fusion approaches that use
deuterium and helium-3 [DOE 2024]. Fusion facilities using D-T fuel will consume tritium at a rate of
55.8 kg of tritium per GW of fusion power per year [IAEA 2026].

In 2023, the DOE Office of Science, via its Fusion Energy Sciences program, completed an initial high-
level assessment of the potential demand for enriched light isotopes at the demonstration and deployment
scale-up phases (e.g., up to 10% of the US electricity market). DOE’s strategy is to collaborate with
partners to ensure viable supplies of fusion fuel isotopes, including domestic sources, by the mid-to-late
2030s to support potential fusion deployment scale-up per the expected timeline for deployment of fusion
energy [DOE 2024]. Several other organizations, primarily researchers and potential feedstock suppliers,

are also estimating the amount of accountable nuclear material that will be needed to support the fusion
industry. Examples are given in the following sections.

3.1 TRITIUM

Significant quantities of tritium will be needed to support fusion R&D and technology demonstration
facilities. In this phase of development, facilities are not expected to produce the tritium needed.
Commercial fusion plants will produce the tritium they need to fuel their reactors, so tritium will not be
needed from outside sources after the fusion reactors become fully operational.

Current inventory and DOE production will meet DOE national security needs [DOE 2021], but DOE
tritium production/inventories are not expected to be able to meet the fusion demand.

Virtually all commercially available tritium is produced as a by-product in Canada deuterium uranium
(CANDU) fission reactors. Estimates of the availability of tritium to meet the fusion energy demand vary.
In 2022, Kyoto Fusioneering models indicated that fusion demand is likely to begin to deplete the
available stockpile as early as 2035 [Pearson 2018]. Beyond 2050, tritium is unlikely to be produced by
CANDU reactors (end of life), so supply is highly uncertain [Pearson 2022]. This assessment is in general
agreement with a 2022 article in Science that noted that several ongoing research efforts were focused on
the tritium supply issue [Clery 2022].

Recent information indicates that Canada is refurbishing reactors to extend operations for the next 20–40
years and is exploring opportunities for new, larger reactors; these steps could extend the supply of
tritium to well beyond 2057 [Suppiah 2025].

3.2 LITHIUM

Enriched lithium-6 is used in fusion reactor blankets to produce tritium to fuel the fusion reaction.
Significant quantities of enriched lithium-6 will continue to be needed when fusion plants are
commercialized. A breeding blanket for a fusion pilot plant will likely require tens of tons of enriched
lithium-6 [Pearson 2022]. Current available inventories and production capabilities for enriched lithium-6
are not projected to meet the future fusion demand.

The column exchange (COLEX) process is the only historically proven way to produce lithium-6 at scale.
The process is expensive, energy intensive, and environmentally damaging. DOE production ended in
1963. The process is now banned under the Minamata treaty (ban on new uses of large quantities of
mercury for industrial purposes). The existing DOE inventory will not be available for commercial use
[Pearson 2022]. Russia and China produce the current supply of lithium-6, but they use most of it for their
own needs [Power Technology 2025]. The current lithium mining industry in the US is largely in Nevada.
Production will need to be increased to meet demand for the upcoming fusion and fission industries
[Jowitt 2026].

The fusion community is beginning to look for methods for enriching lithium. Among US vendors, for
example, Hexium in Texas is using atomic vapor laser isotope separation (AVLIS) [Griswold 2026],
Molten Salt Solutions in New Mexico is using a proprietary process [Power Technology 2025; Molten
Salt Solutions 2024; Elling 2026], Kairos Power is using a proprietary molten salt process [Kruizenga
2026], and Licube is using electrochemical technology that combines electrodialysis with lithium
lanthanum titanate membranes [Monroe 2026].

3.3 DEUTERIUM/HEAVY WATER

Deuterium supply is less of a concern within the fusion community than supplies of tritium and lithium-6
because of deuterium’s current commercial availability.

DOE no longer produces heavy water, and the current inventory is being retained for DOE defense
programs [DOE 2021]. Estimates indicate that nondefense requirements could be met by procuring heavy
water on the international market because restrictions on international suppliers would not apply to non-
weapons-related users such as the fusion industry. Canada and India, which rely on heavy water for
nuclear power plants for electricity, are the largest producers of heavy water [Ragheb 2021]. Canada is
also partnering with Kyoto Fusioneering to meet the deuterium needs of the D-T fusion system it is
pursuing. Canada has historically produced heavy water for commercial applications and has the
capability to produce deuterium. Other vendors also exist in the supply chain; however, production may
need to be scaled up to meet the growing need.

Other countries with heavy water production facilities include Argentina, Iran, Romania, and Russia
[Ragheb 2021].

 4. ISSUES TO BE CONSIDERED

4.1 SUPPLY AND DEMAND

The fusion industry’s demand for accountable nuclear materials will depend on the technologies used by
the fusion energy companies.

The international nature of fusion commercialization must also be considered when engaging with the
fusion community. Research and development facilities and pilot plants will be developed in several
countries. Key suppliers of accountable nuclear materials to support fusion are expected to be outside the
US. There are disparities between US and international accountability practices.

4.2 NONPROLIFERATION AND SAFEGUARDS

Fusion plants are expected to pose a lower nuclear proliferation risk than nuclear fission reactors. Fusion
plants do not use any special nuclear material used in nuclear weapons. However, D-T fusion plants and
the high-energy neutrons they produce will have the potential to breed plutonium from natural uranium or
uranium-233 from thorium. In addition, D-T fusion plants will have significant inventories of tritium and
lithium-6, which are used in advanced nuclear weapons.

A question has arisen regarding the role of the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons
(NPT) with respect to fusion. Because fusion does not use source material, special fissionable material, or
the fission nuclear fuel cycle, commercial fusion applications fall outside the current NPT regime.
However, consideration is being given to whether the nuclear nonproliferation regime should be extended
to include future fusion plants [Hua 2022]. DOE and NNSA are supporting the path toward an appropriate
nonproliferation approach in collaboration with the US fusion industry and other stakeholders
[DOE 2024].

4.3 WASTE MANAGEMENT

Fusion waste is significantly different from fission nuclear power plant waste, and the quantity of waste
from fusion plants will be much larger. Fusion waste does not contain many long-lived isotopes; it mainly
contains tritium and other activation isotopes. Many publications indicate that the fusion industry should

 7

be able to avoid creating high-level waste, but the volume of fusion low-level waste (LLW) will be
significant, both in terms of volume and volume per unit of electricity produced [Gonzalez De Vicente
2022]. R&D is needed to find ways to minimize waste and develop cost-effective waste separation,
recycling, and disposal pathways [DOE 2024].

The dominant fusion wastes will be composed primarily of structural materials; these wastes will be
produced during routine maintenance and the decommissioning of reactors. Because fusion reactions will
inherently generate high-energy neutrons—especially in D-T fusion systems and, to a lesser extent, in
other fusion fuel cycles—fusion will produce activated and tritiated waste. The amount and type of waste
will depend on reactor type and construction materials. In addition, waste categorization and disposal
requirements vary from country to country. It has often been assumed that waste from fusion reactors will
not require deep geological repository disposal [DOE 2024, Gonzalez De Vicente 2022], but some studies
have indicated that this may not be the case.

Previous studies have shown that fusion reactor waste may not meet LLW requirements; thus, geological
disposal may be required [Bailey, 2021]. Many components requiring disposal will be constructed of
stainless steel, and this material has been the focus of many waste evaluations. Bailey investigated the
waste expectations for steels from European DEMO-like fusion reactors, and the results suggested that
steels are not likely to meet the majority of LLW requirements when they are exposed to near-plasma
neutron fluxes [Bailey 2021]. At lower neutron fluxes, typical vacuum vessels may be able to meet some
LLW criteria. The study concluded that steels should not be expected to be consistently classified
internationally as LLW 100 years after reactor shutdown. Currently available steels may not meet LLW
criteria when subjected to the highest neutron fluxes expected in fusion reactors. Waste disposal systems
that include global limits on β + γ specific activity are not likely to be able to accept fusion steel waste as
LLW because of the production of longer-lived β emitters such as niobium-94, carbon-14, and nickel-63.
The lower fluxes associated with containment conditions would allow steels to more readily meet LLW
requirements, but many of the best-performing steels in the containment systems may not possess the
structural and mechanical properties required. The steels with the worst adherence to LLW criteria in
lower flux regions typically fail to meet LLW criteria because of excessive nickel-63 or niobium-94
activity. These results suggest that vessel and in-vessel steels may not meet LLW criteria 100 years after
removal from reactors.

Nicholas reported that modeling of the European DEMO-like fusion reactor indicates that the steel used
for the first wall, breeder blanket, and divertor components will always exceed the UK’s reduced-
activation criterion and hence will be classified as intermediate-level waste (ILW) [Nicholas 2021]. In
fact, more ILW may be produced by fusion than by fission. Fusion-specific structural steels will therefore
be classified as ILW, with half-lives of thousands of years, under the UK protocol and will represent a
significant volume of radioactive waste. Recent EU DEMO designs require 1,300–1,500 t of steel that
will be highly irradiated and thus become ILW. Furthermore, any beryllium used in a breeder blanket
could exceed the reduced-activation criterion due to natural uranium impurities (which activate to become
Pu-239 and Am-241 in trace amounts, which have extremely long half-lives). Under current UK nuclear
law, ILW from fusion plants, like ILW from fission plants, requires geological disposal.

The US Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) classifies waste based on activity/m3, whereas other
countries use activity/kg to define LLW and ILW limits. The US has a Greater-Than-Class C
classification rather than an ILW classification. Bailey used a density of 7.9 g/cm3 to include US
requirements in waste classification evaluations [Bailey 2021]. These evaluations indicated that the
blanket region and the vacuum vessel could possibly exceed Greater-Than-Class C limits for shallow
ground burial at commercial disposal sites that have NRC licenses.

 8

The NRC has recently taken steps toward developing a fusion regulatory framework that integrates fusion
machines into the existing by-product material approach, which avoids classifying fusion energy systems
as utilization facilities [NRC 2026]. The aim is to implement this approach via changes to Title 10 of the
Code of Federal Regulations, Parts 20, 30, 37, 50, 51, 72, 110, 150, 170, and 171. The proposed
amendments include changes to the definitions of fusion machine, particle accelerator, and by-product
material, as well as changes to requirements related to content of application, recordkeeping, inspection,
intruder assessment for waste disposal sites, and environmental reports. The intention is to provide a
technology-neutral framework that covers fusion approaches currently under development. In a rapidly
evolving landscape, the NRC may impose case-by-case requirements, as needed, for fusion machines with
unanticipated safety and security elements.

Waste characteristics and disposal requirements should be considered when selecting material for and
designing fusion energy systems. These systems may generate waste that requires deep geological
repository disposal and activate impurities in material to produce transuranic elements. The recent NRC
regulatory framework for fusion systems will make this unlikely for fusion reactors in the near-term
development and demonstration phases.

 5. PATH FORWARD

The amounts of tritium, lithium-6, and deuterium required by the fusion industry will depend on fuel
types, breeding technologies, blankets, and technology improvements. The supply and demand estimates
for these materials must be routinely reviewed as fusion technologies mature.

Ways to engage with the fusion industry include, but are not limited to, interacting with other DOE
agencies, attending workshops and conferences such as those coordinated by the International Atomic
Energy Agency, and interacting with private sector companies through organizations such as the Fusion
Industry Association.

During the R&D and technology demonstration phases, DOE could contribute accountable materials to
advance fusion power development. Over the long term, there is potential for DOE to obtain accountable
materials from fusion suppliers to support DOE needs. The potential effect on DOE management of
accountable materials warrants NA-ESH-12’s continued engagement with the fusion energy community.
