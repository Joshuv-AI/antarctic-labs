Adverse Effects of V2V Adoption on Road Safety
Zhenqi Liu, Philip N. Brown, Keith Paarporn

Abstract
Vehicle-to-vehicle (V2V) communication is expected to improve road safety and reduce congestion. However, prior work shows that V2V information sharing under partial adoption may increase congestion and decrease safety. We study whether increasing V2V adoption itself affects road safety. We propose a corrected version of an existing model and analyze its behavior under varying adoption levels. We show that, in some cases, increased V2V adoption can increase accident probability. Moreover, under an optimal signaling policy, the system can ensure that accident probability is non-increasing in the adoption level.



keywords

Transportation systems; vehicle-to-vehicle communication; signaling; game theory; partial adoption; traffic safety; equilibrium analysis.

1
Introduction

Vehicle-to-vehicle (V2V) communication is an emerging smart transportation technology that allows equipped vehicles to exchange information about road hazards. Such systems have the potential to improve road safety by warning drivers of road hazards or dangerous conditions before they encounter them. However, transportation systems are shaped not only by technical performance, but also by the behavioral responses of strategic drivers. When drivers change their behavior in response to information, the resulting aggregate behavior can feed back into the safety or congestion state of the road system. Thus, understanding the effect of V2V communication requires an equilibrium perspective.

Equilibrium models of transportation systems go back at least to Wardrop’s classical principles, which distinguish individually stable route choices from system-optimal traffic assignments
(
13
)
. A large literature on selfish routing and traffic equilibrium shows that individually optimal driver behavior need not produce system-optimal outcomes
(
5
)
, and that transportation equilibria can exhibit counterintuitive phenomena such as Braess-type paradoxes
(
6
)
. Information can introduce an additional source of counterintuitive behavior. Driver information systems may improve route choice and network performance, but they may also create concentration, overreaction, or other adverse effects
(
3
;
2
)
. In particular, heterogeneous access to information can change equilibrium outcomes in nontrivial ways
(
10
)
, and providing additional information to a subset of drivers can even make informed drivers worse off, a phenomenon known as informational Braess’ paradox
(
1
)
.

These observations connect naturally to the literature on information design. Bayesian persuasion studies how a sender can influence a receiver’s action by choosing what information to reveal
(
9
)
, and information design provides a broader framework for studying how information structures affect strategic behavior
(
4
)
. Recent work has applied these ideas to transportation networks, studying how public signals, private recommendations, or partial participation in information systems can be used to regulate equilibrium traffic flows
(
14
;
11
;
15
)
. These works show that full information disclosure is not always optimal and that the design of information provision can substantially affect equilibrium performance.

Our work is most closely related to the V2V hazard-warning model of
7
, which studies partial adoption of V2V communication in a nonatomic driver population. In that model, only a fraction of drivers are V2V-equipped, and equipped drivers may condition their behavior on whether a warning signal is displayed. They show that sharing hazard information only with V2V-equipped drivers can paradoxically increase the equilibrium frequency of accidents relative to no information sharing. An extended journal version further develops this information-design perspective for V2V communication, including false positives and social-cost considerations
(
8
)
. These results are part of a broader theme in which emerging transportation technologies can have unintended equilibrium effects
(
12
)
.

In this paper, we revisit the partial-adoption V2V signaling model under a corrected accident-probability consistency equation. Motivated by the adverse effects identified in
7
, we ask whether the original consistency model should be revised, what happens as V2V adoption increases under the corrected model, and whether the resulting adverse effects can be mitigated. Our main contributions are as follows:

•

We identify a consistency issue in the original accident-probability model: unsignaled and signaled V2V behaviors are contingent on different information states and should not be averaged as simultaneous reckless masses. We formulate the corrected consistency equation.

•

Under the corrected model, we derive an explicit equilibrium-region characterization and use it to analyze the effect of V2V adoption. This characterization shows that, under fixed signaling probabilities, increasing V2V adoption can increase the equilibrium accident probability, and that any such local perverse effect can occur only in two specific equilibrium regions.

•

We show that the perverse effect can be eliminated by optimizing the signaling probability. Specifically, if the signaling probability is chosen optimally at each adoption level, then the optimized equilibrium accident probability is weakly decreasing in V2V adoption.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section
2
presents the corrected V2V signaling model. Section
3
states the main results. Section
4
provides the equilibrium characterization and proofs. Section
5
discusses implications of the results, and Section
6
concludes.

2
Model

2.1
Game Setup

The population of drivers is modeled as a continuum with total mass one. A fraction
y\in[0,1]
of drivers are V2V-equipped, which we call V2V drivers, while the remaining fraction
1-y
are not, which we call non-V2V drivers. We refer to
y
as the V2V adoption level. A driver can choose either to drive carefully or recklessly. We let
a\in\{\mathrm{C},\mathrm{R}\}
denote a driver’s action, where
\mathrm{C}
denotes careful driving and
\mathrm{R}
denotes reckless driving.

Let
A
denote the event that an accident occurs. Conditional on an accident occurring, the V2V system may detect it. We write the detection probability as
q(y)
, reflecting its dependence on the V2V adoption level. Throughout this manuscript, we assume that
q:[0,1]\to[0,1]
is continuous and weakly increasing, so a higher V2V adoption level makes accident detection weakly more likely. We do not impose strict monotonicity unless explicitly stated.

In the V2V setting, once an accident is detected, the system designer can choose whether to display the detected accident information to V2V-equipped drivers. We denote by
\beta\in[0,1]
the probability that this information is displayed to V2V-equipped drivers.

Let
S
denote the event that a warning signal is shown to a V2V driver. This event happens only when an accident occurs, the V2V system detects it, and the system displays the detected accident information with probability
\beta
. Therefore,

\Pr(S)=\Pr(A)q(y)\beta.

In our model, the accident probability
\Pr(A)
is endogenously determined. Driver behavior influences the accident probability, and the accident probability, together with the primitive game parameters, in turn influences driver behavior. Careful drivers do not negatively affect road safety, while the total mass of reckless drivers determines how likely an accident is to occur. Thus, let
x
denote the behavior profile encoding the mass of reckless drivers, and let
P(x;\beta,y)
denote the accident probability induced by
x
,
\beta
, and
y
. Then
\Pr(A)=P(x;\beta,y)
, and

\Pr(S)=P(x;\beta,y)q(y)\beta.

(1)

The population is naturally divided into non-V2V drivers and V2V-equipped drivers. Non-V2V drivers do not receive warning signals, while V2V-equipped drivers may condition their behavior on whether a warning signal is displayed. Thus, a V2V driver has two possible information states: unsignaled and signaled. We denote by
x_{\mathrm{n}}\in[0,1-y]
the reckless mass of non-V2V drivers, by
x_{\mathrm{vu}}\in[0,y]
the reckless V2V mass on the unsignaled event
S^{c}
, and by
x_{\mathrm{vs}}\in[0,y]
the reckless V2V mass on the signaled event
S
. The behavior profile is therefore written as

x=(x_{\mathrm{n}},(x_{\mathrm{vu}},x_{\mathrm{vs}})).

Although one may informally refer to non-V2V, unsignaled V2V, and signaled V2V drivers as separate groups, this notation should be understood as a contingent behavior profile of the V2V population under the unsignaled and signaled events. In particular,
x_{\mathrm{vu}}
and
x_{\mathrm{vs}}
are not added simultaneously in a realized information state. Therefore, we define the unsignaled-state and signaled-state total reckless masses by

d_{\mathrm{u}}(x):=x_{\mathrm{n}}+x_{\mathrm{vu}},\qquad d_{\mathrm{s}}(x):=x_{\mathrm{n}}+x_{\mathrm{vs}}.

When the profile
x
is clear from context, we simply write
d_{\mathrm{u}}
and
d_{\mathrm{s}}
.

We define the function
p:[0,1]\to[0,1]
, which maps a realized total reckless driver mass to the corresponding accident probability. Throughout the manuscript, we assume that
p
is continuous and weakly increasing, so a larger reckless driver mass makes an accident weakly more likely. We do not impose strict monotonicity unless explicitly stated.

The consistency equation for
P(x;\beta,y)
is then

P(x;\beta,y)=\bigl(1-\Pr(S)\bigr)p(d_{\mathrm{u}})+\Pr(S)p(d_{\mathrm{s}}).

(2)

This consistency equation can be solved explicitly for
P(x;\beta,y)
. Substituting (
1
) into (
2
) and solving for
P(x;\beta,y)
gives

P(x;\beta,y)=\frac{p(d_{\mathrm{u}})}{1+\beta q(y)\bigl(p(d_{\mathrm{u}})-p(d_{\mathrm{s}})\bigr)}.

(3)

The expression in (
3
) removes the recursive appearance of
P(x;\beta,y)
. In Section
4.1
, we verify that this expression is well-defined and yields a valid probability for the equilibrium characterization.

2.3
Corrected Accident Probability Model

We retain the behavioral and cost structure of the original model
(
7
)
, but replace its consistency equation with (
2
). The original consistency equation is

P(x;\beta,y)=p\Bigl(x_{\mathrm{n}}+\bigl(1-\Pr(S)\bigr)x_{\mathrm{vu}}+\Pr(S)x_{\mathrm{vs}}\Bigr).

To explain the correction, define the random variable
D
as the realized total reckless mass. For a given profile
x
, let

D=\begin{cases}d_{\mathrm{u}}(x),&\text{on }S^{c},\\
d_{\mathrm{s}}(x),&\text{on }S.\end{cases}

The original formulation effectively evaluates accident risk at an averaged reckless mass. In contrast, the corrected consistency equation evaluates the accident function separately in the unsignaled and signaled states and then averages the resulting accident probabilities. This distinction matters because
p
maps the realized reckless mass in a given information state to the accident probability in that state. The accident probability in one realized information state should not be evaluated using reckless masses from other states.

In simple terms, the original consistency equation evaluates accident probability as
p\bigl(\mathbb{E}[D]\bigr)
, whereas the corrected consistency equation evaluates accident probability as
\mathbb{E}\bigl[p(D)\bigr]
. Generally, unless
p
is linear,

\mathbb{E}\bigl[p(D)\bigr]\neq p\bigl(\mathbb{E}[D]\bigr).

(6)

2.4
Notation

Although
P
depends on all primitive parameters, so that

\Pr(A)=P(x;\beta,y,r,p,q),

we primarily study how the induced accident probability varies with the signaling probability
\beta
and the adoption level
y
, treating
r,p,q
as fixed primitives. Therefore, we suppress this dependence and write
P(x;\beta,y)
. When
\beta
and
y
are fixed or clear from context, we simply write
P(x)
.

We now state the two main consequences of the corrected model. First, increased V2V adoption can increase equilibrium accident probability under fixed non-optimal signaling probabilities. Second, this perverse effect disappears when the signaling probability is chosen optimally at each adoption level.

3.1
Perverse Effect of V2V Adoption

Under the corrected model, we characterize the equilibrium profiles in Section
4.1
. Let
P^{\mathrm{ne}}(\beta,y)
denote the equilibrium accident probability under this characterization. Our first main result shows that increased V2V adoption can increase equilibrium accident probability.

Theorem 1

There exist model parameters satisfying the assumptions above and a fixed signaling probability
\beta\in[0,1]
such that the equilibrium accident probability

P^{\mathrm{ne}}(\beta,y)

is increasing over an interval of V2V adoption levels
y
.

The proof further shows that such a local increase can occur only through the parameter regions
\mathcal{R}_{3}
and
\mathcal{R}_{4}
, which are defined formally in Section
4.1
. Fig.
1
plots
P^{\mathrm{ne}}
as a function of
y
for fixed signaling probabilities
\beta=0.05
and
\beta=0.35
. The corresponding heatmap in Fig.
2
, generated using the same primitive parameters, shows that the increasing segments occur in the regions
\mathcal{R}_{3}
and
\mathcal{R}_{4}
. Although the increase in this illustrative instance is modest, it demonstrates the mechanism; larger increases can occur for other parameter values.

3.2
Optimal Signaling Removes the Perverse Effect

One might expect that increasing V2V adoption always improves road safety. Theorem
1
shows that this need not be true under fixed non-optimal signaling probabilities. This raises a natural design question: can the system designer choose the signaling probability at each adoption level so that increased V2V adoption does not make the road less safe? Our second main result answers this question.

Theorem 2

For fixed primitives
r,p,q
, define an optimal signaling policy
\beta^{*}:[0,1]\to[0,1]
by selecting, for each
y\in[0,1]
,

\beta^{*}(y)\in\arg\min_{\beta\in[0,1]}P^{\mathrm{ne}}(\beta,y).

Then the optimized equilibrium accident probability

P^{\mathrm{ne}}(\beta^{*}(y),y)

is weakly decreasing in
y
.

Fig.
1
illustrates this result: the trace under
\beta=\beta^{*}(y)
is weakly decreasing, while traces under fixed non-optimal signaling probabilities may exhibit increasing segments. Additionally, the trace
\beta=\beta^{*}(y)
in Fig.
2
illustrates how the optimal policy is chosen as a function of adoption level. A constructive characterization of such an optimal policy is given in Proposition

4
Proofs

By (
6
), the corrected consistency equation generally differs from the original one when
p
is nonlinear. Therefore, we establish the equilibrium characterization and endpoint optimality under the corrected model. The proofs are organized in three parts:

•

First, we verify the well-definedness of (
3
) and characterize the equilibrium outcomes generated by the corrected consistency equation.

•

Second, we analyze the designer’s optimization problem over the signaling probability and construct one optimal signaling policy.

•

Finally, we prove Theorems
1
and
2
using these intermediate results.

4.1
Equilibrium Region Characterization

We first collect the notation needed to state the equilibrium region characterization. The two behavioral thresholds are defined by

P_{\mathrm{n}}=\frac{1}{1+r},\qquad P_{\mathrm{vu}}(\beta,y)=\frac{1}{1+r(1-\beta q(y))}.

These are the accident probabilities at which the costs of reckless and careful driving are equal for non-V2V drivers and unsignaled V2V drivers, respectively. We also define the following candidate accident probabilities:

P_{1}(\beta,y)=\frac{p(1)}{1+\beta q(y)\bigl(p(1)-p(1-y)\bigr)},

P_{2}(x_{\mathrm{n}};\beta,y)=\frac{p(x_{\mathrm{n}}+y)}{1+\beta q(y)\bigl(p(x_{\mathrm{n}}+y)-p(x_{\mathrm{n}})\bigr)},

P_{3}(\beta,y)=\frac{p(y)}{1+\beta q(y)\bigl(p(y)-p(0)\bigr)},

P_{4}(x_{\mathrm{vu}};\beta,y)=\frac{p(x_{\mathrm{vu}})}{1+\beta q(y)\bigl(p(x_{\mathrm{vu}})-p(0)\bigr)},

and

P_{5}=p(0).

Table
2

, it becomes the equilibrium characterization table. For each

{pf}

[Lemma

4.2
Optimal Signaling

We next turn from equilibrium characterization to the designer’s problem of choosing the signaling probability. The next proposition gives one explicit optimal signaling policy, using a tie-breaking rule in favor of no signaling.

Proposition 6

Suppose
p
and
q
are strictly increasing. With ties broken in favor of no signaling, one such optimal signaling policy
\beta^{*}
can be selected as

\beta^{*}(y)=\begin{cases}1,&P_{1}(1,y)<P_{\mathrm{n}},\ y>0,\ q(y)>0,\\[3.0pt]
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(9)

The proof of the proposition proceeds in three steps: first we show how regions are ordered as the signaling probability varies, then we prove endpoint optimality, and finally we compare the optimized endpoint values. The first step is to show that, for fixed adoption level, the realized equilibrium region can move only in one direction as the signaling probability increases.

Lemma 7

Fix
y
. As
\beta
increases, the realized equilibrium regions can move only through a contiguous subchain of

\mathcal{R}_{5}\to\mathcal{R}_{4}\to\mathcal{R}_{3}\to\mathcal{R}_{2}\to\mathcal{R}_{1}.

(10)

In particular, the transition cannot move backward or skip an intermediate region.

{pf}

[Lemma
7
]
If
q(y)=0
, then
k=\beta q(y)=0
for all
\beta
, so the realized region does not change with
\beta
. Hence the realized path is a one-element subchain. We therefore assume
q(y)>0
. Then
k=\beta q(y)
increases with
\beta
. Using the endpoint descriptions of
\mathcal{R}_{2}
and
\mathcal{R}_{4}
, the equality-region conditions can be written as

\mathcal{R}_{4}:\quad P_{5}\leq P_{\mathrm{vu}}(\beta,y)\leq P_{3}(\beta,y),

\mathcal{R}_{2}:\quad P_{3}(\beta,y)\leq P_{\mathrm{n}}\leq P_{1}(\beta,y).

Moreover, by Lemma
5
, the same endpoint relations also give the global ordering

P_{5}\leq P_{3}(\beta,y)\leq P_{1}(\beta,y).

As
k
increases,
P_{\mathrm{vu}}(\beta,y)
increases, while
P_{3}(\beta,y)
and
P_{1}(\beta,y)
weakly decrease;
P_{\mathrm{n}}
and
P_{5}
are fixed. This ordering is illustrated in Fig.
3
. Hence the relevant crossing conditions can only switch in the order

P_{\mathrm{vu}}=P_{5}\;\;\to\;\;P_{\mathrm{vu}}=P_{3}\;\;\to\;\;P_{3}=P_{\mathrm{n}}\;\;\to\;\;P_{1}=P_{\mathrm{n}}.

These crossings correspond to the transitions

\mathcal{R}_{5}\to\mathcal{R}_{4},\qquad\mathcal{R}_{4}\to\mathcal{R}_{3},\qquad\mathcal{R}_{3}\to\mathcal{R}_{2},\qquad\mathcal{R}_{2}\to\mathcal{R}_{1}.

These transitions can occur only in the forward direction. For example, once
P_{\mathrm{vu}}(\beta,y)\geq P_{5}
, the system cannot return to
\mathcal{R}_{5}
, because
P_{\mathrm{vu}}(\beta,y)
increases with
k
and
P_{5}
is fixed. Similarly, once
P_{\mathrm{vu}}(\beta,y)\geq P_{3}(\beta,y)
, the system cannot return to
\mathcal{R}_{4}
, because
P_{\mathrm{vu}}(\beta,y)
increases while
P_{3}(\beta,y)
weakly decreases. The same reasoning applies to the crossings
P_{3}(\beta,y)=P_{\mathrm{n}}
and
P_{1}(\beta,y)=P_{\mathrm{n}}
. Equality may persist over an interval of
\beta
, but the ordering cannot reverse. Thus the realized path cannot move backward.

Finally, the fixed ordering
P_{5}\leq P_{3}(\beta,y)\leq P_{1}(\beta,y)
prevents any intermediate region from being skipped. The crossing
P_{\mathrm{vu}}(\beta,y)=P_{3}(\beta,y)
cannot occur before
P_{\mathrm{vu}}(\beta,y)=P_{5}
, because
P_{5}\leq P_{3}(\beta,y)
. Similarly,
P_{3}(\beta,y)=P_{\mathrm{n}}
cannot occur before
P_{\mathrm{vu}}(\beta,y)=P_{3}(\beta,y)
, because
P_{\mathrm{n}}\leq P_{\mathrm{vu}}(\beta,y)
; and
P_{1}(\beta,y)=P_{\mathrm{n}}
cannot occur before
P_{3}(\beta,y)=P_{\mathrm{n}}
, because
P_{3}(\beta,y)\leq P_{1}(\beta,y)
. Therefore the realized region path can only be a contiguous subchain of (
10
), and this completes the proof.

This region ordering rules out interior optima and leads to endpoint optimality.

Lemma 8

Fix
y\in[0,1]
. The equilibrium accident probability is minimized by an endpoint signaling probability. That is,

\min_{\beta\in[0,1]}P^{\mathrm{ne}}(\beta,y)=\min\{P^{\mathrm{ne}}(0,y),P^{\mathrm{ne}}(1,y)\}.

{pf}

[Lemma
8
]
Fix
y\in[0,1]
. By Lemma
7
, as
\beta
increases, the realized equilibrium region can move only through a contiguous subchain (
10
). By Proposition
3
, the corresponding equilibrium accident probabilities are

P_{5},\qquad P_{\mathrm{vu}}(\beta,y),\qquad P_{3}(\beta,y),\qquad P_{\mathrm{n}},\qquad P_{1}(\beta,y).

As
\beta
increases,
P_{5}
and
P_{\mathrm{n}}
are constant,
P_{\mathrm{vu}}(\beta,y)
weakly increases, and
P_{3}(\beta,y)
and
P_{1}(\beta,y)
weakly decrease. Therefore, along any realized subchain,
P^{\mathrm{ne}}(\beta,y)
can only be constant or increasing before reaching
\mathcal{R}_{3}
, and constant or decreasing after leaving
\mathcal{R}_{4}
. Thus the minimum of

over

must occur at one of the endpoints.

Endpoint optimality reduces the designer’s problem to comparing the two endpoint signaling probabilities,
\beta=0

\beta=1

Lemma 9

For each

, let

P^{\mathrm{ne}}(\beta^{*}(y),y)=\begin{cases}p(0),&P_{\mathrm{n}}<p(0),\\[3.0pt]
\min\{P_{\mathrm{n}},P_{1}(1,y)\},&p(0)\leq P_{\mathrm{n}}\leq p(1),\\[3.0pt]
P_{1}(1,y),&p(1)<P_{\mathrm{n}}.\end{cases}

(11)

{pf}

[Lemma

]
Fix
y\in[0,1]
. By Lemma

, it is enough to compare the two endpoint signaling probabilities
\beta=0
and
\beta=1
. When
\beta=0

, so by Proposition

P^{\mathrm{ne}}(0,y)=\begin{cases}p(0),&P_{\mathrm{n}}<p(0),\\[3.0pt]
P_{\mathrm{n}},&p(0)\leq P_{\mathrm{n}}\leq p(1),\\[3.0pt]
p(1),&p(1)<P_{\mathrm{n}}.\end{cases}

We compare the full-signaling endpoint against this benchmark. First suppose
P_{\mathrm{n}}<p(0)
. By Lemma

{pf}

5
Discussion

The results above show that signaling is useful only when the system can enter the safe region
\mathcal{R}_{1}
, where
P_{1}(\beta,y)<P_{\mathrm{n}}
. This condition is restrictive. Indeed, since
q(y)\leq 1
and
p(1-y)\geq p(0)
, we have

\frac{p(1)}{1+p(1)-p(0)}\leq P_{1}(1,y).

Thus, even under the most favorable detection case,
P_{1}(1,y)
cannot fall below the left-hand side. Therefore, for
\mathcal{R}_{1}
to be attainable, it is necessary that

\frac{p(1)}{1+p(1)-p(0)}<P_{\mathrm{n}}=\frac{1}{1+r}.

Assuming
p(1)>0
, this is equivalent to

r<\frac{1-p(0)}{p(1)}.

Hence
\mathcal{R}_{1}
is truly a “safe” region: for signaling to be useful, the accident-cost parameter must not be too large relative to the baseline accident-risk function. In this sense, accident warnings are beneficial only when the underlying accident-risk environment is sufficiently mild for behavioral adaptation to reduce accident risk.

6
Conclusion

This paper revisits a partial-adoption V2V signaling model under a corrected accident-probability consistency equation. We show that increased V2V adoption can have adverse effects under non-optimal signaling probabilities, but that these effects can be eliminated by choosing the signaling policy optimally. Several extensions remain for future work. First, allowing noisy warning signals would provide a more general model of V2V information reliability. Second, incorporating network structure or dynamic adoption would allow the model to capture spatial and temporal effects that are abstracted away in the present aggregate formulation.
