 ABSTRACT

MiniFuel irradiation experiments have become a key capability for accelerated evaluation of advanced and
high-burnup nuclear fuels in the High Flux Isotope Reactor. As the number of irradiated MiniFuel targets
entering postirradiation examination (PIE) has increased, improvements to the PIE workflow were needed
to support more repeatable target disassembly, subcapsule processing, specimen recovery, and downstream
measurements. This report summarizes FY 2026 improvements developed and implemented at the
Irradiated Fuels Examination Laboratory to improve the MiniFuel target-to-specimen (T2S) workflow and
related PIE activities.

T2S improvements included a controlled target disassembly tool, a computer numerical control (CNC)-
based contingency method for target opening, prepuncture subcapsule leak testing, fission gas release
process improvements, and CNC subcapsule disassembly for thermometry recovery and fuel specimen
visualization. Additional PIE improvements focused on reducing downstream measurement bottlenecks
through installation of an in-cell gamma counting fixture and development of microscopy transfer and
specimen preparation approaches for higher dose or remotely handled MiniFuel specimens.

These process improvements supported recovery and examination of irradiated specimens while adding
quality-control information via leak testing and improving the connection between specimen recovery and
postirradiation data collection.

 1. INTRODUCTION

Since the inception of the MiniFuel irradiation design at Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) in 2017
[1], several programs and irradiation campaigns have leveraged and adapted this capability to perform
accelerated irradiation testing of miniature fuel specimens. The MiniFuel irradiation vehicle design,
previously documented in journal publications [1][2] and shown in Figure 1 [3], has become a standard
vehicle for separate-effects irradiation experiments supporting advanced fuel development, high-burnup
(HBU) fuel studies, and fuel fragmentation, relocation, and dispersal (FFRD) evaluations [3].

 1

 Figure 1. MiniFuel irradiation vehicle design. From left to right: removable beryllium (RB) and vertical
experiment facility (VXF) MiniFuel vehicles comprising the Al basket loaded with targets, a single MiniFuel target
 containing six subcapsules, a typical subcapsule, and the various fuel configurations that have been irradiated [3].

A primary technical driver for the MiniFuel design is its ability to screen fuel concepts under controlled
irradiation conditions using small fuel specimens. MiniFuel experiments can accommodate a range of fuel
chemistries and geometries, including disks, kernels, and tristructural-isotropic (TRISO)-bearing
configurations. The fuel specimens are sealed within subcapsules containing a heater tube (also referred to
as filler) and passive SiC thermometry (TM); six subcapsules are stacked within each MiniFuel target. The
filler provides gamma heating, allowing the steady-state fuel temperature during irradiation to be
established primarily by the experiment design, while burnup accumulation can be varied by changing the
irradiation position, including removable beryllium (RB) and vertical experiment facility (VXF) locations,
as well as axial position within the High Flux Isotope Reactor (HFIR) core. This setup provides a practical
means of partially decoupling fuel temperature from burnup rate, allowing candidate fuel concepts to be
irradiated across temperatures representative of selected in-service reactor conditions. The resulting
specimens can then be evaluated through focused postirradiation examination (PIE) measurements such as
fission gas release (FGR) and microstructural characterization. To confirm irradiation temperature, SiC TM
recovered from the subcapsules is tested after irradiation via dilatometry [4][5]. Although the small
specimen configuration limits statistical breadth, it provides an efficient screening approach before
progressing to larger-scale irradiation tests.

As use of the MiniFuel platform has expanded, the PIE workflow has shifted from supporting individual
irradiation campaigns to supporting a growing inventory of irradiated targets requiring repeatable, higher-
throughput processing. To date, 43 MiniFuel targets have been assembled and inserted for irradiation in
HFIR. At the beginning of FY 2026, 12 targets were in the PIE phase, including five HBU targets [6]. Table
1 summarizes the MiniFuel targets at ORNL’s Irradiated Fuels Examination Laboratory (IFEL) that were
pending disassembly and/or PIE during FY 2026. The quantity and diversity of these targets created a need
to standardize the target-to-specimen (T2S) workflow and reduce process constraints that could delay
recovery of fuel specimens, TM, and associated PIE data.

Following irradiation, MiniFuel targets are transported to IFEL for disassembly and PIE. The legacy
MiniFuel PIE workflow, shown in Figure 2, was developed and successfully used to recover specimens and
collect postirradiation data from earlier MiniFuel experiments [7][8].

 Figure 2. Workflow of the PIE process used since the first MiniFuel experiment.

However, the increase in the number of targets entering PIE, together with the broader range of fuel forms,
irradiation conditions, and postirradiation characterization needs, exposed several process limitations.
These limitations were associated not with a single operation but with the overall integration of the
workflow. Key constraints included facility availability for sequential operations, repeated material
transfers between work locations, handling-risk drivers during specimen recovery, limited process checks
before FGR puncture, and characterization bottlenecks associated with gamma counting and microscopy.
For higher-burnup experiments, these constraints became more significant because specimen recovery,

 3

condition assessment, and timely data collection are critical to interpreting FGR, microstructure, and FFRD-
relevant behavior.

To address these constraints, a T2S process was investigated and implemented during FY 2026. In this
report, T2S refers to the integrated sequence used to transition from an irradiated MiniFuel target to
recovered fuel specimen and TM, including target opening, subcapsule recovery, subcapsule leak testing,
FGR measurement, subcapsule opening, specimen visualization, and TM recovery. The goal of the T2S
effort was to improve throughput, reduce handling risk, increase process repeatability, and provide better
quality-control information before irreversible operations such as puncture and disassembly.

In addition to the T2S workflow, PIE data collection improvements were investigated to reduce downstream
bottlenecks after specimen recovery. These improvements included development of an in-cell gamma
counting capability to support measurement of fuel specimens in recovered configurations and development
of microscopy preparation and transfer approaches to support characterization of higher-dose MiniFuel
specimens.

This report documents MiniFuel PIE process improvements investigated and implemented during FY 2026
using the targets available at IFEL. Section 2 describes improvements to the T2S workflow, including target
disassembly, subcapsule leak testing, FGR measurements, and subcapsule disassembly. Section 3 addresses
TM decontamination. Section 4 describes PIE data collection improvements for gamma counting and
microscopy. Together, these efforts support a more standardized and higher-throughput MiniFuel PIE
workflow for timely collection of FGR, microstructural, swelling, and FFRD-relevant data.

 2. T2S IMPROVEMENTS

2.1 TARGET DISASSEMBLY

Target disassembly is the first step of the MiniFuel PIE process and enables recovery of the individual
subcapsules for subsequent leak testing and FGR measurements. In the legacy workflow, the target was
opened using a low-speed saw, and then the subcapsules were recovered from the target tube. This process
was generally successful, but it relied on gravity-assisted removal of the subcapsule stack after target
opening. For some targets, friction or mechanical interference between the subcapsules and target wall
necessitated additional manipulation to complete recovery. As MiniFuel experiments increased in number
and expanded to higher-temperature and higher-burnup conditions, target disassembly was identified as a
process step where improved control and repeatability could reduce handling-risk drivers during specimen
recovery.

To improve target disassembly throughput and reduce process variability, the low-speed saw was moved
from Station 3 to Station 4, where subcapsules are also punctured for FGR measurements. Consolidating
these operations in Station 4 reduced reliance on an extensively used work location and better aligned target
opening, subcapsule recovery, and FGR preparation within the same process area. In addition, a dedicated
target disassembly tool was designed to replace gravity-assisted recovery with controlled axial
displacement of the subcapsule stack.

The target disassembly tool was developed to provide a more repeatable method for removing subcapsules
after the target ends are opened. The tool holds the target horizontally and uses a manipulator-compatible
push rod to advance the subcapsules out of the target tube in a controlled manner. This approach maintains
the order and orientation of the recovered subcapsules while reducing the need for uncontrolled mechanical
agitation during recovery. The disassembly tool is shown in Figure 3, and the principal components are
listed in Table 2.

The target disassembly guidelines, including the use of the new disassembly tool are detailed in a Standard
Operating Guideline (SOG) [9]. In summary, the MiniFuel target is cut on both ends under the endcap
welds. The target is then placed horizontally onto the disassembly tool, clamped between fixtures, and
aligned with the tool’s push rod. The handle is rotated to advance the push rod into the target tube until the
first subcapsule becomes visible at the opposite end of the target. Once the subcapsule is mostly out of the
target tube, it is removed using the manipulator. The operation continues until all subcapsules have been
recovered while maintaining subcapsule order and orientation. Figure 4 illustrates the controlled recovery
sequence, including endcap removal, horizontal placement in the tool, axial subcapsule displacement, and
recovery of the subcapsule stack.

This new disassembly procedure with custom disassembly tool was successfully used to disassemble four
targets: HBU02, HBU03, HBU04, and UCN03. The target disassembly tool is the preferred approach when
the subcapsule stack can be displaced axially after removal of the target ends. However, a contingency
method is required for targets in which subcapsule diameter, target condition, or mechanical interference
prevents controlled axial removal.

One target, UCN01, contained subcapsules with larger outer diameters that could not be dislodged by the
disassembly tool. Therefore, an alternative target disassembly method was developed and implemented
using the existing computer numerical control (CNC) machine in Station 7 [12]. In this approach, the
MiniFuel target was clamped using the fourth-axis rotary unit with an 1/2 in. inner diameter ER-40 collet.
Material was removed longitudinally using a solid carbide endmill with a 3/16 in. diameter and five flutes.
The spindle was operated at 5,000 rpm with a feed rate of 5 in./min, and all passes were executed using a
constant cut depth of 0.01 in. until the subcapsule stack was exposed. The machining operation was repeated
from the opposite end of the target to complete the longitudinal cut. Additional longitudinal cuts were then
performed after rotating the target 180° along its long axis, allowing the target to be opened and the
subcapsules removed.

Together, the target disassembly tool and CNC longitudinal cut method provide a preferred recovery path

2.2 SUBCAPSULE LEAK TESTING

Subcapsule leak testing was added to the MiniFuel T2S workflow during FY 2026 as a process control step
before FGR. The need for this step was identified after several subcapsules, particularly from experiments
irradiated in removable beryllium (RB) positions, showed little or no pressure increase during puncture and
abnormally low measured gas release. These observations indicated that some subcapsules may have lost
hermeticity before puncture. Because FGR measurements depend on the subcapsule retaining its internal
gas inventory until puncture, a leak test step provides important quality control information for interpreting
FGR results and for reducing the likelihood of unnecessary over-puncture during processing.

 7

The leak testing approach was developed to provide a practical screening method compatible with in-cell
T2S operations. Three methods were evaluated and incorporated into the process in order of increasing
sensitivity:

 1. Bubble testing
 2. Subcapsule volume estimation using the FGR table
 3. Pressure transient evaluation using the FGR table
The leak testing methods are documented in a SOG [10]. The first method provides a qualitative indication
of larger leaks. The second provides a semiquantitative assessment of whether the internal subcapsule
volume is in communication with the external test volume. The third was intended to detect slower leaks
that may not be evident from the first two methods.

2.2.1 Bubble Testing

Bubble testing was adapted from established MiniFuel subcapsule preirradiation leak testing practices and
implemented for in-cell use. The method uses a radiation-tolerant vacuum chamber, a low-viscosity test
fluid, and camera observation to identify bubbles emerging from a subcapsule under vacuum. This approach
is consistent with industry-standard bubble emission leak testing methods described in ASTM E515 and
ASTM E493, which provide guidance on qualitative leak detection using liquid immersion and vacuum
techniques. For this application, a continuous stream of bubbles from a discrete location is interpreted as
evidence of a leak, and isolated bubbles or bubbles from trapped air locations are treated more cautiously.

The method was first evaluated using six dummy subcapsules. These dummy subcapsules did not contain
fuel specimens or fuel dishes but were otherwise representative of MiniFuel subcapsules. One dummy
subcapsule was fabricated with an intentional seal omission to provide a known failed condition. The
remaining dummy subcapsules were processed through the standard welding and examination sequence.
Bubble testing correctly identified the intentionally failed subcapsule and several additional dummy
subcapsules that showed evidence of incomplete or degraded sealing. Subsequent volume testing was
consistent with the bubble test results for the failed dummy subcapsules.

2.2.2 Volume Testing

Volume testing was implemented using the MiniFuel FGR system with the addition of valve V8 near the
pressure transducer, as shown in Figure 6. This modification allows the transducer and known standard
volume to be isolated from the in-cell puncture head. The same modification also supports pressure transient
evaluation. The FGR system schematic, modified for leak testing, is adapted from the MiniFuel gas
puncture and 85Kr collection procedure [11].

 8

 Figure 6. MiniFuel fission gas release system, as modified for leak testing. Schematic adapted from literature
 [11] by adding valve V8.

The volume-based method compares the expected sealed subcapsule volume with the apparent volume
measured when the subcapsule is loaded into the puncture system. The apparent volume is determined using
pressure–volume relationships by expanding a known reference volume into the system and measuring the
resulting pressure change, allowing the effective system volume to be calculated. If a subcapsule is sealed,
then the internal gas volume is not in communication with the FGR table volume, and the measured volume
remains near the expected external displacement volume. If a subcapsule is leaking, the internal subcapsule
volume communicates with the test system and appears as additional measured volume. The calculation
sequence, variable definitions, and suggested interpretation thresholds for the volume-based method are
provided in the corresponding SOG [10].

For the tested subcapsules, confirmed sealed cases generally produced volume excess values in the range
of approximately −0.1 to 0.1 cm3. Values between approximately 0.1 and 0.15 cm3 were treated as
potentially indicative of leakage, and subcapsules ultimately identified as leaking generally showed higher
volume excess values. Because the method depends on expected geometry and pressure measurements, it
should be treated as semiquantitative and interpreted together with bubble test observations.

2.2.3 Transient Pressure Evaluation

Pressure transient evaluation was included to address the possibility of very slow leaks that might not be
clearly identified by bubble testing or by the volume-based method. In this test, either the puncture-side
volume or table-side volume is pressurized while the other side is evacuated. After an equilibration period,
valve V8 is opened and pressure is monitored over several minutes. A gradual pressure change after the
valve is opened would indicate a slow leak between the subcapsule interior and the test system.

 9

No useful results were obtained from the pressure transient method during the FY 2026 testing summarized
in this report. The leaks that were identified appeared to communicate quickly enough that they were
already evident from bubble testing or volume testing. The method remains a potential supplemental
diagnostic for cases where slower leak behavior is suspected, but it was not the primary contributor to leak
identification in the present work.

The FY 2026 results also suggest that a meaningful fraction of RB-irradiated subcapsules exhibited
evidence consistent with loss of hermeticity before puncture. Potential contributors include the more
aggressive irradiation conditions associated with RB positions, subcapsule weld performance under those
conditions, and mechanical effects associated with postirradiation handling and disassembly. The available
observations indicate that many of the leaking subcapsules showed evidence of leakage near weld locations;
additional evaluation is needed to determine the root cause(s).

Based on these results, future MiniFuel T2S processing should continue to use bubble testing and
subcapsule volume estimation before FGR puncture. The leak test results provide a process control basis
for deciding whether to proceed with puncture, interpreting abnormally low FGR measurements, and
reducing the risk of over-puncture when a failed subcapsule is unlikely to show a pressure increase. In this
role, leak testing is a diagnostic activity as well as a quality-control step that improves the robustness and
interpretability of the MiniFuel T2S workflow.

2.3 FISSION GAS RELEASE MEASUREMENTS

FGR measurements are a key PIE data product for MiniFuel experiments and are a central step in the T2S
workflow. As the number of MiniFuel targets available for PIE increased during FY 2026, FGR puncture
throughput became an important constraint on downstream specimen recovery and characterization.
Therefore, improvements to the FGR process were implemented to increase process reliability, reduce
schedule sensitivity, and better integrate FGR measurements with the new leak testing step described in
Section 2.2.

Four areas were addressed during FY 2026: increasing cold trap inventory, establishing preventive
maintenance for puncture rig consumables, updating the operating procedure to reflect current best
practices, and developing a revised puncture rig concept that would orient the subcapsule to reduce the
likelihood of material loss if the top of the subcapsule is compromised during puncture.

 12

FGR measurements are performed by puncturing the subcapsule and sweeping released gas through cold
traps that capture fission gas for subsequent gamma counting. Two traps are plumbed in series: the first trap
is intended to capture the released fission gas, and the second trap serves as a backup (Figure 6). At the start
of FY 2026, only three cold traps were available, limiting the number of punctures that could be performed
before traps had to be regenerated. Five additional cold traps were added to the inventory. This addition
increased available trap capacity and reduced the potential for puncture delays associated with trap
availability.

Process reliability was also improved by evaluating puncture rig consumables and establishing a preventive
maintenance approach. Figure 9 shows the consumable components in the puncture assembly. During early
FY 2026 operations, additional troubleshooting was needed to address small system leaks. The O-ring
under the lid was identified as a recurring maintenance item, and replacement after approximately 12
punctures was adopted to reduce the likelihood that seal degradation would interrupt the process. Following
puncture of HBU01-5, the internal size 010 O-ring was also identified as failed. Because this component
could not be replaced in-cell, replacement of the puncture rig was required. This replacement provided an
opportunity to increase the thickness of the lid O-ring, improving operational tolerance for in-cell use. The
puncture pin remains under evaluation to determine an appropriate replacement interval. FY 2026 operating
experience was also incorporated into the MiniFuel gas puncture operating procedure, Mini-Fuel-GP-001
[11]. Updates included abnormal-condition guidance for puncture events involving subcapsule-top
shearing, lack of pressure response, or other conditions that could increase the risk of over-puncture or
specimen displacement.

Figure 9. Cross section of the puncture assembly, highlighting the items that are consumable in the puncture

The FGR process was also evaluated from the perspective of risks due to handling. In the current puncture
configuration, the subcapsule is oriented with the neck end downward, where “neck end” refers to the
smaller-diameter end of the subcapsule shown in Figure 10. Several HBU01 subcapsules experienced
shearing near the neck end during puncture, and no pressure increase was observed. This condition created
the potential for internal components, including TM and fuel specimens, to be displaced during the puncture
operation. Therefore, a revised puncture rig concept was developed in which the neck end would be oriented

 13

upward during puncture, as shown in Figure 10. This revised orientation is intended to retain internal
components more effectively if the subcapsule is compromised during puncture. The revised design had
been quoted but had not been installed in-cell at the time of this report.

The FGR results should be interpreted together with puncture response and leak test information (Table 3).

The HBU01-1 and HBU01-2 subcapsules were punctured before the in-cell leak testing process was

effects.

for FC04 are consistent with that expectation. FGR results for FC03 were not finalized at the time of this
report because the as-built, as-irradiated calculation required to estimate total 85Kr inventory had not yet
been completed.

Overall, the FY 2026 FGR work improved the reliability and throughput of the MiniFuel T2S workflow
while demonstrating the value of coupling FGR puncture with prepuncture leak testing. The process
improvements reduced sensitivity to cold trap availability, established a maintenance basis for puncture rig
consumables, and identified design changes that could further reduce handling risk during puncture. Most
importantly, integrating leak test information into FGR interpretation provides a clearer basis for
distinguishing fuel-related gas release behavior from artifacts associated with subcapsule loss of hermeticity
before puncture.

2.4 SUBCAPSULE DISASSEMBLY

Subcapsule disassembly is the final mechanical recovery step in the MiniFuel T2S workflow. This step
provides access to the internal filler, passive TM, and fuel specimen after FGR measurements are complete.
The objective of the improved subcapsule disassembly process is to controllably open the subcapsule,
recover the TM, and visually assess the fuel specimen while minimizing additional handling and limiting
the potential for recovery-induced damage.

In the legacy workflow, MiniFuel subcapsules were opened using a low-speed saw to cut the top of the
subcapsule below the FGR puncture location. This approach was generally successful, but it introduced
several process limitations. The cut location was sensitive to the puncture geometry and burr formation,
and incomplete burr removal could prevent extraction of the internal components. When this occurred, the
subcapsule required additional processing before disassembly could proceed. The legacy approach also
required movement between work locations; such movement increased process time and introduced
additional handling steps. In some cases, interaction between the saw blade and internal filler could rotate
the filler inside the holder, creating a potential mechanical load path to the fuel specimen or TM.

To improve process repeatability and reduce handling risk, a CNC subcapsule disassembly method was
developed using the existing CNC machining capability in Station 7 [12]. A dedicated fixture was designed
to hold the subcapsule in an upright orientation while the top of the subcapsule is machined away. This
configuration allows controlled removal of material above the subcapsule shoulder, exposing the filler
without relying on a saw cut through the punctured region. The method is documented in the associated
SOG [13].

In the CNC disassembly process, the subcapsule is secured in the fixture and aligned with the end mill using
an ER-20 collet with a 10 mm inner diameter. Material was removed using a solid carbide endmill with a
5/64 in. diameter and four flutes. The spindle was operated at 5,000 rpm with a feed rate of 5 in./min, and
all passes were executed using a constant infeed of approximately 0.005 in. The CNC end mill was
programmed to machine the top of the subcapsule from its outside diameter to its inside diameter, removing
all the material above the subcapsule shoulder and revealing the top of the internal filler tube.

The filler can then be removed using manipulators or tweezers, allowing the TM to be recovered and
packaged for transfer to the Low-Activation Materials Development and Analysis (LAMDA) laboratory.
With the filler removed, the fuel specimen can be visually assessed in place at the bottom of the opened
subcapsule to document whether it appears intact, cracked, fragmented, or powdered. After this visual
assessment, the filler can be returned to the opened subcapsule, and the assembly can be transferred to a
storage can with an insert that maintains the fuel/filler configuration until subsequent disassembly or
characterization. Figure 11 shows representative steps in the CNC subcapsule disassembly process,
including subcapsule placement, fixture alignment, machining, filler removal, fuel specimen visualization,
and TM recovery.

Overall, the CNC subcapsule disassembly method improved the robustness of the T2S workflow by

 3. TM DECONTAMINATION

Passive SiC TM recovered from MiniFuel subcapsules is used to confirm irradiation temperature by
postirradiation dilatometry [4][5]. To complete this step, recovered TM must be decontaminated
sufficiently to permit transfer from IFEL to LAMDA. During FY 2026, a decontamination campaign was
performed on TM recovered from the RB02 MiniFuel target to reduce dose rates and removable
contamination to levels suitable for transfer.

Initial surveys of the recovered RB02 TM indicated removable alpha contamination greater than
2,000 dpm/100 cm2 and dose rates greater than 100 mrem/h at 30 cm. These values exceeded transfer limits
and required additional decontamination before the TM could be released from the hot cell. In addition,
beta contamination greater than 106 dpm/100 cm2 prevented loadout from the hot cell for further out-of-cell
decontamination. Two HNO3-based methods were evaluated: hot HNO3 Soxhlet extraction and room-
temperature ultrasonic cleaning in concentrated HNO3.

Hot HNO3 Soxhlet extraction had previously been applied successfully to RB01 TM and was treated as the
preferred decontamination approach for highly contaminated TMs. In this method, the TM is placed in a
custom quartz thimble and exposed to two consecutive 24 h reflux cycles in concentrated HNO3, with the
acid replaced between cycles. Following extraction, the samples are rinsed with deionized water and
methanol and allowed to dry before transfer. The effectiveness of this method is attributed to increased
contaminant solubility under boiling HNO3 reflux conditions.

 18

Because the Soxhlet apparatus was unavailable during the RB02 campaign, room-temperature ultrasonic
cleaning in concentrated HNO3 was used as an initial alternative. TM samples RB02-01, RB02-03, RB02-
04, and RB02-06 were immersed in concentrated HNO3 and ultrasonically agitated, followed by water and
methanol rinses. This initial treatment reduced contamination sufficiently for transfer of RB02-01, but it
was not sufficient for the remaining three samples.

A second decontamination campaign was then performed on RB02-03, RB02-04, and RB02-06. These
samples underwent additional extended HNO3 treatments, including overnight soaking and ultrasonic
agitation in fresh acid, followed by water and methanol rinses. After these additional cycles, all remaining
RB02 TM samples met the requirements for transfer out of the hot cells for further out-of-cell
decontamination. Final in-cell dose rates and removable contamination levels are summarized in Table 6.

The RB02 results confirmed that HNO3-based decontamination can enable transfer of MiniFuel TM for

 4. PIE DATA COLLECTION IMPROVEMENTS

4.1 GAMMA COUNTING SYSTEM

Gamma counting is an important PIE measurement for MiniFuel fuel specimens because it provides
radionuclide inventory information that supports interpretation of irradiation history, fission product
retention, and specimen condition. Historically, MiniFuel gamma counting was performed using the
Irradiated Microsphere Gamma Analyzer (IMGA) after fuel specimens were recovered from opened
subcapsules and transferred into small glass vials. This approach provided useful data for earlier MiniFuel
campaigns, but it introduced two limitations for higher-throughput PIE. First, competing demands for
IMGA access could delay data collection. Second, the requirement to remove and transfer fuel specimens

 19

before counting increased handling needs, which is undesirable for small kernels, TRISO particles, broken
disks, or high-dose specimens.

To reduce this bottleneck, a new MiniFuel gamma counting capability was developed for use in the east
hot cell at IFEL. The system uses the existing gamma collimator installed through the east hot-cell wall and
adds a dedicated in-cell mechanical positioning fixture for MiniFuel specimens. The fixture is designed to
hold a can containing an opened MiniFuel subcapsule and recovered fuel specimen in a repeatable position
relative to the collimator. This configuration allows gamma spectra to be collected from the fuel specimen
in the recovered configuration after CNC subcapsule opening without requiring immediate transfer to
IMGA or additional specimen handling.

The existing collimator provides a line of sight from the hot cell to an out-of-cell detector through a sapphire
window. Figure 12 shows the in-cell end of the collimator system, including the sapphire window. The
MiniFuel positioning fixture was designed to mount on the in-cell portion of the collimator and provide
controlled sample positioning while maintaining a clear gamma-ray path to the detector.

 Figure 12. A view of the sapphire window at the end of the shield plug in IFEL’s east hot cell.

The mechanical positioning fixture provides both vertical and horizontal adjustment of the sample can
relative to the collimator. Vertical adjustment is provided through a slotted feature, and horizontal
adjustment is provided by a manual linear slide. The aluminum sample stage is configured so that no
additional material is placed between the sample can and the sapphire window, minimizing additional
attenuation. When the system is not in use, the sample stage can be rotated away from the collimator line
of sight, allowing the fixture to remain installed and reducing setup time for future measurements. The
positioning system was installed in-cell in early June 2026. Figure 13 shows the fixture design and final
mounted configuration.

4.2 MICROSCOPY

Microscopy provides essential PIE information for MiniFuel specimens such as microstructural condition,
irradiation-induced phase evolution, cracking, swelling, and fuel-specific degradation features. As
MiniFuel campaigns expand to higher-burnup and higher-activity specimens, microscopy throughput is
constrained by two linked challenges: (1) transferring higher-dose specimens to microscopy instruments
within radiological work controls and (2) preparing small fuel specimens in a repeatable configuration
suitable for advanced electron microscopy. During FY 2026, work focused on both challenges by
developing a microscopy transfer system concept and implementing improved specimen preparation
fixtures for MiniFuel samples.

4.2.1 Microscope Transfer System

The first MiniFuel specimens irradiated in HFIR RB positions were queued for advanced microscopy
during FY 2026. These specimens were irradiated at higher temperature and burnup than previous MiniFuel
experiments conducted in VXF positions. During attempted loading of these specimens into the Scios 2
DualBeam focused ion beam/scanning electron microscopy (FIB/SEM) instrument, radiological conditions
limited benchtop handling and instrument loading. Most specimens exceeded the allowable dose-rate or
removable-contamination limits established for the applicable radiological work permit, preventing routine
microscopy analysis. As a result, only 1 of the 10 disks from the campaign was successfully characterized,
limiting the ability to evaluate microstructure and irradiation response for that specimen set.

 23

To address this limitation, a removable transfer system concept was developed for the Scios 2 FIB/SEM
instrument. The system is intended to increase the allowable envelope for specimen exchange by providing
a local enclosed volume around the microscope loading door. The enclosure would be maintained under
negative pressure using a portable HEPA-filtered system, reducing the likelihood that removable
contamination could migrate out of the exchange area during loading. This concept supports higher-dose
MiniFuel microscopy by improving contamination control during sample exchange rather than relying
solely on lower specimen activity or more restrictive manual handling.

The proposed system consists of a removable welded acrylic enclosure with sealing surfaces at the
microscope interface, a duct connection to a portable HEPA system, and a removable inlet cover for standby
configuration. The HEPA system would be located above the instrument and connected by a short duct
path. Figure 16 shows the proposed transfer system concept in standby and operating configurations.

  Figure 16. Simplified CAD model of the transfer system around the microscope chamber. (left) In standby
 mode and (right) operation modes.

The transfer system was sized using a target average inlet velocity of 80 ft/min through the enclosure
opening, consistent with standard laboratory hood design practice. Required volumetric flow rate and static
pressure were estimated based on enclosure geometry and duct characteristics, including diameter, length,
and expected friction losses. These estimates were used to confirm that a suitable portable HEPA system
could provide the necessary flow and pressure performance while allowing operational adjustment.

Radiation Protection Services supported selection of a portable HEPA system capable of maintaining a
controlled negative pressure environment within the enclosure. The selected system provides sufficient
static pressure capacity to accommodate expected losses associated with the enclosure and duct
configuration and allows adjustment to support varying sample conditions and radiological control
requirements.

The transfer system design has been reviewed with the hot-cell facility and represents a viable
implementation path for improving microscopy access for higher-dose MiniFuel specimens. Although not
yet installed, the system is expected to enhance contamination control during sample exchange and expand
the range of specimens that can be handled within approved radiological limits.

4.2.2 Specimen Preparation for Microscopy Analysis

Microstructural characterization of MiniFuel specimens requires preparation of small fuel samples in a
stable, polished configuration suitable for SEM and related analytical techniques. MiniFuel disks are
significantly smaller than conventional fuel specimens and are often processed in relatively large batches

 24

during hot-cell operations. In addition, specimens recovered from irradiation may be thin, fragile, cracked,
fragmented, or highly radioactive. These factors make conventional metallographic preparation approaches
inefficient or impractical; standard mounts are oversized for MiniFuel geometries and do not readily support
high-throughput, remotely handled preparation. Therefore, preparation methods must support repeatable
mounting, controlled material removal, efficient batch processing, and compatibility with hot-cell
equipment and microscope loading constraints.

To address these challenges and improve preparation throughput and repeatability, two custom fixtures
were developed: the POL5001 MiniFuel specimen holder and the POL5002 polishing clamp. The POL5001
holder captures the MiniFuel disk in a small well for epoxy mounting and includes depth indicators
corresponding to 1 mm, 2 mm, and 3 mm polishing depths. Its reduced size, compared with a standard
metallographic mount, supports later SEM loading where microscope chamber clearances are limited and
minimizes excess mounting material that could interfere with imaging. The POL5002 polishing clamp holds
the POL5001 holder during preparation on the larger hot-cell auto-polisher, allowing multiple MiniFuel
specimens to be processed more efficiently using equipment designed for larger mounts while maintaining
consistent alignment and applied force. Figure 17 shows the holder and polishing clamp.

The resulting surrogate specimens were inspected by optical microscopy, confirming that the revised
polishing conditions produced an acceptable 0.5 µm finish for the tested geometries. Figure 21 shows
optical micrographs of the polished surrogate specimens.

The fixture-development and surrogate-polishing results demonstrate a practical preparation pathway for
MiniFuel microscopy specimens. The POL5001 holder, POL5002 polishing clamp, and

 27

assembly/disassembly fixture support hot-cell-compatible specimen mounting and polishing while
reducing reliance on one-at-a-time preparation of small mounts. Together with the proposed microscope
transfer system, these improvements are expected to support a more complete MiniFuel PIE data collection
workflow by reducing barriers between specimen recovery, sample preparation, and advanced microscopy
characterization.

 5. CONCLUSIONS

The work completed during FY 2026 improved the MiniFuel PIE workflow from target receipt through
fuel specimen recovery and selected data-collection steps. The T2S process was developed to reduce
unnecessary transfers between work locations, improve repeatability of recovery operations, and provide
additional process control information before irreversible steps such as FGR puncture and subcapsule
opening.

The revised T2S workflow consolidated target cutting, subcapsule recovery, leak testing, and FGR
measurements in Station 4; subcapsule opening, fuel specimen visualization, and TM recovery were
performed in Station 7. This arrangement reduced the number of facility transfers required during the
mechanical recovery sequence and provided a clearer process path from irradiated target to recovered fuel
specimen and TM.

The T2S process, as illustrated in Figure 22, was implemented on multiple targets and subcapsules at IFEL
during FY 2026. All targets present at IFEL at the beginning of FY 2026 were disassembled. Despite delays
associated with FGR puncture rig replacement and development of new process steps, approximately 50%
of the subcapsules at IFEL were punctured during the FY. Approximately 75% of the punctured subcapsules
were subsequently disassembled, with TM recovered and the fuel specimen visualized. A detailed status
summary for the FY 2026 target and subcapsule inventory is provided at the end of this section in Table 8.

 Figure 22. T2S process workflow.

Several specific process improvements were implemented or demonstrated:

 • A target disassembly tool was designed, fabricated, and installed in the hot cell to recover
 subcapsules by controlled axial displacement after target opening. The tool was successfully used
 for HBU02, HBU03, HBU04, and UCN03 targets. A CNC-based target disassembly method was

 28

 also developed as a contingency approach for targets in which axial subcapsule removal was not
 feasible.

 • Subcapsule leak testing was added to the T2S workflow as a process control step before FGR
 puncture. Bubble testing and volume-based leak testing provided information on subcapsule
 hermeticity and helped interpret cases with little or no pressure increase during puncture. The leak
 test results were generally consistent with puncture response and FGR behavior for cases where all
 information was available.

 • FGR process reliability was improved by increasing cold-trap inventory, identifying puncture rig
 consumables requiring preventive maintenance, updating operating guidance, and developing a
 revised puncture rig concept intended to reduce the potential for material displacement during
 puncture. The FY 2026 FGR results also showed the importance of interpreting measured gas
 release together with leak test information and puncture response.

 • CNC subcapsule disassembly was implemented to provide controlled access to the filler, TM, and
 fuel specimen after puncture. This method reduced dependence on the prior saw-based opening
 process and allowed fuel specimen condition to be documented before additional handling.

TM decontamination methods were evaluated for RB02 TM. Transfer of recovered TM for postirradiation
temperature confirmation was enabled by HNO3-based decontamination. Hot HNO3 Soxhlet extraction
remains the preferred method for highly contaminated TM, but room-temperature ultrasonic cleaning in
concentrated HNO3 can be used as an alternative when additional treatment cycles are acceptable.

PIE data collection improvements were also developed to reduce downstream bottlenecks after specimen
recovery. A MiniFuel gamma counting fixture was designed, fabricated, and installed in IFEL’s east cell
using the existing collimator. Future work includes measurements on a calibrated source and MiniFuel
specimens.

Microscopy workflow improvements were pursued through development of a removable transfer system
concept for the Scios 2 FIB/SEM instrument and through implementation of custom specimen-preparation
fixtures. The transfer system concept has not yet been installed, but it provides a path for improving
contamination control during microscope sample exchange for higher-dose MiniFuel specimens. The
POL5001 specimen holder, POL5002 polishing clamp, and associated assembly/disassembly fixture were
tested using surrogate specimens and supported in-cell polishing to a 0.5 µm finish.

Overall, the FY 2026 work improved the MiniFuel PIE process by establishing a more repeatable T2S
workflow, adding leak testing before FGR puncture, improving FGR process reliability, implementing
controlled subcapsule opening, and developing additional data collection capabilities for gamma counting
and microscopy preparation. Remaining work includes completion of FGR measurements and subcapsule
disassembly for the remaining inventory, additional validation of the gamma counting system and
implementation on MiniFuel specimens, implementation and testing of the microscopy transfer system, and
continued evaluation of subcapsule hermeticity loss observed in several RB-irradiated experiments.
