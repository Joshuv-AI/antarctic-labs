THE CAUSE OF INCREASED ELECTRICAL
EXCITABILITY.
By N. MORRIS, M.D., B.Sc., F.R.F.P.S.G.,
Professor of Physiology, The Anderson College of Medicine; Extra
Dispensary Physician, Royal Hospital for Sick Children.
It has long been known that in conditions of tetany, whether
idiopathic or gastric, there is a distinct increase in nervous
and muscular excitability. The facial phenomenon is
pathognomonic of latent tetany, and since Erb's classical
work in 1874 the degree of hyperexcitability of the peripheral
nerves has been taken as an index of the presence and severity
of the condition, and also of the value of therapeutic measures
adopted. It is the purpose of this paper to indicate the
underlying cause of this hyperexcitability in the neuro-
muscular apparatus. That this cause acts on the peripheral
mechanism and not through the central nervous system has
been clearly shown b/ the work of MacCallum.1 He circulated
the blood of a dog suffering from tetany through the blood-
vessels of the amputated leg of a healthy animal, and found
a marked increase in the electrical excitability (E.E.) of that
leg. It has also been demonstrated that this increase in the
E.E. persists even after the peripheral part has been separated
from the central nervous system by section of the nerves.
The peripheral neuro-muscular apparatus or neuromyone
can be considered as consisting of nerve-fibre, nerve-ending,
receptive substance, and muscle substance. In this investigation
no endeavour has been made to determine the part played
by each component, but the evidence leads one to think that
the receptive substance situated between the nerve-ending
and muscle-tissue proper is the part, changes in which lead
to variations in excitability.

A full description of the methods employed has been given
by Noel Paton, Findlay, and Watson,2 and Morris,3 but a
few general remarks may be made. In health, the cathodal
contraction (K.C.C.) is much the strongest, the cathodal opening
(K.O.C.) one being the weakest, while the anodal contraction
(A.C.C. and A.O.C.) are intermediate. In tetany the most
marked change is noticed with the K.O.C., and this increase
in excitability of the cathodal opening stimulus is the index
commonly used. In passing, it may be mentioned that some
investigators emphasised qualitative changes in tetany, the
anodal contractions becoming stronger than even the cathodal
closing one. This qualitative change cannot meanwhile be
considered of any diagnostic value, owing to the irregularity
with which it occurs. It is therefore safe to take the. K.O.C.
as a clear indication of the degree of electrical excitability
of the neuromyone.
It will be well now to consider the various theories that
have been advanced to explain this condition of electrical
hyperexcitability; of these, the principal are the following:?
A. The presence of a toxic substance.
B. The presence in the blood of relative excess of sodium.
G. Decrease in the calcium content.
D. A condition of acidosis.
E. A condition of alkalosis.
A. Presence of a toxic substance.?Of the number suggested
one may mention ammonia, ergamine, and guanidin. Any one
of these substances may produce an increase in the E.E., but it
has been clearly shown that hyperexcitability can be experi-
mentally produced without causing an increase in the organism
of any of these bodies. Chvostek4 has described a case of
a tetany attack following tuberculin injection, and he considered
that tuberculin?like toxaemia, pregnancy, and other conditions
?is simply an exciting factor in an organism predisposed
to tetany. It may therefore be concluded that increased E.E.
in presence of a toxic substance is due not to the specific poison,

but to some deeper disturbance of metabolism which may be
excited by that poison.
B. Presence of excess of sodium.?Administration of excess of
sodium salts has long been upheld as a cause of tetany seizures.
Campbell5 has reported a case of tetany following a rectal
injection of saline. There must have been some marked
predisposition to tetany, for saline is frequently administered
;per rectum in the prevention and inhibition of convulsive
seizures, caused probably by toxic absorption from the bowels.
Miss Henderson, in the course of some work (unpublished),
found that administration of common salt to children had
no influence on the electrical reactions. Several experiments
were performed on cats; isotonic solutions of sodium chloride
were injected, but the E.E. remained unchanged. There was in
each case a marked rise in temperature, varying from 1 to 3
degrees Fahr., confirming previous observations. This pyrexia
indicates a disturbance of metabolism, and in this way it is
possible that the administration of sodium salts to children
with the spasmophilic diathesis would be sufficient to excite
a convulsion.
C. Diminution of calcium.?Much of the evidence bearing
on the influence of calcium on neuromyal excitability is
contradictory, and as Noel Paton, Findlay, and Watson2
remark, " There is no conclusive evidence as regards the
relationship of calcium to the condition of tetany." Some of
the more recent researches merit attention. Howland and
Marriott6 made analyses of the blood in cases of idiopathic
tetany in children, and found a distinct diminution in the
calcium content of the blood; they obtained benefit by calcium
therapy. As cases of tetany very often recover without any
specific treatment, it is difficult to decide how much the
administration of calcium really influenced the recovery. As
regards the deficient calcium content of the blood, several later
investigators?Harrop,7 Goldman and Grant,8 and others?have
failed to detect any marked deviation from the normal.
To throw some light on the influence of calcium on the E.E.,
calcium was injected intravenously into animals, with the result

that the E.E. was invariably lowered. Calcium has therefore
a sedative effect on neuromyal excitability. That the converse
does not also hold good is demonstrated by a series of analyses
before and after experimental alteration of the E.E. In all
cases, no matter by what method the E.E. was altered, the
calcium content of the blood remained unchanged. There still
remains the possibility that although calcium is present in
normal amounts it is in great part inactive?for example,
precipitated as very minute particles held in suspension by the
colloids of the serum. This point still requires investigation,
but, as will be shown later in the paper, the aid of this
speculation does not need to be invoked to explain the
hyperexcitability. In conclusion, it may be stated that any
change in the calcium content of the serum will probably alter
the excitability of an organism with the " diathesis spasmophile,"
but that, of itself, decreased calcium is not the cause of
hyperexcitability.
D. A condition of acidosis.?Elias9 stated that general
hyperexcitability of the peripheral nervous system followed
on injections of relatively small amounts of acid. Far from
this result being confirmed, it has been shown by numerous
workers?Wilson, Stearns, and Janney,10 and others?that the
symptoms of tetany are relieved by injection of acid. It is
true that Togawa11 found a condition of acidosis in dogs
suffering with experimental tetany. Later work?Hastings
and Murray12?has not confirmed this. It must be remembered
that it is very difficult to obtain blood during the actual tetany
attack, as this soon passes off', leaving the animal less excitable
than normal. Several experiments were done to study the
effect of acids, and all of them went to prove that acid had
a marked lowering effect on the E.E. of the neuromyone.
E. Presence of alkalosis.?L. Blum,13 in 1913, reported in
a patient suffering with diabetic coma the occurrence of
convulsions following the administration of sodium bicarbonate,
and ceasing when the bicarbonate was stopped. This observa-
tion has, since then, been confirmed, both clinically and
experimentally, indicating that an alkalosis or increase in the

alkali content of the blood led to an increased excitability
of the nervous system. That this is accompanied by an
increased E.E. of the neuromyone was shown by the intravenous
administration of alkalis to animals, all of which gave
indications of a rise in the E.E., unless that excitability was
previously high.
Apart from the increase in E.E., administration of alkalis
has the following definite effects:?
1. The alkaline reserve is increased and the hydrogen-ion
concentration is diminished.
2. The supply of oxygen to the tissues is decreased.
The change in the alkaline reserve and hydrogen-ion
concentration is taken by many workers as the ultimate cause
of hyperexcitability. M. H. Grant,14 working on the isolated
nerve-muscle preparation of the frog, showed that the only
hydrogen-ion concentration capable of producing increased E.E.
was one markedly lower than what is found in the mammalian
organism suffering with tetany. It is difficult to know how
far experiments on isolated organs, such as the frog's gastroc-
nemius, are applicable to the organism as a whole, but in
this case other experiments on the neuromyone in situ tend to
support the view deduced by Miss Grant from her experiments,
viz., that the increase in the alkali content of the blood (or
decrease in the hydrogen-ion concentration) is not the chief
factor in the production of increased E.E. It was found that
hyperexcitability could be produced, accompanied by no change
in the hydrogen-ion concentration, and even with a decrease
in the alkali reserve.
There remains the second effect of alkali administration to
be considered, viz., the reduced supply of oxygen to the
tissues. Hill and Flack15 noted that during hyperpnoea there
frequently occurred spastic condition of the muscles and
twitchings of the facial muscles (suggestive of the facial
phenomenon in clinical tetany). Their polygraph tracings
show that the radial pulse became weak, and at times
disappeared with each inspiration; they concluded that the
forced ventilation by its mechanical interference with the
circulation lessened the oxygenation. The most interesting
point about this work is that when forcible breathing of

that the oxygen supply to the tissues is of great importance
in determining the excitability of neuro-muscular tissue.
The causes of anoxaemia, or defective oxygen supply, have
been classified by Haldane16 as follows
(a) Defective oxygen saturation of arterial blood.
(b) Slowing of the circulation, so that an excessive proportion
of oxygen is used up in systemic capillaries.
(c) Defective haemoglobin content.
(d) Increased difficulty in the dissociation of the oxyhaemo-
globin.
(a) Defective oxygen saturation of the arterial blood was
produced experimentally by the induction of asphyxia. The
oxygen supply to the tissues was determined by analysis of
the arterial and venous bloods. In every case in which the
" head of oxygen " (i.e., difference between the oxygen contents
of arterial and, venous bloods) was markedly lowered, there was
a very substantial increase in electrical excitability. In the
early stages of asphyxia there was practically no alteration in
the E.E., but it has been shown by Morris17 that the head of
oxygen undergoes practically no decrease until there is a
marked degree of asphyxia.
Histamine has been shown to have no effect when applied
direct to the skeletal muscles, but a marked stimulating action
on the musculature of the bronchioles and pulmonary arterioles.
Accordingly, a rapid decrease in oxygen intake is caused.
This was found to be accompanied by a fall in the head
of the oxygen and an increased E.E. of the neuromyone.
(b) Slowing of the circulation was produced by lowering
the temperature, and here also the resultant diminution in
the oxygen supply was accompanied by hyperexcitability
of the neuromyone.
(c) Experimental anaemia results in a fall of the total
haemoglobin content for a considerable time, although the total
volume of blood soon becomes normal. In every case the
E.E. was increased by the production of an anaemia, and there
also occurred general tremors of greater or less intensity.

(d) It has long been known that administration of sublethal
doses of cyanides depresses oxidation processes. Thus, the
colour of the blood throughout both arteries and veins remains
typically arterial owing to the oxyhemoglobin not being
dissociated. The result is that although the blood as it leaves
the lungs has its normal amount of oxygen, yet the tissues
are unable to acquire any. The action of cyanide is probably
of the nature of an inhibition of peroxidase. Thus, Cushny18
mentions that the presence of cyanide prevents the splitting
up of hydrogen peroxide by normal blood as occurs when
blood and peroxide are mixed, as in the guaiac and ozonic
ether reaction. The effect of cyanide administration on the E.E.
of the neuromyone was immediate, leading to a very marked
increase of excitability. The increase depended on the relative
amount of cyanide injected, but was always accompanied by
general convulsions, indicating the profound stimulating effect
of an acute anoxaemia on the motor centres in the brain
and cord.
Further support to the view that defective supply of oxygen
is the underlying cause of increased excitability is given by
a study of the effects of alcohol. When it is administered
so as to produce concentration of alcohol in the blood exceeding
0*7 per cent there occurs an increase of excitability, together
with a diminished supply of oxygen to the tissues, as indicated
by the head of oxygen.
The evidence adduced indicates quite clearly (1) that any
method whereby an anoxaemia is produced also leads to an
increase in the E.E. of the neuromyone; and (2) that any
increase in the E.E. is acccompanied by a diminished supply
of oxygen to the tissues.
Clinically, we find that increased E.E. may occur in other
conditions than idiopathic tetany. The following are brief
notes of three cases:?
Case 1.?Baby McM., male, set. 19 months; normal birth.
Marked signs of rickets. No evidence or history of tetany.
Has had whooping-cough during past three weeks. Examination
of chest revealed signs of broncho-pneumonia. Electrical
reactions K.O.C., 3*0. (Normal K.O.C. for children is above
5*0.) Died six days later of broncho-pneumonia.

These cases are suggestive as illustrating the association of
respiratory trouble with increased electrical excitability. It is
a common clinical experience to find an acute respiratory
disorder ushered in by a convulsion indicating a general hyper-
excitability. Still19 notes the readiness with which pulmonary
collapse occurs as a result of broncho-pneumonia and bronchitis
in infancy and early childhood, and he further remarks on the
liability to collapse as a result of such conditions as coryza and
hypertrophied tonsils and adenoids. Pulmonary collapse leads
to a marked decrease in the oxygen saturation of the arterial
blood (Morris17), and consequently to a diminished supply of
oxygen to the tissues. . _ . .
Bassett-Smith20 records a case of sprue complicated by
definite tetany. Notes of the case show that there was
a marked anaemia, the red cells being reduced to less than one
and a half million per cm. Oxygen was administered by
inhalation and ether given "per rectum with the result that the
tetany symptoms subsided. Bassett-Smith gives the ether the
credit, but one wonders whether the oxygen was not also of
use. Still19 mentions the administration of oxygen as one of
the methods of treatment recommended in tetany attacks, but
makes no comment on its usefulness. An emergency treatment
in common use by the lay public is the immersion of the patient
up to the neck in a hot bath. Undoubtedly this has in many
cases a sedative effect, and it leads one to consider whether its
value lies in the stimulation of the circulation and increase
of oxygen supply to the tissues that is naturally induced by
a hot bath.

Ferguson,21 in a recent study of social and economic factors in
the causation of rickets, showed quite conclusively (1) that
tetany is closely associated with rickets, and (2) that inadequate

Recently one had the opportunity of investigating, by means
of Leonard Hill's " katathermometer," the conditions of ventila-
tion in the houses of the East End of Glasgow, in those districts
where tetany and rickets are most prevalent. Hill's work23
leads him to conclude that for sedentary workers the dry kata
cooling power should not be below 6'0, and the wet kata cooling
power not below 18*0. On days when in normal houses the
readings were well above these figures, results of less than 5*0
for the " dry " and less than 12 0 for the " wet" readings were
obtained. In the case of the " semi-concealed bed," so common
in Glasgow, the readings were even less. By the kindness of
the Corporation sanitary inspectors, one was enabled to visit
these same houses at night (about 2 A.M.); ventilation was
practically non-existent, and one felt absolutely exhausted after
standing quite still for several moments in such a house. The
dry and wet kata readings were usually about 3*5 and 9*5
respectively. These figures indicate a very poor ventilation.
When infants are taken into the open air they are usually well
muffled up by a shawl so as to limit the amount of oxygen
available. The infant is thus brought up in a state of chronic
oxygen lack, surrounded by an serial jacket, the one character-
istic of which is its low oxygen content. And it is in these
districts, where such a condition of affairs exists, that tetany
and its allied disorders are so prevalent.
