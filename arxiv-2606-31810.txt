The exceptional origin of the strange metal
and the LFL–HFL transition

Abstract




Abstract

We propose an algebraic framework for the strange metal regime of strongly correlated electrons. We show that the exceptional superconformal algebra
D(2,1;\alpha)
admits two distinct contractions of its conformal sector: one to a pair of canonical fermions, the underlying degrees of freedom of the Landau–Fermi liquid (LFL), and one to the algebra of Hubbard operators, which characterise a distinct metallic regime, the Hubbard–Fermi liquid (HFL). We argue that competition between these two metallic states drives the emergence of the strange metal as a
0+1
D superconformal bath. We analyse the resulting thermodynamics, and obtain a parameter-free prediction,
4\pi^{2}\gamma^{-1}=\chi_{s}^{-1}+\chi_{c}^{-1}
, relating the Sommerfeld coefficient to the static spin and charge susceptibilities. We further show that the LFL–HFL transition is discontinuous at low temperature, owing to a degeneracy at the emergence of the HFL, and map out the resulting phase diagram. We connect the framework to microscopic lattice models and to the phenomenology of correlated insulators.

Introduction

The quantum theory of metals rests upon an algebraic assumption: that the electronic correlations are governed by the canonical anti-commutation relations,
\{c_{\sigma},c^{\dagger}_{\sigma^{\prime}}\}=\delta_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}
. This underpins the microscopic derivation of Landau–Fermi liquid (LFL) theory, and its reduction of the quantum many-body problem to coherent quasiparticles. Yet in strongly correlated systems, such as the cuprates and heavy fermion compounds, this framework systematically fails
[
38
,
70
]
. A prominent example is the strange metal, a macroscopic regime whose anomalous thermodynamics have resisted a canonical description
[
52
]
.

We argue that this failure can be traced algebraically. The electron itself admits a second distinct algebraic formulation, introduced by Hubbard in 1965
[
34
]
. In previous work we cast this as the Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{su}(2|2)
, and proposed that it governs a metallic state distinct from the LFL, termed the Hubbard–Fermi liquid (HFL)
[
56
,
58
]
. The Hubbard interaction enters as an emergent chemical potential, splitting the electronic band in two. A consequence is that the Luttinger sum rule, which encodes the topological protection of the LFL, is intrinsically violated here. The two metallic states are not adiabatically connected, and the study of this LFL–HFL transition is a central focus of this work.

We propose an algebraic framework that connects the canonical and Hubbard formulations of the electron. We show that both descend from the exceptional Lie superalgebra
D(2,1;\alpha)
, as distinct contractions of its conformal sector. This algebra is distinguished: it is the only simple Lie superalgebra with continuously deformable structure constants
[
36
]
, parameterised by
\alpha
, and we will show that this governs the LFL–HFL transition. The algebra’s conformal sector encodes a purely temporal scale invariance, which we conjecture arises dynamically from the competing coherence scales of the two metallic states, reducing the system to an effective
0+1
D superconformal bath. Evaluating the resulting Schwarzian thermodynamics in the semiclassical regime, we derive a parameter-free thermodynamic relation:

\frac{4\pi^{2}k_{B}^{2}}{\gamma}=\frac{(g\mu_{B})^{2}}{\chi_{s}}+\frac{e^{2}}{\chi_{c}}

(1)

relating the Sommerfeld coefficient
\gamma
to the static spin and charge susceptibilities
\chi_{s}
,
\chi_{c}
.

We characterise the LFL–HFL transition within this framework. We show that it is discontinuous at low temperature, forced by a degeneracy at the emergence of the HFL, and we argue on entropic grounds that the discontinuity terminates at a finite-temperature critical endpoint. The loss of a coherent spatial description here is the origin of the
0+1
D bath, and we present the resulting phase diagram in
Fig.
1
.

This
0+1
D description connects to, but is distinct from, the SYK
[
60
,
39
]
and DMFT
[
22
]
frameworks. Unlike those, which respectively rely on infinite-range couplings or infinite spatial dimensions, we argue that a
0+1
D bath emerges in finite-dimensional lattices at the LFL–HFL transition. In this way, our approach offers a realisation of local quantum criticality, a dynamical dimensional reduction proposed phenomenologically for the strange metal in cuprate and heavy fermion systems
[
73
,
66
]
.

2
The electronic degree of freedom

The core challenge of many-body quantum physics is the exponential complexity of interacting systems, and an efficient description relies upon identifying appropriate degrees of freedom. A well-chosen degree of freedom isolates the most physically relevant correlations, providing the foundation for an effective quasiparticle description.

Mathematically, a quantum degree of freedom is specified by the algebra it obeys. In Heisenberg’s equations of motion, the dynamics of an operator
\mathcal{O}
are governed by the commutator
[H,\mathcal{O}]
. In the presence of interactions, these equations cannot be closed exactly, and approximations are required. The choice of algebra guides the truncation of these equations, ultimately dictating the dispersive modes. Indeed, any perturbative approximation carries an inherent, often implicit, assumption: that the chosen algebraic framework encodes the system’s dominant physics.

Here, we apply this principle to the electron
[
56
,
58
]
. The Hilbert space of a single electron consists of four basis states:

|\circ\rangle,\quad|\downarrow\rangle,\quad|\uparrow\rangle,\quad|\bullet\rangle

(2)

corresponding to an empty site, a singly occupied site with spin down or up, and a doubly occupied site, respectively. Depending on how transitions between these four states are decomposed, one arrives at two distinct algebraic formulations of the electronic degree of freedom, which characterise two fundamentally different metallic regimes.

2.1
Canonical:
\mathfrak{su}(1|1)\oplus\mathfrak{su}(1|1)

The canonical formulation treats the electron as two independent fermion species expressed through the algebra,

\{c_{\sigma},c^{\dagger}_{\sigma^{\prime}}\}=\delta_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}},

(3)

with
\sigma=\downarrow,\uparrow
denoting the electron’s spin. Formally, the relation
\{c,c^{\dagger}\}=1
gives the Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{su}(1|1)
, and so we denote this canonical formulation as
\mathfrak{su}(1|1)\oplus\mathfrak{su}(1|1)
. This can be viewed as grouping the four basis states as
\big\{|\circ\rangle;|\downarrow\rangle\big\}\otimes\big\{|\circ\rangle;|\uparrow\rangle\big\}
.

The canonical algebra exhibits
U(1)
charge symmetry generated by the number operators,
n_{\sigma}=c^{\dagger}_{\sigma}c_{\sigma}
. Incorporating their commutation relations,

[n_{\sigma},c^{\dagger}_{\sigma^{\prime}}]=\delta_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}c^{\dagger}_{\sigma},\qquad[n_{\sigma},c_{\sigma^{\prime}}]=-\delta_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}c_{\sigma}

(4)

lifts the underlying algebra to
\mathfrak{u}(1|1)\oplus\mathfrak{u}(1|1)
(the
n_{\sigma}
are generators with non-zero supertrace, e.g.
\bra{0}n_{\uparrow}\ket{0}-\bra{\uparrow}n_{\uparrow}\ket{\uparrow}\neq 0
).

A defining characteristic of this canonical formulation is that the anti-commutator yields a scalar operator, the identity. Focusing on a single species, a non-interacting kinetic Hamiltonian,
H=\sum_{i,j}\varepsilon_{ij}c^{\dagger}_{i}c_{j}
, has linear action on the creation operators,
[H,c^{\dagger}_{i}]=\sum_{j}\varepsilon_{ij}c^{\dagger}_{j}
, which allows for the straightforward identification of single-particle modes with dispersion
\varepsilon_{k}
. Interactions can be incorporated through the standard techniques of many-body perturbation theory, which provides the microscopic foundation for describing conventional metals as Landau–Fermi liquids. In particular, the Dyson equation relates the single-particle Green’s function,

G_{k}=\frac{1}{\omega+\mu-\varepsilon_{k}-\Sigma_{k}},

(5)

to the self-energy
\Sigma_{k}
, which encodes the scattering-induced dressing of the bare modes as quasiparticles.

A powerful organizing principle within this perturbative framework is the concept of conserving approximations, due to Kadanoff and Baym
[
5
]
. One defines the Luttinger–Ward functional,
\Phi[G]
, from which the self-energy is obtained as
\Sigma_{k}=\frac{\delta\Phi}{\delta G_{k}}
, and any approximation constructed in this way inherently respects all conservation laws. This functional framework also underpins the Luttinger sum rule
[
44
]
, which relates the electron density to the volume enclosed by the Fermi surface, reflecting a topological protection of the quasiparticle
[
49
]
.

2.2
Hubbard:
\mathfrak{su}(2|2)

We now turn to the Hubbard formulation of the electron, which we cast through the Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{su}(2|2)

[
8
]
. This exposes useful structures within the algebra, along with a continuous parameter
\kappa
which encodes a central extension. We summarize the essential details here, deferring the full algebraic relations and the explicit mapping to Hubbard’s operators to
App.
A
.

The Hubbard algebra mixes all four electronic basis states, and here it is natural to group them as follows:
\big\{|\downarrow\rangle,|\uparrow\rangle;~|\circ\rangle,|\bullet\rangle\big\}.
The algebra is generated by two
\mathfrak{su}(2)
triplets,
\vec{s}
acting on the spin doublet
\{|\downarrow\rangle,|\uparrow\rangle\}
and
\vec{\eta}
acting on the charge doublet
\{|\circ\rangle,|\bullet\rangle\}
, alongside eight fermionic generators
q_{\sigma\nu}
and
q^{\dagger}_{\sigma\nu}
(with
\sigma\in\{\downarrow,\uparrow\}
and
\nu\in\{\circ,\bullet\}
) that act between the doublets. Importantly, the fermionic anti-commutation relations do not yield pure scalars, but instead close back onto the
\mathfrak{su}(2)
generators:

\displaystyle\{q_{\sigma\nu},q^{\dagger}_{\sigma\nu}\}=\frac{1+\kappa^{2}}{4}-\kappa\left(\sigma s^{z}-\nu\eta^{z}\right),

(6)

\displaystyle\{q_{\downarrow\nu},q^{\dagger}_{\uparrow\nu}\}=\kappa s^{+},\qquad\{q_{\sigma\circ},q^{\dagger}_{\sigma\bullet}\}=-\kappa\eta^{+},

\displaystyle\{q_{\uparrow\nu},q^{\dagger}_{\downarrow\nu}\}=\kappa s^{-},\qquad\{q_{\sigma\bullet},q^{\dagger}_{\sigma\circ}\}=-\kappa\eta^{-},

\displaystyle\{q_{\sigma\nu},q_{\sigma^{\prime}\nu^{\prime}}\}=\{q^{\dagger}_{\sigma\nu},q^{\dagger}_{\sigma^{\prime}\nu^{\prime}}\}=\frac{1-\kappa^{2}}{4}\epsilon_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\epsilon_{\nu\nu^{\prime}},

where as coefficients
\sigma
,
\nu
take values
+1
for
\{\uparrow,\bullet\}
and
-1
for
\{\downarrow,\circ\}
, and
\epsilon
is the antisymmetric tensor with convention
\epsilon_{\downarrow\uparrow}=\epsilon_{\circ\bullet}=1
. Together, these generators give the Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{su}(2|2)
, which is exceptionally centrally extended here for
\kappa\neq 1
. Just as the canonical algebra is extended by the number operator, this algebra admits a
U(1)
extension via a generator
\theta
:

\theta=\frac{1}{3}\big(\vec{\eta}\cdot\vec{\eta}-\vec{s}\cdot\vec{s}\big).

(7)

This generator commutes with both spin and charge, and its commutation relations with the fermions take the linear form:

\displaystyle=\frac{1+\kappa^{2}}{4\kappa}q^{\dagger}_{\sigma\nu}-\frac{1-\kappa^{2}}{4\kappa}\epsilon_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\epsilon_{\nu\nu^{\prime}}q_{\sigma^{\prime}\nu^{\prime}}

(8)

\displaystyle[\theta,q_{\sigma\nu}]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1+\kappa^{2}}{4\kappa}q_{\sigma\nu}+\frac{1-\kappa^{2}}{4\kappa}\epsilon_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\epsilon_{\nu\nu^{\prime}}q^{\dagger}_{\sigma^{\prime}\nu^{\prime}}.

Incorporating
\theta
lifts the algebra to
\mathfrak{u}(2|2)
, and it is this full algebra, evaluated at
\kappa=1
, that maps to Hubbard’s graded projection operators.

The Hubbard and canonical algebras offer complementary bases for the operators acting on an electronic site. In terms of the canonical fermion, the
q^{\dagger}_{\sigma\nu}
take a non-linear form,

\displaystyle q^{\dagger}_{\sigma\circ}=\frac{1+\kappa}{2}c_{\bar{\sigma}}-\kappa n_{\sigma}c_{\bar{\sigma}},\qquad q^{\dagger}_{\sigma\bullet}=\sigma\big(\frac{1-\kappa}{2}c^{\dagger}_{\sigma}+\kappa n_{\bar{\sigma}}c^{\dagger}_{\sigma}\big),

(9)

\displaystyle q_{\sigma\circ}=\frac{1+\kappa}{2}c^{\dagger}_{\bar{\sigma}}-\kappa n_{\sigma}c^{\dagger}_{\bar{\sigma}},\qquad q_{\sigma\bullet}=\sigma\big(\frac{1-\kappa}{2}c_{\sigma}+\kappa n_{\bar{\sigma}}c_{\sigma}\big).

with
\bar{\sigma}=-\sigma
. Remarkably, the inverse relations are linear:

c^{\dagger}_{\downarrow}=q_{\uparrow\circ}-q^{\dagger}_{\downarrow\bullet},\qquad c^{\dagger}_{\uparrow}=q_{\downarrow\circ}+q^{\dagger}_{\uparrow\bullet},

(10)

termed a splitting of the electron (as opposed to fractionalisation, which takes a product form
[
62
,
12
]
). This has a powerful consequence: the electronic Green’s function decomposes into an exact linear combination of the Green’s functions of the
q^{\dagger}_{\sigma\nu}
. Thus, a systematic computation of the
q^{\dagger}_{\sigma\nu}
matrix Green’s functions offers an alternative route by which to organise electronic correlations.

The additional generator
\theta
plays a distinguished role here. Acting on the basis states, it weights the spin and charge doublets oppositely:
\theta|\sigma\rangle=-\tfrac{1}{4}|\sigma\rangle
and
\theta|\nu\rangle=\tfrac{1}{4}|\nu\rangle
. Physically, this is equivalent to the onsite Hubbard interaction, up to a shift of the chemical potential. Owing to the linear action of
\theta
on the
q^{\dagger}_{\sigma\nu}
, the Hubbard
U
functions as an
emergent chemical potential
, entering the
q^{\dagger}_{\sigma\nu}
matrix Green’s function only as a shift of frequency.

The parameter
\kappa
provides the algebra with a canonical limit. As
\kappa\to 0
, the non-scalar contributions to the
q^{\dagger}_{\sigma\nu}
anti-commutation relations vanish, reducing them to those of canonical fermions:
q_{\uparrow\circ}
and
q^{\dagger}_{\downarrow\bullet}
merge into
c^{\dagger}_{\downarrow}
, and
q_{\downarrow\circ}
and
q^{\dagger}_{\uparrow\bullet}
merge into
c^{\dagger}_{\uparrow}
. We offer a physical interpretation of
\kappa
in terms of correlated hopping in
Sec.
5.1.1
.

This points towards a systematic computation of
q^{\dagger}_{\sigma\nu}
Green’s functions. Bare modes can be identified from the scalar part of the anti-commutator, Eq. (
6
), and scattering organised perturbatively in
\kappa
. The Hubbard interaction plays its special role here. The small
\kappa
divergence of Eq. (
8
) is balanced by scaling the Hubbard coupling
U
by
\kappa
, with the consequence that the emergent chemical potential
U
influences the bare modes directly. When translated to the electronic Green’s function via the splitting relations, Eq. (
10
), increasing
U
at fixed
\kappa
results in a splitting of the electronic band in two.

For small
U
, the split bare bands may overlap, and low-energy scattering is both intra-band and inter-band. As
U
is increased, these bands pull apart, a gap opens in the electronic spectrum, and thereafter all inter-band scattering is suppressed. In this regime, it follows from Landau’s phase space argument that the decay rate from intra-band scattering scales as
\omega^{2}
at low energy, at least at leading order in
\kappa
. The resulting dispersive modes are thus long-lived quasiparticles at this level of approximation. Since the electronic Green’s function is a linear combination of
q^{\dagger}_{\sigma\nu}
matrix Green’s functions, the corresponding poles carry over to the electron.

Once the split bands are separated by a gap, perturbative scattering cannot merge them, and it follows that the Luttinger sum rule is generically violated.

2.2.1
The Hubbard–Fermi liquid

We argue that the Hubbard formulation of the electron characterises a legitimate state of electronic matter, a metal with long-lived quasiparticles organised by the
\mathfrak{su}(2|2)
algebra. As this is fundamentally distinct from a Landau--Fermi liquid (LFL), we term this instead a Hubbard--Fermi liquid
1
1

1

There is a notational conflict here. The Heavy Fermi liquid, observed in local moment systems, shares the acronym HFL. A notable aspect of the
\mathfrak{su}(2|2)
paradigm, however, is that it extends naturally to incorporate a local moment
[
55
]
, as detailed in
App.
B
. We take the perspective that if the HFL is a valid state of matter, then the Heavy Fermi liquid is an instance of it.
(HFL).

Hubbard operators, and related approaches, have long been employed to study the interacting electron problem
[
34
,
59
,
75
,
47
,
50
]
. There is an important distinction to make however. These works treated Hubbard operators as a computational basis for computing the electronic Green’s function, with violation of the Luttinger sum rule an artifact of an uncontrolled approximation. We argue that by regarding the Hubbard algebra as a true degree of freedom, the violation is an intrinsic feature that distinguishes the metallic state from a LFL.

There is an obstacle to establishing the HFL however. It is not straightforward to develop a perturbative expansion which respects conservation laws order by order. Within the canonical formalism, the anti-commutator yields a scalar identity, which guarantees the applicability of Wick’s theorem and enables the construction of the Luttinger–Ward functional
\Phi
order by order. The existence of
\Phi
rests upon
\frac{\delta\Sigma_{k}}{\delta G_{k^{\prime}}}=\frac{\delta\Sigma_{k^{\prime}}}{\delta G_{k}}
, which is a flatness condition for
\Phi
. In contrast, the Hubbard anti-commutation relations yield non-scalar terms, the spin and charge generators. While in the limit
\kappa\to 0
the algebra flattens, for finite
\kappa
these non-canonical contributions render Wick’s theorem inapplicable, and their inherent curvature obstructs the definition of a corresponding
\Phi
-functional. This raises the question of how to formulate a systematic conserving expansion for the non-canonical degree of freedom, and offers an explanation for why there is no known counterpart of the Luttinger sum rule.

This obstruction to a clean perturbative expansion is not unique to the Hubbard electron. A direct parallel is found in localized magnetism, where the physical operators obey the
\mathfrak{su}(2)
spin algebra. With respect to a polarised reference state
\ket{\uparrow}
, the algebra is cast as

[a,a^{\dagger}]=1-\tfrac{1}{S}n,\quad[n,a^{\dagger}]=a^{\dagger},\quad[n,a]=-a,

(11)

with
a=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2S}}S^{+}
,
a^{\dagger}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2S}}S^{-}
,
n=S-S^{z}
, and spin wave theory is formulated as an expansion about the large-
S
limit. As above, no
\Phi
-derivable approximation scheme is known to exist. The standard technique, based on the Holstein-Primakoff mapping to canonical bosons
[
33
]
, results in a mixing of symmetry sectors across different orders in the
1/S
expansion. While workarounds exist, each has their trade-offs, and a single coherent framework remains an open challenge
[
3
,
71
,
42
]
. Nevertheless, spin-wave theory offers a controlled and quantitatively predictive description of magnetically ordered matter, the absence of a
\Phi
-functional notwithstanding.

We therefore proceed on the assumption that the HFL is also a legitimate state, with the obstruction a problem of method rather than of existence. In doing so, we will place the HFL within a broader algebraic structure, which clarifies both its origin and its relationship to the LFL.

2.3
Competing metallic regimes

We have argued that the canonical and Hubbard formulations of the electron are not merely complementary mathematical bases, but that they characterise two distinct metallic states. The canonical formulation underpins the LFL, where the charge carrier density is fixed by the Luttinger sum rule. The Hubbard formulation underpins the HFL, where the Hubbard interaction serves as an emergent chemical potential that splits the electronic band in two, and the Luttinger count is generically violated.

This mismatch of the Luttinger count is a signature of the distinct nature of the two regimes. Within the LFL, the Luttinger count is a topological property of the Fermi surface, protected so long as the quasiparticles remain coherent. As the HFL Fermi surface violates the Luttinger count, any transition from a LFL to a HFL requires a Fermi surface rearrangement. The two metals cannot be adiabatically connected, any path joining them at
T=0
must be non-analytic.

The LFL–HFL transition is not confined to the infrared. The splitting of the electronic band in two requires a global redistribution of spectral weight, across the energy scale of the emergent chemical potential
U

[
14
]
. This distinguishes it from conventional transitions, in which the spectral rearrangement is confined to the low-energy vicinity of the Fermi surface.

This large-scale spectral rearrangement presents a theoretical hurdle. It cannot be captured perturbatively, neither by truncating the equations of motion of the canonical many-body perturbation theory, nor through an as-yet-unestablished non-canonical expansion. Instead, an overarching algebraic framework is required, and this is what we propose in
Sec.
3
.

We can frame the location of the LFL–HFL transition by considering the competing coherence scales of the two metallic regimes. Deep within the LFL and HFL phases, their ground states are protected by their respective coherence scales
T^{*}_{\text{LFL}}
and
T^{*}_{\text{HFL}}
, below which their dispersive quasiparticle modes are robust. However, as the system is tuned towards the LFL–HFL phase boundary, these protection mechanisms compete. We may identify the transition regime by the condition where these coherence scales intersect,
T^{*}_{\text{LFL}}\sim T^{*}_{\text{HFL}}
, and we will argue in
Sec.
4
that this occurs at finite temperature.

At temperatures above this intersection, the coherent quasiparticle of either metal is lost. We argue that this is a critical regime, born of LFL–HFL competition, which realises an emergent superconformal symmetry, governed by the algebraic structure of
Sec.
3
.

3
The exceptional Lie superalgebra
D(2,1;\alpha)

In this section we detail the exceptional Lie superalgebra
D(2,1;\alpha)
, and demonstrate that it encompasses and extends the canonical and Hubbard formulations of the electron. First introduced in Kac’s classification
[
36
]
, it is the only simple Lie superalgebra with continuously deformable structure constants. We employ here a non-compact real form, in which one bosonic sector is the conformal algebra
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
, which underlies the emergent scale invariance of
Sec.
4
. We detail the correspondence to the compact form in
App.
C
, and also highlight some notable limits.

The algebra comprises the conformal triplet
D
,
E_{+}
,
E_{-}
, together with two bosonic
\mathfrak{su}(2)
triplets
J^{a}_{b}
,
J^{\alpha}_{\beta}
, and two fermionic quartets
Q^{a\alpha}
and
Q^{\dagger}_{a\alpha}
, with indices
a
and
\alpha
both taking values in
\{1,2\}
. The three sets of bosonic generators mutually commute and obey:

\displaystyle[D,E_{+}]=E_{+},\quad[D,E_{-}]=-E_{-},\quad[E_{+},E_{-}]=2D,

(12)

\displaystyle[J^{a}_{b},J^{c}_{d}]=\delta^{c}_{b}J^{a}_{d}-\delta^{a}_{d}J^{c}_{b},\qquad[J^{\alpha}_{\beta},J^{\gamma}_{\delta}]=\delta^{\gamma}_{\beta}J^{\alpha}_{\delta}-\delta^{\alpha}_{\delta}J^{\gamma}_{\beta}.

The fermionic generators transform under the
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
conformal generators as:

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}Q^{\dagger}_{a\alpha},\qquad\qquad\qquad\;[D,Q^{a\alpha}]=-\frac{1}{2}Q^{a\alpha},

(13)

\displaystyle[E_{+},Q^{a\alpha}]

\displaystyle=\epsilon^{ab}\epsilon^{\alpha\beta}Q^{\dagger}_{b\beta},\qquad\qquad\;[E_{+},Q^{\dagger}_{a\alpha}]=0,

\displaystyle[E_{-},Q^{\dagger}_{a\alpha}]

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{ab}\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}Q^{b\beta},\qquad\qquad[E_{-},Q^{a\alpha}]=0,

and in the fundamental and conjugate representations of the two
\mathfrak{su}(2)
factors:

\displaystyle=\delta^{c}_{b}Q^{a\gamma}-\frac{1}{2}\delta^{a}_{b}Q^{c\gamma},\qquad[J^{a}_{b},Q^{\dagger}_{c\gamma}]=-\delta^{a}_{c}Q^{\dagger}_{b\gamma}+\frac{1}{2}\delta^{a}_{b}Q^{\dagger}_{c\gamma}

(14)

\displaystyle[J^{\alpha}_{\beta},Q^{c\gamma}]

\displaystyle=\delta^{\gamma}_{\beta}Q^{c\alpha}-\frac{1}{2}\delta^{\alpha}_{\beta}Q^{c\gamma},\qquad[J^{\alpha}_{\beta},Q^{\dagger}_{c\gamma}]=-\delta^{\alpha}_{\gamma}Q^{\dagger}_{c\beta}+\frac{1}{2}\delta^{\alpha}_{\beta}Q^{\dagger}_{c\gamma}.

The anticommutators of the fermionic generators close onto the bosonic generators, weighted by structure constants
c_{1}
,
c_{2}
,
c_{3}
:

\displaystyle\{Q^{a\alpha},Q^{\dagger}_{b\beta}\}=c_{1}\delta_{b}^{a}\delta^{\alpha}_{\beta}D-c_{2}\delta^{\alpha}_{\beta}J^{a}_{b}-c_{3}\delta_{b}^{a}J^{\alpha}_{\beta},

(15)

\displaystyle\{Q^{\dagger}_{a\alpha},Q^{\dagger}_{b\beta}\}=-c_{1}\epsilon_{ab}\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}E_{+},\qquad\{Q^{a\alpha},Q^{b\beta}\}=c_{1}\epsilon^{ab}\epsilon^{\alpha\beta}E_{-},

which are constrained to obey:

c_{1}+c_{2}+c_{3}=0,

(16)

as the requirement of the super-Jacobi identity. Because an overall rescaling of the supercharges rescales the structure constants uniformly, the algebra depends on a single continuous parameter,
\alpha=c_{3}/c_{2}
.

Due to the non-compact
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
sector, unitary representations of this algebra are generally infinite-dimensional. We will now show, however, that it is possible to contract the conformal sector and project down to a
4
dimensional representation in two distinct ways.

3.1
Maximal contraction

We contract all generators simultaneously via Inönü–Wigner scaling
[
35
]
:

\tilde{D}=\lambda D,\qquad\tilde{E}_{\pm}=\lambda E_{\pm},\qquad\tilde{J}^{a}_{b}=\lambda J^{a}_{b},\qquad\tilde{J}^{\alpha}_{\beta}=\lambda J^{\alpha}_{\beta},\qquad\tilde{Q}^{a\alpha}=\lambda^{1/2}Q^{a\alpha},\qquad\tilde{Q}^{\dagger}_{a\alpha}=\lambda^{1/2}Q^{\dagger}_{a\alpha}.

(17)

Taking
\lambda\to 0
, the commutators scale as
[\tilde{J},\tilde{J}]\propto\lambda
and
[\tilde{J},\tilde{Q}]\propto\lambda
, vanishing entirely. Thus, the non-Abelian bosonic generators get flattened into commuting central charges, while the fermionic anti-commutators survive exactly:

\displaystyle\{\tilde{Q}^{a\alpha},\tilde{Q}^{\dagger}_{b\beta}\}=c_{1}\delta_{b}^{a}\delta^{\alpha}_{\beta}\tilde{D}-c_{2}\delta^{\alpha}_{\beta}\tilde{J}^{a}_{b}-c_{3}\delta_{b}^{a}\tilde{J}^{\alpha}_{\beta},

(18)

\displaystyle\{\tilde{Q}^{\dagger}_{a\alpha},\tilde{Q}^{\dagger}_{b\beta}\}=-c_{1}\epsilon_{ab}\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}\tilde{E}_{+},\qquad\{\tilde{Q}^{a\alpha},\tilde{Q}^{b\beta}\}=c_{1}\epsilon^{ab}\epsilon^{\alpha\beta}\tilde{E}_{-},

leaving the coefficients
c_{i}
untouched. We can now diagonalise the bosonic sectors, and setting
\tilde{E}_{\pm}=0
, the brackets
\{\tilde{Q},\tilde{Q}\}
and
\{\tilde{Q}^{\dagger},\tilde{Q}^{\dagger}\}
vanish. Absorbing the continuous eigenvalues of the surviving central charges into the coefficients results in:

\{\tilde{Q}^{a\alpha},\tilde{Q}^{\dagger}_{b\beta}\}=\hat{c}_{1}\delta_{b}^{a}\delta^{\alpha}_{\beta}-\hat{c}_{2}\delta^{\alpha}_{\beta}(\sigma^{z})^{a}_{b}-\hat{c}_{3}\delta_{b}^{a}(\sigma^{z})^{\alpha}_{\beta},

(19)

where the new coefficients are unconstrained (the super-Jacobi identity here follows trivially from
[\tilde{J},\tilde{Q}]=0
). The right-hand side of this anti-commutator defines a diagonal
4\times 4
matrix in the basis of the operators
(a,\alpha)\in\{(1,1),(1,2),(2,1),(2,2)\}
:

\{\tilde{Q},\tilde{Q}^{\dagger}\}=\text{diag}(\hat{c}_{1}-\hat{c}_{2}-\hat{c}_{3},\;\;\hat{c}_{1}-\hat{c}_{2}+\hat{c}_{3},\;\;\hat{c}_{1}+\hat{c}_{2}-\hat{c}_{3},\;\;\hat{c}_{1}+\hat{c}_{2}+\hat{c}_{3}).

(20)

Generically, these eigenvalues are all non-zero, resulting in four independent fermion pairs, yielding a minimal representation of dimension
2^{4}=16
. This can be reduced to
4
by constraining the coefficients to decouple two of these pairs. Imposing unitarity, this requires setting either
\hat{c}_{1}=|\hat{c}_{2}|
with
\hat{c}_{3}=0
, or
\hat{c}_{1}=|\hat{c}_{3}|
with
\hat{c}_{2}=0
. Fixing the overall normalization to
|\hat{c}_{i}|=1/2
, we thus obtain four possibilities for the two surviving canonical pairs, parameterized by
(\hat{c}_{1},\hat{c}_{2},\hat{c}_{3})
:

\displaystyle(1/2,\,0,\,-1/2)

\displaystyle:\quad\{\tilde{Q}^{a1},\tilde{Q}^{\dagger}_{b1}\}=\delta^{a}_{b},

(21)

\displaystyle(1/2,\,0,\,1/2)

\displaystyle:\quad\{\tilde{Q}^{a2},\tilde{Q}^{\dagger}_{b2}\}=\delta^{a}_{b},

\displaystyle(1/2,\,-1/2,\,0)

\displaystyle:\quad\{\tilde{Q}^{1\alpha},\tilde{Q}^{\dagger}_{1\beta}\}=\delta^{\alpha}_{\beta},

\displaystyle(1/2,\,1/2,\,0)

\displaystyle:\quad\{\tilde{Q}^{2\alpha},\tilde{Q}^{\dagger}_{2\beta}\}=\delta^{\alpha}_{\beta}.

That is, the algebra contracts here to
\mathfrak{su}(1|1)\oplus\mathfrak{su}(1|1)
.

3.2
Partial contraction

Alternatively, we can take advantage of the flexibility of the coefficients
c_{i}
to contract just a single bosonic sector. Specifically, we contract the conformal sector via Beisert scaling
[
8
]
:

c_{1}=\epsilon,\quad c_{2}=-1,\quad c_{3}=1-\epsilon,

(22)

with the
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
generators also scaled by
\epsilon
:

\tilde{D}=\epsilon D,\qquad\tilde{E}_{+}=\epsilon E_{+},\qquad\tilde{E}_{-}=\epsilon E_{-},

(23)

so that
\tilde{D}
,
\tilde{E}_{\pm}
become central in the
\epsilon\to 0
limit. Here the fermionic generators are not scaled, but instead the scaling of
c_{1}
balances the limit:

\displaystyle\{Q^{a\alpha},Q^{\dagger}_{b\beta}\}=\delta_{b}^{a}\delta^{\alpha}_{\beta}\tilde{D}+\delta^{\alpha}_{\beta}J^{a}_{b}-\delta_{b}^{a}J^{\alpha}_{\beta},

(24)

\displaystyle\{Q^{\dagger}_{a\alpha},Q^{\dagger}_{b\beta}\}=-\epsilon_{ab}\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}\tilde{E}_{+},\qquad\{Q^{a\alpha},Q^{b\beta}\}=\epsilon^{ab}\epsilon^{\alpha\beta}\tilde{E}_{-}.

These, along with the surviving relations of the
J
generators, define the centrally extended
\mathfrak{su}(2|2)
algebra, or
\mathfrak{su}(2|2)\ltimes\mathbb{R}^{3}
. As above, generically this has a minimal representation of dimension
16
, but this can be shortened to
4
under the condition
[
7
]
:

\tilde{D}^{2}-|\tilde{E}|^{2}=1/4,

(25)

where
|\tilde{E}|^{2}\equiv\tilde{E}_{+}^{\dagger}\tilde{E}_{+}=-\tilde{E}_{+}\tilde{E}_{-}
. In this way, we obtain the Hubbard algebra of the electron. It will prove important that here
\alpha=\frac{c_{3}}{c_{2}}=-1
, an inescapable consequence of the super-Jacobi constraint, Eq. (
16
), upon scaling
c_{1}
to zero.

3.3
Electronic interpretation

We thus see that
D(2,1;\alpha)
admits two contractions of the conformal sector, which lead to the two formulations of the electronic degree of freedom presented in
Sec.
2
.

To make this explicit, let us first focus on the appearance of the Hubbard algebra through the partial contraction. The two
\mathfrak{su}(2)
algebras are identified as spin and charge by mapping the indices as follows,

a,b\in\{1,2\}\leftrightarrow\{\downarrow,\uparrow\},\quad\alpha,\beta\in\{1,2\}\leftrightarrow\{\circ,\bullet\},

(26)

and their generators as projections onto the Pauli matrices
\sigma^{i}
as
\tfrac{1}{2}\text{tr}(J^{T}\sigma^{i})
:

s^{i}=\tfrac{1}{2}J^{a}_{b}(\sigma^{i})^{a}_{b},\quad\eta^{i}=\tfrac{1}{2}J^{\alpha}_{\beta}(\sigma^{i})^{\alpha}_{\beta},

(27)

with
i\in\{x,y,z\}
. The fermionic generators then follow as
q_{\sigma\nu}=\sqrt{\kappa}Q^{\sigma\nu}
and
q^{\dagger}_{\sigma\nu}=\sqrt{\kappa}Q^{\dagger}_{\sigma\nu}
, with the central charges taking values
\tilde{D}=\frac{1+\kappa^{2}}{4\kappa}
and
\tilde{E}_{\pm}=\mp\frac{1-\kappa^{2}}{4\kappa}
.

For the maximal contraction we found four distinct limits that yield pairs of canonical fermions. From the identification above, we see that these correspond to the decoupling of one of the
\mathfrak{su}(2)
sectors. This admits a natural physical interpretation. From the perspective of the Hubbard formulation of the electron, the natural reference state is at half-filling (
\braket{\vec{\eta}}=0
) and zero magnetic polarisation (
\braket{\vec{s}}=0
), while for a canonical electron it is at the extremes: an empty or full band, or a fully magnetically polarised state. Let us say a system realises a Mott insulator, then we expect the HFL regime to govern the vicinity of half-filling, and we can regard the two charge differentiated pairs,
\{\tilde{Q}^{\sigma\circ},\tilde{Q}^{\dagger}_{\sigma^{\prime}\circ}\}=\delta^{\sigma}_{\sigma^{\prime}}
and
\{\tilde{Q}^{\sigma\bullet},\tilde{Q}^{\dagger}_{\sigma^{\prime}\bullet}\}=\delta^{\sigma}_{\sigma^{\prime}}
, as the canonical degrees of freedom for LFL regimes which extend from the band edges. Alternatively, if the system exhibits a correlation induced spin gap (say for
U<0
), then the two spin differentiated pairs,
\{\tilde{Q}^{\uparrow\nu},\tilde{Q}^{\dagger}_{\uparrow\nu^{\prime}}\}=\delta^{\nu}_{\nu^{\prime}}
and
\{\tilde{Q}^{\downarrow\nu},\tilde{Q}^{\dagger}_{\downarrow\nu^{\prime}}\}=\delta^{\nu}_{\nu^{\prime}}
, map to the LFL regimes extending from the extremes of magnetic polarisation.

The identification of Eq. (
26
) lifts naturally to the full
D(2,1;\alpha)
algebra. We emphasise, however, that away from the contracted limits, the fermionic generators,
Q^{\sigma\nu}
and
Q^{\dagger}_{\sigma\nu}
, no longer admit a strict electronic interpretation, as their anti-commutation relations yield the generators of the non-compact
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
.

In the following section we will interpret this as an emergent scale invariance, manifesting a dissolution of the electron, and enabling a transition between the canonical and Hubbard formulations of the electronic degree of freedom.

4
The strange metal and its exceptional thermodynamics

In
Sec.
2
we presented the canonical and Hubbard forms of the electron as two distinct ways to characterise metallic behaviour, the LFL and HFL. In
Sec.
3
, we showed that the exceptional Lie superalgebra
D(2,1;\alpha)
admits contractions of its conformal symmetry in two distinct limits, which map precisely onto these two electronic algebras. We now argue that
D(2,1;\alpha)
provides the organising principle for the LFL–HFL transition, with its conformal
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
sector governing an emergent temporal dynamics, enabling a reorganisation of the electronic spectrum. This is our conjecture for the exceptional origin of the strange metal: it is a manifestation of an emergent superconformal symmetry arising from competition between the two metallic states.

The emergence of
D(2,1;\alpha)
as a macroscopic symmetry presents a strict kinematic constraint. Its conformal sector,
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
, is the conformal algebra of a zero-dimensional space, with purely temporal scale invariance. Because the superalgebra lacks spatial generators, this emergent symmetry requires that spatial coherence is lost. This can be viewed at the level of states: a single electronic site has four, while
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
requires an infinite dimensional Hilbert space. With the loss of spatial coherence, the electron merges with its local environment, giving rise to a description of the system as a
0+1
D bath with a continuous spectrum in the thermodynamic limit.

We now proceed to examine the thermodynamics of this
0+1
D superconformal system. We will show that this framework offers a self-consistent description of the LFL–HFL transition. In particular, it enables us to understand the origin of this dimensional reduction from the perspective of the metallic states, and we return to this in
Sec.
4.2.3
.

4.1
Thermodynamics of the
0+1
D bath

The thermodynamics of a
0+1
D conformal bath is determined by its pattern of symmetry breaking, following the Schwarzian effective action methodology developed for the SYK family of models
[
39
,
46
,
19
]
. In general, this works as follows. In the strict infrared limit, the system is invariant under an infinite-dimensional super-reparameterization symmetry, which is spontaneously broken down to its global supergroup by the ground state. Moving away from the infrared limit, finite energy scales explicitly break the full super-reparameterization symmetry, lifting the corresponding zero-modes into pseudo-Goldstone bosons and their fermionic partners.

For the bath at hand, the super-reparameterization symmetry is the super-Virasoro extension of
D(2,1;\alpha)

[
24
,
63
]
, known in the high-energy literature as the large
\mathcal{N}=4
superconformal algebra, which we present in
App.
D
. Its bosonic sector is

\text{Diff}(S^{1})\ltimes\big(L(SU(2))\times L(SU(2))\times L(U(1))\big),

(28)

with
\text{Diff}(S^{1})
the group of mappings from the circle to itself, and
L(G)
the loop group of mappings from the circle to
G
. The two
L(SU(2))
factors are the spin and charge current algebras, and
L(U(1))
is a decoupled
U(1)
current which is required to linearise the full algebra
2
2

2

D(2,1;\alpha)
also admits a minimal super-Virasoro extension which is non-linear
[
24
]
. An attempt to construct the corresponding Schwarzian effective action yields trivial dynamics
[
21
]
. The non-linearity also obstructs the fermionic localisation of the path integral, employed for computing the one-loop exact quantum correction in
Sec.
4.1.3
. We do not consider this non-linear variant here.
.
The fermionic sector comprises the four
D(2,1;\alpha)
supercurrents, together with four free fermions associated with the decoupled
U(1)
current.

The ground state breaks this symmetry to the global supergroup
D(2,1;\alpha)\times U(1)
. The resulting pseudo-Goldstone bosonic modes are fluctuations of the temporal, spin and charge sectors, along with the decoupled
U(1)
phase.

We highlight that the additional
U(1)
here is distinct from the
U(1)
generated by the Hubbard
\theta
of
Sec.
2.2
. Its current
I_{n}
possesses a zero mode,
I_{0}
, that commutes with all generators of
D(2,1;\alpha)
. This zero mode can be interpreted as adding a central element, which
D(2,1;\alpha)
, being simple, does not contain.

4.1.1
Bosonic effective action

We will focus our analysis on a saddle-point evaluation of the Schwarzian effective action, for which we can restrict our attention to the bosonic sector. The fermionic modes, being Grassmannian, do not contribute to the saddle as their classical expectation values vanish. The effective action for the pseudo-Goldstone fluctuations identified above is then
[
32
,
41
]
:

S_{eff}=-M\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau\left[\{f,\tau\}+\lambda_{s}\text{Tr}(U_{s}^{-1}\partial_{\tau}U_{s})^{2}+\lambda_{c}\text{Tr}(U_{c}^{-1}\partial_{\tau}U_{c})^{2}+\lambda_{a}(U_{a}^{-1}\partial_{\tau}U_{a})^{2}\right].

(29)

Here
f
denotes time reparameterisation, and
\{f,\tau\}=\frac{f^{\prime\prime\prime}}{f^{\prime}}-\frac{3}{2}\left(\frac{f^{\prime\prime}}{f^{\prime}}\right)^{2}
is the Schwarzian derivative, which penalises deviations from the conformal frame. The matrices
U_{s}(\tau),U_{c}(\tau)\in SU(2)
parameterise the fluctuations of the spin and charge sectors, and
U_{a}(\tau)=e^{i\phi_{a}(\tau)}\in U(1)
parameterises the fluctuations of the decoupled phase, with the dynamics of all three captured by Maurer-Cartan kinetic terms. The overall coefficient
M
is an emergent scale that sets the stiffness of the bath.

The relative couplings
\lambda_{s}
and
\lambda_{c}
are constrained by
D(2,1;\alpha)
invariance. Let us sketch this briefly. The action is fixed by contracting the currents with the invariant bilinear form
\langle X,Y\rangle\equiv\text{Str}(XY)
, which satisfies the invariance condition:
\langle[X,Y\},Z\rangle=\langle X,[Y,Z\}\rangle
. The bosonic terms, say
\langle D,D\rangle
, can be related through the fermionic anti-commutation relations:
\langle\{Q,Q^{\dagger}\},D\rangle=\langle Q,[Q^{\dagger},D]\rangle
which gives
\langle D,D\rangle=-\langle Q,Q^{\dagger}\rangle
. Obtaining similar equations for the
\langle J,J\rangle
one obtains the relations
\lambda_{s}=-c_{1}/c_{2}
and
\lambda_{c}=-c_{1}/c_{3}
, with the
c_{i}
obeying the super-Jacobi constraint, Eq. (
16
). That is, the couplings are constrained to obey:

\frac{1}{\lambda_{s}}+\frac{1}{\lambda_{c}}=1,

(30)

and we parameterise this as follows:

\lambda_{s}=1+\alpha,\quad\lambda_{c}=1+\frac{1}{\alpha}.

(31)

Applying the same logic to the auxiliary
U(1)
sector of the linearised
algebra (
App.
D
) gives
\lambda_{a}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\lambda_{s}+\lambda_{c})
. This form matches the explicit result of Heydeman, Shi and Turiaci
[
32
]
(their Eq. (3.1)).

There is a subtlety regarding the thermodynamic stability of the bath that is worthwhile to highlight. The conditions
\lambda_{s}=1+\alpha\geq 0
and
\lambda_{c}=\frac{1+\alpha}{\alpha}\geq 0
, yield
\alpha>0
, the stability condition for the bath, together with an isolated point,
\alpha=-1
, where
\lambda_{s}=\lambda_{c}=0
and the bath is degenerate. We return to this isolated point in
Sec.
4.2.1
, where it appears at the emergence of the HFL.

4.1.2
Semiclassical saddle-point

The full partition function takes the form,

Z=e^{S_{0}}\int\mathcal{D}[f,U_{s},U_{c}]\,e^{-S_{b}[f,U_{s},U_{c}]},

(32)

where the prefactor
e^{S_{0}}
encodes the non-zero ground-state degeneracy of the
D(2,1;\alpha)
superconformal bath
[
32
]
. We expect the residual entropy
S_{0}
to be extensive, reflecting the intrinsically local nature of the bath. Here we have also integrated out the decoupled
U(1)
phase, and will treat its contribution to the overall normalisation in
Sec.
4.1.3
.

We now focus on the saddle-point regime,
M\beta\gg 1
, defining the conformal window:

\frac{1}{M}\ll T\ll T_{UV},

(33)

with
T=1/\beta
, and
T_{UV}
the ultraviolet scale at which the bath description is no longer valid.
The saddle point of the time-reparameterization mode,
f(\tau)
, is the conformal transformation mapping the thermal circle to the zero-temperature line:
f(\tau)=\frac{\beta}{\pi}\tan\left(\frac{\pi\tau}{\beta}\right)
. Evaluating the Schwarzian derivative,
\{f,\tau\}=2\pi^{2}/\beta^{2}
, results in the classical effective action:

S_{eff}=-M\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau\left(\frac{2\pi^{2}}{\beta^{2}}\right)=-\frac{2\pi^{2}M}{\beta}=-2\pi^{2}MT.

(34)

The free energy is
F=-T\log Z=-TS_{0}+TS_{eff}=-TS_{0}-2\pi^{2}MT^{2}
, yielding the entropy

S=-\frac{\partial F}{\partial T}=S_{0}+4\pi^{2}MT,

(35)

and specific heat,

C_{v}=-T\frac{\partial^{2}F}{\partial T^{2}}=4\pi^{2}MT.

(36)

From the linear-in-
T
scaling of
C_{v}
, we identify the Sommerfeld coefficient as
\gamma=4\pi^{2}M
.

To determine the static spin and charge susceptibilities, we introduce external background source fields via minimal coupling
D_{\tau}=\partial_{\tau}-A_{\tau}
. Let us focus on spin, and take the source
A_{\tau}^{s}=h_{s}\tfrac{\sigma^{z}}{2}
. Parameterising the fluctuation around the
U(1)
quantisation axis as
U_{s}(\tau)=e^{i\phi_{s}(\tau)\sigma^{z}/2}
, the spin contribution to the action is:

S_{eff}^{s}=-\lambda_{s}M\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau\,\text{Tr}\Big[\frac{i}{2}(\partial_{\tau}\phi_{s}+ih_{s})\sigma^{z}\Big]^{2}=\frac{\lambda_{s}M}{2}\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau\,(\partial_{\tau}\phi_{s}+ih_{s})^{2}.

(37)

This is the action of a compact quantum rotor, with the phase obeying the winding boundary condition
\phi_{s}(\beta)-\phi_{s}(0)=2\pi w
on the thermal circle,
w\in\mathbb{Z}
. In the semiclassical regime the partition sum is dominated by the trivial sector (
w=0
), yielding
\Delta F=-\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda_{s}Mh_{s}^{2}
. The temperature dependence cancels, giving a constant, Pauli-like static spin susceptibility:

\chi_{s}=-\frac{\partial^{2}\Delta F}{\partial h_{s}^{2}}=\lambda_{s}M=(1+\alpha)M.

(38)

The static charge susceptibility follows identically for
A_{\tau}^{c}=h_{c}\tfrac{\sigma^{z}}{2}
:

\chi_{c}=-\frac{\partial^{2}\Delta F}{\partial h_{c}^{2}}=\lambda_{c}M=\frac{1+\alpha}{\alpha}M.

(39)

The non-universal stiffness
M
can be eliminated by introducing the Wilson ratios:

\frac{\chi_{s}}{\gamma}=\frac{1+\alpha}{4\pi^{2}},\quad\frac{\chi_{c}}{\gamma}=\frac{1+\alpha}{4\pi^{2}\alpha},

(40)

which allow for direct experimental determination of
\alpha
. Moreover, combining these yields an exceptional thermodynamic relation:

\frac{1}{\chi_{s}}+\frac{1}{\chi_{c}}=\frac{4\pi^{2}}{\gamma},

(41)

a direct consequence of the super-Jacobi constraint, Eq. (
16
). This constitutes a parameter-free experimental prediction for the
D(2,1;\alpha)
strange metal, at leading order in the intermediate temperature regime of Eq. (
33
). Restoring the fundamental physical constants (
k_{B},\mu_{B},e
), we obtain Eq. (
1
) of the Introduction.

4.1.3
Quantum corrections

The semiclassical approximation receives corrections from the quantum fluctuations of the soft modes. As shown by Stanford and Witten
[
69
]
, the Schwarzian path integral localises and is one-loop exact, via the Duistermaat–Heckman formula, provided the super-reparameterisation symmetry is linearly realised (which is ensured in our case by the decoupled
U(1)
). In the general case, the resulting temperature dependence of the partition function takes the form
[
31
]
:

Z(\beta)\propto\left(\frac{M}{\beta}\right)^{p}e^{\frac{2\pi^{2}M}{\beta}},

(42)

with
p=\frac{N_{b}-N_{f}}{2}
, where
N_{b}
and
N_{f}
are the numbers of bosonic and fermionic generators of the global supergroup. This gives a logarithmic in
T
correction to the entropy,

S=S_{0}+4\pi^{2}MT+p\log(MT)+p,

(43)

and sets a constant offset to the specific heat,

C_{v}=4\pi^{2}MT+p.

(44)

The logarithmic divergence of the entropy at low-
T
is pre-empted by the edge of the conformal window,
T\sim\frac{1}{M}
, where the quantum correction becomes comparable to the saddle contribution and the expansion breaks down.

In our case the global supergroup is
D(2,1;\alpha)\times U(1)
, with
N_{b}=9+1
bosonic and
N_{f}=8
fermionic zero modes
3
3

3

The four fermions of the decoupled
U(1)
multiplet linearise the algebra but are not global zero modes, and so do not enter the count.
, yielding
p=1
. This matches the prefactor obtained for this theory in the holographic setting
[
32
]
.

The one-loop corrections to the susceptibilities are also computable via the localisation technique. Incorporating sources promotes the one-loop prefactor to the full
SU(2)
and fermion determinant, which encodes these corrections. This has been computed in the holographic context
[
32

, and we leave the translation of this result to the electronic setting for future work.

4.1.4
Charge symmetry breaking

So far we have focused on an idealised, manifestly
D(2,1;\alpha)
symmetric, conformal bath. We have motivated this, however, by the LFL–HFL transition, which we generally expect to occur away from half-filling. Doping away from half-filling explicitly breaks the charge
SU(2)
, and here we trace the consequences.

We focus on the charge kinetic term, including a chemical potential
\mu
measured from the particle-hole symmetric point:

S_{eff}^{c}=-\lambda_{c}M\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau\,\text{Tr}\big(U_{c}^{-1}\partial_{\tau}U_{c}-\mu\tfrac{\sigma^{z}}{2}\big)^{2},

(45)

which explicitly breaks the charge symmetry,
SU(2)_{c}\to U(1)_{c}
, lifting the degeneracy of the charge multiplet. As all fermionic modes are charged under
U(1)_{c}
, they acquire a charge gap of order
\mu
.

In the high-temperature regime,
T\gg\mu
, thermal fluctuations overwhelm this gap, and the system effectively retains its scale invariance. As
T
decreases,
\mu
sets a scale, and, as the free energy is even in
\mu
, the leading small-
\mu
correction to the semi-classical exceptional thermodynamic relation is quadratic:

\frac{\gamma}{4\pi^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{\chi_{s}}+\frac{1}{\chi_{c}}\right)-1\propto\left(\frac{\mu}{T}\right)^{2}.

(46)

As the system cools towards
T\sim\mu
, however, this correction grows to
O(1)
. All fermionic modes become gapped, and the emergent superconformal scale invariance of the bath is destroyed.

As
T
drops below this scale, the residual macroscopic entropy of the bath must be quenched. A natural route is that the fermionic modes gain spatial coherence, with the emergence of either the LFL or the HFL, and we return to discuss this in
Sec.
4.2
. However, if the superconformal symmetry is lost at a temperature above where either metallic state can become coherent, then the remaining route is through the opening of a gap via the spontaneous breaking of a residual symmetry,
U(1)_{c}
or
SU(2)_{s}
.

4.2
The fate of the electron

We now consider the fate of the electron in the presence of this
0+1
D bath. We characterise this dynamically through the conformal symmetry at
T=0
. When injecting an electron into the scale-invariant ground state, its asymptotic dynamics are captured by the dominant primary state,
|\Delta_{e}\rangle
, in its Operator Product Expansion (OPE). This gives its scaling dimension,
[c]=\Delta_{e}
, under a scale transformation,
\tau\to\lambda\tau
, dictating the long-time decay of the electronic Green’s function:

G(\tau)=-\langle\mathcal{T}c(\tau)c^{\dagger}(0)\rangle\propto\frac{\text{sgn}(\tau)}{|\tau|^{2\Delta_{e}}}.

(47)

From the anti-commutator of the
D(2,1;\alpha)
fermionic charges, we obtain the Bogomol’nyi-Prasad-Sommerfield (BPS) bound on
\Delta_{e}
, as a requirement of unitarity:

\displaystyle 0\leq\bra{\Delta_{e}}\{Q^{\sigma\nu},Q^{\dagger}_{\sigma\nu}\}\ket{\Delta_{e}}

\displaystyle=c_{1}\bra{\Delta_{e}}\Big(D+\frac{1}{\lambda_{s}}J^{\sigma}_{\sigma}+\frac{1}{\lambda_{c}}J^{\nu}_{\nu}\Big)\ket{\Delta_{e}}

(48)

\displaystyle=c_{1}\Big(\Delta_{e}+\frac{m_{s}}{\lambda_{s}}+\frac{m_{c}}{\lambda_{c}}\Big),

with
c_{1}>0
. For the physical electron, the spin and charge quantum numbers are
m_{s}=\pm\frac{1}{2}
and
m_{c}=\pm\frac{1}{2}
. Optimising the inequality over
m_{s}
and
m_{c}
to obtain the tightest version of the bound, and eliminating the coefficients via the super-Jacobi constraint, this gives:

\Delta_{e}\geq\frac{1}{2}.

(49)

To examine the relevance of spatial hopping, we consider the standard kinetic lattice action:

S_{kin}=\int d\tau\,H_{kin},\quad H_{kin}=-t\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}\left(c^{\dagger}_{i}c_{j}+\text{h.c.}\right).

(50)

Under the
0+1
D RG scaling,
[\tau]=-1
, this dictates the scaling dimension of the hopping parameter:

[t]=1-2\Delta_{e}.

(51)

Let us first consider
\Delta_{e}>\frac{1}{2}
. Here
[t]<0
, hopping is an irrelevant perturbation, and the electron is localised in the superconformal bath. The Green’s function scales anomalously, reflecting the dissolution of a coherent quasiparticle.

Alternatively, as the system is tuned towards the bound
\Delta_{e}=\frac{1}{2}
, the Green’s function scales as
G(\tau)\propto\tau^{-1}
, indicating the emergence of a stable mode with zero scattering rate. Hopping becomes a marginal perturbation
[t]=0
, which frees the electron, and permits a dimensional crossover where a spatial length scale re-emerges and the coherent LFL is restored.

4.2.1
The Hubbard–Fermi liquid transition

Following this discussion for the canonical electron operator
c
, we now turn to the Hubbard formulation. The logic largely carries over, with one major caveat.

The fermionic generators
q
are governed by an identical BPS bound
\Delta_{q}\geq\frac{1}{2}
. Expressing a kinetic term through the
q
,
H_{kin}=-t\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}\big(q^{\dagger}_{i}q_{j}+\text{h.c.}\big)
, the hopping parameter again scales as
[t]=1-2\Delta_{q}
. Within the
0+1
D bath the hopping is thus irrelevant for
\Delta_{q}>\frac{1}{2}
, and the electron is localised.

There is a fundamental obstruction, however, to exiting the bath as
\Delta_{q}\to\frac{1}{2}
. Unlike the canonical case, where the conformal sector can contract without restriction on the value of
\alpha
(
Sec.
3.1
), the contraction to the Hubbard algebra in the Beisert limit requires
\alpha=\frac{c_{3}}{c_{2}}\to-1
(
Sec.
3.2
). This is the isolated degenerate point
\lambda_{s}=\lambda_{c}=0
, separated from the thermodynamically stable regime
\alpha>0
of the bath. We thus conclude that the HFL cannot emerge smoothly from the strange metal. Instead the transition must be a discontinuous at
T=0
, and it is natural to identify this with the rearrangement of the Fermi surface discussed in
Sec.
2.3
.

4.2.2
The finite-temperature endpoint

We argue that this
T=0
discontinuity extends to a finite-temperature endpoint on entropic grounds. At intermediate temperature, thermal fluctuations permit a crossover from the bath to the HFL, and we denote this coherence scale as
T^{*}_{\rm HFL}
, as in
Sec.
2.3
. Likewise, we denote the coherence scale of the LFL as
T^{*}_{\rm LFL}
, and we can ask where these two crossover lines intersect. In the absence of ordering, the intersection point cannot extend down to zero temperature, as this would expose the residual entropy of the superconformal bath. Instead the crossover lines must meet at finite temperature,
T_{e}
, and we interpret this, the termination point of the superconformal bath, as the critical endpoint of a line that extends down to the
T=0
discontinuity.

If ordering does intervene first, then it pre-empts the endpoint, and we discuss this alternative possibility in
Sec.
4.4
.

4.2.3
The loss of spatial coherence

Up to this point, we have conjectured the loss of spatial coherence that gives rise to the
0+1
D bath. In
Sec.
4.2
we showed that the bath is stable: once formed, electronic hopping is an irrelevant perturbation. We now discuss this dimensional reduction from the perspective of the two metallic states.

That the LFL–HFL transition is discontinuous, with a finite-temperature endpoint, has an important consequence: both Fermi liquids remain robust near the transition, with their respective coherence temperatures finite. The quasiparticle weight remains finite, and the transition is non-perturbative from either metallic ground state. Thus, we cannot expect to trace the dimensional reduction within either framework.

We highlight that the transition itself is of an unconventional kind. While it is discontinuous with a finite-temperature endpoint, it is not a conventional symmetry-breaking transition, as the two metallic states are not distinguished by a local order parameter. Instead, they are distinguished by the topology of their Fermi surfaces, each protected by a robust quasiparticle.

Near the transition, both formulations of the electron are reflected in the spectrum, but the low-energy weight is determined by which of the two has the higher coherence scale, with its quasiparticle defining the Fermi surface (
Fig.
1
(a)). Below
T_{e}
the transition is discontinuous, and the Fermi surface rearranges across it. At the approach to
T_{e}
the transition becomes continuous, but the two formulations cannot simultaneously govern the low-energy dynamics as their Fermi surfaces are incompatible. We argue that, where their coherence mechanisms compete, a coherent momentum-space description must break down.

Our conjecture for the strange metal is that here only the local degrees of freedom remain to organise the spectrum, and that these arrange into the uncontracted representation of
D(2,1;\alpha)
, giving rise to the scale-invariant
0+1
D bath at the junction of the two metallic regimes.

4.3
Transport

As the electron is dissolved within the bath, it cannot mediate transport in the usual sense. With no spatial coherence, there can be no Drude response, reflected in the anomalous scaling of the Green’s function, (
47
), for
\Delta_{e}>1/2
. Instead, the system is an incoherent metal
[
28
]
, with transport arising through the diffusion of the conserved charge, spin and energy densities of the bath, in analogy with transport in coupled-SYK lattices
[
26
,
67
]
. The conductivities are set by the Einstein relations,

\sigma_{c}=\chi_{c}D_{c},\qquad\sigma_{s}=\chi_{s}D_{s},

(52)

with the susceptibilities
\chi_{c}
and
\chi_{s}
fixed by the thermodynamics of
Sec.
4.1
. At the semiclassical saddle these susceptibilities are constant, and constrained by the exceptional thermodynamic relation, Eq. (
41
). At this leading order the temperature dependence of the conductivity therefore resides entirely in the diffusion constants,
D_{c}
and
D_{s}
. Their determination requires an analysis of spatial coupling, and we leave this to future work.

5
Microscopic framing

So far our discussion has been completely general, based on the assumption that the LFL and HFL exist as distinct metallic states, and our conjecture that the superconformal algebra
D(2,1;\alpha)
governs the transition between them via an emergent superconformal bath.

We now turn to a microscopic perspective, as an exploration of where the LFL–HFL transition may occur. As a function of band filling, we expect that the HFL regime, when it exists, is naturally anchored around half-filling, while the LFL regime extends from the band edges. A natural question then is whether there are limits in the parameter space of microscopic models where the LFL–HFL transition occurs right at half-filling, while a Mott gap is maintained, or alternatively where the HFL regime extends to the edges of its split bands. Here we leverage some exact results to suggest that the former occurs for the standard Hubbard model, while the latter occurs for the Hubbard-Shastry B (HSB) model.

5.1

We first consider a general single-orbital tight-binding model, and will then focus on a two-parameter subspace with some notable limits around which we will frame our discussion. Specifically we take a nearest-neighbour Hamiltonian on a bipartite lattice,

5.1.3
Mapping the transition

We now consider the location of the LFL–HFL transition as a function of filling. Here we focus on the parent metallic states, overlooking ordering instabilities. We argue that the Hubbard model is a limit where the HFL phase is fully suppressed and the LFL extends to the Mott boundary at half-filling, while a Mott gap is maintained, and that HSB and EKS are models where the HFL extends to the extremes of the fully empty and fully occupied band.

For the Hubbard model this is borne out in the infinite-dimensional limit. Single-site DMFT shows that once a Mott gap opens at half-filling, it is separated from the doped metallic state by a first-order transition, and this remains the case as the strength of the Hubbard interaction is increased
[
22
,
40
]
. Employed as an approximation in finite dimensions, it yields the frequency dependence of a momentum-independent self-energy, with the metallic state obeying the Luttinger sum rule, and the inference is that this describes a LFL that extends to the discontinuous Mott boundary. We highlight that this is consistent with the kinetic term of the Hubbard model having the conventional canonical bilinear form, (
\kappa=\lambda=0
). We suggest that the finite-temperature endpoint
T_{e}
merging with the Mott boundary here is a special feature of this model. Within our framework, this corresponds to the subtle
\alpha\to 0
limit discussed at the end of
Sec.
4.4
, and we will return to this again from both a material and criticality perspective in
Sec.
5.2.1
.

Taking
\kappa>0
introduces correlated hopping and spin and charge interactions. Generically, we expect a HFL regime to open up as these are introduced, with the consequence that the LFL–HFL transition moves away from half-filling.

For the HSB model, the evidence that the HFL regime extends across the whole (split) band is its protected fermionic modes, Eq. (
64
). As these carry momenta
\vec{0}
and
\vec{\pi}
, we interpret these as modes at the edges of the band. Moreover, in the limit to the EKS model at
\kappa\to 1
, and generally when
t_{\pm}=0
, it follows from Eqs. (
65
) and (
66
) that the electronic spectrum is strictly split into two, with the Hubbard interaction shifting the split bands oppositely in the spectrum. We thus suggest that the HFL phase extends across the whole band for the HSB model. This has a notable consequence: the band remains split with a Mott gap for all
0<\kappa\leq 1
, with the gap only vanishing in the non-interacting limit,
\kappa\to 0
. We distinguish this from the Hubbard model, where above 1d the Mott gap only opens at finite
U
. We interpret this as follows. For the Hubbard model the split bands are incoherent, and the Mott gap opens once these shift apart. By contrast, for the HSB model the splitting occurs directly at the centre of the electronic band, opening a gap for
\kappa>0
.

Moreover, this analysis offers an interpretation of the HSB model’s dynamical fermionic symmetry as another special limit of our framework. The edges of the band represent the dilute limits of electronic excitations. As the LFL–HFL transition approaches these extremes, the residual entropy of the
0+1
D bath ceases to be extensive, and the critical endpoint
T_{e}
drops to zero. From the perspective of
D(2,1;\alpha)
, this corresponds to the limit
\alpha\to 1
, where the algebra becomes
\mathfrak{osp}(4|2)
(
App.
C

[

]
.

5.2
Material perspective

This framing may help to understand which microscopic interactions drive the stability of the HFL at finite doping. In turn, this influences the temperature of the ordering instability of the strange metal, as discussed in
Sec.
4.1.4
.

To ground this in a material context, it is instructive to view our framing through the lens of the Zaanen-Sawatzky-Allen (ZSA) classification of transition-metal compounds
[
74
]
. The ZSA scheme distinguishes correlated insulators based on the competition between two energy scales: the on-site Coulomb repulsion
U
of the transition-metal
d
-orbitals, and the charge-transfer energy
\Delta
required to move an electron from the surrounding ligand
p
-orbitals to the metal. Their competition leads to the following dichotomy:

•

Mott-Hubbard insulator:
U<\Delta
. The low-energy excitations are confined to the transition metal sites, and Hubbard
U
is the dominant interaction.

•

Charge-transfer insulator:
\Delta<U
. The low-energy excitations are hybridized metal-ligand states, resulting in an effective single-orbital description with correlated hopping amplitudes, along with local spin and charge interactions.

The Hubbard model is the paradigmatic model of a Mott-Hubbard insulator. Although the specific couplings of the HSB model are fine-tuned and do not map to measured material parameters, we offer the perspective that it can be viewed as an effective single-orbital model of a theoretically idealised charge-transfer insulator.

5.2.1
Mott criticality

We focus now on Mott-Hubbard insulators. These are expected to be well-described by the Hubbard model, and so within our framework the critical endpoint is merged with the Mott boundary. The critical behaviour here is termed Mott criticality, and the prevailing paradigm is a DMFT prediction of a mean-field, liquid-gas Ising universality class
[
40
]
.

The status of this prediction is, however, unsettled. In a canonical Mott-Hubbard insulator Cr-doped V
2
O
3
mean-field scaling is reported

Our framework offers a fresh perspective. As discussed at the end of

We can also track this limit algebraically. The limit

At the level of the semi-classical saddle, this gives the same

Let us offer an interpretation of the disagreement with the DMFT result. In single-site DMFT, the local problem is partitioned: an electron, with its four-state basis, is coupled to a self-consistent

0+1

The best-known charge-transfer insulators are the cuprates. These are thus natural candidates for an experimental realisation of our general framework. We map here the correspondence with their phenomenology at a qualitative level.

6
Discussion

We have developed an electronic interpretation of the exceptional Lie superalgebra
D(2,1;\alpha)
, demonstrating that its structure encodes a phenomenology mapping directly onto the strongly correlated electron problem. Within this framework,
D(2,1;\alpha)
governs an effective
0+1
D superconformal bath, which we interpret as the strange metal. We argued that the LFL and HFL metallic states emerge as spatial coherence is restored, with the canonical and Hubbard formulations of the electron arising as distinct contractions of its conformal sector. We also examined the limit where the HFL is suppressed, and traced the emergence of Mott criticality.

Central to this framework is the LFL–HFL transition. It appears to be of an unconventional kind: it is discontinuous,
with a finite-temperature critical endpoint, yet there is no associated symmetry-breaking. The two metallic states are distinguished not by a local order parameter, but by the topology of their Fermi surfaces, each protected by a robust quasiparticle. Within our framework, this transition has its origin in the deformable structure constants of
D(2,1;\alpha)
: the superconformal bath is thermodynamically stable for
\alpha>0
, whereas the Hubbard contraction is locked to the degenerate point
\alpha=-1
, and thus the HFL cannot emerge continuously from the bath (
Sec.
4.2.1
). We are not aware of a parallel transition of this kind, and it is natural to relate this to the status of
D(2,1;\alpha)
as the only simple Lie superalgebra with continuously deformable structure constants. In this sense, the transition is exceptional in its origin.

From our semiclassical analysis of the thermodynamics of the superconformal bath, we obtained a parameter-free experimental prediction, Eq. (
41
), relating the Sommerfeld coefficient to the static spin and charge susceptibilities. This provides a falsifiable test of our framework. Potential testing grounds are the strange metal regimes of the cuprates
[
38
]
and heavy-fermion compounds
[
70
]
. While the thermal and spin properties of these materials have been extensively characterised, the precise measurement of the charge response has proven more challenging
[
1
]
. More recently, 2D moiré materials, such as magic-angle twisted bilayer graphene, have been shown to exhibit strange metal behaviour
[
11
]
. Here the experimental difficulty is inverted:
\chi_{c}
is accessible via local compressibility measurements
[
51
]
, but
\gamma
and
\chi_{s}
are more challenging owing to the small sample volume.

Beyond direct experimental measurement, our framework can be investigated numerically. Prominent here are the DMFT family of methods
[
22
,
45
]
. Exact in the limit of infinite dimensions, the approach offers a window onto the metallic, insulating and critical regimes within a single framework. Cluster extensions, which incorporate short-range spatial correlations, have been shown to describe discontinuous finite-temperature transition away from half-filling
[
68
]
. While the thermodynamic variables are computable within this approach, we are not aware of a single study that simultaneously extracts all three (
\gamma
,
\chi_{s}
,
\chi_{c}
) for the critical regime. There is a caveat worth highlighting, however. As discussed in
Sec.
5.2.1
, single-site DMFT renders the critical endpoint mean-field. It would be interesting to determine whether cluster extensions can resolve the intrinsically local superconformal criticality predicted by our framework.

Our analysis of the thermodynamics of the superconformal bath leaves several open directions. While we focused primarily on the semiclassical saddle-point approximation, the one-loop quantum correction is exact, as discussed in
Sec.
4.1.3
. For the entropy this can be inferred from the temperature dependence of the source-free partition function, and we obtained a logarithmic correction. The spin and charge response requires incorporating sources, and we leave the analysis of this to future work. It would be of interest in particular to establish whether there is a subleading correction to the exceptional thermodynamic relation, which may have its own thermodynamic signature. The study of transport is another open direction. We have argued that spin and charge must diffuse, as governed by the Einstein relations, and leave the analysis of the diffusion constants to future work.

The Hubbard–Fermi liquid is a pillar of our framework. While we have argued that it is a legitimate state of electronic matter, there is not yet a settled framework for characterising it. We highlighted in
Sec.
2.2.1
that the requirement of a conserving,
\Phi
-derivable perturbative scheme may be too stringent, due to the intrinsic curvature of the Hubbard algebra. As spin-wave theory faces the same obstruction, we view this as a problem of method rather than of existence. The development of a systematic framework for the HFL thus remains an important outstanding direction.

It is instructive to contrast the HFL with other proposals for a metal which violates the Luttinger sum rule. The fractionalised Fermi liquid, FL
∗
, is an approach that factorises the electron,
c\sim f\,b
, accompanied by an emergent gauge field, and is typically formulated at the level of an effective field theory rather than a microscopic operator identity
[
62
]
. In contrast, the HFL is a linear splitting of the electron, e.g.
c^{\dagger}_{\downarrow}=q_{\uparrow\circ}-q^{\dagger}_{\downarrow\bullet}
, such that excitations retain the electronic quantum numbers. Another prominent approach is the notion of Mottness, which ties the Luttinger violation to zeros of the canonical Green’s function
[
53
]
. Within our framework, the canonical quasiparticle remains robust right up to the LFL–HFL transition, beyond which the low-energy spectrum is organised by the Hubbard formulation, and zeros of the canonical Green’s function play no role.

Our framework extends naturally to Kondo lattice systems
[
55
]
, relevant for heavy-fermion compounds
[
70
]
. As detailed in
App.
B
, the local moment is incorporated by enlarging the
\mathfrak{su}(2|2)
representation, with the conduction-electron
\vec{s}
and local moment
\vec{S}
entering through the combination
\vec{\Sigma}=\vec{s}+\vec{S}
. The Kondo coupling then contributes to the emergent chemical potential that splits the band, and the splitting of the electron carries over. In this setting we regard the Heavy Fermi liquid as an instance of the HFL, with the local moment entwined with the electron rather than free to order independently. We can thus view the heavy-fermion phase diagram from the perspective of the LFL–HFL transition, with the strange metal emerging from the rearrangement of the Fermi surface.

Finally, while our proposal for the exceptional origin of the strange metal stands independent of holographic duality, we highlight that our framework has deep roots in the literature of this subject. The algebraic structures we employed have been developed within the study of integrability in the AdS/CFT correspondence
[
6
,
9
]
, notably Beisert’s discovery that the exceptionally extended
\mathfrak{su}(2|2)
algebra governs the integrability of the 1D Hubbard model, and its relationship to
D(2,1;\alpha)

[
8
]
. In addition, the superconformal theories we invoke for the strange metal and Mott criticality have been studied as the near-horizon descriptions of extremal black holes
[
30
,
32
,
31
]
, and these results helped inform our formulation of the
D(2,1;\alpha)
effective action. Looking forward, it may be interesting to explore a holographic description of the LFL–HFL transition.

7
Conclusion

We have developed a unified algebraic framework for the strongly correlated electron problem, encompassing the LFL and HFL metallic states, the strange metal, and Mott criticality. The two metallic states descend as distinct contractions of the exceptional Lie superalgebra
D(2,1;\alpha)
, the unique simple Lie superalgebra with continuously deformable structure constants. We have argued that this deformability is the origin of the transition between the two metals, and of the superconformal strange metal that emerges from it. From the thermodynamics of this critical regime we obtained a parameter-free relation between the Sommerfeld coefficient and the static spin and charge susceptibilities, a prediction by which the framework can be tested.

Appendix A
The Hubbard algebra

Here we provide a summary of the exceptionally centrally extended Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{su}(2|2)

D(2,1;\alpha)
, as detailed in
Sec.
3.2
.

The
\mathfrak{su}(2|2)
algebra is generated by two bosonic
\mathfrak{su}(2)
triplets,
\vec{s}
and
\vec{\eta}
, and eight fermionic generators
q_{\sigma\nu}
and
q^{\dagger}_{\sigma\nu}
, with indices
\sigma\in\{\uparrow,\downarrow\}
and
\nu\in\{\bullet,\circ\}
. The two
\mathfrak{su}(2)
algebras mutually commute, and writing
s^{\pm}=s^{x}\pm is^{y}
and
\eta^{\pm}=\eta^{x}\pm i\eta^{y}
, their
\mathfrak{su}(2)
relations are given by

[s^{+},s^{-}]=2s^{z},\quad[s^{z},s^{\pm}]=\pm s^{\pm},

(69)

[\eta^{+},\eta^{-}]=2\eta^{z},\quad[\eta^{z},\eta^{\pm}]=\pm\eta^{\pm}

(70)

The fermionic generators transform in the fundamental representations of the two
\mathfrak{su}(2)

[s^{z},q^{\dagger}_{\sigma\nu}]=\frac{1}{2}\sigma q^{\dagger}_{\sigma\nu},\quad[s^{\pm},q^{\dagger}_{\mp\nu}]=-q^{\dagger}_{\pm\nu},\quad[s^{\pm},q_{\pm\nu}]=q_{\mp\nu},

(71)

[\eta^{z},q^{\dagger}_{\sigma\nu}]=\frac{1}{2}\nu q^{\dagger}_{\sigma\nu},\quad[\eta^{\pm},q^{\dagger}_{\sigma\mp}]=-q^{\dagger}_{\sigma\pm},\quad[\eta^{\pm},q_{\sigma\pm}]=q_{\sigma\mp},

(72)

where as coefficients the indices take the values
+1
for
\{\uparrow,\bullet\}
and
-1
for
\{\downarrow,\circ\}
. The anti-commutators of the fermionic generators close back onto the bosonic sector, parameterized by a continuous parameter
\kappa
:

\displaystyle\{q_{\sigma\nu},q^{\dagger}_{\sigma\nu}\}=\frac{1+\kappa^{2}}{4}-\kappa\left(\sigma s^{z}-\nu\eta^{z}\right),

(73)

\displaystyle\{q_{\downarrow\nu},q^{\dagger}_{\uparrow\nu}\}=\kappa s^{+},\qquad\{q_{\sigma\circ},q^{\dagger}_{\sigma\bullet}\}=-\kappa\eta^{+},

\displaystyle\{q_{\uparrow\nu},q^{\dagger}_{\downarrow\nu}\}=\kappa s^{-},\qquad\{q_{\sigma\bullet},q^{\dagger}_{\sigma\circ}\}=-\kappa\eta^{-},

\displaystyle\{q_{\sigma\nu},q_{\sigma^{\prime}\nu^{\prime}}\}=\{q^{\dagger}_{\sigma\nu},q^{\dagger}_{\sigma^{\prime}\nu^{\prime}}\}=\frac{1-\kappa^{2}}{4}\epsilon_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\epsilon_{\nu\nu^{\prime}},

where as coefficients
\sigma
,
\nu
take values
+1
for
\{\uparrow,\bullet\}
and
-1
for
\{\downarrow,\circ\}
, and
\epsilon
is the antisymmetric tensor with convention
\epsilon_{\downarrow\uparrow}=\epsilon_{\circ\bullet}=1
.
The algebra is extended to
\mathfrak{u}(2|2)
through the additional generator
\theta
obeying

\displaystyle=\frac{1+\kappa^{2}}{4\kappa}q^{\dagger}_{\sigma\nu}-\frac{1-\kappa^{2}}{4\kappa}\epsilon_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\epsilon_{\nu\nu^{\prime}}q_{\sigma^{\prime}\nu^{\prime}}

(74)

\displaystyle[\theta,q_{\sigma\nu}]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1+\kappa^{2}}{4\kappa}q_{\sigma\nu}+\frac{1-\kappa^{2}}{4\kappa}\epsilon_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\epsilon_{\nu\nu^{\prime}}q^{\dagger}_{\sigma^{\prime}\nu^{\prime}}.

Precisely at
\kappa=1
, the algebra maps to the Hubbard projection operators,
X_{ab}=|a\rangle\langle b|
, satisfying the graded algebra:

[X_{ab},X_{cd}\}_{\pm}=\delta_{bc}X_{ad}\pm\delta_{ad}X_{cb},

(75)

where the anti-commutator is used if and only if both operators are fermionic, and the commutator is used otherwise. The explicit mapping is:

\displaystyle X_{\nu\sigma}

\displaystyle=\bar{\nu}q^{\dagger}_{\bar{\sigma}\nu},\quad

\displaystyle X_{\nu\nu}

\displaystyle=\nu\eta^{z}+\theta+\frac{1}{4},\quad

\displaystyle X_{\bullet\circ}

\displaystyle=\eta^{+},\quad

\displaystyle X_{\circ\bullet}

\displaystyle=\eta^{-},

(76)

\displaystyle X_{\sigma\nu}

\displaystyle=\bar{\nu}q_{\bar{\sigma}\nu},\quad

\displaystyle X_{\sigma\sigma}

\displaystyle=\sigma s^{z}-\theta+\frac{1}{4},\quad

\displaystyle X_{\uparrow\downarrow}

\displaystyle=s^{+},\quad

\displaystyle X_{\downarrow\uparrow}

\displaystyle=s^{-},

where
\bar{\sigma}=-\sigma
and
\bar{\nu}=-\nu
.

We now express the
\mathfrak{su}(2|2)
generators through the canonical fermion
c_{\sigma}
,
c^{\dagger}_{\sigma}
and number operator
n_{\sigma}=c^{\dagger}_{\sigma}c_{\sigma}
. The fermionic generators are:

\displaystyle q^{\dagger}_{\sigma\circ}=\frac{1+\kappa}{2}c_{\bar{\sigma}}-\kappa n_{\sigma}c_{\bar{\sigma}},\qquad q^{\dagger}_{\sigma\bullet}=\sigma\big(\frac{1-\kappa}{2}c^{\dagger}_{\sigma}+\kappa n_{\bar{\sigma}}c^{\dagger}_{\sigma}\big),

(77)

\displaystyle q_{\sigma\circ}=\frac{1+\kappa}{2}c^{\dagger}_{\bar{\sigma}}-\kappa n_{\sigma}c^{\dagger}_{\bar{\sigma}},\qquad q_{\sigma\bullet}=\sigma\big(\frac{1-\kappa}{2}c_{\sigma}+\kappa n_{\bar{\sigma}}c_{\sigma}\big),

and inverting for
c^{\dagger}_{\sigma}
yields the splitting relations:

c^{\dagger}_{\downarrow}=q_{\uparrow\circ}-q^{\dagger}_{\downarrow\bullet},\qquad c^{\dagger}_{\uparrow}=q_{\downarrow\circ}+q^{\dagger}_{\uparrow\bullet}.

(78)

The spin and charge generators are:

\displaystyle s^{+}=c^{\dagger}_{\uparrow}c_{\downarrow},\qquad s^{-}=c^{\dagger}_{\downarrow}c_{\uparrow},\qquad s^{z}=\tfrac{1}{2}(n_{\uparrow}-n_{\downarrow}),

(79)

\displaystyle\eta^{+}=c^{\dagger}_{\downarrow}c^{\dagger}_{\uparrow},\qquad\eta^{-}=c_{\uparrow}c_{\downarrow},\qquad\eta^{z}=\tfrac{1}{2}(n_{\uparrow}+n_{\downarrow}-1),

and together obey the Casimir identity
\vec{s}\cdot\vec{s}+\vec{\eta}\cdot\vec{\eta}=\frac{3}{4}
. The Hubbard generator is:

\theta=\tfrac{1}{3}\big(\vec{\eta}\cdot\vec{\eta}-\vec{s}\cdot\vec{s}\big)=\big(n_{\downarrow}-\tfrac{1}{2}\big)\big(n_{\uparrow}-\tfrac{1}{2}\big).

(80)

Appendix B
The Kondo degree of freedom

We call the combination of an electron and a local
\mathfrak{su}(2)
spin a Kondo degree of freedom. We denote the spin as
\vec{S}
, obeying
\vec{S}\cdot\vec{S}=S(S+1)
, with
S
the size of the spin. The Kondo degree of freedom then has a basis of
4\times(2S+1)
states.

As for the electron, we consider two distinct formulations of the Kondo degree of freedom. The canonical formulation is the conventional one, with the canonical electron
\mathfrak{su}(1|1)\oplus\mathfrak{su}(1|1)
and the
\mathfrak{su}(2)
spin combining as
\mathfrak{su}(1|1)\oplus\mathfrak{su}(1|1)\oplus\mathfrak{su}(2)
. This underlies a description of a LFL interacting with local spins that are free to order.

The second formulation is a natural generalisation of the Hubbard formulation of the electron
[
55
,
58
]
. Just as the non-canonical algebra
\mathfrak{su}(2)
admits a family of representations, so too does
\mathfrak{su}(2|2)
. These can be directly interpreted as incorporating a spin
\vec{S}
, and have dimensionality
4\times(2S+1)
. The Hubbard electron corresponds to the additional spin being a singlet
S=0
. The spin enters the algebra through the combination,

\vec{\Sigma}=\vec{s}+\vec{S},

(81)

which obeys the spin
\mathfrak{su}(2)
relations. This enters the fermionic anticommutation relations as follows:

\displaystyle\{q_{\sigma\nu},q^{\dagger}_{\sigma\nu}\}=\frac{1+\kappa^{2}}{4}-\frac{\kappa}{2S+1}\left(\sigma\Sigma^{z}-\nu\eta^{z}\right),

(82)

\displaystyle\{q_{\downarrow\nu},q^{\dagger}_{\uparrow\nu}\}=\frac{\kappa}{2S+1}\Sigma^{+},\qquad\{q_{\sigma\circ},q^{\dagger}_{\sigma\bullet}\}=-\frac{\kappa}{2S+1}\eta^{+},

\displaystyle\{q_{\uparrow\nu},q^{\dagger}_{\downarrow\nu}\}=\frac{\kappa}{2S+1}\Sigma^{-},\qquad\{q_{\sigma\bullet},q^{\dagger}_{\sigma\circ}\}=-\frac{\kappa}{2S+1}\eta^{-},

\displaystyle\{q_{\sigma\nu},q_{\sigma^{\prime}\nu^{\prime}}\}=\{q^{\dagger}_{\sigma\nu},q^{\dagger}_{\sigma^{\prime}\nu^{\prime}}\}=\frac{1-\kappa^{2}}{4}\epsilon_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\epsilon_{\nu\nu^{\prime}}.

All other relations are the same as those for the Hubbard electron. The additional generator
\theta
obeying,

\displaystyle=\frac{1+\kappa^{2}}{4\kappa}q^{\dagger}_{\sigma\nu}-\frac{1-\kappa^{2}}{4\kappa}\epsilon_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\epsilon_{\nu\nu^{\prime}}q_{\sigma^{\prime}\nu^{\prime}}

(83)

\displaystyle[\theta,q_{\sigma\nu}]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1+\kappa^{2}}{4\kappa}q_{\sigma\nu}+\frac{1-\kappa^{2}}{4\kappa}\epsilon_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\epsilon_{\nu\nu^{\prime}}q^{\dagger}_{\sigma^{\prime}\nu^{\prime}},

can be expressed through the
\mathfrak{su}(2|2)
generators as follows,

\theta=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2S+1}\left(\vec{\Sigma}\cdot\vec{\Sigma}+\frac{1}{3}\vec{\eta}\cdot\vec{\eta}\right).

(84)

Alternatively, employing
\vec{S}\cdot\vec{S}=S(S+1)
and
\vec{s}\cdot\vec{s}+\vec{\eta}\cdot\vec{\eta}=\frac{3}{4}
, we can relate it to the Kondo coupling:

\vec{s}\cdot\vec{S}=\frac{1}{3}\vec{\eta}\cdot\vec{\eta}-\frac{2S+1}{2}\theta-\frac{1+4S^{2}}{8}.

(85)

As for the Hubbard electron, the fermionic Kondo generators again manifest a splitting of the electron:

c^{\dagger}_{\downarrow}=q_{\uparrow\circ}-q^{\dagger}_{\downarrow\bullet},\quad c^{\dagger}_{\uparrow}=q_{\downarrow\circ}+q^{\dagger}_{\uparrow\bullet}.

(86)

They can be expressed in terms of the canonical electron and spin as follows,

\displaystyle q^{\dagger}_{\downarrow\circ}=\frac{1}{2}c_{\uparrow}+\frac{\kappa}{2S+1}\left(\frac{1}{2}c_{\uparrow}-n_{\downarrow}c_{\uparrow}+c_{\downarrow}S^{-}+c_{\uparrow}S^{z}\right),

(87)

\displaystyle q^{\dagger}_{\uparrow\circ}=\frac{1}{2}c_{\downarrow}+\frac{\kappa}{2S+1}\left(\frac{1}{2}c_{\downarrow}-n_{\uparrow}c_{\downarrow}+c_{\uparrow}S^{+}-c_{\downarrow}S^{z}\right),

\displaystyle q^{\dagger}_{\downarrow\bullet}=-\frac{1}{2}c^{\dagger}_{\downarrow}+\frac{\kappa}{2S+1}\left(\frac{1}{2}c^{\dagger}_{\downarrow}-n_{\uparrow}c^{\dagger}_{\downarrow}+c^{\dagger}_{\uparrow}S^{-}-c^{\dagger}_{\downarrow}S^{z}\right),

\displaystyle q^{\dagger}_{\uparrow\bullet}=\frac{1}{2}c^{\dagger}_{\uparrow}-\frac{\kappa}{2S+1}\left(\frac{1}{2}c^{\dagger}_{\uparrow}-n_{\downarrow}c^{\dagger}_{\uparrow}+c^{\dagger}_{\downarrow}S^{+}+c^{\dagger}_{\uparrow}S^{z}\right),

which satisfy Eq. (
82
) upon use of the Casimir identity
\vec{S}\cdot\vec{S}=S(S+1)
.

This Hubbard formulation of the Kondo degree of freedom provides a HFL description of the Kondo lattice. Here the spin is entwined with the electron, and not free to order by itself. The Kondo coupling is conveniently treated in this approach via Eq. (
85
), from which we see it contributes to the emergent chemical potential driving a splitting of the electronic band. Again the Luttinger sum rule is generically violated for this metallic state, and the competition between the LFL+spin and HFL offers a natural interpretation of the phase diagram of Heavy Fermion materials
[
70
]
, with the Heavy Fermi liquid identified as the HFL.

To draw the connection to the strange metal, let us highlight the contractions of
D(2,1;\alpha)
of
Sec.
3
in this setting. For the maximal contraction, the spin belongs to the decoupled part of the representation. For the partial contraction, the shortening condition, Eq. (
25
), generalises to:

\tilde{D}^{2}-|\tilde{E}|^{2}=\frac{2S+1}{4},

(88)

which yields the
4(2S+1)
-dimensional representations of the spin-
S
Kondo degree of freedom
[
7
]
.

Appendix C
The Exceptional Lie Superalgebra
D(2,1;\alpha)

In
Sec.
3
we considered a non-compact real form of the exceptional Lie superalgebra
D(2,1;\alpha)
. Here we detail the compact form, as it may help orient a reader unfamiliar with these structures, and highlight some notable limits.

The algebra
D(2,1;\alpha)
comprises three bosonic
\mathfrak{su}(2)
triplets
J^{i}_{j}
,
J^{a}_{b}
,
J^{\alpha}_{\beta}
, and a fermionic octet
Q^{ia\alpha}
, with all indices taking values in
\{1,2\}
. The
\mathfrak{su}(2)
factors commute with one another and obey the
\mathfrak{su}(2)
relations internally:

\displaystyle=\delta^{k}_{j}J^{i}_{l}-\delta^{i}_{l}J^{k}_{j},

(89)

\displaystyle[J^{a}_{b},J^{c}_{d}]

\displaystyle=\delta^{c}_{b}J^{a}_{d}-\delta^{a}_{d}J^{c}_{b},

\displaystyle[J^{\alpha}_{\beta},J^{\gamma}_{\delta}]

\displaystyle=\delta^{\gamma}_{\beta}J^{\alpha}_{\delta}-\delta^{\alpha}_{\delta}J^{\gamma}_{\beta}.

The fermionic generators transform in the fundamental representation of each
\mathfrak{su}(2)
factor:

\displaystyle=\delta^{k}_{j}Q^{ia\alpha}-\frac{1}{2}\delta^{i}_{j}Q^{ka\alpha},

(90)

\displaystyle[J^{a}_{b},Q^{ic\alpha}]

\displaystyle=\delta^{c}_{b}Q^{ia\alpha}-\frac{1}{2}\delta^{a}_{b}Q^{ic\alpha},

\displaystyle[J^{\alpha}_{\beta},Q^{ia\gamma}]

\displaystyle=\delta^{\gamma}_{\beta}Q^{ia\alpha}-\frac{1}{2}\delta^{\alpha}_{\beta}Q^{ia\gamma},

and their anticommutators close onto the bosonic generators:

\{Q^{ia\alpha},Q^{jb\beta}\}=c_{1}\epsilon^{ab}\epsilon^{\alpha\beta}\epsilon^{ik}J^{j}_{k}+c_{2}\epsilon^{ij}\epsilon^{\alpha\beta}\epsilon^{ac}J^{b}_{c}+c_{3}\epsilon^{ij}\epsilon^{ab}\epsilon^{\alpha\gamma}J^{\beta}_{\gamma},

(91)

weighted by coefficients
c_{1}
,
c_{2}
,
c_{3}
constrained to obey,

c_{1}+c_{2}+c_{3}=0,

(92)

as the requirement of the super-Jacobi identity, and we set
\alpha=c_{3}/c_{2}
.

The non-compact real form employed in
Sec.
3
is obtained by casting the
\mathfrak{su}(2)
factor
J^{i}_{j}
as a conformal
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})

D=J^{2}_{2}=-J^{1}_{1},\quad E_{+}=J^{2}_{1},\quad E_{-}=J^{1}_{2},

(93)

via the reality conditions
D^{\dagger}=D
and
E_{\pm}^{\dagger}=-E_{\mp}
. The other two
\mathfrak{su}(2)

(J^{a}_{b})^{\dagger}=J^{b}_{a}
and
(J^{\alpha}_{\beta})^{\dagger}=J^{\beta}_{\alpha}

Q^{a\alpha}=Q^{1a\alpha},\quad Q^{\dagger}_{a\alpha}=\epsilon_{ab}\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}Q^{2b\beta}.

(94)

There are some notable limits of

\alpha\to 1

\mathfrak{osp}(4|2)

\mathfrak{su}(2)
factors are on a par and combine as
\mathfrak{so}(4)\simeq\mathfrak{su}(2)\oplus\mathfrak{su}(2)
, and the conformal
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
provides the
\mathfrak{sp}(2)

\alpha\to 0
or
\alpha\to\infty
, the algebra becomes
\mathfrak{psu}(1,1|2)
, upon decoupling one of the
\mathfrak{su}(2)
factors and rescaling the generators. The projective
\mathfrak{p}
denotes the absence of the central element. We consider the limit
\alpha\to-1
as a contraction of the conformal sector in
Sec.
3.2
.
