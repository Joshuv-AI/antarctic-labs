In‐Depth optimization and characterization of prostatic and penile vessel imaging with Ferumoxytol‐enhanced magnetic resonance imaging
Authors: Darren Fang, Justin McWIlliams, Christina Wang, Danilo Maziero, Keisuke S. Iwamoto, Amar U. Kishan, J. Paul Finn, Xiaodong Zhong, Dan Ruan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVasculogenic erectile dysfunction (ED) arises from impairment of the vascular supply to the penis and requires reliable quantitative imaging for diagnosis, management, and longitudinal monitoring, particularly after pelvic surgery or radiotherapy. Penile Doppler ultrasound is a manual examination and subject to substantial inter‐observer variability in both acquisition and interpretation.PurposeTo develop and optimize a Ferumoxytol‐enhanced magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) protocol for noninvasive, high‐resolution imaging of prostatic and penile vasculature, and to design and implement study‐specific physical and in silico phantoms for quantitative evaluation under controlled line pair, anatomically realistic, and physiological conditions.MethodsImaging was performed on a 1.5 T MRI scanner using T1‐weighted TurboFLASH and T2‐weighted turbo spin echo sequences using Ferumoxytol doses of 1–4 mg/kg. Line pair phantoms containing tubes with 0.5‐6 mm inner diameter and 0.5‐5 mm spacing were constructed to span the size range of prostatic and penile arteries. An anatomically derived anthropomorphic phantom was fabricated by segmenting penile vasculature from a patient CT angiogram, 3D‐printing the resulting geometry, and embedding Ferumoxytol‐loaded tubing to reproduce realistic vessel tortuosity. Protocol performance was assessed using signal‐to‐noise ratio (SNR), contrast‐to‐noise ratio (CNR), and modulation transfer function metrics (MTF50 and MTF10). Numerical simulations were also performed to evaluate sequence behavior under flow conditions.ResultsTurboFLASH was selected over TSE because Ferumoxytol‐induced T1 shortening provided superior vessel conspicuity. Vessels larger than 1 mm were clearly visualized at all dose levels, with 2 mg/kg identified as the optimal dose. MTF50 indicated a nominal resolution of approximately 3 mm in both phantoms. MTF10 indicated effective resolutions of approximately 2 mm in the line pair phantom and 1 mm in the anthropomorphic phantom. Simulations demonstrated preserved contrast enhancement at a flow velocity of 100 mm/s.ConclusionFerumoxytol‐enhanced MRA shows promise for visualizing major prostatic and penile vessels relevant to ED diagnosis and management, including the accessory pudendal and internal pudendal arteries. Further anthropomorphic phantom refinement and in vivo patient studies are warranted to evaluate broader anatomic variability and clinical feasibility.

INTRODUCTION

Erectile dysfunction (ED), defined as the inability to achieve or maintain an erection sufficient for satisfactory sexual performance, affects an estimated 150 million men worldwide and more than 50% of men aged 40–70 in the United States. 1 , 2 ED can substantially impair quality of life and is often associated with anxiety, reduced self‐esteem, and embarrassment. 2 , 3 Its prevalence is expected to increase with population aging and longer life expectancy. 2 , 4 , 5 , 6 Although ED has multifactorial etiology, vasculogenic mechanisms are a major contributor: arterial flow may be compromised by atherosclerosis or treatment‐related vascular injury after surgery or radiotherapy, while venous leakage can impair maintenance of erection. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10

Penile Doppler ultrasonography (PDU) is currently the standard imaging modality for evaluating penile vascular function. 8 , 9 , 10 However, as a manual examination, PDU suffers from difficulty with standardization, with high inter‐observer variability noted across both technique and interpretation. 11 Magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) offers a more quantitative alternative. Gadolinium‐enhanced MRA has demonstrated utility in imaging prostatic and major penile vessels for detection of vascular abnormalities such as atherosclerotic plaques, arteriovenous shunts, and abnormal venous drainage. 12 , 13 , 14 However, gadolinium‐based contrast agents (GBCAs) rapidly extravasate and require careful bolus timing for first pass imaging. Ferumoxytol, an ultrasmall superparamagnetic iron oxide particle, is an attractive alternative contrast agent. It is approved by the FDA for brain imaging, has demonstrated off‐label utility in cardiovascular MRI, and offers several properties advantageous for vascular imaging. 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 In particular, its long intravascular half‐life (∼15 hours vs. ∼90 min for GBCAs) 19 , 20 eliminates the need for first pass timing and enables steady‐state imaging. 21 Ferumoxytol also offers improved vascular conspicuity, with substantially higher relaxivities compared to conventional GBCAs (r1,r2: Ferumoxytol 19 s−1 mM−1, 65 s−1 mM−1; GBCAs 3–5 s−1 mM−1, 4–9 s−1 mM−1). 22 , 23 , 24

In this study, we developed, optimized, and evaluated a Ferumoxytol‐enhanced MRA protocol for standardized, high‐resolution imaging of prostatic and penile vasculature. To enable quantitative assessment, we designed study‐specific line pair 25 and anthropomorphic phantoms tailored to the spatial scale and geometric complexity of the target vasculature. We further conducted numerical simulations under controlled flow conditions to assess protocol robustness in physiologically relevant dynamic settings.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

We performed a two‐stage optimization of the Ferumoxytol‐enhanced MRI protocol using Feraheme (AMAG Pharmaceuticals, USA) on a 1.5 T clinical MRI scanner (MAGNETOM Sola, Siemens Healthineers, Forchheim, Germany). In the first stage, sequence parameters were optimized for high‐resolution vascular imaging using study‐specific line pair phantoms. In the second stage, imaging performance was evaluated using an anthropomorphic phantom incorporating anatomically realistic vascular geometries and an in silico model for flow simulation.

Line pair phantom construction and design

Figure 1 illustrates the design of the 3D‐printed line pair phantom developed for this study to reflect the spatial scale and resolution requirements of penile vascular imaging, along with the coordinate system used for subsequent image processing. The phantom incorporates Ferumoxytol‐loaded tube pairs with inner diameters (ID) of 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, and 6 mm. These diameters were selected to span the range of vessel calibers reported in literature, from approximately 3 mm for the internal pudendal arteries (IPAs) to approximately 0.6 mm for cavernosal arteries in the flaccid state. 26 , 27 , 28 , 29

Imaging protocol and sequence optimization

Phantoms with varying Ferumoxytol concentrations were immersed in saline to mimic a soft tissue background and imaged using T1‐weighted turbo fast low angle shot (turboFLASH) 31 and T2‐weighted turbo spin echo (TSE) sequences. Acquisition parameters, including sequence type, Ferumoxytol dose, flip angle, echo time, and repetition time, were optimized to maximize vessel conspicuity through qualitative image review and quantitative assessment of signal‐to‐noise ratio (SNR), contrast‐to‐noise ratio (CNR), and the modulation transfer function (MTF). MTF analysis was used to evaluate edge sharpness at the lumen‐wall interface, which is relevant to the visualization of stenosis and plaque‐like boundaries. 32 Image analysis followed the coordinate system shown in Figure 1, where the x,y, and z directions correspond to the line spacing variation, tube diameter variation, and tube longitudinal directions, respectively.

Image preprocessing

All images were preprocessed with the N4 bias‐field correction algorithm. 33 Following preprocessing, intensity profile analysis showed no detectable lumen signal in the 0.5 mm ID tubes, precluding reliable foreground definition. These tubes were therefore excluded from subsequent analysis.

SNR and CNR

For all remaining tubes, regions of interest (ROIs) were defined to measure signal intensity for SNR and CNR estimation. Bounding boxes were manually placed to localize individual tubes, and the enclosed regions were upsampled using cubic spline interpolation to improve the numerical stability of segmentation. Foreground ROIs were then generated using the Chan‐Vese algorithm to obtain consistent and reproducible segmentations. 34 , 35 The Chan‐Vese algorithm segments images I(x) by finding constants c1,c2 and a boundary S between a foreground Ω1 and background Ω2 to minimize Ec1,c2,δ=areaδ+λ1∫Ω1Ix−c12dx+λ2∫Ω2Ix−c22dx.

The weights were set to their default values λ1=λ2=1, and the algorithm was iterated until convergence. Each resulting 3D ROI was subsequently partitioned into 2D slices along the tube axis to increase the effective sample size for statistical analysis. Signal uniformity within each foreground region was further enforced by iteratively eroding until the intensity coefficient of variation (CV) fell below a prespecified threshold value of 0.05.

Let μ and σ denote the intensity mean and standard deviation, respectively. SNR and CNR were computed relative to a homogeneous water background ROI, which was manually selected as a surrogate for extravascular soft tissue: SNR=μROIσbackground,CNR=μROI−μbackgroundσbackground

Both metrics were evaluated across varying tube diameters and dose conditions. For each fixed diameter, one‐sided Mann–Whitney U‐tests were performed at a significance level α = 0.05 to assess pairwise CNR superiority across dose levels. The optimal dose was then selected as the one yielding the highest superiority or statistically non‐inferior CNR across all vessel sizes.

MTF

To quantitatively assess spatial resolution, MTF was computed at multiple Ferumoxytol concentrations using the ISO 12233 edge‐slant method. 36 , 37 , 38 , 39 For each dose level, rectangular ROIs were manually selected in the xz‐plane to capture the lumen‐wall boundary of the 6 mm ID tubes, as shown in Figure 2. Because the ISO 12233 method performs best with long, approximately straight slanted edges, ROIs were chosen to maximize straight‐edge extent while minimizing curvature. The isolated lumen‐wall boundary within each ROI was then used for MTF estimation.

Bounding box ROIs (red, green, blue, yellow) are selected in the xz‐plane to capture the boundary between contrast and walls of 6 mm ID tubes for MTF estimation using the ISO 12233 edge‐slant method.

Spatial resolution was quantified using MTF50 and MTF10, defined as the spatial frequencies (in cycles/mm) at which the MTF fell to 50% and 10% of its maximum value, respectively. These quantities represent perceptual resolution and limiting resolution, respectively. For each dose, we calculated the sample means and standard deviations of MTF50 and MTF10 values and used these statistics to compare resolution performance across dose levels.

Validation under anatomically realistic conditions

To validate conspicuity under anatomically realistic geometric conditions, we developed an anthropomorphic phantom from a CT angiogram (CTA) obtained with IRB approval. This CTA from an 87‐year‐old male with benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) was thresholded to generate a scaffold, which was then 3D printed and fitted with Ferumoxytol‐loaded tubing to approximate long, tortuous vascular trajectories. The completed phantom was immersed in saline and scanned using the T1w TurboFLASH protocol. Figure 3 illustrates the phantom production workflow and shows a resulting T1‐weighted image. For quantitative analysis, a 2D slice containing transverse tube cross sections was selected for estimation of SNR, CNR, and MTF.

Validation under physiologically realistic conditions

To assess performance in the presence of flow, we additionally conducted virtual scanner simulations using the KomaMRI framework. 40 Flow trajectories were modeled using a Lagrangian approach 41 : BZr,t=B0+Gt·rt, dMdt=γM×B−RM−M0, R=11T2T200011T2T200011T1T1, where B denotes the magnetic field, G denotes gradients, r(t) denotes evolving spin positions, and M denotes spin magnetizations. To evaluate the impact of flow on image quality, we adopted a constant‐flow model which enables direct calculation of particle trajectories within the Lagrangian framework. Digital versions of the line pair phantom at the experimentally selected Ferumoxytol dose of 2 mg/kg were generated for flow velocities of 0 cm/s (static), 10 cm/s, and 40 cm/s. The 10 cm/s condition approximates time‐averaged pulsatile flow, whereas 40 cm/s represents a worst‐case scenario corresponding to constant peak systolic velocity and the most severe flow‐related artifacts. 10 The turboFLASH sequence was implemented in PyPulseq as a rapid gradient echo acquisition with centric k‐space ordering and a repetition time equal to the echo spacing (8.4 ms). Simulations compared nonselective and slice‐selective excitation schemes under the assumptions of uniform coil sensitivity and negligible field inhomogeneity and off‐resonance effects. Images were reconstructed using a conventional 3D inverse fast Fourier transform.

The 2 mg/kg dose yielded the highest mean SNR and CNR across all tube diameters. One‐sided Mann–Whitney U tests confirmed statistically significant CNR improvements over 1 mg/kg at 1.5 and 6 mm (p < 0.01). Although the difference at 2 mm did not reach significance, 2 mg/kg was not inferior to any other dose level. Collectively, these results support 2 mg/kg as the empirically optimal dose.

Validation under anatomically realistic conditions

Figure 7 presents a 3D rendering of the 1.5 mm ID tubes in the anthropomorphic phantom acquired with the proposed T1w TurboFLASH sequence. Hyper‐ and hypo‐intensities respectively correspond to Ferumoxytol and the resin infrastructure. Vessel signal was recovered by threshold‐based segmentation. Figure 8 shows a cross‐sectional view of the same tubes together with the corresponding line spread function and MTF curve. Measured SNR and CNR were 30.5 ± 0.6 and 15.0 ± 0.6, respectively. MTF analysis yielded MTF50 and MTF10 values of 0.35 ± 0.02 cycles/mm and 0.73 ± 0.02 cycles/mm, corresponding to spatial resolution estimates of 2.91 ± 0.14 mm and 1.36 ± 0.04 mm, respectively. These values were consistent with the line pair phantom measurements and further supported the robustness of the protocol under anatomically realistic conditions.

Simulations of flow effects

Figure 9 presents simulated images of 1.5 and 6 mm ID tubes at the selected 2 mg/kg Ferumoxytol dose. Under flow conditions, acquisition using nonselective excitations, as commonly employed in rapid 3D imaging, exhibited varying degrees of ghosting artifacts. These artifacts were substantially reduced with slice‐selective excitation.

Comparison of simulated images of 1.5 and 6 mm inner‐diameter tubes containing 2 mg/kg Ferumoxytol, acquired using nonselective and slice‐selective excitation schemes at multiple flow velocities.

Figure 10 reports the SNR, CNR, and MTF measurements from the simulated images obtained with slice‐selective excitation. Under static conditions, SNR and CNR were consistent with experimental measurements. At a flow velocity of 100 mm/s, a modest signal increase relative to the static case was observed, consistent with inflow enhancement from the continual entry of less‐saturated spins into the imaging volume. Resolution was maintained comparable to that of the static condition, as shown in Table 4. At the higher flow velocity of 400 mm/s, corresponding to peak systolic flow speed, however, SNR, CNR, and resolution all degraded substantially because spin displacement during readout produced more severe flow‐induced phase inconsistency.

DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION

In this study, we developed a Ferumoxytol‐enhanced MRA protocol based on a T1‐weighted TurboFLASH sequence. Using study‐specific line pair phantoms, we jointly optimized sequence parameters and contrast dose to maximize vessel conspicuity and spatial resolution. We further validated imaging performance with a patient‐derived anthropomorphic phantom and assessed flow sensitivity under a range of physiologically relevant conditions using virtual scan simulations.

Quantitative analyses of SNR and CNR across multiple Ferumoxytol doses identified 2 mg/kg as the optimal dose, corresponding to an estimated circulating concentration of approximately 0.5 mM for a 75‐kg subject with 5 L of blood volume. At this dose, CNR ranged from approximately 40 to 100 depending on vessel diameter. Signal enhancement remained robust at a flow velocity of 100 mm/s, exceeding the typical end‐diastolic velocity reported for the cavernosal artery (30–50 mm/s). At higher velocities approaching peak systolic conditions, more pronounced flow artifacts were observed, representing a potential limitation for dynamic vascular imaging. 10

Results from both the line pair phantom and the anthropomorphic phantom indicate that the proposed protocol provides adequate conspicuity and spatial resolution for vessels with internal diameters greater than 1 mm and separations greater than approximately 2 mm. This performance is likely sufficient for imaging prostatic arteries and larger penile vessels. 27 However, visualization of submillimeter penile arteries remains beyond the current capability of the protocol and would require further technical refinement to approach the spatial resolution achieved with PDU (∼0.1 mm). 44

Flow simulations demonstrated reasonable robustness under physiologic flow conditions, though more pronounced ghosting artifacts were observed at peak systolic velocity. In future implementations, such artifacts may be mitigated through established flow compensation strategies, including gradient moment nulling and spatial presaturation. 45 , 46 , 47 Overall, these results support the feasibility of Ferumoxytol‐enhanced MRI for controlled visualization of relevant pelvic and penile vasculature across physical phantoms, anatomically realistic geometry, and simulated flow conditions. Further in vivo studies are needed to determine its clinical utility in patients with vasculogenic ED.
