Testing an integrated model of population mental health: study protocol for the epidemiological, two-arm Mannheim incidence and inception cohort study (MAKO)

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionMonitoring of population mental health is now considered to play a vital role in health, risk factor and demographic surveillance systems across the globe. Adopting a public mental health perspective on monitoring and surveillance requires us to consider the entire spectrum of mental health, which broadly ranges from positive mental health and well-being to severe mental disorders, including at-risk populations. This population mental health approach has become the mainstay of public mental health provision in many European countries but there is a dearth of research on this issue in Germany in the past 20 years and fundamental conceptual and methodological issues remain to be addressed at both the national and international levels.Methods and analysisThe Mannheim incidence and inception cohort study is a recently established two-arm population-based incidence (arm 1) and inception cohort (arm 2) study in the catchment area of Mannheim, Germany. In the incidence-arm, case ascertainment of incident cases is carried out for the years of 2024 and 2025 based on clinical assessment via structured clinical interviews and retrospective case record evaluation. In the inception cohort arm, we recruit and follow an epidemiologically characterised population-based cohort of individuals (1) without symptoms (ie, stage 0), (2) meeting the criteria for non-specific psychological distress (ie, stage 1 a) or (3) a Clinical High At-Risk Mental State for severe mental disorder (ie, stage 1b) at baseline and up to four follow-up assessments every 6 months. Assessments include clinical interviews, self-report measures and ecological momentary assessment.Poisson regression will be conducted to quantify variation in incident rates by age, sex and migrant/ethnic minority group status. In the inception cohort arm, we will perform maximum likelihood estimation of hazard rates to estimate rates of transition from earlier stages to later stages. Latent class analysis will be used to identify empirically-based clinical stages to test convergence with contemporary staging models.Ethics and disseminationThe study has received ethical approval from the local ethics committee (Medical Faculty Mannheim, Heidelberg University, 585-21). Results will be published in peer-reviewed journals, presented at academic conferences and disseminated through (social) media to address a broader public audience.Keywords: MENTAL HEALTH, PUBLIC HEALTH, EPIDEMIOLOGY, Schizophrenia & psychotic disorders, Depression & mood disorders, Personality disorders

Background

Monitoring population mental health is now considered to play a vital role in health, risk factor and demographic surveillance systems across the globe. It can serve as a basis for assessing the mental health consequences of societal developments (eg, social, economic and/or pandemic crises1–5), evidence-based service planning6 and determining the impact of public mental health services at the population level.2 6 7 The shift in focus to early intervention for severe mental disorders,2 8 9 prevention as a tangible goal2 and the emphasis on promoting the positive dimensions of mental health10 all imply that adopting a public mental health perspective on monitoring and surveillance requires us to consider the entire spectrum of mental health. This spectrum broadly ranges from positive mental health to severe mental disorders such as psychotic, bipolar, severe depressive and borderline personality disorder.2 7 These approaches now form part of public mental health surveillance in some countries (eg, Canada) but have not been sufficiently adopted in others.11 For the first time in over two decades, the protocol for the current study described in this paper seeks to address this gap, which will allow the precise and robust estimation of the incidence of severe mental disorders in Germany based on primary data.12–15 We also seek to address fundamental conceptual and methodological issues in the epidemiology of population mental health that are essential for the public mental health field to progress at both the national and international levels. For example, in recent years there has been an upsurge of interest in public mental health provision, including mental health promotion, mental health prevention and delivery of treatment and services for severe mental disorders,16 17 as well as renewed interest in the social determinants of mental health.18 These efforts are ultimately geared toward targeting mental health at the population level and centrally build on the seminal ‘population strategy’ advocated by Rose.19 At its core, Rose’s population strategy posits that for continuously distributed traits, the population mean is closely linked to the prevalence of the corresponding disorder. Consequently, small change in the trait distribution (‘shifting the curve’) will be associated with a substantial change in prevalence of the disorder. In mental health, there is evidence that, first, most mental health problems are continuously distributed,20 second, the population mean is associated with the prevalence of mental disorders in that population, and, third, a change in population mean is closely related to a marked change in prevalence in that population.19 21–23 However, there are at least three fundamental conceptual and methodological issues in relation to population-based strategies that remain to be addressed for this approach to realise its full potential.

First, this approach, so far, has primarily focused on the association of the population mean with prevalence but not incidence (ie, the occurrence of new cases in a population over a specified time period) of disorder. If, in an extrapolation of Rose’s approach,19 the effect of mental health promotion and primary prevention produces a leftward shift in the curve, then this could become evident, first and foremost, in the form of a reduction in incidence (rather than prevalence) of disorder. Focusing on incidence will provide an approach that will allow us to understand more fully the link and timing between potential shifts in population mean and subsequent occurrence of new cases of disorder and, hence, make the impact of public mental health services at the population level more readily accessible and measurable.

Meta-analytic evidence suggests that incidence rates of psychosis as one of the most prominent severe mental disorders vary by place and are particularly high in urban areas as well as in many but not all migrant and ethnic minority groups.12–14 24 Further, for migrant and ethnic minority groups, rates of psychosis are highest in areas where they constitute a small proportion of the local population (ie, areas with low ethnic density).13 The latest and largest international incidence study25 from the EU-GEI consortium thoroughly documented marked variation in incidence of psychosis across 17 catchment areas and six countries, which emphasises the need to determine rates at the local level standardised according to important demographic characteristics. However, while variation in incidence rates in terms of place and ethnicity has been reported in many countries, there has been a dearth of research on this issue in Germany,12–14 24 with the last studies to investigate incidence of severe mental disorders based on primary data having been conducted in the 1990s.26–28 Given the well-documented variation in terms of place and, hence, context-dependent nature, incidence rates from international studies do not readily generalise to local contexts and populations in Germany and, thus, remain largely unknown in this country. In fact, only one study to date has reported treated incidence rates by German population and population with migrant minority status, respectively, in Germany.29 The study is based on data from 1974 to 1980, therefore, incidence and especially treated incidence for individuals with migrant and ethnic minority status might have changed dramatically within the past 45 years due to changes in society and the mental healthcare system. It is therefore not surprising that planning services in Germany is largely operated in the absence of evidence on basic epidemiological data on incidence and other important metrics of population mental health, which may lead to population-level strategies that are not equally accessible across diverse groups. Further, evidence on variation in incidence in terms of place and ethnicity for severe mental disorders other than psychosis, such as severe affective disorders, is much more limited not only in national but also international studies.30 It has recently been shown how this can be used to predict needs for care and inform planning services in defined catchment areas in the UK.6 Overall, the limited evidence on (and variation in) the incidence of severe mental disorders reflects a pressing public health issue, particularly in an increasingly diverse society.31 A key next step is to more precisely delineate their link to the population mean, such that more comprehensive monitoring of mental health can be developed and implemented.

The second issue that remains to be addressed is that the ‘population strategy’ by Rose32 has been taken to suggest that a leftward shift in the distribution of mental health is associated with a substantial decrease in prevalence of not only disorder but also, and even more markedly, in those at moderate risk or with subthreshold disorder.16 This proposition builds on an earlier observation by Rose that, for continuously distributed health problems, the largest proportion of disorder may occur in those just below the upper tail of the distribution, given more individuals are located within that range. It assumes, however, that mental health promotion and prevention shift mental health in a uniform way across the population (ie, without impact on the shape of the population curve), disregarding the individuals’ position on the risk distribution; an assumption challenged by evidence demonstrating that public health campaigns have been more successful in benefitting individuals with higher socioeconomic status.33 34 The ‘prevention paradox’ implies that universal interventions that may confer little individual benefit to a large number of people at low or moderate risk may have a greater impact at the population level than selective interventions that may confer considerable individual benefit to a smaller number of individuals at high risk.

A third concern has been raised about population strategies to yield the unintended effect of augmenting social inequalities in health in vulnerable populations such as ethnic minority groups or those living in poverty, who are at higher risk of risk exposure due to shared social characteristics (eg, language barriers, experiences of discrimination, social exclusion), and, hence, reflect a driving factor for mental health inequalities. This becomes the case if population strategies are less effective or less accessible for those at higher risk, compared with those at lower risk. This phenomenon is widely referred to as ‘inequality paradox’.16 However, before this putative paradox can be empirically tested and inform strategies for prevention and intervention, population-based approaches need to be considered and investigated in relation to, and jointly with clinical staging models. These models distinguish and operationalise various levels of low-risk, moderate-risk and high-risk groups, as well as those who ultimately meet clinical thresholds for disorder across the continuum of mental health. Thus, their integration would allow us to increase precision of testing population-level approaches by determining the stages most sensitive to specific strategies, and ultimately, tailoring of interventions. Clinical staging models in psychiatry have primarily been developed within clinical early intervention services9 and have largely emerged separately from approaches to population mental health. Early versions of clinical staging models have focused on a clinical high-risk state for psychosis (CHR-P) for a long time. More recently, evidence on a putative pluripotent risk state35 or extended transdiagnostic phenotype,36 with various exit syndromes (including psychotic, bipolar, severe depressive and borderline personality disorder), has led to an extension of clinical staging to a broad range of severe mental disorders.37–40 This extension is further informed by evidence that symptom severity, functional impairment and complexity of course and outcome play an important role as much in the so-called ‘common mental disorders’ as they do in psychotic and bipolar disorders.16 Latest transdiagnostic clinical staging models seem to converge on distinguishing three symptomatic stages: current non-specific psychological distress with mild to moderate impact on functioning (stage 1a), a broad Clinical High At-Risk Mental State (CHARMS) including attenuated symptoms, trait vulnerability and functional decline (stage 1b), and a first episode of severe mental disorder (stage 2), with psychotic, bipolar, severe depressive and borderline personality disorders as exit syndromes that contribute substantially to disease burden and involve significant impairment.37–40 Asymptomatic individuals are commonly classified as stage 0. While there is meta-analytic evidence on the predictive validity of a CHR-P,41 only a small number of studies have investigated this in relation to a broad CHARMS for severe mental disorders.37–40 42 These studies, in part, used crude operationalisations of clinical stages, included no or very small samples for earlier stages (ie, stage 0, 1a),37 40 and evidence on transition rates in CHARMS remains very limited. Furthermore, the structural validity of categorical clinical stages still requires further empirical testing by latent categorical variable models and through a dimensional lens, as proposed by the Hierarchical Taxonomy of Psychopathology (HiTOP).43–45 Categories generated based on dimensional approaches to psychopathology need to be empirically derived and demonstrate clinical utility (eg, for clinical and preventative decision-making). This evidence is urgently needed for clinical staging models to be considered and understood more fully in the context of population-based and vulnerable population approaches. Methodologically, sampling by clinical stages enables us to generate variance across the continuum of mental health required to test clinical staging and dimensional models empirically based on established measures of these approaches (eg, Comprehensive Assessment of the At Risk Mental State (CAARMS), HITOP-SR).

The fourth issue that needs to be addressed in relation to the population mental health approach is that contemporary models of mental health, including clinical staging and dimensional models, have primarily focused on psychopathology, with absence of symptoms being used synonymously with a state of well-being or positive mental health. However, there is widespread consensus that mental health is much more than just the absence of psychopathology.10 Thus, some governments have started to explore and implement strategies for assessing and monitoring positive mental health at the population level to inform measures for mental health promotion and evaluate policy.7 However, psychometric evidence on measuring positive mental health has rarely been integrated with categorical or dimensional models of psychopathology. For example, HiTOP,43 which quantifies disorders across several hierarchical levels and characterises them dimensionally, has, to date, primarily focused on dimensions of mental disorder rather than positive mental health. Also, clinical staging models commonly include a stage of no symptoms (stage 0) without explicitly covering the measurement range of positive mental health.

Similarly, there is an urgent need to not only consider risk factors (eg, urbanicity,13 ethnic minority status,12 25 46 47 area-level ethnic density13) but also resilience (ie, the ability to recover from and overcome the effects of adversity48) and protective factors, such as social support49 and self-efficacy,50 in relation to clinical stages and dimensions of mental health. This includes digital momentary phenotypes (such as stress reactivity, mood and activity patterns) as investigated in our work based on innovative multimodal ecological momentary assessment (EMA),51–54 which can provide the basis for digital monitoring of population mental health. Monitoring of positive mental health, resilience and protective factors alongside risk factors and levels of mental disorder at the population level is considered a vital task of public mental health7 and should embrace novel EMA-based approaches to real-world monitoring of digital momentary phenotypes in daily life.

Therefore, it is important to extend investigations of dimensional and clinical staging models to the full range of mental health in order to clarify and calibrate the nature of the common ground between positive mental health and mental disorder. This study is agnostic regarding the value which dimensional and clinical staging models each present and seeks to provide a basis for formally validating both approaches. Taken together, integrating the entire continuum of mental health is of considerable importance, but high-quality research is needed first, which allows us to address the fundamental conceptual and methodological issues outlined above.

So far, these issues have been investigated by various fields—including psychiatric epidemiology, early intervention in psychiatry, prevention, mental health promotion, mental health services research, psychometrics and public health—that largely operate in separate silos. Public mental health is geared towards bringing these fields together in interdisciplinary investigations that allow us to understand more fully how the different approaches overlap and relate to each other and, thereby, establish the basic epidemiology of population mental health (including epidemiology of vulnerable and marginalised populations). This is what the current study is designed to achieve. In reviewing and synthesising the state of the art, we propose here an integrated transdiagnostic model of population mental health that encompasses the entire continuum of mental health. It spans and calibrates positive mental health and well-being all the way through to incidence of severe mental disorders and seeks to empirically integrate, for the first time, transdiagnostic clinical stages and dimensions of psychopathology and positive mental health, including how they relate to each other (see figure 1). This will allow us to jointly examine the central link between population mean (and other relevant distributional measures), various levels of risk, access to public mental health services and incidence of full-threshold disorder.

Aim and objectives

The overarching aim of the proposed programme is to establish and delineate the basic epidemiology of population mental health by testing key aspects (ie, incidence, clinical stage transitions) of an integrated transdiagnostic model encompassing the entire continuum of mental health. To this end, we will implement a highly innovative, two-arm population-based incidence and inception cohort study, in a defined catchment area (Mannheim, Germany) using robust statistical methods and digital technology. This two-arm design will allow us to examine conceptual and methodological questions central to population mental health research and provides the basis for evaluating the feasibility of comprehensive monitoring of population mental health.

To achieve our overall aim, this programme has four objectives with corresponding hypotheses:

Objective 1: Incidence

To estimate the incidence of severe mental disorders (ie, psychotic, bipolar, severe depressive and borderline personality disorder) in Germany among the population at-risk, including by demographic and environmental characteristics.

We will meet this objective in the incidence arm of the study by: (1) estimating the treated incidence of severe mental disorders in the defined catchment area of Mannheim, Germany, with an at-risk population of 225 700 between ages 14 and 6455; (2) investigating variation in incidence rates of these disorders by age, sex, neighbourhoods and migrant/ethnic minority group status; and (3) investigating which demographic/risk factors are associated with access to and help-seeking from public mental health services. Methods and analysis

Study design and setting

A two-arm population-based incidence and inception cohort study will be conducted in a tightly defined catchment area (Mannheim) in Germany. In the incidence arm, we aim to identify all cases between ages 14 and 64 with a first (treated) episode of a severe mental disorder meeting inclusion criteria (see Participants section). Case ascertainment will take place over a 2-year period between 1 January 2024 and 31 December 2025 and involve the research team making weekly contact with mental health services in Mannheim to identify cases with a suspected first episode or treatment based on clinical guidelines for severe mental disorder. Clinical teams will be contacted weekly by the research team to optimise identification of potential cases by clinicians. For all individuals with a suspected first episode of severe mental disorder, we will conduct clinical assessments to determine the presence of a research-based diagnosis based on the International Statistical Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) of an untreated first episode of severe mental disorder using the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-5 Clinican Version / Personality Disorders (SCID-5-CV, SCID-5-PD)56 57 and a version of the Nottingham Onset Schedule (NOS)58 modified to apply to all severe mental disorders of interest in this study. Using the NOS, we will carefully trace onset of symptoms, the first contact with mental health services and the beginning of treatment based on clinical guidelines for severe mental disorders to confirm incident status. Case ascertainment will also include retrospective case note evaluation of patients treated at the Central Institute of Mental Health (see section on ‘Data collection’ for further details) and will be further optimised through the population-based inception cohort arm of the study, which will identify individuals who transition to a first episode of severe mental disorder (stage 2) over the case ascertainment period.

The population at-risk, 14–64 years old, in this catchment area is estimated to be 225 700.55 Based on a conservative estimate of 75 incident cases per 100 000 person-years of psychotic and severe affective disorders,25 59 60 and a case ascertainment duration of 2 years, we would expect the incidence arm of the study to yield a sample of approximately n=340 incident cases. Our estimate of incident cases is conservative as these are well within previously reported rates for psychotic and affective disorder only.25 59 60 We will use census data from 2022 to estimate the denominator population and person-years at-risk in the catchment area, stratified by neighbourhood, age group, sex and migrant/ethnic minority group status.

The inception cohort arm of the study entails a prospective, epidemiologically characterised, population-based cohort in which we recruit a quota sample stratified by age, sex, neighbourhood and migrant/ethnic minority group status. Participants will be classified into those who (1) meet criteria for (a) non-specific psychological distress (ie, stage 1a) and (b) a clinical high-risk state for severe mental disorder (CHARMS, ie, stage 1b) or (2) do not meet criteria for stage 1a, 1b and 2 (with the latter including those with severe mental disorder before 2024) (ie, stage 0) drawn from the same catchment area as incident cases (Mannheim, Germany; aged 14–35 years). At baseline (t0), we aim to recruit n=320 individuals with (1) non-specific psychological distress (stage 1a) and (2) asymptomatic individuals (stage 0), with at least n=160 individuals from stage 1a. Moreover, we will recruit at baseline a convenience sample of individuals with CHARMS (stage 1b). This cohort will be recruited at a constant rate over a period of 18 months and followed every 6 months from baseline, with a minimum follow-up time of 12 months for all participants, corresponding to a total duration for recruitment and assessment of 2.5 years. This will ensure that all participants from the inception cohort arm will be followed at 6-month (t1) and 12-month (t2) follow-up, while allowing to collect data, where possible, at 18-month (t3) and 24-month (t4) follow-up, thereby making optimal use of time-to-event (survival) data. Participants

Incidence arm

Eligibility criteria

Incident cases will be all individuals with a first episode or start of first ever treatment for a severe mental disorder according to clinical guidelines (as defined in online supplemental table S1) in public mental health services, meeting the following eligibility criteria: diagnosis of severe mental disorder (psychotic disorders: F20, F22–29, F1x.5; mania: F30, bipolar disorder: F31; severe depression: F32.2, F32.3, F33.2, F33.3; borderline personality disorder: F60.3 in ICD-1061; aged 14–64 years; living in the catchment area of Mannheim, Germany (registered in Mannheim and/or regularly living in Mannheim). Eligibility is assessed based on clinical assessment via structured clinical interviews and retrospective case record evaluation. For clinical assessment via structured clinical interviews, additional inclusion criteria are: willingness to participate, ability to provide informed written consent and consent by legal guardians in case of minors. Exclusion criteria for both clinical assessment via structured clinical interviews and retrospective case record evaluation are as follows: guideline-based treatment by mental health services for the same severe mental disorder before 2024, presence of an organic medical cause, transient symptoms due to acute intoxication (ICD-10, codes F1X.0), insufficient knowledge of German, English, Turkish, or Russian. An additional exclusion criterion for clinical assessment via structured clinical interviews in study assessments is a documented history of intellectual disability. Patients will still be eligible if they received prior diagnosis of or treatment for a different severe mental disorder (as defined in the inclusion criteria) or had prior contact with mental health services for a disorder not included in the severe mental disorders (eg, mild depression).

Inception cohort arm

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria for the inception cohort arm comprise being resident of Mannheim (registered in Mannheim and/or regularly living in Mannheim), age of 14–35 years, mental health state: stage 0, 1a or 1b (see online supplemental table S2 for clinical staging criteria), willingness to participate, ability to give written informed consent and parental consent for minors. Exclusion criteria comprise a current or past diagnosis or treatment for a severe mental disorder (stage 2 and up) (based on self-report or clinician report), a lifetime or current severe mental disorder detected in the study assessments using SCID-5, a documented history of intellectual disability and insufficient knowledge of German, English, Turkish or Russian.

At 6 months (t1) and 12 months (t2), we will seek to follow-up all those who participate at baseline, with transition to later clinical stage (ie, stage 1b, 2) as outcome of primary interest (with stage 2 transition resulting in end of study participation). For each participant, we will carefully document the time to transition to a later stage. We anticipate an attrition rate (ie, attrition for other reasons than ‘administrative censoring’ due to study termination at a fixed point in calendar time) of 10% in each of the two prescheduled follow-up intervals (t0, t1) and (t1, t2). On this basis, we anticipate following, of the original cohort, 90% at 6-month follow-up (t1) and 80% at 12-month follow-up (t2). We will also follow participants, where possible, at 18-month (t3) and 24-month (t4) follow-up, respectively.

Recruitment and quota sampling

In the inception cohort arm, a quota sample stratified by age, sex, neighbourhood and migrant/ethnic minority group status will be drawn from the same geographical area as incident cases. Participants will be recruited to fill quota, where possible, through random sampling (ie, via postal address file from the registry office). Where required, quota sampling will be complemented by non-random sampling, among others, via schools, youth centres, public information stands, sports clubs, religious institutions (eg, mosques), Muslim pastoral care and counselling, food banks, social services, self-help groups, migrant/ethnic minority group associations and services and (social) media, applying recommended strategies to reach and include vulnerable and marginalised groups.62 Online supplemental table S2 shows the clinical staging criteria which will be applied throughout the incidence and inception cohort arm.

Data collection

Clinical assessment (including structured clinical interviews)

Data collection and management is carried out via Research Electronic Data Capture.63 64 EMA data are collected via the movisensXS app using study smartphones or personal smartphones (movisens GmbH). Potentially eligible individuals first undergo a phone call or in-person conversation for prescreening. Based on available and self-reported information on the mental health status and history, individuals complete informed consent procedures (including written informed consent) for the incidence arm (ie, for clinical interviews, not for retrospective case record evaluation) or the inception cohort arm. All participants who provide written informed consent complete a set of screening measures determining whether CAARMS and SCID-5-CV sections on current symptoms are required, and thus the length of the subsequent interview session. The full interview, including lifetime and current symptoms, is conducted if participants meet one of the following criteria: (1) presence of psychotic symptoms on the Prodromal Questionnaire-1665 indicated by a total number of symptoms of ≥5 or a distress score of ≥866; (2) current difficulties in emotion regulation (at least one of the subscale cut-offs on the Difficulties in Emotion Dysregulation—Short Form67 indicated by a Strategies subscale score of ≥3; Non-Acceptance subscale score of ≥3; Impulse subscale score of ≥2.5; Goals subscale score of ≥3.5; Awareness subscale score of ≥3.5; or Clarity subscale score of ≥3 (3) presence of hypomanic symptoms: Hypomania Checklist-3268 score of ≥1469; (4) reduced functioning: Work and Social Adjustment Scale70 score of ≥1070; (5) elevated psychological distress: K1071 score of ≥2072; (6) currently help-seeking (intentions): seeking or in contact with or referral for professional mental health support.

The duration of the diagnostic interview addressing affective symptoms, psychotic symptoms, schizotypal personality disorder symptoms, borderline personality disorder symptoms, severe mental disorder onset and treatment, and psychosocial functioning is expected to take between 1 and 2 hours to administer in the inception cohort arm and between 1 and 3 hours in the incidence arm. Interviews can take place on site or online via an encrypted video conferencing system. Following the interview, participants receive a link to complete a set of self-report measures (approximate duration: 90–120 min). For a comprehensive list of constructs and measures used at each assessment time point, see online supplemental table S3. Online supplemental table S4 provides an overview and of the language versions used for German-speaking, English-speaking, Turkish-speaking, Russian-speaking and Ukrainian-speaking participants. All participants eligible for the inception cohort arm will be invited to complete assessments at 6-month and 12-month follow-up, and some participants, who are included in the study in the first years of the study period at 18-month and 24-month follow-up. Methods to reduce attrition include reminders, update and thank-you emails and additional compensation for participation in 18-month and 24-month follow-ups. Retrospective case record evaluation

Retrospective case record evaluation is conducted on clinical case notes from mental health services. Specifically, clinical case notes are retrospectively screened on a regular basis for patients admitted between January 2024 and February 2026 and evaluated for meeting criteria for (treated) incidence during the case ascertainment period (1 January 2024 to 31 December 2025) by trained research staff. The Operation CRITeria checklist (OPCRIT)73 items will be used to complete a case note evaluation procedure to assess affective and psychotic symptoms (appearance and behaviour, speech and form of thought, affect and associated features, abnormal beliefs and ideas, abnormal perceptions). Adapting the evaluation procedure to reflect DSM-5 diagnoses, items on the duration of symptoms and the link between symptom onset and substance use were added to the original OPCRIT item set and symptoms were rated on a yes (+) or no (−) scale to apply an algorithm extracting accurate DSM-5 diagnoses from case records. The nine SCID-5-PD items for BPD are entered to extract BPD diagnoses if five or more criteria were confirmed. Clinical diagnoses are entered and cross-checked with OPCRIT checklist-based/SCID-5-PD generated diagnoses. In case of discrepancies, the OPCRIT checklist-based/SCID-5-PD generated diagnoses will be used. In case OPCRIT checklist-based/SCID-5-PD applied algorithms do not supply a diagnosis, the clinical diagnosis is used, and this is flagged in the data set in order to enable sensitivity analyses. To evaluate whether the respective severe mental disorder was incident in 2024–2025, onset and prior guideline treatment of the diagnosis are evaluated using an adapted version of the NOS.58 Online supplemental table S1 shows the definitions of guideline treatment for the diagnostic groups in this study. Based on available information in the case records, the trained researchers classify cases with one of the target severe mental disorders as ‘definitely not incident’ (if prior contact for guideline-based treatment of the same diagnoses was documented), ‘unsure—insufficient information on treatment history’, ‘unsure—insufficient diagnostic information’ (eg, provisional diagnosis), ‘definitely incident’ (if first manifestation or first contact for guideline-based treatment of the severe mental disorder was documented by clinicians) or ‘not to be classified—insufficient data available’ (if both treatment history and a confirmed diagnosis are missing). Training of staff includes participation in a training session on OPCRIT items, DSM-5 criteria for respective diagnoses of severe mental disorder, an internal manual on conducting and documenting the case note evaluation and regular consensus meetings.

EMA allocation and sampling

All participants are asked to complete EMA using our established and validated EMA protocol.52 Participants are asked to complete EMA at each time point (ie, at baseline plus at least two follow-up time points). This study uses a hybrid time-contingent and event-contingent EMA design. In the time-contingent design component with a blocked (semi-)random time schedule, the EMA includes eight assessments per day for 6 days, with EMA prompts being scheduled at semirandom time points within predefined blocks to ensure time is evenly sampled throughout the day. Participants can define the daily time frame within which they are willing to complete EMA prompts. Participants receive an alarm (sound and/or vibration) to complete the EMA. A window is scheduled between prompts, ensuring there are at least 30 min between consecutive EMA prompts. Participants have 20 min to respond to a prompt. Participants are block-randomised at a 1:1 ratio (using blocks of 10) to receive either the climate change distress (n=120) or interpersonal experience (n=120) EMA version. The climate change distress EMA includes 33–45 items and the interpersonal experience EMA includes 21–39 items (depending on response patterns in the branching of items). In each version, an event-contingent design component is included, with participants being able to self-initiate an EMA in case something in their life reminded them of climate change (climate change distress EMA) or they experienced an interpersonal interaction (interpersonal experience EMA). In the inception cohort arm, participants receive the same EMA version at follow-up as at baseline.

Leakage study

Quantitative

The inception cohort study arm will allow us to identify incident cases not identified in our case ascertainment procedures. Case records of potential incident cases classified during the first case record evaluation as ‘unsure—insufficient information on treatment history’ or ‘not to be classified—insufficient information on diagnostic process’ will be followed up at the end of the case ascertainment period to include potential new information from additional contacts with mental health services.

Qualitative

In order to identify sources of biases in recruitment and case ascertainment procedures and ultimately incident rates, a qualitative leakage study will be carried out. Specialised and non-specialised treating clinicians (general practitioners, psychiatrists, clinical psychologists and other mental health professionals from inpatient and outpatient services) as well as individuals with severe mental disorder will be interviewed to understand the social, economic and cultural contexts of individuals with an untreated severe mental disorder, barriers to accessing care and factors associated with a long duration of untreated illness. Interviews will especially address social, economic and cultural determinants.

Sample size rationale

The incidence arm of this study is primarily powered to examine variation in the incidence of severe mental disorders by important demographic characteristics using Poisson regression (H1.1). Sample size calculation was conducted to examine the capacity of our sample size to detect a small effect size of an incidence rate ratio (IRR) of~1.5 (ie, an IRR previously reported for the elevated rates of psychosis in migrant/ethnic minority groups, an incidence rate under standard risk conditions of 21.4 per 100 000 person-years)74 and the proportion of participants with migrant/ethnic group minority status of 18.4%, which yielded a required sample size of n=152, with a power of 80% when testing at one-sided level α=0.05. All else being equal, we found that, using the same statistical procedure, the anticipated sample size of 340 incident cases is sufficient for detecting an even smaller IRR of 1.30 with the same power (even though meta-analytic evidence suggests larger effect sizes for migrant/ethnic minority groups (IRRs of 2.1–5.614 24) and urban areas (IRRs of 2.175).

Sample size calculation for the inception cohort arm was conducted to assess the capacity of our sample size to test our hypotheses on rates from earlier to later stages. Specifically, sample size calculation on the accuracy of the estimates to be obtained for transition rates from a given clinical stage at baseline, with transition from stage 1a to any later clinical stage (ie, stage 1b, 2) as primary outcome (H2.1) showed that, assuming a probability of 0.369 for observing a transition event at 12-month follow-up (as previously reported for individuals transitioning from stage 1a to stage 1b40 and allowing for an attrition rate of 20% at 12-month follow-up, the ratio of confidence limits to λ, 1.6 as a fairly strict requirement can be met with the target sample size of n=160 individuals in stage 1a at baseline. Sample size calculation for comparing a subpopulation with standard transition risk (∏ 0), and the subpopulation at increased risk for transition due to exposure to a risk factor ∏1) further indicated that, based on a transition probability of 0.369 during (t0, t2) and applying Schoenfeld’s76 sample size formula, even a small effect of HR=1.60 (ie, smaller than previously reported for, eg, migrant/ethnic minority group status, for which a larger HR of around 2.6 can be assumed for stage transitions77) can be detected with the target sample size of n=320 (as will be reached jointly for stage 0 and stage 1a) with a power of 80% when testing at α=0.05 (H2.2). This holds a fortiori true for a log-rank test of the (alternative) hypothesis that transition rates from stage 1a to any later stage (stage 1b or 2) are larger than those from stage 0 to any later stage (stage 1a, 1b or 2) (H2.1).

Statistical analysis

Treated incidence of severe mental disorder

The primary diagnosis of severe mental disorder will be obtained via three routes for participants to be included in the incidence analysis. For incident cases identified based on clinical assessment via structured clinical interview, the primary diagnosis will be based on the SCID-5-CV/PD interviews and, where required, validated further based on case records. For incident cases identified via case record evaluation, the primary diagnosis will be based on the case note evaluation using the OPCRIT checklist. If this procedure does not yield a diagnosis, but the clinical diagnosis matches inclusion criteria, it will be entered as the primary diagnosis for sensitivity analyses. While all psychotic disorders will be handled as mutually exclusive and major depressive disorder and bipolar disorder are mutually exclusive, cases could be incident for more than one severe mental disorder during the case ascertainment period. For primary incidence analyses, we will apply a hierarchy of a primary incidence diagnosis of affective and psychotic disorder (see figure 3), and count each individual with multiple diagnoses only within their primary incidence diagnosis in order to prevent inflated estimates of incidence rates due to errors and uncertainties in differential diagnosis. A primary incidence of borderline personality disorder will always be reported separately from this hierarchy.

Hierarchy of diagnoses for incident cases with a severe mental disorder based on the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 5th edition.

In the incidence arm, crude incidence rates and 95% CIs by age, sex, migrant/ethnic minority group status and neighbourhood will be estimated and presented per 100 000 person-years. Direct standardisation will then be used to compare incidence rates between neighbourhoods and compute age-adjusted, sex-adjusted and migrant/ethnic minority group-adjusted incidence rates. Rates in each neighbourhood will be standardised using the 2022 census data, stratified by age, sex and migrant/ethnic minority group status. Poisson regression will be conducted to quantify variation in incidence rates by age, sex, urbanicity and migrant/ethnic minority group status, with random effects to account for the multilevel data structure (H1.1). We will inspect random effects for neighbourhood and their 95% CI of models for psychotic disorders compared with models of other disorders (H1.2(a)). A categorical variable for diagnostic group and a diagnostic group×ethnic/migrant minority status will be added to address H1.2(b). The commonly observed age×sex interaction will be tested to address H1.3. Further, a two-way interaction effect for individual-level migrant/ethnic minority group status×area-level migrant/ethnic minority density will be fitted to investigate whether, for migrant/ethnic minority groups, rates will be highest in areas where they constitute a small proportion of the local population (H1.4). We will test whether differences between directly observed incidence rates will be statistically significant.

Clinical stage transitions

In the inception cohort arm, we will perform maximum likelihood estimation of hazard rates of exponential distributions under noninformative right censorship78 to estimate rates of transition from earlier stages to later stages. Poststratification weights will be implemented to adjust for sample deviations from the Mannheim population on age, sex and ethnic/migrant minority group status. We will further use Cox regression to test whether rates of transition from earlier to later clinical stages will be greater in individuals in stage 1a than in stage 0 (H2.1), and whether transition rates will be associated with demographic, risk and protective factors (H2.2), while controlling for potential confounders. Testing the regression coefficient for deviation from zero in a Cox model with single dummy covariate is asymptotically equivalent to a log-rank test for differences between two survival functions (with sample size calculations based on the formula by Schoenfeld.76

Transdiagnostic stages, dimensions, momentary phenotypes, population mean

Latent class analysis will be used to identify empirically-based clinical stages (ie, subgroups/latent classes) that account for associations among the observed categorical variables for assessing mental health at baseline (H3.1). We will fit a series of models (in MPlus V.8) to determine the classification that best represents associations among observed variables. Consistent with the number of hypothesised clinical stages of contemporary staging models, we expect to identify at least four latent classes (ie, stage 0, 1a, 1b, 2), with the class of no symptoms/well-being separating into at least two additional distinct classes that improve model fit. Latent class membership will be used in subsequent analyses to establish convergence with contemporary staging models (H3.2). Multidimensional item response modelling will be conducted on observer-rated, self-rated and EMA data on symptoms, well-being and positive mental health (in MPlus V.8) to examine whether a bifactor model with general and specific factors of transdiagnostic and momentary phenotypes provides the best model fit compared with correlated multidimensional and other alternative item response models. We will use (multilevel) multinomial regression models to investigate whether factor scores for the identified dimensions and momentary phenotypes will be associated with clinical stages at baseline, while controlling for potential confounders (H3.3). Multinomial receiver operating characteristic analysis79 will be used to investigate whether factor scores allow for accurate classification into clinical stages and establish optimal cut-off values based on sensitivity, specificity and area under the curve. Poisson regression will be conducted to test whether the population mean and distribution (and other measures of central tendency, as appropriate for the distribution) of transdiagnostic dimensions, digital momentary phenotypes and established measures of mental health will be associated with incidence of severe mental disorder (H3.4). We will carefully examine and model the distribution of mental health data and, based on previous work,21 23 define populations by stratifying our incidence sample according to four a priori selected characteristics (ie, age, sex, urbanicity, ethnicity) to examine the relationship between population mean and incidence in each of these populations. We will further use Cox regression to test, in our inception cohort sample, stratified according to the same a priori selected characteristics, whether changes in the population mean (in transdiagnostic dimensions, digital momentary phenotypes and established measures) predict an increased risk for transition from earlier to later clinical stages in each population (H3.5).

Discussion

Monitoring of population mental health is a central aspect of health, risk and protective factor and demographic surveillance systems across the globe. Adopting a public mental health perspective on monitoring and surveillance requires us to consider the entire spectrum of mental health. While this population mental health approach has become an integral part of public mental health provision in many European countries, research on this issue in Germany remains very limited. Also, fundamental conceptual and methodological issues need to be addressed, and evidence on validity (and ultimately, utility) of dimensional and clinical staging approaches needs to be generated to provide an integrated approach for monitoring population mental health in an optimally efficient way that can be adapted and applied elsewhere to tackle the pressing public mental health challenges of our time at both the national and international levels.

The current two-arm incidence and inception cohort study will move beyond previous research by allowing us to examine fundamental conceptual and methodological issues in contemporary public mental health and establish the nationwide feasibility of design and methods for more comprehensive monitoring of population mental health. To achieve this, a combined, two-arm incidence and inception cohort study reflects the optimal design in terms of both efficiency and capacity for delineating the basic epidemiology of population mental health. Given the variation in neighbourhood characteristics and migrant and ethnic minority populations in the city of Mannheim, the methodology will offer a unique population laboratory to study variation in incidence and clinical stage transitions in terms of place and ethnicity with high external validity. This design will allow for re-establishing the basic epidemiology of severe mental disorders for the first time in over 20 years in Germany. Specifically, this study will generate evidence on the treated incidence of severe mental disorder by important sociodemographic characteristics based on primary data in Germany, while, at the same time, considering and investigating, in detail, the interface between incidence of severe mental disorder, contemporary transdiagnostic clinical staging models, dimensional representations of psychopathology (ie, HiTOP) and positive mental health, across the entire spectrum of mental health and well-being. While sampling by clinical stages initially adopts a categorical approach, all individuals along the continuum of mental health (from asymptomatic to severe mental disorder) are included in the current study and carefully characterised using established measures of clinical staging and dimensional approaches. Ultimately, empirically delineating clinical categories (such as stages) with clinical utility from transdiagnostic dimensional measures of mental (ill-)health will benefit clinical and preventative decision-making as well as mental health surveillance in the public mental health field.

Social and ethnic inequities in mental health play an important role and have been delineated, for example, in the inequality paradox16 for a long time; however, robust and appropriate evidence to empirically identify its driving factors remains limited. We are taking critical steps to capture the diversity of the population and to enable and increase participation of individuals and groups who may be vulnerable, marginalised and hard-to-reach due to structural and contextual factors, and who are often underrepresented in mental health research.80–82 First, given risk and protective factors, as well as access to and help-seeking from mental health services may differ for individuals with and without knowledge of German language, the present study implements multilanguage assessment and relies on a multilingual and multicultural team. Second, we actively involve local stakeholders and relevant organisations in contact with residents in potentially vulnerable or marginalised positions due to structural and contextual factors that are still far too often not included in research despite forming a critical part of the population, such as migrant and ethnic minority groups or people currently living in poverty. To engage communities and create mutually beneficial collaborations, mental health professionals from our research team offer activities such as conversation groups or lectures about mental health topics relevant to the specific community. Including these organisations’ representatives, we ultimately aim to conduct research jointly with, rather than only about, these populations and communities. In addition, the option to participate remotely in assessments via online video call and database systems reduces barriers for participation.

Taken together, by combining categorical, clinical staging and dimensional approaches to mental health and well-being, promoting community engagement and reducing barriers to participation, the current incidence and inception cohort study seeks to address several aspects crucial in putative pathways to social and ethnic inequities in mental health.17 Examining incidence of severe mental illness along with barriers to access appropriate mental healthcare, we seek to provide an evidence base for planning of mental health services and reduction of barriers. We will carefully evaluate the feasibility of the proposed methodology and its potential to be extended to large-scale investigations including nation-wide monitoring of population mental health, as a basis for establishing comprehensive surveillance systems and thereby contribute to laying the ground for assessing the impact of novel public mental health interventions and services at the population level.

The study design of the Mannheim incidence and inception cohort study study encompasses several methodological strengths. However, the following considerations need to be taken into account once results will be interpreted. While the cohort arm will epidemiologically characterise the population of young people living in Mannheim in terms of strata of age, sex, neighbourhood and migrant/ethnic minority status, availability of data from the national statistics office and stratum size did not allow for socioeconomic status to be included as an additional stratum for quota sampling. Given ample evidence regarding the role of socioeconomic (dis)advantage in mental health33 34, the final composition of the cohort sample in terms of education and income will have to be critically reflected. Moreover, focusing efforts on the local study site of Mannheim will allow for findings to be generalised primarily to populations and regions with a similar demographic make-up, which we will seek to examine in a transportability evaluation. Also, multi-site investigations will be required to examine in more detail potential regional differences in the epidemiology of population mental health.

Ethics and dissemination

The study has received ethical approval from the local ethics committee (Medical Faculty Mannheim, Heidelberg University, 585-21) and is conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants (and in case of minors, their legal guardians) sign informed written consent. Results will be published in peer-reviewed journals and presented at academic conferences. Moreover, attention will be given to dissemination through (social) media to address a broader public audience.
