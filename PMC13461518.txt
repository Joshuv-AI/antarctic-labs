Case Report: transformation to SCLC from EGFR-mutated squamous cell lung carcinoma during osimertinib treatment
Authors: Takashi Yamana, Kazuhiko Hirano, Risa Yoshioka, Yusuke Mizuhashi, Kasumi Tsukamoto, Yukiko Abe, Hiroaki Kodama, Mitsuhiro Kamimura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSomatic EGFR mutations are prevalent in lung adenocarcinoma but are exceptionally rare in primary squamous cell carcinoma (SCC). Consequently, while histologic transformation to small cell lung carcinoma (SCLC) is an established resistance mechanism to EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) in adenocarcinoma, its occurrence in EGFR-mutated SCC remains virtually uncharacterized, representing a significant knowledge gap in thoracic oncology.Case descriptionWe report the case of a 42-year-old male initially diagnosed with stage IIIA EGFR-mutated SCC. Following definitive chemoradiotherapy, the patient experienced disease recurrence and was sequentially treated with erlotinib plus ramucirumab, followed by osimertinib. Upon clinical progression during osimertinib therapy, a repeat biopsy was performed. Pathological and immunohistochemical evaluations revealed a complete phenotypic shift from the initial TTF-1-negative, p40-positive SCC to a TTF-1-positive SCLC, while retaining the original EGFR exon 19 deletion. The patient exhibited a favorable clinical response to a subsequent regimen of carboplatin, etoposide, and durvalumab.ConclusionThe retention of the EGFR mutation in the present case enabled the definitive diagnosis of histologic transformation from the original squamous cell carcinoma, confirming a shared clonal origin rather than a second primary malignancy. This case underscores the critical clinical necessity of routine resistance monitoring and repeat biopsies with comprehensive histological profiling at the time of TKI failure, regardless of the initial non-small cell lung cancer histology”.

Introduction

“Lung cancer remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, characterized by substantial histological heterogeneity. It is broadly categorized into non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), which accounts for approximately 85% of cases, and small cell lung cancer (SCLC), comprising the remaining 15% (1, 2). Crucially, these two categories arise from distinct cells of origin—with NSCLC typically originating from epithelial lineages and SCLC from neuroendocrine progenitor cells—and exhibit fundamentally different biological behaviors (1, 2). Consequently, their clinical management and therapeutic strategies differ substantially. For advanced NSCLC, the integration of molecular targeted therapies and immune checkpoint inhibitors has significantly improved clinical outcomes (1). Concurrently, for SCLC, the addition of anti-PD-L1 antibodies to standard platinum-etoposide chemotherapy has established a new frontline standard of care, offering improved outcomes in an otherwise aggressive disease course (2, 3). Despite these distinct treatment frameworks, tumor evolution driven by selective therapeutic pressure presents a formidable challenge, highlighting the critical biologic significance of lineage plasticity and histologic evolution as major mechanisms of acquired resistance”.

Although epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) have drastically improved the prognosis of patients with EGFR-mutated non-small cell lung carcinoma (NSCLC), acquired resistance remains a major clinical challenge. Histologic transformation to small cell lung carcinoma (SCLC) is a well-recognized resistance mechanism, occurring in approximately 3% to 15% of EGFR-mutated NSCLC cases treated with EGFR-TKIs (4, 5). While the vast majority of previously reported cases involve transformation from adenocarcinoma, transformation from EGFR-mutated squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) is exceedingly rare (6, 7). Here, we report a unique case of EGFR-mutated SCC that transformed into SCLC during osimertinib treatment, providing further insight into the tumor plasticity and resistance mechanisms of this rare subset.

Case description

Patient presentation and initial diagnosis

A 42-year-old Asian male (former smoker, Brinkman index: 35) presented with left-sided chest pain. He had no significant past medical history or family history. The computed tomography (CT) revealed a left segment 3 (S3) mass with hilar lymph node metastasis (cT2aN2M0, Stage IIIA) (Figure 1A), without distant involvement on PET-CT and brain MRI. A CT-guided biopsy confirmed squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) with immunohistochemistry (IHC) showing p40(+) and negative thyroid transcription factor-1(TTF-1), CD56, (Figure 2A) and napsin A (data not shown). Molecular profiling identified an EGFR exon 19 deletion and PD-L1 expression of 50%–74%.

First-line therapy and sequential TKI treatment

Despite these biomarkers, the patient received definitive concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT; 60 Gy/30 fractions with two cycles of cisplatin/vinorelbine), achieving complete remission (Figure 1B). Consolidation durvalumab was initiated but discontinued after eight cycles following disease progression with the development of multiple bone metastases (sacrum and ilium) (Figures 1F, G). After palliative radiotherapy (30 Gy/10 fractions) to the bone, second-line therapy was required. Considering the interstitial lung disease (ILD) risk associated with osimertinib following immune checkpoint inhibitors, erlotinib plus ramucirumab was selected.

This regimen maintained disease control for 22 months until Grade 2 proteinuria necessitated a switch to osimertinib (80 mg daily). After 14 months of stable disease, a new mass emerged in the previously irradiated left S3 field (Figure 1D).

Histological and molecular characterization of transformation

Bronchoscopic re-biopsy revealed small cell lung carcinoma (SCLC) transformation (Figure 2B). The neuroendocrine phenotype was confirmed via CD56, synaptophysin, and TTF-1 positivity. Notably, TTF-1 converted from negative to positive upon transformation, while p40 switched from positive to negative (Figure 2B). The serum tumor marker profile further supported the diagnosis of SCLC transformation; a significant elevation in pro-gastrin-releasing peptide (ProGRP) was observed, while squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) antigen levels remained within the normal range (Figure 3). Molecular analysis confirmed the retained original EGFR exon 19 deletion, establishing a clonal link between the initial SCC and the transformed SCLC rather than a second primary malignancy.

Subsequent treatment and clinical outcome

Although PET-CT and brain MRI showed no evidence of other new metastases, curative stereotactic radiotherapy was not considered a viable option because the recurrence occurred within a previously irradiated field. The patient was subsequently treated with four cycles of carboplatin, etoposide, and durvalumab, achieving a significant partial response (RECIST) (Figure 1E). Maintenance durvalumab is currently ongoing with sustained disease control. The detailed course of treatment is shown in Figure 3. The patient provided written informed consent for this report.

Discussion

In this case, EGFR-mutated squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) transformed into small cell lung carcinoma (SCLC) following EGFR-tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) therapy. A notable feature was the conversion of TTF-1 expression; while the initial pure SCC was p40-positive and TTF-1-negative, the transformed SCLC exhibited strong TTF-1 expression and neuroendocrine markers. TTF-1 is a lineage-specific transcription factor typically expressed in adenocarcinomas and SCLC, but not in SCC. Despite this immunohistochemical shift, the retained EGFR exon 19 deletion confirms a shared clonal origin. This evidence refutes the possibility of a metachronous second primary SCLC, instead highlighting the capacity of EGFR-mutated SCC clones for molecular reprogramming.

The frequency of EGFR mutations in SCC varies according to detection sensitivity and the presence of occult adenocarcinoma components. A study of resected pure SCC in China reported EGFR mutations in 3.4% of cases (8). Notably, among never-smoker Asian females, EGFR mutations were identified in 29.9% of SCC cases, a frequency comparable to that observed in adenocarcinoma (9). Conversely, genomic analyses in North American populations revealed a near-total absence of classical EGFR mutations, with only rare variants (e.g., L861Q) identified (10). These findings underscore significant regional, racial, and genetic variations in the molecular landscape of lung SCC.

SCLC transformation is a recognized resistance mechanism in EGFR-mutated NSCLC, occurring in 3% to 15% of patients (6, 7). The median time to transformation ranges from 17 to 24 months and appears consistent across different EGFR-TKI generations (7). Transformation occurs more frequently in tumors harboring EGFR exon 19 deletions than in those with L858R mutations (7). Importantly, previous cohorts of transformed SCLC predominantly consisted of adenocarcinoma; SCC histology was conspicuously absent in these series (6, 7). To the best of our knowledge, while a few reports have described the transformation of SCC (where EGFR mutation status was unknown) to SCLC following immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) therapy (11, 12), no prior cases have documented EGFR-mutated SCC transforming to SCLC after TKI therapy. This highlights the scarcity of this specific clinical presentation.

The mechanism of SCLC transformation is primarily attributed to the inactivation of tumor suppressor genes, such as RB1 and TP53 (6). Recent studies have also highlighted the relevance of NOTCH, phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/AKT, and MYC signaling pathways (13, 14). However, as most existing reports focus on adenocarcinoma-to-SCLC transformation, the mechanisms specific to SCC-to-SCLC transition remain poorly understood. Oser et al. proposed that SCLC may originate from alveolar type 2 (AT2) cells, which are the typical progenitors of adenocarcinoma (15), whereas SCC is considered to arise from basal cells. SCLC, particularly of the peripheral type, exhibits a high rate of TTF-1 expression, indicating a tumor origin from terminal respiratory unit cells (such as AT2 cells and club cells) and representing a key commonality with lung adenocarcinoma (16). Furthermore, recent research has revealed that p63, a master regulator of squamous differentiation, actively blocks squamous-to-neuroendocrine transdifferentiation (17). These divergent cellular origins, combined with the p63-mediated molecular barrier, likely account for the clinical scarcity of SCLC transformation originating from SCC. These molecular mechanisms contextualize the distinctive features of the present case, in which TTF-1-negative SCC transformed to TTF-1-positive SCLC.

Distinguishing transformed SCLC (t-SCLC) from de novo SCLC is clinically and biologically paramount. While both entities share classical neuroendocrine features and near-universal co-inactivation of RB1 and TP53, they exhibit distinct molecular landscapes and therapeutic responses (6, 7, 15). Most notably, t-SCLC characteristically retains the founding oncogenic driver mutations—such as the EGFR exon 19 deletion observed in our patient—confirming its clonal lineage from the preceding non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), a feature virtually absent in de novo SCLC (15). Clinically, t-SCLC runs an even more aggressive course, demonstrating a shorter duration of response to standard platinum-etoposide chemotherapy and a worse overall prognosis compared to its de novo counterpart (6, 7). Furthermore, their responsiveness to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) differs; while adding ICIs to frontline chemotherapy yields a survival benefit in de novo extensive-stage SCLC, t-SCLC typically shows poor ICI efficacy, reflecting the ‘cold’ immune microenvironment inherent to EGFR-mutant clones (6, 7). Our patient’s favorable and sustained response to carboplatin, etoposide, and durvalumab is therefore noteworthy, highlighting the need to further explore the unique microenvironmental features of squamous-derived t-SCLC.

Systemic chemotherapy remains the cornerstone of treatment for SCLC transformed from EGFR-mutated NSCLC. However, the prognosis is generally poor, with recent international data reporting a median progression-free survival (PFS) of only 2 months and a median overall survival (OS) of 9 months (6, 7). Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have shown limited efficacy as monotherapy in this setting, and their addition to chemotherapy has not yet been demonstrated to provide a significant survival benefit (6, 7). These clinical characteristics appear to mirror the poor ICI responsiveness typically observed in the original EGFR-mutated NSCLC, although the current evidence base is constrained by the rarity of the condition. In the present case, we opted for a combination of chemotherapy and an ICI, which is currently the established first-line standard of care for de novo extensive-stage SCLC (18). Although this ICI-combined regimen proved clinically effective for our patient despite the generally poor outcomes reported in the literature, further accumulation of t-SCLC cases is highly warranted to validate this approach. Furthermore, given that taxanes such as paclitaxel have demonstrated efficacy in late-line settings for t-SCLC (6), we consider them a viable therapeutic option for future disease progression.

Several limitations should be acknowledged in this study. First, due to the lack of comprehensive genomic profiling such as next-generation sequencing, the precise molecular mechanisms and sequential genetic alterations underlying the SCC-to-SCLC transformation could not be fully characterized. Second, as a single case report, further larger clinical studies are warranted to validate the generalizability of our therapeutic outcomes.

Conclusion

We have reported a rare case of EGFR-mutated SCC that transformed into SCLC following EGFR-TKI therapy. This case illustrates that the molecular reprogramming and lineage plasticity typically observed in adenocarcinomas can also occur within the squamous cell lineage, potentially overcoming p63-associated lineage constraints. Our findings underscore the importance of performing re-biopsies and comprehensive molecular profiling at the time of disease progression, even in patients with an initial squamous histology. Further elucidation of the underlying molecular mechanisms is warranted to optimize therapeutic strategies and improve outcomes for this distinct subset of patients.
