Abstract
. We describe additive surjections on direct sum of matrix algebras that preserve singularity in one direction. As an application, we classify additive surjections on finite-dimensional C ∗ C^{\ast} -algebras that preserve mutual strong Birkhoff–James orthogonality in one direction.

1.
Introduction, preliminaries and statement of the main results

In a normed space
X
we lack an inner product; therefore, a notion of orthogonality is required that coincides with the standard definition

x\perp y\Leftrightarrow\langle x,y\rangle=0

whenever
X
is an inner product space (throughout this paper, the scalar field is assumed to be the field of complex numbers
\mathbb{C}
, unless explicitly mentioned otherwise). One of the most studied notions is the Birkhoff–James orthogonality, which has been investigated for more than 90 years (see the pioneering works of Birkhoff
[
2
]
and James
[
7
]
,
[
6
]
,
[
5
]
).

We say that two vectors
x,y\in X
are Birkhoff–James (BJ for short) orthogonal, and write
x\perp_{BJ}y
, if

\|x+\lambda y\|\geq\|x\|,\quad\text{for all }\lambda\in\mathbb{C}.

In
[
17
]
, Arambašić and Rajić naturally extended the above definition to (right) Hilbert
C^{\ast}
-modules over a
C^{\ast}
-algebra
\mathfrak{A}
. Namely, for two elements
x,y\in X
we say that
x
is strongly BJ orthogonal to
y
, and write
x\perp_{BJ}^{S}y
, if

\|x+yc\|\geq\|x\|\quad\text{for all }c\in A.

Then, in
[
15
]
, Arambašić et al. defined the mutual version of the relation previously considered. Namely, for two elements
x,y\in X
, we say that
x
and
y
are mutual strong BJ orthogonal, denoted by
x\mathrel{\perp\!\!\!\!\perp}^{S}_{BJ}\,y
, if
x\perp_{BJ}^{S}y
and
y\perp_{BJ}^{S}x
.

In this paper, we will consider a
C^{\ast}
-algebra
\mathfrak{A}
, regarded as a right Hilbert
C^{\ast}
-module over itself. So
a\in\mathfrak{A}
is strong BJ orthogonal to
b\in\mathfrak{A}
if for any
c\in\mathfrak{A}
there holds

(1.1)

\|a+bc\|\geq\|a\|.

The essence of this relation can be studied by the orthograph introduced in
[
15
]
. Recall that the vertices of this orthograph are all elements of the projective space over
\mathfrak{A}
, i.e.,
\{[a]=\mathbb{C}a;\;\;a\in\mathfrak{A}\setminus\{0\}\}
, and two vertices
[x]
and
[y]
form an edge if
x\mathrel{\perp\!\!\!\!\perp}^{S}_{BJ}\,y
for some representatives
x\in[x]
and
y\in[y]
. In
[
15
]
its diameter and isolated vertices were determined in case when
\mathfrak{A}=B(H)
, where
H
is Hilbert space, and also in case when
\mathfrak{A}
is a commutative
C^{\ast}
-algebra. This was essentially used to determine linear preservers of mutual strong BJ orthogonality on
B(H)
(see
[
16
]
). Later, in
[
8
]
, isolated vertices in an arbitrary
C^{\ast}
-algebra are described, and an estimate of the diameter is given. Finally, in
[
23
]
, this orthograph was considered when
\mathfrak{A}
is a finite-dimensional
C^{\ast}
-algebra, i.e.,

(1.2)

\mathfrak{A}\cong M_{n_{1}}(\mathbb{C})\oplus M_{n_{2}}(\mathbb{C})\oplus\dots\oplus M_{n_{k}}(\mathbb{C}),

for some natural numbers
k,n_{1},n_{2},\dots,n_{k}
up to permutation, and the diameter was determined. To avoid confusion, in the two aforementioned papers the orthograph on the projective space was considered, whereas here our ambient space will be the entire
C^{\ast}
-algebra. We will repeatedly use a strong version of the well-known Stampfli-Magajna-Bhatia-Šemrl theorem (for the proof see
[
17
, Proposition 2.8]
; see also papers
[
22
]
,
[
19
]
and
[
1
]
for a retrospective).

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
A,B\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
. Then
A\perp_{BJ}^{S}\,B
if and only if there is a unit vector
x\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
such that
\|Ax\|=\|A\|
and
B^{\ast}Ax=0
.

In this paper, we study additive preservers. They have been studied for many years (see, for example, the papers
[
21
]
and
[
10
]
). Recently, there was a renewed interest in additive preservers of the orthogonality relation — see the paper of Wójcik
[
24
, Section 4]
, where he proved that the additive preserver of BJ orthogonality on real normed space is automatically linear and hence, from the famous result by Oman
[
20
, Lemma 3.3, page 38]
, it is a scalar multiple of an isometry. We remark in passing that Blanco and Turnšek
[
3
]

extended this result by classifying bijections preserving BJ orthogonality in both directions on projective spaces induced by reflexive smooth spaces.
Also, in two papers by Li, Liu, and Peralta (see
[
11
]
and
[
12
]
), Wójcik’s result was extended to complex normed spaces. In
[
11
, Proposition 2.2]
it was shown that an additive map
\Phi
, which preserves the BJ orthogonality between two complex normed spaces
X,Y
, with
\dim X\geq 2
and admitting a conjugation (i.e., there exists a conjugate-linear and involutive isometry on
X
) is automatically real-linear; if, in addition,
\Phi
is surjective and preserves BJ orthogonality in both directions, then it is automatically continuous. This result was extended in
[
12
, Lemma 2.14]
where it was shown that
\Phi
admits a unique real-linear extension to a BJ preserver on completions of
X
and
Y
provided that, besides the previous assumptions on
X
, we also have that
X^{\ast}
is strictly convex.

Our main contribution here is the following result. Recall that, for a given orthonormal basis
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, the map

J\colon x=\sum\langle x,e_{i}\rangle e_{i}\mapsto\overline{x}:=\sum\overline{\langle x,e_{i}\rangle}e_{i}

is a conjugate-linear isometry on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, and it induces a conjugate-linear isometry on a
C^{\ast}
-algebra
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
by transforming a matrix
X\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
to

X\mapsto JXJ.

It is immediate that
JXJ=\overline{X}
is an (entrywise) conjugation of the elements of
X
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
be a finite-dimensional complex
C^{\ast}
-algebra, not isomorphic to
\mathbb{C}
,
\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathbb{C}
and
M_{2}(\mathbb{C})
, and let
\Phi\colon\mathfrak{A}\to\mathfrak{A}
be an additive surjection. Then, the following are equivalent

(i)

\Phi
preservers mutual strong BJ orthogonality.

(ii)

There exists a permutation
\pi
of minimal ideals of
\mathfrak{A}
, which respects their dimension, a positive scalar
\gamma
and unitary elements
u,v\in\mathfrak{A}
such that

\Phi(a)=\gamma u\pi(a)^{\dagger}v,

where
x\mapsto x^{\dagger}
denotes a map which is identity on some (possibly none) minimal ideals and entrywise conjugation with respect to a fixed orthonormal basis on other minimal ideals.

We remark that the structure of additive preservers of mutual strong BJ orthogonality on
\mathbb{C}
,
\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathbb{C}
or
M_{2}(\mathbb{C})
,
is different; we refer to the last section for more details.

Clearly, each map which preserves strong BJ orthogonality also preserves mutual strong BJ orthogonality. Hence, as an immediate corollary, we can classify additive preserves of strong BJ orthogonality:

Corollary 1.3
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
1.2
, an additive surjection
\Phi\colon\mathfrak{A}\to\mathfrak{A}
preserves strong BJ orthogonality if and only if it equals one of the forms in item (ii) if Theorem
1.2
.

Our main tool will be a reduction to preservers of singularity. Since this is of independent interest, we shall state it here, after clarifying the following terminology: Any permutation
\pi
of
\{1,.\dots,k\}
induces a linear bijection, again denoted by
\pi
, on a block-diagonal matrix algebra
\mathcal{A}=\bigoplus_{1}^{k}M_{n_{i}}(\mathbb{F})
, given by
\bigoplus A_{i}\mapsto\bigoplus A_{\pi(i)}
. For example, the transposition
\tau=(1,2)
will map
A\oplus B
into
B\oplus A
.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
\mathbb{F}
be a field of characteristic zero and let
k,n_{1},\dots,n_{k}\geq 1
be integers. The following is equivalent for an additive surjection

\Phi\colon M_{n_{1}}(\mathbb{F})\oplus\dots\oplus M_{n_{k}}(\mathbb{F})\to M_{n_{1}}(\mathbb{F})\oplus\dots\oplus M_{n_{k}}(\mathbb{F}).

(i)

\Phi
maps singular elements into singular elements.

(ii)

There exists a permutation of blocks
\pi
, which respects their dimension, such that

\Phi(M_{n_{i}}(\mathbb{F}))=\pi(M_{n_{i}}(\mathbb{F})).

Moreover, if
n_{i}\geq 2
there exist a field endomorphism
\sigma_{i}\colon\mathbb{F}\to\mathbb{F}
and invertible matrices
P_{i},Q_{i}\in M_{n_{i}}(\mathbb{F})
,
(i=1,\dots,k)
, such that

\Phi\colon A_{i}\mapsto P_{i}\pi(A_{i})^{\sigma_{i}}Q_{i}\quad\hbox{ or }\quad\Phi\colon A_{i}\mapsto P_{i}\pi(A_{i}^{T})^{\sigma_{i}}Q_{i};\qquad A_{i}\in M_{n_{i}}(\mathbb{F}).

2.
Additive preservers of singularity

Within this section, we prove Theorem
1.4
. We remark that the main idea, which is a reduction to additive rank-one nonincreasing maps, comes from a paper by Fošner and Šemrl,
[
4
]
. Throughout this section,

\mathcal{A}=M_{n_{1}}(\mathbb{F})\oplus M_{n_{2}}(\mathbb{F})\oplus\dots\oplus M_{n_{k}}(\mathbb{F})

is a block-diagonal algebra over the field
\mathbb{F}
, with
\mathrm{char}(\mathbb{F})=0
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
m_{1},m_{2}\geq 1
be integers and
A_{22}\in\mathrm{GL}_{m_{2}}(\mathbb{F})
.
Then

\begin{pmatrix}pI_{m_{1}}+A_{11}&A_{12}\\
A_{21}&A_{22}\end{pmatrix}\in M_{m_{1}+m_{2}}(\mathbb{F})

is invertible except for at most finitely many values
p\in\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,\dots\}
.

Proof.

Observe that
\det\begin{pmatrix}pI_{m_{1}}+A_{11}&A_{12}\\
A_{21}&A_{22}\end{pmatrix}
is a polynomial in
p
of degree
m_{1}
. Moreover, the coefficient of
p^{m_{1}}
is equal to
\det(A_{22})
which is nonzero. Hence, it has at most
m_{1}
zeros (some of which may lie outside
\mathbb{N}
), and the claim follows.
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\Phi\colon\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}
be an additive, surjective map that preserves singularity in one direction. Then
\Phi
maps rank-one operators to rank-one operators or to zero.

Proof.

Suppose, to the contrary, that there exists a rank-one matrix in
\mathcal{A}
whose
\Phi
-image is a matrix of rank
\ell
(
2\leq\ell<N:=n_{1}+n_{2}+\dots+n_{k}
) in
\mathcal{A}
.
By pre- and post- composing
\Phi
with suitable transformations of the form
A\mapsto PAQ
, where
P
and
Q
are invertible block-diagonal matrices (such map preserves singularity), we may assume that

\Phi(E_{11})=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{k}\left(I_{n_{i}^{\prime}}\oplus 0_{n_{i}-n_{i}^{\prime}}\right),

where
n_{i}^{\prime}
can be zero, with at least one
n_{i}^{\prime}\geq 1
.
Define
F=I-\Phi(E_{11})
and a (compression) subspace
\mathcal{V}=F\mathcal{A}F|_{R(F)}
; notice that

(2.1)

\mathcal{V}=\bigoplus_{n^{\prime}_{i}\geq 1}M_{n^{\prime}_{i}}(\mathbb{F})\subseteq M_{N-\ell}(\mathbb{F})\subseteq M_{N-2}(\mathbb{F}).

Let
T
be an arbitrary element in
\mathcal{A}
. We will show that the compression of
\Phi(T)
on
\mathcal{V}
is singular, which contradicts the surjectivity of
\Phi
.

If
\mathop{\mathrm{rk}}(T)\leq N-2
, then
pE_{11}+T
is singular for every
p\in\mathbb{N}
(by rank argument). By additivity and singularity preserving,
p\Phi(E_{11})+\Phi(T)
is also singular for every
p\in\mathbb{N}
. Assume that
\Phi(T)
is invertible in
\mathcal{V}
. Regarding the blocks of
\mathcal{A}
we have three possibilities:

n_{i}^{\prime}=0,\quad n_{i}^{\prime}=n_{i},\ \hbox{ and }\quad 0<n_{i}^{\prime}<n_{i}.

In a block with
n_{i}^{\prime}=0
the matrix
\Phi(pE_{11}+T)|_{M_{n_{i}}(\mathbb{F})}=pI_{n_{i}}+\Phi(T)|_{M_{n_{i}}(\mathbb{F})}
is singular if and only if
-p
is an eigenvalue of
\Phi(T)|_{M_{n_{i}}(\mathbb{F})}
, and there are at most finitely many such integers
p
.

A block with
n_{i}^{\prime}=n_{i}
belongs completely to
\mathcal{V}
, and by the assumption,
\Phi(pE_{11}+T)|_{M_{n_{i}}(\mathbb{F})}=0_{n_{i}}+\Phi(T)|_{M_{n_{i}}(\mathbb{F})}
is invertible there for each integer
p
.

Finally in a block with
0<n_{i}^{\prime}<n_{i}
we get that
\Phi(T)
is invertible except for at most finitely many values
p\in\mathbb{N}
by Lemma
2.1
.

Combined, this shows that, except for at most finitely many integers
p
, all blocks of a singular matrix
\Phi(pE_{11}+T)
are invertible, a contradiciton. Indeed, the compression of
\Phi(T)
to
\mathcal{V}
must be singular whenever
\mathop{\mathrm{rk}}T\leq N-2
.

Next, if
\mathop{\mathrm{rk}}(T)=N-1
, write
T
as a sum of
N-1
rank-one matrices
T_{1},\dots,T_{N-1}
from
\mathcal{A}
. Then, by the preceding result, for any sum of
N-2
or fewer elements of the set
\{T_{1},T_{2},\dots,T_{N-1}\}
, its
\Phi
-image is singular in
\mathcal{V}
. By (
2.1
) we may consider
\mathcal{V}
to be embedded into
M_{N-2}(\mathbb{F})
, and applying
[
4
, Lemma 2.1]
(for
m=N-1>n=N-2
) we get
\Phi(T)
is singular in
\mathcal{V}
.

If
\mathop{\mathrm{rk}}(T)=N
we write
T
as a sum of
N
rank-one matrices
T_{1},\dots,T_{N}\in\mathcal{A}
, and apply the previous arguments to show that
\Phi(T)
is again singular in
\mathcal{V}
.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\Phi\colon\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}
be an additive, surjective map that preserves singularity in one direction. Then
\Phi
maps each block
M_{n_{i}}(\mathbb{F})
onto some block
M_{n_{j}}(\mathbb{F})
.

Proof.

Since
\Phi
is an additive surjection, there exists a rank-one element
R\in\mathcal{A}
with
\Phi(R)\neq 0
. By Lemma
2.2

\mathop{\mathrm{rk}}\,\Phi(R)=1
, so there exists
j\in[1,k]
such that
\Phi(R)\in M_{n_{j}}(\mathbb{F})
. Let
i
be the index of the block containing
R
. Denote

R=x\otimes y\quad\text{\ where\ }\quad x,y\in 0\oplus\mathbb{F}^{n_{i}}\oplus 0;

(here and throughout, given
x,y\in\mathbb{F}^{n}
we denote by
x\otimes y
a rank-one matrix, which, in a standard basis, maps
z
into
(y^{\ast}z)x
, where
y^{\ast}
denotes a conjugated transpose of a column vector
y
).
Now, if for some
w\in 0\oplus\mathbb{F}^{n_{i}}\oplus 0
it holds
0\neq\Phi(x\otimes w)\in M_{n_{\ell}}(\mathbb{F})
where
n_{\ell}\neq n_{j}
, then
x\otimes y+x\otimes w
is rank-one, however
\Phi(x\otimes y+x\otimes w)
is rank two, a contradiction. The same argument shows that
\Phi(z\otimes y)\in M_{n_{j}}(\mathbb{F})
for all
z\in 0\oplus\mathbb{F}^{n_{i}}\oplus 0
.

Now, take an arbitrary rank-one
z\otimes w\in M_{n_{i}}(\mathbb{F})
with
z,w\in 0\oplus\mathbb{F}^{n_{i}}\oplus 0
. Then, by the above,

(2.2)

\Phi(x\otimes w),\Phi(z\otimes y)\in M_{n_{j}}(\mathbb{F}).

If at least one of them is nonzero, say
\Phi(x\otimes w)\neq 0
, we can repeat the above arguments, to deduce that also
\Phi(z\otimes w)\in M_{n_{j}}(\mathbb{F})
. However, if both elements of (
2.2
) are zero, then consider a rank-one
(x+z)\otimes(y+w)
, which
\Phi
maps into
\Phi(x\otimes y)+0+0+\Phi(z\otimes w)
again of rank-one at most. Since
0\neq\Phi(x\otimes y)\in M_{n_{i}}(\mathbb{F})
, we necessarily have that
\Phi(z\otimes w)\in M_{n_{j}}(\mathbb{F})
. It now follows by the additivity that the whole block
M_{n_{i}}(\mathbb{F})
is mapped into
M_{n_{j}}(\mathbb{F})
.

Thus,
\Phi
maps each block
M_{n_{i}}(\mathbb{F})\subseteq\mathcal{A}
into some block
M_{n_{j}}(\mathbb{F})
. Since there are finitely many blocks, it must permute the blocks and must map
M_{n_{i}}(\mathbb{F})
onto
M_{n_{j}}(\mathbb{F})
otherwise it would not be surjective.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Using the Lemma
2.3
, we can denote

\Phi_{i}\colon=\Phi|_{M_{n_{i}}(\mathbb{F})}\colon M_{n_{i}}(\mathbb{F})\to M_{n_{j}}(\mathbb{F}).

Now we are in a position to apply the result
[
13
, Theorem 2; Corollary 3]
to
D=D_{1}=\mathbb{F}
and
m=n=n_{i},p=q=n_{j}
. Cases (i) and (ii) of
[
13
, Corollary 3]
are excluded by surjectivity if
n_{j}\geq 2
(see also
[
14
]
for more results in this direction). Therefore, when
n_{j}\geq 2
, we conclude that the only remaining possibilities are

(iii)
\Phi_{i}(A)=P_{i}A^{\sigma_{i}}Q_{i}
and

(iv)
\Phi_{i}(A)=(P_{i}A^{\sigma_{i}}Q_{i})^{T}
,

where
P_{i}\in M_{n_{j},n_{i}}(\mathbb{F})
is surjective and
Q_{i}\in M_{n_{i},n_{j}}(\mathbb{F})
is injective (by surjectivity of
\Phi_{i}
) and
\sigma_{i}\colon\mathbb{F}\to\mathbb{F}
is a field isomorphism.

We claim
\Phi_{i}
maps the block
M_{n_{i}}(\mathbb{F})
to a block of the same size. Indeed, if
n_{i}\geq 2
, we observe that
\dim\Phi_{i}(M_{n_{i}}(\mathbb{F}))\leq\dim M_{n_{i}}(\mathbb{F})
since
\Phi_{i}
is semilinear. Consequently, if
\mathcal{A}
contains a block of size greater than one, then only a block of maximal possible size in
\mathcal{A}
can be mapped to a block of maximal size in
\mathcal{A}
. Since we know that
\Phi
permutes the blocks we can continue these arguments to complete the proof.
∎

3.
Additive preservers of mutual strong Birkhoff-James orthogonality

We will apply the results of Theorem
1.4
in the case
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{C}
to clasify mutual strong BJ orthogonality preservers on finite-dimensional
C^{\ast}
-algebra
\mathfrak{A}
. Recall that they are all of the form (
1.2
), and recall that
A
is mutually BJ orthogonal to
B
if
A\perp^{S}_{BJ}\,B
and
B\perp^{S}_{BJ}\,A
, where the relation of strong BJ orthogonality,
\perp^{S}_{BJ}
, was defined at (
1.1
).

We will frequently use the following three simple observations: (i) if
B\perp^{S}A
for an invertible matrix
A
, then
B=0
. Namely, if we put
C=-A^{-1}B
in relation
\|B+AC\|\geq\|B\|
we immediately get
B=0
. See also
[
15
, Lemma 1.2]
and
[
8
, Proposition 2.5 and 2.6]
. Also, (ii)
A\mathrel{\perp}^{S}_{BJ}A
if and only if
A=0
. Finally, (iii) for a rank-one operator
A
, the relation
A\mathrel{\perp\!\!\!\!\perp}^{S}_{BJ}\,B
is equivalent to
A^{\ast}B=0
(see
[
18
, Proposition 2.3]
).

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
be a finite-dimensional
C^{\ast}
-algebra not isomorphic to
\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathbb{C}
. Furthermore, let
\Phi\colon\mathfrak{A}\to\mathfrak{A}
be an additive map that preserves a mutual strong BJ orthogonality in one direction. Then
\Phi
preserves the singularity.

Proof.

If
\mathfrak{A}=\mathbb{C}
, the statement of Lemma
3.1
is trivially valid. Indeed, the only singular element in
\mathbb{C}
is 0, and
\Phi(0)=0
.

If
\mathfrak{A}=M_{2}(\mathbb{C})
, suppose, to the contrary, that there exists a nonzero singular element (and thus of rank-one, which we denote by
x\otimes y
) that is mapped into an invertible element. Applying
\Phi
to the relation

x\otimes y\mathrel{\perp\!\!\!\!\perp}^{S}_{BJ}\,x^{\prime}\otimes z

(here and throughout,
x^{\prime}
denotes any vector perpendicular to
x
, with
\|x^{\prime}\|=\|x\|
) we obtain
\Phi(x^{\prime}\otimes z)=0
for an arbitrary
z\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
, because
\Phi(x\otimes y)
is invertible. Then by Theorem
1.1

(x+x^{\prime})\otimes y\mathrel{\perp\!\!\!\!\perp}^{S}_{BJ}\,(x-x^{\prime})\otimes y,

so applying additive
\Phi
on both sides, we get

\Phi(x\otimes y)\mathrel{\perp\!\!\!\!\perp}^{S}_{BJ}\,\Phi(x\otimes y),

which is impossible by invertibility of
\Phi(x\otimes y)
(in fact, it would imply
\Phi(x\otimes y)=0
).

In all other cases, we can embed
\mathfrak{A}
into
M_{N}(\mathbb{C})
where
N=n_{1}+n_{2}+\dots+n_{k}
and show that
\Phi(T)
is singular whenever
T
is singular. First, suppose that
\mathop{\mathrm{rk}}\,T\leq N-2
. Let

T=\sigma_{1}x_{1}\otimes y_{1}+\sigma_{2}x_{2}\otimes y_{2}+\dots+\sigma_{N-2}x_{N-2}\otimes y_{N-2},\ \sigma_{1}\geq\sigma_{2}\geq\dots\geq\sigma_{N-2}\geq 0,

be its singular value decomposition (SVD for short) and let us assume the opposite, that
\Phi(T)
is invertible. We extend the set
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{N-2}\}
to an orthonormal basis
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{N-2},x_{N-1},x_{N}\}
and similarly with
y_{i}
’s. Then, by Theorem
1.1
, we know that

T\mathrel{\perp\!\!\!\!\perp}^{S}_{BJ}\,x_{i}\otimes y_{i},\ \text{where\ }i\in\{N-1,N\},

and applying
\Phi
on both sides, we get
\Phi(T)\mathrel{\perp\!\!\!\!\perp}^{S}_{BJ}\,\Phi(x_{i}\otimes y_{i})
and therefore
\Phi(x_{i}\otimes y_{i})=0,\ i\in\{N-1,N\}
because
\Phi(T)
is invertible. Also, again by Theorem
1.1

T+p\,x_{N-1}\otimes y_{N-1}\mathrel{\perp\!\!\!\!\perp}^{S}_{BJ}\,T+p\,x_{N}\otimes y_{N},

for
p\in\mathbb{N}
greater than
\sigma_{1}
. Applying additive
\Phi
we get
\Phi(T)\mathrel{\perp\!\!\!\!\perp}^{S}_{BJ}\,\Phi(T)
, so
\Phi(T)=0
, which contradicts the assumed invertibility of
\Phi(T)
.

Finally, suppose
\mathop{\mathrm{rk}}T=n-1
. Again, let

T=\sigma_{1}x_{1}\otimes y_{1}+\sigma_{2}x_{2}\otimes y_{2}+\dots+\sigma_{N-1}x_{N-1}\otimes y_{N-1},\ \sigma_{1}\geq\sigma_{2}\geq\dots\geq\sigma_{N-1}>0,

be its SVD and let us assume the opposite, that
\Phi(T)
is invertible. Then, by Theorem
1.1

T\mathrel{\perp\!\!\!\!\perp}^{S}_{BJ}\,x_{N}\otimes y_{N}\pm x\otimes y,

where
x\perp\{x_{N},x_{1}\}
and
y\perp y_{N}
are both nonzero (they exist because
N\geq 3
). From the invertibility of
\Phi(T)
, we conclude that
\Phi(x_{N}\otimes y_{N}\pm x\otimes y)=0
and then from the additivity
\Phi(x_{N}\otimes y_{N})=0
and
\Phi(x\otimes y)=0
. In particular, by taking
x=\sigma_{i}x_{i}
and
y=y_{i}
for
2\leq i\leq N-1
, we conclude that
\Phi(\sigma_{i}x_{i}\otimes y_{i})=0
. So

\Phi(\sigma_{1}x_{1}\otimes y_{1})=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N-1}\Phi(\sigma_{i}x_{i}\otimes y_{i})=\Phi(T)

is invertible, which is impossible by the previous part of our proof.
∎

As we show in the next example, Lemma
3.1
does not hold in case
\mathfrak{A}=\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathbb{C}
.

Example 3.2
.

Let us define
\Phi\colon\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathbb{C}
as follows:

\Phi(\lambda,\mu)=\lambda(1,1).

By definition
(\lambda_{1},\mu_{1})\mathrel{\perp\!\!\!\!\perp}^{S}_{BJ}\,(\lambda_{2},\mu_{2})
for two nonzero elements in
\mathfrak{A}
if and only if
\lambda_{1}=\mu_{2}=0
or
\mu_{1}=\lambda_{2}=0
. In both cases, one element is of the form
(0,\mu)
, so it is mapped to
(0,0)
by
\Phi
. So,
\Phi
preserves mutual strong BJ orthogonality, however, it maps a singular element
(1,0)
to invertible
(1,1)
.

Now, we are in a position to apply Theorem
1.4
by which

\Phi\colon A_{i}\mapsto P_{i}\pi(A_{i})^{\sigma_{i}}Q_{i}\quad\hbox{ or }\quad\Phi\colon A_{i}\mapsto P_{i}\pi(A_{i}^{T})^{\sigma_{i}}Q_{i};\qquad A_{i}\in M_{n_{i}}(\mathbb{F}).

The permutation of blocks
\pi
is clearly a linear isometry, hence preserves the mutual strong BJ orthogonality. By composing
\Phi
with its inverse
\pi^{-1}
, we can and will assume that
\pi
is the identity permutation. Let us now eliminate the second option (namely, transposition) on blocks of dimension at least two.

Lemma 3.3
.

The map
\Phi_{i}\colon M_{n_{i}}(\mathbb{C})\to M_{n_{i}}(\mathbb{C})
for
n_{i}\geq 2
, defined by
\Phi_{i}(A)=P_{i}\left(A^{T}\right)^{\sigma_{i}}Q_{i}
does not preserve the mutual strong BJ orthogonality in one direction.

Proof.

This is essentially proven in
[
16
, Lemma 2.6]
, but we reprove it for the sake of convinience. Let
E
be any rank-one orthogonal projection with rational entries. Then
(E^{T})^{\sigma_{i}}=E
. Also,
E(I-E)=0
, so
E\mathrel{\perp\!\!\!\!\perp}^{S}_{BJ}\,(I-E)
, which implies that
P_{i}(E^{T})^{\sigma_{i}}Q_{i}\mathrel{\perp\!\!\!\!\perp}^{S}_{BJ}\,P_{i}((I-E)^{T})^{\sigma_{i}}Q_{i}
. This simplifies into

P_{i}EQ_{i}\mathrel{\perp\!\!\!\!\perp}^{S}_{BJ}\,P_{i}(I-E)Q_{i}.

Since
P_{i}EQ_{i}
is a rank-one, we conclude
(P_{i}EQ_{i})^{\ast}P_{i}(I-E)Q_{i}=0
, that is,

Q_{i}^{\ast}EP_{i}^{\ast}P_{i}(I-E)Q_{i}=0.

Since
Q_{i}
is invertible,
EP_{i}^{\ast}P_{i}(I-E)=0
, that is,

EP_{i}^{\ast}P_{i}=EP_{i}^{\ast}P_{i}E

for all rank-one
orthogonal projections
E
with rational coeficients. Insert
E=e_{i}\otimes e_{i}
, where
e_{1},e_{2},\dots,e_{n_{i}}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{i}}
is a standard orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{C}^{n_{i}}
to deduce that
P_{i}^{\ast}P_{i}
is a diagonal matrix. Insert also
E=\frac{1}{2}(e_{1}+e_{k})\otimes(e_{1}+e_{k})
to deduce that
P_{i}^{\ast}P_{i}
is a scalar multiple of identity. This implies that the map

\Psi_{i}=P_{i}^{\ast}P_{i}\Phi_{i}:A\mapsto(A^{T})^{\sigma_{i}}Q_{i}

also preserves a mutual strong BJ orthogonality.
To finish the proof, note that

e_{1}\otimes e_{1}\mathrel{\perp\!\!\!\!\perp}^{S}_{BJ}\,e_{2}\otimes(e_{1}+e_{2})

so applying
\Psi_{i}
we get

(e_{1}\otimes e_{1})Q_{i}\mathrel{\perp\!\!\!\!\perp}^{S}_{BJ}\,((e_{1}+e_{2})\otimes e_{2})Q_{i}.

Since both elements are of rank-one, it follows that
Q_{i}^{\ast}((e_{1}+e_{2})\otimes e_{2})^{\ast}(e_{1}\otimes e_{1})Q_{i}=0
. Then invertibility of
Q_{i}
implies that

e_{2}\otimes e_{1}=((e_{1}+e_{2})\otimes e_{2})^{\ast}(e_{1}\otimes e_{1})=0,

which is a contradiction.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

If the map
\Phi_{i}\colon M_{n_{i}}(\mathbb{C})\to M_{n_{i}}(\mathbb{C}),n_{i}\geq 2
defined by
\Phi_{i}(A)=P_{i}A^{\sigma_{i}}Q_{i}
preserves the mutual strong BJ orthogonality in one direction, then
\sigma_{i}(\lambda)=\lambda
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
or
\sigma_{i}(\lambda)=\overline{\lambda}
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
. Moreover,
P_{i}
is a scalar multiple of a unitary operator.

Proof.

On rank-one operators, mutual strong BJ orthogonality is equivalent to range orthogonality, so for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
and
\ell\neq j

(e_{\ell}+\lambda e_{j})\otimes e_{\ell}\mathrel{\perp\!\!\!\!\perp}^{S}(\bar{\lambda}e_{\ell}-e_{j})\otimes e_{\ell}.

Applying
\Phi_{i}
on both sides gets

P_{i}(e_{\ell}+\sigma_{i}(\lambda)e_{j})\otimes Q_{i}^{\ast}e_{\ell}\mathrel{\perp\!\!\!\!\perp}^{S}P_{i}(\sigma_{i}(\bar{\lambda})e_{\ell}-e_{j})\otimes Q_{i}^{\ast}e_{\ell},

from where we can conclude

(3.1)

(e_{\ell}^{\ast}+\overline{\sigma_{i}(\lambda)}e_{j}^{\ast})P_{i}^{\ast}P_{i}(\sigma_{i}(\bar{\lambda})e_{\ell}-e_{j})=0.

Now, if we put
\lambda=q\in\mathbb{Q}
, knowing that
\sigma_{i}(q)=q
, we get

(3.2)

(e_{\ell}^{\ast}+qe_{j}^{\ast})P^{\ast}P(qe_{\ell}-e_{j})=0

for every rational number
q
. Denote elements of positive matrix by
P_{i}^{\ast}P_{i}:=(a_{\ell j})_{1\leq\ell,j\leq n}
.
From (
3.2
) we have that

\overline{a_{\ell j}}q^{2}+(a_{\ell\ell}-a_{jj})q-a_{\ell j}=0,

so
a_{\ell j}=0
and
a_{\ell\ell}=a_{jj}
. This proves that
P_{i}^{\ast}P_{i}
is a scalar multiple of identity and returning to the equality (
3.1
), we conclude that

\sigma_{i}(\overline{\lambda})=\overline{\sigma_{i}(\lambda)}.

So,
\sigma_{i}
is the identity or conjugation map.
∎

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}=\bigoplus_{1}^{k}M_{n_{i}}(\mathbb{C})
and let
J\subseteq\{1,\dots,k\}
. Then, a real-linear map
\dagger\colon\mathfrak{A}\to\mathfrak{A}
, defined by

\begin{cases}A_{i}^{\dagger}=A_{i};&\hbox{ if $i\in J$ }\\
A_{i}^{\dagger}=\overline{A_{i}};&\hbox{ otherwise}\end{cases}.

preserves mutual strong BJ orthogonality.

Proof.

This is an easy consequence of the fact that
\|(A_{1},\dots A_{k})\|=\max\|A_{i}\|=\max\|\overline{A_{i}}\|
(the last identity follows from SVD of
A_{i}
).
∎

We next consider several subcases of low dimensional blocks separately. The first one is an abelian
C^{\ast}
-algebra
\mathfrak{A}=\mathbb{C}^{k},k\geq 3
. Recall that in such
\mathfrak{A}
the additive mutual strong BJ preserver will still permute the blocks, but at the moment we have not yet found its structure within each block.

3.1.
Case
\mathbb{C}^{k},k\geq 3

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
\Phi=(\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{k})\colon\mathbb{C}^{k}\to\mathbb{C}^{k},k\geq 3
be an additive map that preserves a mutual strong BJ orthogonality in one direction. Then

\Phi(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{k})=\gamma u(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{k})^{\dagger},

where
\gamma>0
,
u\in\mathbb{C}^{k}
is a unitary and
a^{\dagger}
is defined in Theorem
1.2
.

Proof.

We will divide the proof into several steps.

Step 1.

|\varphi_{1}(x)|=\dots=|\varphi_{k}(x)|
for every
x\in\mathbb{C}
.

To see this note that
\|(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\|=\max\|x_{i}\|
. It hence follows by definition of strong BJ orthogonality that
(x,\dots x,0_{i},x,\dots,x)\mathrel{\perp\!\!\!\!\perp}^{S}_{BJ}\,(x,\dots,x,0_{j},x,\dots,x)
, whenever
i\neq j
.
Applying
\Phi
this gives

(\varphi_{1}(x),\dots,0_{i},\dots,\varphi_{k}(x))\mathrel{\perp\!\!\!\!\perp}^{S}_{BJ}\,(\varphi_{1}(x),\dots,0_{j},\dots,\varphi_{k}(x)),

from where we deduce

|\varphi_{j}(x)|\geq|\varphi_{\ell}(x)|\quad\text{\ for all $\ell\neq i$}\quad\hbox{ and\ }\quad|\varphi_{i}(x)|\geq|\varphi_{\ell}(x)|\quad\text{\ for all $\ell\neq j$}.

Continuing in the same manner, by taking all possible
i\neq j
, we conclude that
|\varphi_{1}(x)|=|\varphi_{2}(x)|=\dots=|\varphi_{k}(x)|
for every
x\in\mathbb{C}
.

Step 2.

If
\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{C}
and
|\lambda|=|\mu|
then
|\varphi_{i}(\lambda)|=|\varphi_{j}(\mu)|
for every
1\leq i,j\leq k
.

To see this, note that
(\lambda,\mu,0,\dots,0)\mathrel{\perp\!\!\!\!\perp}^{S}_{BJ}\,(0,\mu,\mu,\dots,\mu)
, so applying
\Phi
we get

(\varphi_{1}(\lambda),\varphi_{2}(\mu),0,\dots,0)\mathrel{\perp\!\!\!\!\perp}^{S}_{BJ}\,(0,\varphi_{2}(\mu),\varphi_{3}(\mu),\dots,\varphi_{k}(\mu)),

and we can conclude
|\varphi_{1}(\lambda)|\geq|\varphi_{2}(\mu)|
. Now, if we change
(0,\mu,\mu,\dots,\mu)
to
(\lambda,0,\lambda,\dots,\lambda)
, we also get
|\varphi_{2}(\mu)|\geq|\varphi_{1}(\lambda)|
, so
|\varphi_{2}(\mu)|=|\varphi_{1}(\lambda)|
. By Step 1,
|\varphi_{i}(\lambda)|=|\varphi_{1}(\lambda)|=|\varphi_{2}(\mu)|=|\varphi_{j}(\mu)|
for every
1\leq i,j\leq k
, as claimed.

Step 3.

If
\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{C}
and
|\lambda|>|\mu|
then
|\varphi_{j}(\lambda)|\geq|\varphi_{i}(\mu)|
for every
1\leq i,j\leq k
.

To see this, note that
(\lambda,\mu,0,\dots,0)\mathrel{\perp\!\!\!\!\perp}^{S}_{BJ}\,(0,\mu,\mu,\dots,\mu)
, so applying
\Phi
we get

(\varphi_{1}(\lambda),\varphi_{2}(\mu),0,\dots,0)\mathrel{\perp\!\!\!\!\perp}^{S}_{BJ}\,(0,\varphi_{2}(\mu),\varphi_{3}(\mu),\dots,\varphi_{k}(\mu)),

so
|\varphi_{1}(\lambda)|\geq|\varphi_{2}(\mu)|
, which by Step 2 proves the claim.

Step 4.

\varphi_{i}
is continuous for every
1\leq i\leq k
.

To see this, choose
\varepsilon>0
and let
n\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
\frac{|\varphi_{i}(1)|}{n}<\varepsilon
. Define
\delta=\frac{1}{n}
. Then if
|\alpha-\beta|<\delta
, using additivity, the fact that
|n(\alpha-\beta)|<1
and Step 3, respectively, we get

|\varphi_{i}(\alpha)-\varphi_{i}(\beta)|=\frac{1}{n}\left|\varphi_{i}(n(\alpha-\beta))\right|\leq\frac{1}{n}|\varphi_{i}(1)|<\varepsilon.

Step 5.

\varphi_{j}(a+bi)=a\varphi_{j}(1)+b\varphi_{j}(i)
for every
1\leq j\leq k
and every
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
.

To see this, let
\gamma_{j}:=\varphi_{j}(1)
. By additivity,
\varphi_{j}(a)=a\cdot\gamma_{j}
for every
a\in\mathbb{Q}
. Then, by continuity (Step 4)
\varphi_{j}(a)=a\cdot\gamma
for every
a\in\mathbb{R}
. Similarly, defining
\delta_{j}:=\varphi_{j}(i)
and using additivity and continuity, we get
\varphi_{j}(bi)=bi\cdot\delta_{j}
. The statement then follows directly from the additivity of the mapping
\varphi_{j}
.

Step 6.

\varphi_{j}(\lambda)=\varphi_{j}(1)\lambda
for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
or
\varphi_{j}(\lambda)=\varphi_{j}(1)\overline{\lambda}
for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
.

To see this, by Step 2,
|\varphi_{j}(\lambda)|
is constant for every
|\lambda|=1
. Then by Step 5

|c\cdot\gamma_{j}\pm\sqrt{1-c^{2}}\cdot\delta_{j}|

is constant for every
c\in[-1,1]
. If
\gamma_{j}=0
, then
\delta_{j}=0
, so
\varphi_{j}\equiv 0
, which contradicts surjectivity. Hence,

\left|c\pm\sqrt{1-c^{2}}\frac{\delta_{j}}{\gamma_{j}}\right|

is constant for every
c\in[-1,1]
. By inserting
c=1
, we get that this constant is equal to 1, and hence,
\left|c\pm\sqrt{1-c^{2}}\frac{\delta_{j}}{\gamma_{j}}\right|=1
for every
c\in[-1,1]
. Inserting
c=0
gives further

(3.3)

|\delta_{j}|=|\gamma_{j}|.

Finally, inserting
c=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
, gives

(3.4)

\left|1\pm\tfrac{\delta_{j}}{\gamma_{j}}\right|=\sqrt{2}.

It follows from (
3.3
) and (
3.4
) that
\frac{\delta_{j}}{\gamma_{j}}=\pm i
. Consequently,
\varphi_{j}(a+bi)=a\gamma_{j}+bi\gamma_{j}=(a+bi)\gamma_{j}
or
\varphi_{j}(a+bi)=a\gamma_{j}-bi\gamma_{j}=(a-bi)\gamma_{j}
for every
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
, which was the desired result.

Step 7.

Conclusion of the proof.

Define
\gamma=|\varphi_{1}(1)|>0
and
u=\left(\frac{\varphi_{1}(1)}{|\varphi_{1}(1)|},\frac{\varphi_{2}(1)}{|\varphi_{1}(1)|},\dots,\frac{\varphi_{k}(1)}{|\varphi_{1}(1)|}\right)
, which is unitary by Step 1. Then, by Step 6, the map
\Phi
has the desired form.
∎

3.2.
Case
\mathbb{C}\oplus M_{n_{j}}(\mathbb{C}),n_{j}\geq 2
.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\Phi=(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2})\colon\mathbb{C}\oplus M_{n_{j}}(\mathbb{C})\to\mathbb{C}\oplus M_{n_{j}}(\mathbb{C}),n_{j}\geq 2
, where
\varphi_{2}(A)=AQ
for some invertible matrix
Q\in M_{n_{j}}(\mathbb{C})
. If
\Phi
is an additive mutual strong BJ orthogonality preserver, then
Q
is a scalar multiple of a unitary and
\varphi_{1}(\lambda)=\varphi_{1}(1)\lambda
for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
or
\varphi_{1}(\lambda)=\varphi_{1}(1)\overline{\lambda}
for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
. In addition,
\|Q\|=|\varphi_{1}(1)|
.

Proof.

Note that for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{C},|\lambda|\leq 1
and
x\in\mathbb{C}^{2},\|x\|=1
the operator
(\lambda,x\otimes x)
attains its norm at
(0,x)
, so by Theorem
1.1
we have

(\lambda,x\otimes x)\mathrel{\perp\!\!\!\!\perp}^{S}_{BJ}\,(\lambda,x^{\prime}\otimes x^{\prime}),

where
x^{\prime}
denotes any vector perpendicular to
x
, with
\|x^{\prime}\|=\|x\|
. Applying
\Phi=(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2})
, we get

(\varphi_{1}(\lambda),(x\otimes x)Q)\mathrel{\perp\!\!\!\!\perp}^{S}_{BJ}\,(\varphi_{1}(\lambda),(x^{\prime}\otimes x^{\prime})Q).

It follows, again by Theorem
1.1
that

(3.5)

\|Q^{\ast}x\|=\|(x\otimes x)Q\|\geq|\varphi_{1}(\lambda)|.

Assume, in addition
|\lambda|=1
. Notice that
(0,I)
attains its norm on
(0,x^{\prime})
while
(\lambda,x\otimes x)
attains its norm on
(1,0)
, so from Theorem
1.1

(\lambda,x\otimes x)\mathrel{\perp\!\!\!\!\perp}^{S}_{BJ}\,(0,I).

Applying
\Phi
we get

(\varphi_{1}(\lambda),(x\otimes x)Q)\mathrel{\perp\!\!\!\!\perp}^{S}_{BJ}\,(0,Q),

so by the definition of strong BJ orthogonality, keeping in mind that
Q
is invertible, we necessarily have

|\varphi_{1}(\lambda)|\geq\|(x\otimes x)Q\|=\|Q^{\ast}x\|.

Together with (
3.5
), it follows that
\|Q^{\ast}x\|=|\varphi_{1}(\lambda)|
for all unit vectors
x\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
and
|\lambda|=1
. Consequently,
Q^{\ast}
is a scalar multiple of a unitary and
|\varphi_{1}|
is constant on a unit circle.

Moreover, (
3.5
) now implies
|\varphi_{1}(\lambda)|\leq\|Q^{\ast}x\|
for all
|\lambda|\leq 1
with an equality for
|\lambda|=1
. Hence, as in the proof of Steps 4-6 of Lemma
3.6
,
\varphi_{1}
is continuous, additive, and actually of the stated form.
∎

3.3.
Case
M_{n_{\ell}}(\mathbb{C})\oplus M_{n_{j}}(\mathbb{C}),n_{\ell},n_{j}\geq 2
.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
\Phi=(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2})\colon M_{n_{\ell}}(\mathbb{C})\oplus M_{n_{j}}(\mathbb{C})\to M_{n_{\ell}}(\mathbb{C})\oplus M_{n_{j}}(\mathbb{C}),n_{\ell},n_{j}\geq 2
, where
\varphi_{i}(A)=AQ_{i}
for some invertible matrices
Q_{1}\in M_{n_{\ell}}(\mathbb{C})
and
Q_{2}\in M_{n_{j}}(\mathbb{C})
. If
\Phi
is a mutual strong BJ orthogonality preserver, then
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
are scalar multiples of unitaries and
\|Q_{1}\|=\|Q_{2}\|
.

Proof.

Note that for all unit vectors
x\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{\ell}}
, the operator
(x\otimes x,I)
attains its norm on
(x,0)
, so by Theorem
1.1
we have

(3.6)

(x\otimes x,I)\mathrel{\perp\!\!\!\!\perp}^{S}_{BJ}\,(x^{\prime}\otimes x^{\prime},I).

Applying
\Phi
we get

((x\otimes x)Q_{1},Q_{2})\mathrel{\perp\!\!\!\!\perp}^{S}_{BJ}((x^{\prime}\otimes x^{\prime})Q_{1},Q_{2}),

which, again by Theorem
1.1
and invertibility of
Q_{2}
implies that

\|x\otimes Q_{1}^{\ast}x\|\geq\|Q_{2}\|.

Therefore,

\|Q_{1}\|=\|Q_{1}^{\ast}\|\geq\|Q_{1}^{\ast}x\|=\|x\otimes Q_{1}^{\ast}x\|\geq\|Q_{2}\|,

holds for all unit vectors
x\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{\ell}}
. Moreover, applying
\Phi
on a pair

(I,x\otimes x)\mathrel{\perp\!\!\!\!\perp}^{S}_{BJ}\,(I,x^{\prime}\otimes x^{\prime}),

for unit vector
x\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{j}}
, we obtain analogously that

\|Q_{2}\|\geq\|Q_{2}^{\ast}x\|\geq\|Q_{1}\|

holds for all unit vectors
x\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{j}}
. Consequently,
\|Q_{1}\|=\|Q_{2}\|
, and both
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
are scalar multiples of unitaries.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

As mentioned in (
1.2
) we may assume that
\mathfrak{A}
is a block-diagonal matrix algebra.
Now, by Lemma
3.1

\Phi
preserves singularity and hence by Theorem
1.4
there exists a permutation of blocks
\pi
, which respects their dimension, such that

\Phi(M_{n_{i}}(\mathbb{C}))=\pi(M_{n_{i}}(\mathbb{C})).

Notice that
\pi
is a linear
\ast
-isometry, so it preserves mutual strong BJ orthogonality in both direciotns. Therefore, by composing
\Phi
with
\pi^{-1}
we obtain an additive surjection (denoted again by
\Phi
), which still preserves mutual strong BJ orthogonality, and satisfies

\Phi(M_{n_{i}}(\mathbb{C}))=M_{n_{i}}(\mathbb{C}).

Hence, we may decompose

\Phi=\Phi_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus\Phi_{k}.

Assume
n_{i}\geq 2
. Then, by Theorem
1.4
, there exists a field endomorphism
\sigma_{i}\colon\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
and invertible matrices
P_{i},Q_{i}\in M_{n_{i}}(\mathbb{C})
, such that

\Phi_{i}\colon A_{i}\mapsto P_{i}A_{i}^{\sigma_{i}}Q_{i}\quad\hbox{ or }\quad\Phi_{i}\colon A_{i}\mapsto P_{i}(A_{i}^{T})^{\sigma_{i}}Q_{i}.

The second case is impossible by Lemma
3.3
. In the first case, by Lemma
3.4
,
P_{i}=\lambda_{i}U_{i}
is a scalar multiple of unitary and
\sigma_{i}
is identity or complex conjugation. Hence if we replace
\Phi
by a map
U^{\ast}\Phi
, for a unitary
U=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{k}U_{i}
, with
U_{\ell}=1
if
n_{\ell}=1
, we can and will assume that

\Phi_{i}\colon A_{i}\to A_{i}^{\dagger}(\lambda_{i}Q_{i});\quad A_{i}\in M_{n_{i}}(\mathbb{C})\text{\ with\ }n_{i}\geq 2.

Assume
n_{i}=1
. By the assumptions on
\mathfrak{A}
we have that
\mathfrak{A}
is either abelian with at least three blocks, or else there exists a block with
n_{j}\geq 2
. In the first case, by Lemma
3.6
we imediately get a form as stated in (ii) of Theorem
1.2
. In the second case,
\Phi_{i}
is a scalar multiple of identity on
\mathbb{C}
or a scalar multiple of complex conjugation on
\mathbb{C}
, while
Q_{j}
is a scalar multiple of a unitary.

By Lemma
3.5
, if we compose the additive
\Phi=\bigoplus\Phi_{i}
with a map
\Psi\colon\mathfrak{A}\to\mathfrak{A}
, which is identity on blocks with
\Phi_{i}
linear, and entrywise conjugation on blocks with
\Phi_{i}
conjugate-linear, we will get a linear preserver of mutual strong BJ orthogonality. Clearly,
\Phi_{i}
must also preserves mutual strong BJ orthogonality. Now, we are in a position to apply a known result
[
16
, Theorem 2.7]
by which
Q_{i}=\mu_{i}V_{i}
is a scalar multiple of a unitary if
n_{i}\geq 3
. The same conclusion holds also if
n_{i}=2
by Lemma
3.8
. We may hence replace
\Phi
by
\Phi V^{\ast}
for a unitary
V=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{k}V_{i}
, with
V_{\ell}=1
if
n_{\ell}=1
to achieve that

\Phi(A_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus A_{k})=(\lambda_{1}\mu_{1}A_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus\lambda_{k}\mu_{k}A_{k}).

By the last statements in Lemmas
3.7
and
3.8
we conclude that

|\lambda_{1}\mu_{1}|=\dots=|\lambda_{k}\mu_{k}|=\gamma>0.

Hence

\Phi(A_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus A_{k})=\gamma U(A_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus A_{k}),

for a unitary
U=\left(\frac{\lambda_{1}\mu_{1}}{\gamma}I_{n_{1}},\dots,\frac{\lambda_{k}\mu_{k}}{\gamma}I_{n_{k}}\right)
.
∎

4.
Concluding remarks

Let us show by examples that the conclusions of Theorem
1.2
are not valid if its assumptions fail. Recall than if
\mathfrak{A}=\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathbb{C}
, then a nonstandard (nonsurjective) linear preserver of mutual strong BJ orthogonality was already given in Example
3.2
.

Example 4.1
.

If
\mathfrak{A}=\mathbb{C}
, then
a\perp_{BJ}^{S}b
if and only if one of
a,b\in\mathbb{C}
vanishes, which is, therefore, equivalent to
a\mathrel{\perp\!\!\!\!\perp}^{S}_{BJ}b
. Here, every additive map on
\mathbb{C}
preserves a mutual strong BJ orthogonality.

Example 4.2
.

If
\mathfrak{A}=\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathbb{C}
, an additive surjection
\Phi\colon\mathfrak{A}\to\mathfrak{A}
defined by

\Phi(\lambda,\mu)=(\lambda,2\mu)

preserves a mutual strong BJ orthogonality, but it is not of the form stated in Theorem
1.2
.

Example 4.3
.

If
\mathfrak{A}=M_{2}(\mathbb{C})
, and an additive surjection
\Phi\colon\mathfrak{A}\to\mathfrak{A}
preserves mutual strong BJ orthogonality, then by Lemma
3.1
and Theorem
1.4
we have
\Phi(A)=PA^{\sigma}Q
or
\Phi(A)=P(A^{T})^{\sigma}Q
for some invertible
P,Q\in M_{2}(\mathbb{C})
and field isomorphism
\sigma\colon\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
. The second case is impossible by Lemma
3.3
. By Lemma
3.4

P
is a scalar multiple of a unitary operator and
\sigma
is an identity or conjugation. If needed, we can replace
\Phi
by
X\mapsto\overline{\Phi(X)}
(entrywise conjugation) to achieve that
\Phi
is linear. Finally (this part is actually from
[
16
, Theorem 2.9.]
,
\Phi(A)=PAQ
is a mutual strong BJ preserver for every invertible
Q
.

Remark 4.4
.

If we do not impose additivity, there will be wild-types of strong BJ orthogonality preservers; see
[
9
, Theorem 3.27 and Example 3.30]
.
