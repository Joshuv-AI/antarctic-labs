Resonant delay in a stationary quantum clock: Lifting the threshold mask
Paul C. W. Davies, Damien A. Easson

Abstract
Quantum transit times have a long history of inequivalent definitions, including phase times, dwell times, and quantum-clock constructions. In this context we revisit the Salecker–Wigner–Peres stationary quantum clock as a phase-sensitive scattering observable, with clock time defined by the energy derivative of the transmission phase shift across the interaction region. For real compactly supported one-dimensional potentials, we show that the raw stationary Peres clock generically contains a universal 1 / E 1/\sqrt{E} continuum-edge term whose coefficient is fixed by low-energy scattering data. For the attractive square well, this threshold singularity is inherited from the vanishing exterior momentum and the associated scattering matching, rather than from resonant delay itself. We derive the exact stationary clock time for the square well and introduce a new threshold-subtracted clock observable. Away from exceptional zero-energy tuning, the subtraction removes the universal low-energy term and isolates the resonant contribution. Comparison with the dwell time and the transmission Wigner phase delay shows that the threshold-subtracted clock acquires the expected local Lorentzian form near isolated transmission resonances. Near the continuum edge, if ε \varepsilon denotes the detuning from threshold, the resonant peak grows only as ε − 1 / 2 \varepsilon^{-1/2} , whereas the unsubtracted threshold background grows as ε − 3 / 2 \varepsilon^{-3/2} . A symmetric barrier–well–barrier cavity and a numerical asymmetric two-step attractive well provide complementary controls. The result is a new threshold-subtracted stationary-clock candidate that separates universal threshold kinematics from pole-sensitive resonant delay.

II
Square-well setup and stationary clock definition

We consider the one-dimensional attractive square well

V(x)=\begin{cases}-\,V_{0},&0<x<a,\\
0,&x<0\ \text{or}\ x>a,\end{cases}\qquad V_{0}>0,

(1)

with stationary Schrödinger equation

-\psi^{\prime\prime}(x)+V(x)\psi(x)=E\psi(x),\qquad E>0.

(2)

We use

k=\sqrt{E},\qquad q=\sqrt{E+V_{0}},\qquad\kappa:=\sqrt{V_{0}}.

(3)

For left incidence, the transmission amplitude is

t(E)=\frac{e^{-ika}}{\cos(qa)-i\dfrac{k^{2}+q^{2}}{2kq}\sin(qa)}.

(4)

We define the stationary clock phase by restoring the free phase across the interaction region,

\delta_{P}(E):=\arg\!\big[t(E)e^{ika}\big],\qquad\tau_{P}(E):=\derivative{\delta_P}{E}.

(5)

Remark 1

(Operational meaning)
.

The quantity
\tau_{P}(E)
should be understood as a stationary,
phase-sensitive scattering observable rather than as a single-event arrival
time. In principle, the phase
\arg[t(E)e^{ikL}]
can be extracted by
interferometric comparison of the transmitted wave with a reference wave, or
equivalently by reconstructing the complex transmission amplitude over a narrow
energy window. The clock time is then the energy derivative of this measured
phase. The threshold-subtracted clock
\tau_{\rm sub}
is operationally
defined by the same phase measurement together with the independently determined
low-energy threshold coefficient
\ell_{\rm thr}
. Thus the subtraction removes a universal continuum-edge
contribution from a measurable stationary phase-time observable.

In this paper, “stationary SWP/Peres clock time” refers to this stationary phase-time convention obtained from the phase of
t(E)e^{ika}
. We do not use alternative postselected regional-clock definitions in which the clock is coupled only to a specified subregion and the resulting quantity may coincide with a dwell-type time. The quantity
\tau_{P}(E)
is the raw stationary SWP/Peres clock time whose threshold structure we seek to disentangle
13
;
4
.

III
A general low-energy threshold theorem

Before turning back to the square well, it is useful to isolate the threshold structure of the stationary clock in a model-independent form. We therefore consider a general real compactly supported potential with

\operatorname{supp}V\subset[0,L].

(6)

Writing
E=k^{2}
with
k>0
, let
t(k)
denote the transmission amplitude for left incidence. The stationary Peres phase and clock time are defined as in Sec.
II
, with the free phase restored across the support length
L
:

\delta_{P}(E):=\arg\!\big[t(k)e^{ikL}\big],\qquad\tau_{P}(E):=\derivative{\delta_P}{E}.

(7)

To state the threshold law precisely, let

\mathsf{T}(k)=\begin{pmatrix}A(k)&B(k)\\
C(k)&D(k)\end{pmatrix}

(8)

denote the transfer matrix across the support, defined in Appendix
A
. Since the Schrödinger equation depends on
k
only through
k^{2}
, the entries are even functions of
k
and admit expansions

\displaystyle A(k)

\displaystyle=A_{0}+A_{2}k^{2}+A_{4}k^{4}+O(k^{6}),

\displaystyle B(k)

\displaystyle=B_{0}+B_{2}k^{2}+B_{4}k^{4}+O(k^{6}),

\displaystyle C(k)

\displaystyle=C_{0}+C_{2}k^{2}+C_{4}k^{4}+O(k^{6}),

\displaystyle D(k)

\displaystyle=D_{0}+D_{2}k^{2}+D_{4}k^{4}+O(k^{6}),

(9)

with real coefficients.

The relevant threshold distinction is between the generic sector
C_{0}\neq 0
and the exceptional sector
C_{0}=0
. As shown in Appendix
A
, the condition
C_{0}=0
is equivalent to the existence of a nontrivial bounded zero-energy solution on
\mathbb{R}
.

Theorem 1

(Low-energy threshold law for the stationary Peres clock)
.

Let
V
be real and compactly supported in
[0,L]
.

(i)

Generic sector.
If
C_{0}\neq 0
, then

\delta_{P}(E)=\delta_{*}-2\ell_{\rm thr}\sqrt{E}+O(E^{3/2}),\qquad\tau_{P}(E)=-\frac{\ell_{\rm thr}}{\sqrt{E}}+O(\sqrt{E}),\qquad E\to 0^{+},

(10)

where

\ell_{\rm thr}:=-\frac{A_{0}+D_{0}}{2C_{0}},

(11)

and
\delta_{*}
is an
E
-independent phase determined by the sign of
C_{0}
and the branch choice.

(ii)

Exceptional sector.
If
C_{0}=0
, then
A_{0}D_{0}=1
and
A_{0}+D_{0}\neq 0
. Define

t_{0}:=\frac{2}{A_{0}+D_{0}},\qquad\tilde{\ell}_{\rm thr}:=-\frac{B_{0}-C_{2}}{2(A_{0}+D_{0})}.

(12)

Then

\delta_{P}(E)=\arg t_{0}-2\tilde{\ell}_{\rm thr}\sqrt{E}+O(E^{3/2}),\qquad\tau_{P}(E)=-\frac{\tilde{\ell}_{\rm thr}}{\sqrt{E}}+O(\sqrt{E}),\qquad E\to 0^{+}.

(13)

Here
t_{0}
is real and nonzero. In special cases, such as the symmetric square-well half-bound tuning, one further has
t_{0}=\pm 1
.

Corollary 1

(Generic threshold subtraction)
.

In the generic sector, the threshold-subtracted clock

\tau_{\rm sub}(E):=\tau_{P}(E)+\frac{\ell_{\rm thr}}{\sqrt{E}}

(14)

is finite at threshold, in fact

\tau_{\rm sub}(E)=O(\sqrt{E}),\qquad E\to 0^{+}.

(15)

The proof follows from the low-energy transfer-matrix expansion given in Appendix
A
.

Remark 2
.

Within the generic sector
C_{0}\neq 0
, the coefficient
\ell_{\rm thr}
may vanish on special parameter subsets. In that case the displayed
1/\sqrt{E}
contribution is absent and the next term in the low-energy expansion controls the threshold behavior. When referring to a generic threshold divergence, we assume
\ell_{\rm thr}\neq 0
.

Remark 3

(Barrier tunneling versus barrier-top crossing)
.

The threshold law is not restricted to attractive wells. It also applies to
positive barriers. For example, consider the square barrier

V(x)=U,\qquad 0<x<a,\qquad U>0.

For fixed
U>0
and
a>0
, the low-energy limit
E\to 0^{+}
is an
under-barrier scattering problem: the asymptotic momentum
k=\sqrt{E}

vanishes, while the interior wave number is imaginary. The zero-energy
Cauchy-data transfer matrix is

T_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}\cosh(\mu a)&\mu^{-1}\sinh(\mu a)\\
\mu\sinh(\mu a)&\cosh(\mu a)\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mu=\sqrt{U}.

Hence

\ell_{\rm thr}=-\frac{A_{0}+D_{0}}{2C_{0}}=-\frac{\coth(\mu a)}{\mu},

and the stationary Peres clock has the generic low-energy behavior

\tau_{P}(E)=-\frac{\ell_{\rm thr}}{\sqrt{E}}+O(\sqrt{E})=\frac{\coth(\sqrt{U}\,a)}{\sqrt{U}\,\sqrt{E}}+O(\sqrt{E}).

This is a fixed-barrier low-energy asymptotic. It is not uniform in the
removed-barrier limits
a\to 0
or
U\to 0
, where
C_{0}\to 0
and the
problem leaves the generic
C_{0}\neq 0
sector. The apparent divergence of the
coefficient in those limits therefore reflects noncommuting limits, not a
physical time for a nonexistent barrier.

This should be distinguished from the barrier-top limit
E\to U
, where the
interior evanescent wave number vanishes but the finite-width transfer matrix
is regular. Thus the subtraction developed here removes the external
low-energy threshold contribution, not a singularity at the top of the barrier.

IV
Exact square-well clock time and threshold subtraction

We now specialize the general threshold theorem of Sec.
III
to the attractive square well, where the transmission amplitude, stationary clock time, threshold coefficient, and subtracted clock can all be written explicitly. The square well therefore provides a useful analytic laboratory for exhibiting the general threshold obstruction and its removal.

Proposition 1

(Exact stationary clock time)
.

For the attractive square well, the stationary Peres clock time is

\tau_{P}(E)=\frac{ak^{2}q\,(k^{2}+q^{2})-\dfrac{1}{2}(q^{2}-k^{2})^{2}\sin(2qa)}{kq\Big(4k^{2}q^{2}+(q^{2}-k^{2})^{2}\sin^{2}(qa)\Big)}.

(16)

Proof.

Write

t(E)e^{ika}=\frac{1}{A-iB},\qquad A=\cos(qa),\qquad B=\frac{k^{2}+q^{2}}{2kq}\sin(qa).

(17)

Then

\tau_{P}(E)=\frac{A\,B^{\prime}-B\,A^{\prime}}{A^{2}+B^{2}},

(18)

where primes denote derivatives with respect to
E
. Straightforward algebra yields Eq. (
16
).
∎

In the language of Theorem
1
, the generic square-well sector is characterized by
\sin(\kappa a)\neq 0
, while the exceptional zero-energy-resonant / half-bound sector is characterized by
\sin(\kappa a)=0
.

The exact expression immediately reveals two distinct low-energy regimes: the generic case
\sin(\kappa a)\neq 0
and the threshold-resonant / half-bound tuning
\sin(\kappa a)=0
.

Proposition 2

(Generic threshold law)
.

Assume
\sin(\kappa a)\neq 0
. Then as
E\to 0^{+}
,

\tau_{P}(E)=-\frac{\cot(\kappa a)}{\kappa\sqrt{E}}+O(\sqrt{E}).

(19)

Equivalently,

\tau_{P}(E)\sim\frac{C_{\rm thr}}{\sqrt{E}},\qquad C_{\rm thr}=-\frac{\cot(\kappa a)}{\kappa}.

(20)

Proof.

Expanding Eq. (
16
) at small
k=\sqrt{E}
using

q=\sqrt{\kappa^{2}+k^{2}}=\kappa+\frac{k^{2}}{2\kappa}+O(k^{4})

(21)

gives Eq. (
19
).
∎

The leading singular term in Eq. (
19
) is the threshold obstruction in the raw stationary clock. This motivates the threshold-subtracted clock time

\tau_{\rm sub}(E):=\tau_{P}(E)+\frac{\cot(\kappa a)}{\kappa\sqrt{E}},\qquad\sin(\kappa a)\neq 0.

(22)

Corollary 2

(Finite threshold-subtracted clock time)
.

For
\sin(\kappa a)\neq 0
,

\tau_{\rm sub}(E)=O(\sqrt{E}),\qquad E\to 0^{+},

(23)

and in particular

\tau_{\rm sub}(E)\to 0\qquad(E\to 0^{+}).

(24)

The exceptional threshold-resonant / half-bound tuning is handled separately.

Proposition 3

(Threshold-resonant case)
.

If
\sin(\kappa a)=0
, then the generic subtraction (
22
) is not the correct one. Instead,

\tau_{P}(E)=\frac{a}{8\sqrt{E}}+O(\sqrt{E})\qquad(E\to 0^{+}).

(25)

Proof.

At the threshold-resonant tuning
\sin(\kappa a)=0
, one has
\kappa a=n\pi
for some integer
n
. Write
k=\sqrt{E}
and

q=\sqrt{\kappa^{2}+k^{2}}=\kappa+\frac{k^{2}}{2\kappa}+O(k^{4}).

Then

qa=n\pi+\frac{ak^{2}}{2\kappa}+O(k^{4}),

so

\sin(qa)=(-1)^{n}\frac{ak^{2}}{2\kappa}+O(k^{4}),\qquad\sin(2qa)=\frac{ak^{2}}{\kappa}+O(k^{4}).

Also,

q^{2}-k^{2}=\kappa^{2}

exactly, and

q=\kappa+O(k^{2}).

Substituting into Eq. (
16
), the numerator becomes

ak^{2}q(k^{2}+q^{2})-\frac{1}{2}(q^{2}-k^{2})^{2}\sin(2qa)=\frac{1}{2}a\kappa^{3}k^{2}+O(k^{4}),

while the denominator is

kq\Bigl(4k^{2}q^{2}+(q^{2}-k^{2})^{2}\sin^{2}(qa)\Bigr)=4\kappa^{3}k^{3}+O(k^{5}).

Therefore

\tau_{P}(E)=\frac{a}{8k}+O(k)=\frac{a}{8\sqrt{E}}+O(\sqrt{E}),

which proves Eq. (
25
).
∎

A useful amplitude-level perspective is obtained by expanding the transmission factor itself. For
\sin(\kappa a)\neq 0
,

t(E)e^{ika}=\frac{2ik}{\kappa\sin(\kappa a)}\left[1-2i\frac{\cot(\kappa a)}{\kappa}k+O(k^{2})\right],

(26)

so the divergent phase derivative is directly tied to the low-energy threshold factor.

V
Comparison with dwell time and Wigner phase delay

The interior wavefunction can be written as

\psi_{II}(x)=A\,e^{iqx}+B\,e^{-iqx},

(27)

and the dwell time is defined by
15

\tau_{D}(E):=\frac{1}{j_{\rm in}}\int_{0}^{a}\differential x\,|\psi_{II}(x)|^{2},\qquad j_{\rm in}=2k.

(28)

In the units of Eq. (
2
), the probability current is

j=2\,\mathrm{Im}(\psi^{*}\psi^{\prime})
, so a unit-amplitude incoming plane wave has

j_{\rm in}=2k
. A direct calculation gives

\tau_{D}(E)=\frac{k\left[2a(k^{2}+q^{2})+\dfrac{q^{2}-k^{2}}{q}\sin(2qa)\right]}{2\Big(4k^{2}q^{2}+(q^{2}-k^{2})^{2}\sin^{2}(qa)\Big)}.

(29)

Proposition 4

(Threshold behavior of the dwell time)
.

For
\sin(\kappa a)\neq 0
,

\tau_{D}(E)=\sqrt{E}\,\frac{2a\kappa+\sin(2\kappa a)}{2\kappa^{3}\sin^{2}(\kappa a)}+O(E^{3/2}),\qquad E\to 0^{+}.

(30)

Hence the dwell time is threshold-benign: it vanishes as
\sqrt{E}
. At the threshold-resonant tuning
\sin(\kappa a)=0
,

\tau_{D}(E)=\frac{a}{4\sqrt{E}}+O(\sqrt{E}).

(31)

Proof.

Using Eq. (
29
) with
k=\sqrt{E}
and
q=\sqrt{\kappa^{2}+k^{2}}
, first consider the generic case
\sin(\kappa a)\neq 0
. As
k\to 0
,

q=\kappa+\frac{k^{2}}{2\kappa}+O(k^{4}),\qquad q^{2}-k^{2}=\kappa^{2},\qquad q^{2}+k^{2}=\kappa^{2}+2k^{2},

and

\sin(2qa)=\sin(2\kappa a)+O(k^{2}),\qquad\sin^{2}(qa)=\sin^{2}(\kappa a)+O(k^{2}).

Therefore the numerator becomes

k\left[2a(k^{2}+q^{2})+\frac{q^{2}-k^{2}}{q}\sin(2qa)\right]=k\left[2a\kappa^{2}+\kappa\sin(2\kappa a)\right]+O(k^{3}),

while the denominator is

2\Big(4k^{2}q^{2}+(q^{2}-k^{2})^{2}\sin^{2}(qa)\Big)=2\kappa^{4}\sin^{2}(\kappa a)+O(k^{2}).

Hence

\tau_{D}(E)=k\,\frac{2a\kappa^{2}+\kappa\sin(2\kappa a)}{2\kappa^{4}\sin^{2}(\kappa a)}+O(k^{3})=\sqrt{E}\,\frac{2a\kappa+\sin(2\kappa a)}{2\kappa^{3}\sin^{2}(\kappa a)}+O(E^{3/2}),

which is Eq. (
30
).

Now consider the threshold-resonant / half-bound case
\sin(\kappa a)=0
, so
\kappa a=n\pi
for some integer
n
. Then

q=\kappa+\frac{k^{2}}{2\kappa}+O(k^{4}),\qquad qa=n\pi+\frac{ak^{2}}{2\kappa}+O(k^{4}),

and therefore

\sin(2qa)=\frac{ak^{2}}{\kappa}+O(k^{4}),\qquad\sin^{2}(qa)=\frac{a^{2}k^{4}}{4\kappa^{2}}+O(k^{6}).

Substituting into Eq. (
29
), the numerator becomes

k\left[2a(k^{2}+q^{2})+\frac{q^{2}-k^{2}}{q}\sin(2qa)\right]=2a\kappa^{2}k+O(k^{3}),

while the denominator is

2\Big(4k^{2}q^{2}+(q^{2}-k^{2})^{2}\sin^{2}(qa)\Big)=8\kappa^{2}k^{2}+O(k^{4}).

Hence

\tau_{D}(E)=\frac{a}{4k}+O(k)=\frac{a}{4\sqrt{E}}+O(\sqrt{E}),

which is Eq. (
31
).
∎

Thus the dwell time and the raw stationary clock behave very differently at threshold: the former generically vanishes as
\sqrt{E}
, while the latter diverges as
1/\sqrt{E}
.

For comparison, define the transmission Wigner phase delay by
17
;
15

\tau_{W}(E):=\derivative{E}\arg t(E).

(32)

Since

\arg\!\big[t(E)e^{ika}\big]=\arg t(E)+ka,

(33)

we have

\tau_{W}(E)=\tau_{P}(E)-\frac{a}{2\sqrt{E}}.

(34)

Thus the transmission Wigner phase delay also inherits a threshold divergence, reinforcing that the low-energy singularity is not unique to the raw stationary clock.

VI
Transmission resonances and threshold masking

The transmission probability is

T(E)=|t(E)|^{2}=\frac{1}{1+\dfrac{V_{0}^{2}}{4E(E+V_{0})}\sin^{2}(qa)}.

(35)

Its maxima occur at

qa=n\pi,\qquad E_{n}=\frac{n^{2}\pi^{2}}{a^{2}}-V_{0},\qquad E_{n}>0.

(36)

We refer to
E_{n}
as the transmission-resonance energies of the square well.

VI.1
Local resonant form of the subtracted clock

Write

E=E_{n}+\Delta E,\qquad q_{n}:=\sqrt{E_{n}+V_{0}}=\frac{n\pi}{a},\qquad k_{n}:=\sqrt{E_{n}}.

(37)

Near
E_{n}
,

qa=n\pi+\frac{a}{2q_{n}}\Delta E+O(\Delta E^{2}),

(38)

so

\sin(qa)\approx(-1)^{n}\frac{a}{2q_{n}}\Delta E,\qquad\cos(qa)\approx(-1)^{n}.

(39)

Expanding the exact amplitude near
E_{n}
gives the local arctangent phase profile and hence the following result.

Proposition 5

(Local resonant form of the subtracted clock time)
.

Assume
\sin(\kappa a)\neq 0
, so that the generic subtraction
(
22
) is defined. Let
E_{n}>0
satisfy
Eq. (
36
). Then near
E=E_{n}
,

\tau_{\rm sub}(E)\approx\frac{H_{n}}{1+H_{n}^{2}(E-E_{n})^{2}}+\text{smooth background},

(40)

where

H_{n}=\frac{a(2E_{n}+V_{0})}{4\sqrt{E_{n}}(E_{n}+V_{0})}.

(41)

Equivalently,

\tau_{\rm sub}(E)\approx\frac{\Gamma_{n}/2}{(E-E_{n})^{2}+(\Gamma_{n}/2)^{2}}+\text{smooth background},\qquad\Gamma_{n}=\frac{8\sqrt{E_{n}}(E_{n}+V_{0})}{a(2E_{n}+V_{0})}.

(42)

Proof.

Assume
\sin(\kappa a)\neq 0
, so that
\tau_{\rm sub}
is defined by
Eq. (
22
). Let
E_{n}>0
satisfy
q_{n}a=n\pi
, with

q_{n}=\sqrt{E_{n}+V_{0}}=\frac{n\pi}{a},\qquad k_{n}=\sqrt{E_{n}}.

Write

E=E_{n}+\Delta E.

Since
q(E)=\sqrt{E+V_{0}}
, one has

q=q_{n}+\frac{\Delta E}{2q_{n}}+O(\Delta E^{2}),\qquad qa=n\pi+\frac{a}{2q_{n}}\Delta E+O(\Delta E^{2}).

Therefore

\sin(qa)=(-1)^{n}\frac{a}{2q_{n}}\Delta E+O(\Delta E^{2}),\qquad\cos(qa)=(-1)^{n}+O(\Delta E^{2}).

Using

t(E)e^{ika}=\frac{1}{A-iB},\qquad A=\cos(qa),\qquad B=\frac{k^{2}+q^{2}}{2kq}\sin(qa),

and noting that the prefactor in
B
is smooth near
E_{n}
, we may evaluate it to first order at
E_{n}
:

\frac{k^{2}+q^{2}}{2kq}=\frac{2E_{n}+V_{0}}{2k_{n}q_{n}}+O(\Delta E).

Hence

B=(-1)^{n}\frac{2E_{n}+V_{0}}{2k_{n}q_{n}}\frac{a}{2q_{n}}\Delta E+O(\Delta E^{2})=(-1)^{n}H_{n}\,\Delta E+O(\Delta E^{2}),

where

H_{n}=\frac{a(2E_{n}+V_{0})}{4\sqrt{E_{n}}(E_{n}+V_{0})}.

Since also

A=(-1)^{n}+O(\Delta E^{2}),

it follows that

A-iB=(-1)^{n}\Bigl(1-iH_{n}\Delta E+O(\Delta E^{2})\Bigr).

Therefore, on a fixed smooth phase branch,

\delta_{P}(E)=\arg\!\bigl[t(E)e^{ika}\bigr]=\delta_{n}+\arctan\!\bigl(H_{n}\Delta E\bigr)+O(\Delta E^{2}),

for some constant phase
\delta_{n}
. Differentiating with respect to
E

gives

\tau_{P}(E)=\frac{H_{n}}{1+H_{n}^{2}(E-E_{n})^{2}}+\text{smooth background}.

Since the subtraction term

\frac{\cot(\kappa a)}{\kappa\sqrt{E}}

is smooth near every fixed resonance energy
E_{n}>0
, passing from

\tau_{P}
to
\tau_{\rm sub}
only changes the smooth background term.
Thus

\tau_{\rm sub}(E)\approx\frac{H_{n}}{1+H_{n}^{2}(E-E_{n})^{2}}+\text{smooth background}.

Finally, defining

\Gamma_{n}:=\frac{2}{H_{n}}=\frac{8\sqrt{E_{n}}(E_{n}+V_{0})}{a(2E_{n}+V_{0})},

one rewrites the Lorentzian as

\frac{H_{n}}{1+H_{n}^{2}(E-E_{n})^{2}}=\frac{\Gamma_{n}/2}{(E-E_{n})^{2}+(\Gamma_{n}/2)^{2}},

which proves Eqs. (
40
) and (
42
).
∎

VI.2
Dwell-time resonance comparison

Using Eq. (
29
) and freezing the slowly varying numerator at
E_{n}
while keeping the quadratic resonant dip in the denominator gives

\tau_{D}(E)\approx\frac{\tau_{D,n}^{\max}}{1+\left(\dfrac{E-E_{n}}{\Delta_{n}/2}\right)^{2}}+\text{smooth background},

(43)

with peak height

\tau_{D,n}^{\max}=\frac{a(2E_{n}+V_{0})}{4\sqrt{E_{n}}(E_{n}+V_{0})},

(44)

and width scale

\Delta_{n}=\frac{8\sqrt{E_{n}}(E_{n}+V_{0})}{aV_{0}}.

(45)

In particular,

\tau_{D,n}^{\max}=\tau_{{\rm sub},n}^{\max},\qquad\frac{\Delta_{n}}{\Gamma_{n}}=\frac{2E_{n}+V_{0}}{V_{0}}.

(46)

For a resonance approaching threshold,
E_{n}\ll V_{0}
, the widths coincide asymptotically and both peak heights scale as
E_{n}^{-1/2}
.

This comparison clarifies the role of the subtraction: the threshold-subtracted stationary clock does not coincide globally with the dwell time, but near an isolated square-well resonance it exposes the same leading resonant peak height and the same near-threshold scaling, up to smooth background terms.

VI.3
Quantitative threshold masking

To exhibit the masking mechanism explicitly, write

\kappa a=n\pi-\varepsilon,\qquad 0<\varepsilon\ll 1.

(47)

Then the corresponding resonance lies at

E_{n}\approx\frac{2\kappa\varepsilon}{a}.

(48)

At the resonance energy,

\tau_{{\rm sub},n}^{\max}\sim\tau_{D,n}^{\max}\sim\varepsilon^{-1/2},

(49)

whereas the raw threshold background scales as

|\tau_{\rm thr}(E_{n})|\sim\varepsilon^{-3/2}.

(50)

Hence

\frac{\tau_{{\rm sub},n}^{\max}}{|\tau_{\rm thr}(E_{n})|}\sim\frac{\tau_{D,n}^{\max}}{|\tau_{\rm thr}(E_{n})|}\sim\varepsilon,\qquad\varepsilon\to 0^{+}.

(51)

Equation (
51
) is the quantitative statement that the unsubtracted stationary clock becomes progressively worse at resolving a resonance as that resonance approaches threshold, even though the resonant peak itself is growing.

VII
Control example: local resonant structure in a symmetric barrier–well–barrier cavity

As a complementary control example, we consider a symmetric
barrier–well–barrier cavity
6
;
3
;
11
.
This geometry is not needed for the threshold theorem, but it connects naturally
to the tunneling-time and resonant-tunneling setting and makes the local
Breit–Wigner structure especially transparent once the smooth threshold
background is separated off.

Consider

V(x)=\begin{cases}U,&0<x<b,\\
-\,V_{0},&b<x<b+a,\\
U,&b+a<x<2b+a,\\
0,&x<0\ \text{or}\ x>2b+a,\end{cases}\qquad U>0,\quad V_{0}>0,

(52)

and restrict for definiteness to

0<E<U,\qquad k=\sqrt{E},\qquad q=\sqrt{E+V_{0}},\qquad\kappa_{B}=\sqrt{U-E}.

(53)

VII.1
Exact cavity composition from the transfer matrix

To avoid any ambiguity associated with mismatched asymptotic wavenumbers, we formulate the cavity exactly in terms of the scattering amplitudes of a
single
barrier with distinct left- and right-incidence amplitudes. Let the left barrier occupy
0<x<b
, separating the exterior medium of wavenumber
k
from the cavity medium of wavenumber
q
. Denote the corresponding amplitudes by

r_{L}(E),\qquad t_{L}(E),\qquad r_{R}(E),\qquad t_{R}(E),

(54)

where
r_{L}
and
t_{L}
refer to incidence from the exterior side, while
r_{R}
and
t_{R}
refer to incidence from the cavity side. In terms of incoming/outgoing amplitudes,

b_{L}=r_{L}a_{L}+t_{R}c_{-},\qquad c_{+}=t_{L}a_{L}+r_{R}c_{-}.

(55)

Equivalently, the exact transfer matrix of the left barrier is

\binom{c_{+}}{c_{-}}=M_{L}(E)\binom{a_{L}}{b_{L}},\qquad M_{L}(E)=\frac{1}{t_{R}(E)}\begin{pmatrix}t_{L}t_{R}-r_{L}r_{R}&r_{R}\\
-r_{L}&1\end{pmatrix}.

(56)

The right barrier is the mirror image of the left one. Writing
d_{+}
and
d_{-}
for the right- and left-moving amplitudes at the left edge of the right barrier and
(b_{R},a_{R})
for the outgoing/incoming amplitudes in the exterior region on the far right, its exact transfer matrix is

\binom{b_{R}}{a_{R}}=M_{R}(E)\binom{d_{+}}{d_{-}},\qquad M_{R}(E)=\frac{1}{t_{L}(E)}\begin{pmatrix}t_{L}t_{R}-r_{L}r_{R}&r_{L}\\
-r_{R}&1\end{pmatrix}.

(57)

Propagation across the central well of width
a
is described by

\binom{d_{+}}{d_{-}}=P_{q}(a)\binom{c_{+}}{c_{-}},\qquad P_{q}(a)=\begin{pmatrix}e^{iqa}&0\\
0&e^{-iqa}\end{pmatrix}.

(58)

Thus the full cavity transfer matrix is exactly

\binom{b_{R}}{a_{R}}=M_{R}(E)P_{q}(a)M_{L}(E)\binom{a_{L}}{b_{L}}.

(59)

For left incidence on the full cavity, one sets

a_{L}=1,\qquad a_{R}=0,\qquad b_{R}=t_{\rm bwb}(E),

(60)

with
b_{L}
the total reflection amplitude. Eliminating the internal amplitudes gives the exact composition law

t_{\rm bwb}(E)=\frac{t_{L}(E)t_{R}(E)e^{iqa}}{1-r_{R}(E)^{2}e^{2iqa}}.

(61)

This is the correct cavity formula for the symmetric barrier–well–barrier structure. The crucial point is that the numerator involves the distinct left- and right-incident single-barrier transmission amplitudes, while the denominator involves the cavity-side reflection amplitude
r_{R}
.

It is useful to parameterize

r_{R}(E)=\sqrt{\rho(E)}\,e^{i\phi_{R}(E)},\qquad 0\leq\rho(E)<1,

(62)

and

t_{L}(E)t_{R}(E)=\mathcal{T}(E)e^{i\chi(E)},

(63)

so that

t_{\rm bwb}(E)=\frac{\mathcal{T}(E)e^{i(\chi+qa)}}{1-\rho(E)e^{i\Theta(E)}},\qquad\Theta(E):=2qa+2\phi_{R}(E).

(64)

Define the stationary clock phase by restoring the free phase across the support
L:=2b+a
,

\delta_{P}^{\rm bwb}(E):=\arg\!\big[t_{\rm bwb}(E)e^{ikL}\big].

(65)

Then

\delta_{P}^{\rm bwb}(E)=\delta_{\rm sm}(E)-\arg\!\big[1-\rho(E)e^{i\Theta(E)}\big],

(66)

where

\delta_{\rm sm}(E):=\chi(E)+q(E)a+k(E)L

(67)

is smooth on the scale of an isolated resonance.

VII.2
Local resonant form

Transmission resonances occur at the cavity quantization condition

\Theta(E_{n})=2\pi n,\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z}.

(68)

Let

\rho_{n}:=\rho(E_{n}),\qquad\Theta_{n}^{\prime}:=\evaluated{\dv{\Theta}{E}}_{E_n}.

(69)

Near
E=E_{n}
, one has

1-\rho(E)e^{i\Theta(E)}=(1-\rho_{n})-\rho^{\prime}_{n}(E-E_{n})-i\rho_{n}\Theta^{\prime}_{n}(E-E_{n})+O\!\left((E-E_{n})^{2}\right).

(70)

Under the slow-variation assumption, the real linear term gives only a
subleading asymmetric correction to the local line shape and is neglected at
leading Breit–Wigner order.

Proposition 6

(Local resonant form in the cavity geometry)
.

Let
E_{n}
satisfy Eq. (
68
), and assume the resonance is isolated and the barrier amplitudes vary slowly across the resonant window. Then

\tau_{P}^{\rm bwb}(E)=\tau_{\rm sm}^{\rm bwb}(E)+\operatorname{sgn}(\Theta_{n}^{\prime})\,\frac{\Gamma_{n}/2}{(E-E_{n})^{2}+(\Gamma_{n}/2)^{2}}+O(E-E_{n}),

(71)

where

\tau_{\rm sm}^{\rm bwb}(E):=\derivative{\delta_{\rm sm}}{E}

(72)

is a smooth background term and

\Gamma_{n}:=\frac{2(1-\rho_{n})}{\rho_{n}\,|\Theta_{n}^{\prime}|}>0

(73)

is the local Breit–Wigner width parameter.

Proof.

Equation (
66
) shows that the singular resonant behavior comes entirely from the cavity denominator. Using Eq. (
70
),

-\arg\!\big[1-\rho(E)e^{i\Theta(E)}\big]\approx\arctan\!\left(\frac{\rho_{n}\Theta_{n}^{\prime}}{1-\rho_{n}}(E-E_{n})\right),

(74)

and differentiation gives a Lorentzian contribution with overall sign
\operatorname{sgn}(\Theta_{n}^{\prime})
. Writing the result in terms of the manifestly positive width (
73
) yields Eq. (
71
).
∎

In the opaque-barrier regime
1-\rho_{n}\ll 1
, one has
\Gamma_{n}\ll 1
and the resonance becomes parametrically narrow. Thus the barrier–well–barrier geometry provides a clean control example in which the stationary clock time exhibits an essentially textbook local Breit–Wigner structure.

VII.3
Generic threshold subtraction for the cavity

The same compact-support theorem applies to the full barrier–well–barrier
potential. Thus, in the generic sector, the leading threshold contribution is
not heuristic: it is fixed by the zero-energy Cauchy-data transfer matrix of the
full cavity.

Let

\mu:=\sqrt{U},\qquad\kappa:=\sqrt{V_{0}},\qquad\alpha:=\mu b,\qquad\theta:=\kappa a.

At
E=0
, the Cauchy-data transfer matrices for one barrier and for the
central well are

M_{B}=\begin{pmatrix}\cosh\alpha&\mu^{-1}\sinh\alpha\\
\mu\sinh\alpha&\cosh\alpha\end{pmatrix},\qquad M_{W}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&\kappa^{-1}\sin\theta\\
-\kappa\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}.

The full zero-energy transfer matrix is

\mathsf{T}_{0}=M_{B}M_{W}M_{B}=\begin{pmatrix}A_{0}&B_{0}\\
C_{0}&D_{0}\end{pmatrix}.

Here
\mathsf{T}_{0}
is the Cauchy-data transfer matrix appearing in
Theorem
1
, not the incoming/outgoing
amplitude transfer matrix used above to compose the finite-energy cavity.
For the symmetric cavity,
A_{0}=D_{0}
, with

A_{0}=D_{0}=\cos\theta\,\cosh(2\alpha)+\frac{\mu^{2}-\kappa^{2}}{2\mu\kappa}\sin\theta\,\sinh(2\alpha),

and

C_{0}=\mu\cos\theta\,\sinh(2\alpha)+\sin\theta\left(\frac{\mu^{2}}{\kappa}\sinh^{2}\alpha-\kappa\cosh^{2}\alpha\right).

Therefore, away from a full-cavity zero-energy resonance,
C_{0}\neq 0
, the
generic threshold coefficient is

\ell_{\rm thr}^{\rm bwb}=-\frac{A_{0}+D_{0}}{2C_{0}}=-\frac{A_{0}}{C_{0}}.

The corresponding threshold-subtracted cavity clock in the generic sector is

\tau_{\rm sub}^{\rm bwb}(E):=\tau_{P}^{\rm bwb}(E)+\frac{\ell_{\rm thr}^{\rm bwb}}{\sqrt{E}}.

Near any fixed isolated resonance
E_{n}>0
, this subtraction changes only the
smooth background. The local Breit–Wigner contribution in
Eq. (
71
) is therefore unchanged, while the leading

1/\sqrt{E}
continuum-edge threshold term is removed.

At an exceptional full-cavity zero-energy resonance,
C_{0}=0
, this generic
subtraction must be replaced by the exceptional coefficient from
Theorem
1
, which depends on the next
low-energy transfer-matrix data.

IX
Discussion

The central lesson from this work is that the raw stationary SWP/Peres clock mixes two effects that should be separated: a continuum-edge contribution fixed by low-energy scattering data and the pole-sensitive resonant response. In the attractive square well this separation is analytic, and with our current normalization, the local dwell-time peak and the threshold-subtracted clock peak have the same leading height near an isolated resonance. The dwell time therefore already sees the resonant structure without the generic threshold obstruction, while the raw stationary clock and the transmission Wigner phase delay both inherit that obstruction.

The barrier–well–barrier cavity and the numerical asymmetric two-step attractive well together support the interpretation that the subtraction mechanism is not simply a square-well phenomenon. The cavity shows analytically that, once the smooth threshold contribution is separated off, the remaining stationary clock response has the expected local Breit–Wigner form near an isolated resonance, while the two-step well provides a complementary numerical non-square control example in which the threshold background and its subtraction remain stable.

A natural question is whether the same mechanism extends beyond the present one-dimensional setting. The underlying threshold logic suggests that an analogous effect should arise at least in the
s
-wave sector of short-range three-dimensional scattering. Indeed, for low-energy three-dimensional scattering one has
\delta_{0}(k)\sim-a_{s}k
in the
s
-wave channel, with
a_{s}
the
s
-wave scattering length, so any stationary clock quantity built from the corresponding phase derivative is expected to inherit an analogous
1/\sqrt{E}
threshold background, whereas higher partial waves are less singular at threshold
17
;
8
. In that sense, the threshold subtraction developed here may have a broader interpretation as a way of separating universal continuum-edge kinematics from pole-sensitive delay, although a systematic three-dimensional formulation lies beyond the scope of the present work.

A.1
Transfer matrix representation

Consider

-\psi^{\prime\prime}(x)+V(x)\psi(x)=k^{2}\psi(x),\qquad k>0,

(78)

with
V
real and supported in
[0,L]
.

Let
u(x,k)
and
v(x,k)
be the fundamental solutions on
[0,L]
determined by

u(0,k)=1,\qquad u^{\prime}(0,k)=0,\qquad v(0,k)=0,\qquad v^{\prime}(0,k)=1.

(79)

The transfer matrix across the support is

\mathsf{T}(k):=\begin{pmatrix}u(L,k)&v(L,k)\\
u^{\prime}(L,k)&v^{\prime}(L,k)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}A(k)&B(k)\\
C(k)&D(k)\end{pmatrix}.

(80)

Equivalently,
\mathsf{T}(k)
maps the initial data

(\psi(0),\psi^{\prime}(0))
to
(\psi(L),\psi^{\prime}(L))
. It is therefore a
Cauchy-data, or initial-value, transfer matrix rather than an
incoming/outgoing amplitude transfer matrix.
Because the Wronskian of
u
and
v
is unity,

A(k)D(k)-B(k)C(k)=1.

(81)

For left incidence,

\psi(x)=\begin{cases}e^{ikx}+r(k)e^{-ikx},&x<0,\\
t(k)e^{ikx},&x>L.\end{cases}

(82)

Matching the Cauchy data at
x=0
and
x=L
yields

t(k)e^{ikL}=\frac{2ik}{\Delta(k)},\qquad\Delta(k):=ik\big(A(k)+D(k)\big)+k^{2}B(k)-C(k).

(83)

A.2
Evenness and the zero-energy criterion

Since the equation depends on
k
only through
k^{2}
, the transfer-matrix entries are even functions of
k
. Hence, as
k\to 0
,

\displaystyle A(k)

\displaystyle=A_{0}+A_{2}k^{2}+A_{4}k^{4}+O(k^{6}),

\displaystyle B(k)

\displaystyle=B_{0}+B_{2}k^{2}+B_{4}k^{4}+O(k^{6}),

\displaystyle C(k)

\displaystyle=C_{0}+C_{2}k^{2}+C_{4}k^{4}+O(k^{6}),

\displaystyle D(k)

\displaystyle=D_{0}+D_{2}k^{2}+D_{4}k^{4}+O(k^{6}),

(84)

with all coefficients real because
V
is real.

The generic/exceptional distinction is encoded in the zero-energy problem

-u^{\prime\prime}(x)+V(x)u(x)=0.

(85)

Let
u(x,0)
denote the solution normalized by
u(0,0)=1
,
u^{\prime}(0,0)=0
. For
x<0
this extends as the constant solution
u\equiv 1
, while for
x>L
one has

u(x,0)=A_{0}+C_{0}(x-L).

(86)

Therefore
u(x,0)
is bounded on
\mathbb{R}
if and only if

C_{0}=0.

(87)

Thus
C_{0}=0
is the zero-energy-resonant, or half-bound, case:
a solution that is constant on the left remains bounded rather than growing

•

the
generic
sector is
C_{0}\neq 0
;

•

the
exceptional
sector is
C_{0}=0
.

A.3
Generic sector

Assume first that
C_{0}\neq 0
. In this case the denominator

\Delta(k)
has a nonzero real constant term, so the leading threshold

O(k)
imaginary term to this
constant term. Introduce the real combinations

S_{0}:=A_{0}+D_{0},\qquad S_{2}:=A_{2}+D_{2},\qquad R_{2}:=B_{0}-C_{2}.

(88)

Using (
84
) in (
83
) gives

\Delta(k)=-C_{0}+iS_{0}k+R_{2}k^{2}+iS_{2}k^{3}+O(k^{4}).

(89)

Factor out the constant term:

\Delta(k)=-C_{0}\Bigl[1-ia\,k-b\,k^{2}-ic\,k^{3}+O(k^{4})\Bigr],

(90)

where

(91)

Expanding the inverse,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Delta(k)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{C_{0}}\frac{1}{1-ia\,k-b\,k^{2}-ic\,k^{3}+O(k^{4})}

Hence
