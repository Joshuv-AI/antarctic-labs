Executive Summary
The permitting process for electric vehicle supply equipment (EVSE) varies significantly across
authorities having jurisdiction (AHJs), who regulate localities spanning urban to rural spaces.
This variance, which manifests in multiple components of the process including the steps to
obtain a permit, the code requirements, and the type of permit or reviews required (i.e., electrical,
building, zoning), can lead to uncertainty surrounding EVSE project requirements and time
delays in both the development and review of project plans. Ultimately, these delays add time
and cost to the projects and are a commonly noted barrier to successful EVSE installation
(Gilliand and Graff 2023; Nelder and Rogers 2019; O’Grady and Way 2019).

The Stations Tool for Automated Permitting (S-TAP) is a spreadsheet-based tool designed to
validate nonresidential EVSE projects against code requirements and reduce the time required
for AHJs (i.e., the permitting authority) to issue the permit. The tool consists of a questionnaire
that evaluates project inputs based on widely adopted model codes 1 (National Electric Code and
International Building Code) and select local regulations, through a series of validation logic.
The consistent evaluation of project specifications based on set model codes standards within the
tool, with some elements that are designed for AHJ-specific inputs, provides the effect of
streamlining the codes that define the EVSE project design and installation. The goal of the tool
is to reduce the time required during the permitting process for the AHJ staff to review the plan
and evaluate the EVSE project for code compliance, specifically for high-frequency and low-
complexity projects, thereby allowing AHJ staff to focus time and attention on higher-
complexity projects.

This user manual provides an overview of S-TAP, including the methodology leveraged to
develop the tool, technical guidance on how to use the tool, and the purpose or use case for this
tool with different audiences.

1 Introduction
The permitting process for electric vehicle supply equipment (EVSE) varies significantly across
authorities having jurisdiction (AHJs), who regulate localities spanning urban to rural spaces.
This variance, which manifests in multiple components of the process including the steps to
obtain a permit, the code requirements, and the type of permit or reviews required (i.e., electrical,
building, zoning), can lead to uncertainty surrounding EVSE project requirements and time
delays in both the development and review of project plans. Ultimately, these delays add time
and cost to the projects and are a commonly noted barrier to successful EVSE installation
(Gilliand and Graff 2023; Nelder and Rogers 2019; O’Grady and Way 2019).

In response to these variabilities across AHJs, the National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR)
developed the Stations Tool for Automated Permitting (S-TAP) to provide an option that
streamlines code requirements and permit issuance for nonresidential EVSE. S-TAP is a
spreadsheet-based permitting tool designed to validate EVSE projects against code requirements
and reduce the time required for AHJs (i.e., the permitting authority) to issue the permit. The tool
leverages two primary model codes, 2 the National Fire Protection Association (NFPA) 70
National Electric Code (NEC) (code years 2020 and 2023), the International Building Code
(IBC) (code years 2021 and 2024) developed by the International Code Council (ICC), and any
other relevant model codes referenced within the IBC and NEC as the primary code basis, while
also incorporating common standards from local codes and regulations to inform some project
design elements.

The tool consists of a questionnaire that evaluates project inputs based on these codes, through a
series of validation logic. While some code components related to local regulations can be
adjusted by AHJs to meet their relevant codes, most of the questions are based in the same model
codes, providing the effect of standardizing the codes that define the EVSE project design and
installation. Additionally, the incorporation of validation logic within the tool ensures consistent
application of the codes and reduces the time required for AHJ staff to calculate and confirm
code compliance. S-TAP is designed to evaluate nonresidential EVSE installations that are high-
frequency and low-complexity projects, thereby allowing AHJ staff to focus time and attention
on higher-complexity projects.

Researchers with experience in automated permitting developed this tool with insights from
AHJs, EVSE installers, EVSE equipment manufacturers, and code officials, in addition to
leveraging learnings from relevant research in the permitting space. This manual provides an
overview of the methodology leveraged to develop the tool, technical guidance on how to use the
tool, and the purpose or use case for this tool with different audiences.

2 Methodology
The team leveraged stakeholder interviews with code officials, installers, AHJs, and existing
permitting companies to develop a baseline understanding of the EVSE permitting process and
best practices when developing expedited permitting platforms. Building from this foundational
knowledge and a synthesis of model codes regulating EVSE installation, the team then
developed a series of questions and associated logic designed to evaluate code compliance,
emulating the code checks that often happen during the plan review phase of a permitting
process. The research team held a series of one-on-one and group feedback sessions with
installers, code officials, AHJs, and other relevant stakeholders to review the initial
questionnaire. The team leveraged these feedback sessions to identify which type of projects
require additional review beyond the tool’s capabilities and how to handle local variation such as
zoning and parking regulations, and to ensure that all critical safety compliance is checked and
verified through the tool as it relates to electrical and building code standards.

There are several defining components that create the framework for the tool: the code basis, the
eligible projects or tool use cases, and the extent to which the tool automates the plan review
(i.e., code compliance check) portion of the permitting process.

2.1 S-TAP Code Basis
The tool primarily integrates the two most recent code cycles for IBC (2021 and 2024) and
NFPA 70 (i.e., NEC) (2020 and 2023), as well as all other referenced model codes within these
two codes. These are the more commonly adopted code cycles among AHJs, and as they
continue updating their electrical and building codes, newer editions will be the most relevant
going forward. Because EVSE is relatively new technology, newer code cycles include more
detailed requirements and best practices.

Establishing the basis for the tool in model codes is important because these model codes are the
standard from which locally adopted building and electrical codes are derived. However, EVSE
installation in most AHJs is subject to a series of model codes and local codes (see Figure 2), and
the locally adopted version of the model code may differ (or go above and beyond) what is set by
the national or international standard. This variance in local and model code implementation
highlights a key challenge for installers who may need to adjust designs and plans for each
specific location.

2.2 Tool Use Cases
The tool is designed to validate a subset of nonresidential EVSE installations that include scopes
for high-frequency and low-complexity projects. The focus on high-frequency projects enables
the tool to have greater impact, reducing the volume of projects that require in-depth review from
AHJ staff who may already be constrained on capacity. Low-complexity projects are well
situated for automation because they can be objectively validated for code compliance based on
the standard model codes, requiring little to no additional review from AHJ staff. This
framework reserves the review and approval of more technically complex projects for AHJ staff.

Table 2 outlines the types of nonresidential EVSE installations that are eligible and not eligible
for code validation through the tool. Systems and project types outside the current scope (i.e., not
eligible) are being tracked for future development.

The tool’s questionnaire and logic are designed to focus on the code elements that must be
validated before the permit is issued and construction begins, while reserving some components
that will be evaluated through inspections during and after project construction. In addition to
completing a project submission by utilizing the tool, jurisdictions may require submission of
site plans, single-line diagrams, and equipment specifications. These additional submissions may
be reviewed by the staff prior to permit issuance or may be leveraged during inspections to
ensure that the information validated within the tool matches what is outlined in the plans and
on-site. With this in mind, staff time will still be required for aspects such as pre-application
meetings, inspections, or certain reviews such as structural reviews, which are not currently
incorporated into the tool.

2.3 Tool Components
The spreadsheet-based tool includes the following tabs (sections):

 • Introduction: Provides a high-level overview of the tool and how to use it.
 • AHJ Settings: Designed for AHJs to complete with relevant local code information that
 will feed into the tool’s validation. This tab will remain visible and accessible to the
 installers as a reference while filling out the EVSE Permit Form. Section 3.3 provides
 details on how to fill out this tab.
 • EVSE Permit Form: Permit validation form that the installer should complete, prior to
 submitting the entire spreadsheet to the AHJ.
 • Issues To Address: Automatically summarizes error messages prompted by installer
 inputs in the EVSE Permit form, that indicate what components of the system need to be
 changed to meet code compliance or to be eligible for validation through the tool.
 • Next Steps: Outlines the next steps in the process for the installer regarding how to
 proceed and submit the permit application.
 • Definitions and Resources: Defines key terms used in the tool and lists relevant
 resources related to codes.
 • Notes: Place for the installer to leave notes for the AHJ. For example, the installer may
 want to reference a specific row in the permit form and comment on where in the plan set
 (additional submission) the AHJ might find additional information.
 • Parking Calculator: Calculator that models how the maximum allowable parking
 decrease is calculated. An AHJ should leverage this calculator when determining the
 appropriate percentages by property use. An installer may reference this to better
 understand how the parking validation is calculated.
 • Standard Ampere Ratings: Reference table.
Sections 3 and 4 discuss these components in detail, providing instructions to AHJs, installers,
and other users on how to implement the tool and use it to validate code compliance for project
installations.

3 How To Implement the Tool
This tool functionally operates as a questionnaire designed to validate project plans for code
compliance. The tool has features that an AHJ will tailor or preset. An AHJ wishing to utilize the
tool as part of their permitting process would adjust the settings in the tool to match their
relevant codes and make it available for download on their website. The installer would then
download the tool, fill in the project’s specifications, and then submit to the AHJ the completed
tool and validated project plan with the remaining required permit application documents. The
AHJ would review the completed tool, permit application, and relevant documents to ensure
consistency within the plans and then issue a permit. Because the tool provides most of the
validation necessary to ensure code compliance, the review time for the AHJ would be
significantly reduced. In the scenario where the tool flags an input as “invalid by tool”, the AHJ
may still choose to allow an installer to submit the tool (with certain project components
validated) while planning for some additional reviews (i.e., structural reviews) before issuing the
permit.

There are several benefits to this high-level approach to utilizing the tool. First, the jurisdiction
reduces the amount of time spent ensuring project plans meet standard codes that are objectively
evaluated by calculations. Leveraging the tool for code validation ensures consistency across
projects in how code compliance is evaluated. Second, the installer receives immediate feedback.
While utilizing the tool, if the installer inputs specifications that do not meet code, they will
receive that feedback in real time and can adjust project specifications to meet code before
submitting the permit application. This real-time feedback additionally reduces the number of
times that the plans go back and forth between installer and AHJ with requested changes.
Finally, the tool helps ensure that installers are submitting complete applications that meet
standard code requirements.

3.1 AHJ Preparation Before Tool Implementation
AHJs should first consider their current permitting process for EVSE and determine which steps
the tool can replace or complement elements of that process. Figure 3 illustrates the typical steps
in a permitting process and to what extent the tool addresses or automates each step. For AHJs
who differentiate from the steps outlined below, they may individually consider how the tool
might address their steps.

Next, AHJs should consider what their current process is for the “plan review” step or,
evaluating the project plan for code compliance. For example:

 • If the AHJ has an expedited 7 permit process for certain EVSE: The tool can simply

 o Consider when (or whether) additional reviews may be needed beyond the tool

 • Determine a method for submission (e.g., email, incorporation into existing online

 leveraging the tool’s validation for code compliance checks.

3.2 Preparing the Tool: AHJ Settings Tab
The AHJ Settings tab is the primary place where an AHJ will need to input relevant local code
information to ensure that the tool validates certain specifications based on select local codes.
Filling out the AHJ Settings tab may be a light lift for AHJs who have minimal local regulations
related to EVSE. For AHJs with more extensive EVSE related codes, this tab might take several
hours to complete. This step in the process will require individuals with expertise in land use
regulations (i.e. zoning, parking) such as a planner, and someone familiar with local building and
electrical codes such as a building official or plan reviewer. This section walks through how to
appropriately complete this tab. For the purposes of this tab, blue cells are cells with requested
inputs, and gray cells do not require AHJ input.

The AHJ should first fill out the basic information at the top of the tab, indicating a point of
contact and contact information should the installer have questions about the tool or process.
Next, the AHJ should indicate which IBC and NEC code cycle matches their adopted codes. The
EVSE Permit Form will automatically adjust to incorporate the relevant code cycle based on this
selection.

The General Settings section includes required settings that the AHJ must fill out. The tool
comes with default standards built in; however, the AHJ should review its zoning, land use, or
development code to determine the appropriate standard to input. If the AHJ’s codes do not
provide these specific standards, the AHJ may choose to establish their own standards or utilize
the default standards. To the right of the General Settings table is a space for AHJ staff to link
relevant code references and resources, such a map of historic district areas, if applicable.

The AHJ will also need to fill out the Existing Property Uses and Permitted Parking Space
Decrease section (see Figure 4). The list of property uses, which should be adjusted by the AHJ
to match the categories in their code or land use regulations, will be used to automatically
generate a drop-down in the EVSE Permit Form so that the installer can select the existing
property use for the site. The corresponding percentages indicate the maximum allowable
parking decrease and should be updated by the AHJ. Often in EVSE installations, the total
number of parking spaces in an existing parking lot will reduce due to the removal of spots to
accommodate access aisles and equipment. Additionally, most EVSE spaces are designated for

EVs and/or EV charging only, thus “reducing” the number of standard parking spaces. The EVSE
Permit Form will take the total number of existing parking spaces and subtract any spaces
removed due to equipment or design for the spaces, and then additionally subtract the number of
EVSE parking spaces that will be installed. That remaining number will then be used against the
initial total to calculate the percentage decrease. This is the percentage decrease that the AHJ is
asked to input into this Existing Property Uses and Permitted Parking Space Decrease table.

 Figure 4. Settings for existing property uses and permitted parking space decrease
The use of a percentage decrease is intended to allow the AHJ to set a standard for how much
parking decrease (by percentage) is allowed per property use type before it is flagged and
potentially needs additional review by the AHJ. This allows simple installations that do not
significantly impact the number of total parking spaces at a property to move through without
complications while flagging extreme cases where installations might remove 50% of standard
parking in a parking lot, for example. The Parking Calculator tab (see Figure 5) is designed to
model the calculation that happens within the EVSE Permit Form so that AHJs can test out
different percentages with different-sized parking lots and EVSE installations, to better
understand how the percentage that they set impacts certain designs.

It is important to note that the parking calculation built into this tool focuses on how the
proposed project impacts the parking lot and user experience within the parking lot (i.e. are there
enough standard or non-EVSE parking spaces to meet customer needs). This calculation does not
incorporate all possible parking requirements at the local level.

 Figure 5. Parking calculator
Most AHJs have specific requirements about the minimum number of off-street parking spaces
required for certain commercial uses. Some AHJs and states also set standards on how EV
charging spaces are intended to count toward those minimums. Because S-TAP is designed to be
a tool that broadly accounts for model codes and some variation at the local level, it is not
designed to account for the specifics of off-street parking minimums and how EV spaces count
toward those minimums. AHJs that choose to require additional off-street parking space
requirements, or other related parking requirements, should include links to the parking code
documents and additional references on how EVSE spaces are counted towards parking
maximums or minimums.

The next section is the Optional AHJ Code Inclusion. The AHJ will need to determine if they
want to include these components in the validation check, and then select “include” or “exclude”
for each component. If the AHJ chooses to include the component, there will be additional
follow-up questions.

“What is the property zoned for?”: Select the “Include” if the AHJ wishes to input zoning
code information into the tool. In order to leverage this feature, the AHJ should have a zoning
code that indicates in which zones EVSE is permitted as an accessory use.
The AHJ should then fill out the Zoning Categories table (see Figure 6) to match their actual
zoning code. The Zoning Key column is not utilized in the tool for validation checks but may be
a helpful reference for the AHJ and installer. For each zone, the AHJ must indicate if EVSE is
permitted as an accessory use.

Additionally, the AHJ must indicate the minimum allowable distance that the EVSE (both
charger and equipment) is permitted from the property line. This entry will replace the entry

4 Using the Tool for Project Validation
S-TAP is designed to verify whether a project’s specifications meet code compliance. The EVSE
Permit Form is where the installer or contractor will fill out all relevant information for the
project and receive real-time feedback on whether the project meets code requirements.

4.1 Guide for Installers Filling Out the Form
The AHJ will not need to change any settings on the EVSE Permit Form tab. The installer should
work from top to bottom through the form.

General tips:
 • Blue cells are required for the installer to fill out; all questions, unless grayed out, must
 be answered.
 • Gray cells are the result of a calculation based on previous inputs.
 • Questions that are grayed out in full are inputs that are not required for this type of
 installation.
 • Code references are included to the right of certain input rows, if the installer wishes to
 better understand the model code requirements related to a certain question. If a question
 relates to a local code outlined in the AHJ Settings tab it will say so.
 • Validation checks will appear to the left of the questions. A green box with a checkmark
 indicates that the answer is valid; a red box with an “x” means that the answer is invalid
 based on code compliance; an orange circle indicates that the answer is invalid based on
 tool compatibility, or that the project is not eligible for code validation through the tool.
 If you need further information on why an answer is invalid, review the Issues To
 Address tab, which will summarize error messages for each invalid response.
 • The legend on the EVSE Permit Form (see Figure 8) outlines many of these tips.

 Figure 8. Legend for the EVSE permit form
The form includes the following high-level sections:

 • Contractor Information: Basic information (such as license information and site
 address) for the installer or contractor to fill out. This information will be validated
 separately by the AHJ after submission.
 • Scope and Site Characteristics: General-scope questions that help eliminate projects
 that may not be eligible for validation through this tool and provide general project site
 information for the AHJ.
 • Site Use and Zoning: Questions about existing property use, site zoning, and key site

 questions in this section are optional for an AHJ to include. Most of these questions are

 spaces meet parking space requirements established by the AHJ.

 panelboards, DCFCs, or a combination of all.
 • Accessibility: Inputs to validate that the project design meets accessibility requirements

4.2 Evaluating Project Validation
When an installer finishes filling out the EVSE Permit Form, the installer should check for
project validation before submitting to an AHJ. The validation symbol will indicate if an input is
valid, invalid based on code compliance, or invalid based on project eligibility with the tool (see
Figure 7 for the legend). Each invalid row must be addressed to ensure project validation through
the tool. For inputs that are invalid due to code compliance, the installer should refer to the code
reference on the right-hand side of the form to understand what code specifications need to be
met. For inputs that are invalid due to project eligibility with the tool, the installer should
communicate with the AHJ to determine how to proceed. The AHJ may wish for the installer to
use S-TAP for partial project validation while also requiring additional reviews that the tool is
not able to validate (e.g., structural reviews).

The Issues To Address tab is another resource that will provide a summary of the invalid
responses so that the installer can go through each instance and address them. Each entry in the
Issues to Address tab has a reference line number that indicates its location within the EVSE
Permit Form and clicking on that reference will take the user to the appropriate location within
the EVSE Permit Form.

When the AHJ receives the tool as part of a permit application, the AHJ should check the Issues
To Address tab to ensure that there are no outstanding issues with code compliance that the AHJ
has not approved. The AHJ should make sure that the installer included the attachments listed
out in the Next Steps tab. Finally, the AHJ should review the EVSE Permit Form and validate the
contractor information, site address, and other additional features that are required through their
process.

Ultimately, a project that has met all of the validation checks within the tool has been “validated”
based on the selected model codes (2023 NEC and 2024 IBC, or 2020 NEC and 2021 IBC) and
local code inputs from the AHJ Settings tab. 9 Most AHJs have additional steps in place, after
permit approval, to confirm that projects are built as planned and in a way that meets code. AHJs
should still implement such measures, including inspections, to ensure safe infrastructure
installation.

5 Additional Opportunities for Implementation
While the previous sections describe how an AHJ can utilize this tool to create an EVSE permit
process in which the tool checks project specifications for code compliance as part of the process
to issue a permit, installers, permitting companies, and AHJs may leverage the tool for additional
benefit. Installers may wish to determine if a specific project plan or new design meets basic
code requirements for the relevant model codes. In this scenario, an installer would download the
tool with default AHJ settings (or adjust these settings according to a specific location) and test
out various project plans to better understand whether components are meeting code compliance.

Permitting companies may also leveraging the tool as they consider building out software-
integrated methods to automate EVSE permitting. The Excel-based tool outlines the
recommended inputs and associated logic to validate a project. The tool, which has been vetted
by code officials, experienced installers, and jurisdictions, provides a framework through which
a permitting company may choose to build their own platform.

For AHJs who may not be interested in directly utilizing the tool but would rather implement
their own EVSE expedited permit application, the questionnaire may still serve as guidance.
AHJs might choose to utilize similar questions or validate similar code compliance topics as
what is presented in the tool. For this purpose, the tool is designed to be transparent about what
information is requested and how that information is being used to ensure code compliance.

Ultimately, this tool will help provide both increased consistency in project evaluation within an
AHJ and uniformity in expectations across all AHJs who leverage this tool for project
evaluation. This decreases ambiguity for installers and sets clear expectations about code
requirements while paving the way for future growth in the automated permitting industry.

6 Frequently Asked Questions
 1. What is S-TAP?
 S-TAP is a spreadsheet-based plan review questionnaire designed to evaluate whether
 an EVSE system design is code compliant and safe. The tool incorporates key
 questions related to building, electrical, structural, planning, and zoning aspects of a
 proposed EVSE installation. If the system design and inputs are determined to be
 code compliant, the installer can expect to receive an expedited permit from the
 participating AHJ.
 2. How was S-TAP developed?
 The S-TAP was developed by NLR—with funding from the Joint Office of Energy
 and Transportation––in collaboration with AHJs, EVSE installers, EVSE equipment
 manufacturers, code officials, and national safety organizations.
 3. What types of systems are eligible for S-TAP review?
 S-TAP is designed to review proposed commercial and multifamily EVSE
 installations in existing parking lots or parking garages. Systems may include Level 2
 and DCFC units, which can be wall- or pedestal-mounted.
 The following systems are not currently eligible for review through S-TAP:
 • New construction projects
 • EVSE with energy storage systems
 • EVSE with PV systems
 • Wireless (inductive) charging
 • Bidirectional charging
 • EVSE installations at gas stations
 • EVSE chargers that are considered primary use (i.e., charging hubs).
 Project types that are not currently eligible for review through S-TAP are being
 tracked for a future product roadmap.
 4. What model code years does S-TAP enforce?
 S-TAP validates the 2023 and 2020 versions of NFPA 70 NEC and the 2021 and
 2024 versions of the IBC developed by the ICC, as well as any other model codes
 referenced within them. Additional model codes and local codes/regulations are not
 currently incorporated into the tool but are being tracked for a future product
 roadmap.
 5. How does S-TAP check for code compliance?
 The tool provides a standard questionnaire regarding EVSE specifications and
 certifications, site conditions, installation methods, and other essential details. The
 tool validates answers to each question based on specific code specifications and
 system calculations. The tool provides instant feedback to installers when responses
 are not code compliant or not compatible with the tool.
 The tool also provides a list of required “uploads” that should be submitted to the
 jurisdiction approving the permit.

 18

 For AHJs leveraging the tool within their permit process, it remains the AHJ’s
 responsibility to check that the specifications in the tool match the other forms
 submitted as part of the permit application (i.e., site plan if required).
  6. How does S-TAP benefit EVSE installers?
 For EVSE installers, S-TAP expedites approvals and reduces delays, so projects can
 move from planning to construction more quickly and reliably. Because the process is
 standardized, installers spend less time correcting mistakes or resubmitting
 applications, which helps keep projects on track and on time. In the long run, this
 efficiency allows companies to take on more work, serve more customers, and build
 stronger reputations for delivering timely, hassle-free EV charging installations.
  7. How does S-TAP benefit AHJs?
 For AHJs, S-TAP saves staff time and energy. Routine and standardized projects are
 automatically validated for code compliance prior to the installer submitting a permit
 application, which minimizes the amount of time staff spend verifying the project
 specifications and issuing a permit. This allows staff to focus on the more complex
 projects that truly need their attention. The system also improves safety by applying
 consistent code checks every time, defining the relevant requirements early in the
 process, and reducing the risk of errors or oversights. Perhaps most importantly,
 faster and more predictable processing helps communities expand their EV charging
 infrastructure without overburdening local departments, while also improving public
 satisfaction with permitting services.
  8. How is S-TAP used?
 Participating AHJs will provide the spreadsheet file (or a link to the spreadsheet) on
 their webpage, along with relevant EVSE permitting information. EVSE installers in
 their area can fill out the spreadsheet, check their project for code compliance, and
 provide the approved spreadsheet (along with any other required documentation)
 directly to the AHJ when applying for their permit. The AHJ will then review S-
 TAP’s output, perform any additional reviews and verifications that they see fit, and
 issue the permit for the installation directly to the installer.
  9. How much does S-TAP cost?
 S-TAP is free for both AHJs to offer and EVSE installers to use. It does not influence
 permitting fees established by the AHJ. Installers should note that AHJs have the
 opportunity to customize certain variables within the tool and should ensure that they
 contact the AHJ to obtain the correct tool.
  10. Is S-TAP customizable based on jurisdiction requirements?
 There are several aspects of S-TAP that are customizable by jurisdiction such as use
 allowances within the zoning code, installation distance from the property line, and
 certain parking variables.
  11. Does S-TAP assume liability for permits issued?
 As is the case today with all EVSE projects permitted via the traditional pathway, all
 liability for code compliance remains with the installer.
