Myeloid-derived suppressor cells mitigate inflammation in periodontal disease
Authors: Raza Ali Naqvi, Araceli Valverde, Salvador Nares, Thomas E. Van Dyke, Afsar R. Naqvi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryMyeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) are immature, immunosuppressive myeloid cells whose role in periodontal disease (PD) is not well defined. Here, we show that gingiva from PD patients exhibit increased infiltration of MDSCs, including granulocytic (G-MDSC) and monocytic (M-MDSC) subsets, alongside elevated CD4+ T cells and CD19+ B cells. Functionally, these MDSC subsets suppress autologous CD4+ T cell proliferation in co-culture, confirming their immunoregulatory capacity. In a murine ligature-induced periodontitis (LIP) model, both gingival MDSC subsets increased over time and correlated with CD4+ T cell and CD19+ B cell infiltration. Adoptive transfer of G-MDSCs and M-MDSCs into LIP mice reduced gingival CD4+ T cell infiltration and pro-inflammatory cytokines, while increasing arg1, Il-10, and CD4+CD25+FoxP3+ Tregs. In contrast, anti-Gr-1 depletion exacerbated periodontal inflammation, reduced Treg counts, and increased alveolar bone loss. Collectively, these findings identify anti-inflammatory and osteoprotective roles for MDSCs in PD and support their therapeutic potential for periodontal inflammation.

Introduction

Periodontal diseases (PD) are a group of infectious, inflammatory disorders of the supporting structures of the teeth, affecting about 750 million people worldwide.1 PD is initiated by a dysbiotic oral biofilm and is followed by an aberrant host immune response. Periodontal dysbiosis is now understood as a shift from a symbiotic microbial community to a structurally and functionally altered consortium that promotes chronic inflammation. Rather than being driven by a single pathogen, periodontitis arises through polymicrobial synergy, wherein diverse microbial species cooperate to enhance virulence, evade host defenses, and remodel the local environment. Keystone pathogens such as Porphyromonas gingivalis, even at low abundance, can disproportionately modulate host immune responses and disrupt microbial homeostasis. This leads to ecological imbalance, characterized by an overrepresentation of pathobionts and inflammation-adapted species that thrive in a nutrient-rich, inflammatory niche. The resulting feedforward loop between dysbiotic microbiota and host immune responses sustains tissue destruction, including alveolar bone loss.2,3,4 Thus, PD reflects a breakdown in host-microbe homeostasis driven by coordinated microbial interactions and an imbalanced immune response, rather than the action of individual pathogens alone.5

Moreover, PD is associated with multiple systemic diseases, including neurological disorders, rheumatoid arthritis, obesity, cardiovascular disease, and diabetes.6,7,8,9 Therefore, modulating periodontal inflammation has the potential to improve overall health. Current periodontal treatment relies on mechanical debridement of plaque biofilm/calculus (tartar) and surgical intervention, but these approaches have yielded limited long-term success.3,4 Therefore, the development of innovative strategies that can significantly reduce the periodontal inflammation is of high clinical significance. An overt and persistent pro-inflammatory microenvironment is a primary determinant of PD progression.10 Multiple lines of evidence, including work from our group, indicate that PD progression is accompanied by: (1) increased accumulation of macrophages (Mφ), dendritic cells (DCs), and T and B lymphocytes in diseased gingival biopsies5,11,12; (2) elevated levels of immunoglobulins, cytokines, and inflammation-associated genes in inflamed human gingiva13,14; and (3) non-resolving inflammation that can persist or relapse even after routine periodontal therapy.15 Together, these findings underscore the profoundly dysregulated immune responses that characterize PD.

Myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) are a heterogeneous population of immature myeloid cells16,17 that exert immune suppression through increased arginase-1 (ARG1) and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) activity, as well as by inducing T cell apoptosis. Based on their origin and phenotype, MDSCs are broadly classified into two subsets: monocytic (M-MDSCs) and granulocytic (G-MDSCs), both of which exhibit potent immunosuppressive activity.17,18,19 MDSCs have been shown to have inhibitory effects in mouse tumor models and human cancers.20 In humans, MDSCs are characterized by co-expression of CD11b and CD33 and low levels of HLA-DR (class II MHC). In mice, CD11b and Gr-1 identify MDSCs within an HLA-DRlo-equivalent population.16,21 More recently, early-stage MDSCs (e-MDSCs) have been described as CD33+CD11b+HLA-DRlo cells that lack the myeloid markers CD14 and CD66b.22,23 Multiple reports demonstrate that MDSCs can serve as an effective therapy for autoimmune and inflammatory diseases, including inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), experimental autoimmune arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE),24 and in maintaining immune tolerance during organ transplantation.25 However, the functional role of gingival MDSCs in PD pathobiology remains poorly defined. Thus, modulation of the pro-inflammatory immune microenvironment via MDSCs based cell-based therapies may represent a promising strategy to treat PD or limit disease progression.

In this study, we characterized MDSCs infiltration in subjects with PD and validated their infiltration kinetics in a murine model of ligature-induced periodontitis (LIP). Data from both human and murine experiments confirm homing of MDSCs to the gingiva yet elevated CD4+ T cells and CD19+ B cells are persistent in PD. We asked whether endogenous MDSC responses in periodontitis are sufficient to regulate local inflammation, and whether manipulating MDSC abundance affects disease progression. In conjunction with chronic inflammation. PD is also characterized by progressive alveolar bone loss, highlighting the close interplay between immune responses and bone remodeling. Immune cells, including Th17 cells, contribute to disease progression by producing RANKL, a key osteoclastogenic factor that drives the differentiation and activation of osteoclasts.26 Therefore, this study aims to systematically investigate the immunoregulatory and osteoprotective activities of gingival MDSCs using complementary in vitro, ex vivo, and in vivo approaches in a ligature-induced model of periodontitis and bone loss. Using adoptive transfer and functional depletion experiments in the LIP model, we demonstrate that MDSCs exert robust immunoregulatory functions in vivo and contribute to the preservation of periodontal tissue integrity by exerting osteoprotective effects, potentially through indirect regulation of osteoclastogenic inflammation.

Results

Gingival infiltration of M-MDSCs and G-MDSCs occurs in periodontal disease

In PD, excessive accumulation of pro-inflammatory myeloid and lymphoid cells suggests impaired regulatory immune function.5,11,12,27 However, the role of myeloid immunoregulatory cells in PD is less explored. We asked whether MDSCs, a key innate immunoregulatory population, are present in inflamed gingiva in PD patients. To address this, gingival tissues were collected from healthy and PD subjects (Table 1), and MDSCs were characterized by multiparametric flow cytometry. In human gingival samples, total MDSCs were identified by first gating on HLA-DR- cells, followed by selection of the CD11b+CD33+ population, which defined the total MDSC compartment (HLA-DR-CD11b+CD33+) in this setting. We then further resolved MDSC subsets by evaluating CD14 and CD15 expressions to distinguish monocytic (M-MDSC) and polymorphonuclear/granulocytic (PMN-/G-MDSC) populations. This established gating strategy provided confidence in the accurate identification of both MDSC subsets. Figure 1A illustrates the evaluation of two distinct populations: M-MDSCs (CD11b+CD33+HLA-DRloCD14+) and G-MDSCs (CD11b+CD33+HLA-DRlow CD15+) in the gingiva from healthy and periodontitis patients. CD11b+CD33+HLA-DRlo cells were defined as total MDSCs and were significantly more abundant in inflamed versus healthy gingiva (7.2% ± 1.2% vs. 2.2% ± 0.45%; p < 0.005) (Figure 1B). MDSCs are a heterogeneous population of myeloid cells with a strong capacity to suppress both innate and adaptive immune responses. Several studies have shown that MDSC proportions increase under proinflammatory conditions, such as elevated IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β, functioning as a negative feedback mechanism to limit excessive or chronic inflammation and maintain immune homeostasis.28,29 Consistent with these observations, RT-qPCR analysis revealed a significant upregulation of the MDSC-associated marker CD33 (fold change: 14.1 ± 1.34; p < 0.001) (Figure 1C), along with increased expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6 (fold change: 23.1 ± 3.20; p < 0.001) and TNF-α (fold change: 16.3 ± 2.02; p < 0.001) in gingival biopsies from periodontitis patients compared with healthy controls. MDSCs enhance Treg numbers from a preexisting Treg pool through their secreted mediators, including ARG1, NO, reactive oxygen species (ROS), IL-10, and TGF-β.33,34,35 Similar to MDSCs, we observed a significant increase in CD4+CD25+FoxP3+ Tregs in the gingiva of PD subjects compared with healthy controls (2.8% ± 0.8% vs. 1.0% ± 0.2%; p < 0.005) (Figures 2E and 2F). These results indicate active lymphoid (Treg) and myeloid (MDSC) immunoregulatory pathways in PD patients but are unable to control the disease progression.

Next, we asked whether infiltrating gingival MDSCs exert immunoregulatory functions. Sorted G-MDSCs and M-MDSCs cells were co-cultured with CFSE-labeled autologous CD4+ T cells obtained from the inflamed gingiva of periodontitis subjects at various ratios in the presence of P. gingivalis lysate as antigen. CFSElo cells were considered the proliferating population. As expected, CD4+ T cells cultured alone showed minimal proliferation (1.29% ± 0.27%), whereas stimulation with anti-CD3/CD28 beads induced robust proliferation (80.1% ± 4.52%). In the presence of P. gingivalis lysate, we observed a high proliferative response (71.8% ± 5.10%). However, co-culture with either monocytic or granulocytic MDSCs markedly reduced CD4+ T cell proliferation compared with stimulated control cells. In the presence of M-MDSCs, proliferation decreased to 48.6% ± 4.09% and 24.1% ± 3.57% at MDSC:T cell ratios of 1:10 and 1:5, respectively. Similarly, in the presence of G-MDSCs, proliferation decreased to 45.6% ± 3.9% and 29.4% ± 2.5% at the same ratios. Thus, MDSC-treated CD4+ T cells consistently exhibited reduced proliferation relative to the stimulated control group, with the strongest suppression observed at the higher MDSC-to-T cell ratio (Figures 2G and 2H). Overall, these findings indicate that higher frequencies of gingival MDSCs will exert potent immunosuppressive function, as reflected by marked reduction in CD4+ T cell proliferation ex vivo.

Infiltration of murine MDSCs during disease progression in an LIP model

To evaluate the homing of MDSCs during the onset and progression of PD in a controlled setting, we examined their infiltration kinetics in a murine model of LIP. This model was previously established by our group to dissect mechanisms underlying PD pathogenesis.36 Placement of a silk ligature between the first and second maxillary molars induces microbial accumulation, leading to inflammation and subsequent alveolar bone loss by day 8. We assessed MDSC infiltration in the gingiva at 2-, 4-, and 8-day post-ligature (DPL). In the mouse model, MDSCs were identified by first gating on IA/IE− cells, as previously described, followed by selection of the CD11b+ population.16 These cells were then further subdivided into monocytic MDSCs (M-MDSCs; CD11b+Ly6C+Ly6G−I-A/I-E-) and granulocytic MDSCs (G-MDSCs; CD11b+Ly6G+Ly6CloI-A/I-E-).16,37 Single-cell suspensions from LIP gingiva revealed a time-dependent increase in the infiltration of MDSC subsets. Compared with gingiva from mice without ligature, M-MDSCs increased from 13.32% ± 1.7% at 4 DPL to 20.4% ± 2.1% at 8 DPL (p < 0.0001), while G-MDSCs increased from 16.5% ± 1.1% to 22.3% ± 8.2% from 4 to 8 DPL, respectively (p < 0.0001) (Figures 3B–3D). We also observed a significant increase in gingival CD4+ T cell infiltration in the LIP model, from 6.8% ± 1.15% at 4 DPL (p < 0.001) to 16.7% ± 3.0% at 8 DPL (p < 0.001) (Figures 3E and 3F), indicating establishment and progression of PD in this model.

Next, we evaluated the gingival expression of pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines by RT-qPCR, and observed significantly higher expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines tnf-α (fold change: 6.1 ± 1.1 [4 DPL] and 16.4 ± 1.7 [8 DPL]; p < 0.001) and il-1β (4.5 ± 0.6 [4 DPL] and 14.4 ± 0.9 [8 DPL]; p < 0.001). Concomitantly, compared to pro-inflammatory cytokines, we have found a slight increase of il-10 (fold change: 0.99 ± 0.12 [4 DPL] and 2.07 ± 0.66 [8 DPL]; p < 0.001) and arg1 (fold change: 1.69 ± 0.22 [4 DPL] and 2.54 ± 1.03 [8 DPL]) in LIP compared with control (no ligature) mice (Figure 3G). These results show that in vivo disease progression is accompanied by a time-dependent increase in gingival infiltration of MDSC subjects (M- and G-MDSCs), CD4 T cells, and higher expression of inflammatory cytokines and correlates with similar profiles observed in human PD subjects.

Adoptive transfer of MDSCs attenuates disease progression in the LIP model

MDSCs have been shown to promote tissue repair in multiple immune-mediated diseases.17,18,19,38 MDSCs suppress pro-inflammatory T cell (CD4/CD8) and B cell functions via production of immunoregulatory molecules such as IL-10, ARG1, and iNOS.37,38,39 We hypothesized that an imbalance in MDSCs maintenance during inflammation would augment the clinical manifestations of PD and asked whether reduced MDSC infiltration or inefficient in vivo functional activity contributes to PD progression. To address this, we performed adoptive transfer of MDSCs to examine their role in PD pathogenesis in our LIP model.

Figure 4A shows a schematic of experimental design. For adoptive transfer studies, the MDSCs were obtained from the bone marrow of naive, wild-type healthy donor mice matched for age and sex. After defining MDSC populations in the bone marrow by flow cytometry (Figure 4B), we sorted M-MDSCs and G-MDSCs (Figure S1). The sorted populations showed high purity, with 93.0% purity for M-MDSCs and 94.8% purity for G-MDSCs. Cell viability was additionally assessed using the zombie UV fixable viability kit (BioLegend), a dye that is excluded by viable cells. The corresponding viabilities were 95.5% for M-MDSCs and 93.7% for G-MDSCs (Figure S1). Both subsets of MDSC suppressed CD4+ T cells proliferation in a dose-dependent manner. CFSElo cells were considered the proliferating population (see gating strategy; Figure S2). The percentage of CD4+ T cells proliferation was significantly reduced in the presence of either M-MDSCs or G-MDSCs at higher ratios (40.6% ± 5.1% at 1:10 and 22.2% ± 2.7% at 1:5) compared with splenocytes stimulated with anti-CD3/CD28 (54.3% ± 3.9%; positive control). Unstimulated CD4+ T cells served as the negative control and showed negligible proliferation (0.1% ± 0.04%) (Figures S3A and S3B). Next, we adoptively transferred CFSE-labeled M- and G-MDSCs into separate LIP animals via the retro-orbital route to limit PD progression. Because MDSCs do not substantially proliferate over this time frame, CFSE dilution was not a limiting factor for their detection in gingiva. At 48 h post-delivery, we detected significant percentages of CFSE+ cells in the gingiva of LIP mice infused with either M-MDSCs (0.25% ± 0.07%) or G-MDSCs (0.18% ± 0.03%), strongly suggesting homing of adoptively transferred MDSCs to the inflamed gingiva (Figures 4C and 4D). We then adoptively transferred 1.5 × 106 unlabeled M- or G-MDSCs into mice after ligature placement and monitored their impact on PD progression at 8 DPL. Interestingly, we observed significantly reduced CD4+ T cells infiltration in LIP mice that received M-MDSCs (8.1% ± 1.4%; p < 0.001) or G-MDSCs (4.6% ± 1.2%; p < 0.001) compared with ligature-only controls (18.1% ± 2.2%) (Figures 4E and 4F). The reduced proportion of CD4+ T cells in the gingiva of MDSC-treated LIP mice reflects a diminished degree of periodontal inflammation.40,41,42

To further investigate this, we quantified the expression of pro-inflammatory (tnf-α and il-1β) and anti-inflammatory (il-10, and arg1) cytokines. Compared with ligature alone, transcript levels of tnf-α (16.8 ± 1.7 [ligature alone] vs. 4.5 ± 0.9 [ligature + M-MDSC] and 7.6 ± 0.8 [ligature + G-MDSC]; p < 0.001) and il-1β (14.5 ± 0.8 [ligature alone] vs. 4.9 ± 0.9 [ligature + M-MDSC] and 7.4 ± 0.7 [ligature + G-MDSC]; p < 0.001) were significantly reduced at 8 DPL in mice that underwent adoptive transfer of either M-MDSCs or G-MDSCs. Conversely, expression of the anti-inflammatory mediators il-10 (fold change: 10.3 ± 0.5 for M-MDSCs and 7.9 ± 1.1 for G-MDSCs; p < 0.001) and arg1 (fold change: 9.1 ± 0.95 for M-MDSCs and 5.3 ± 0.7 for G-MDSCs; p < 0.001) was significantly increased in mice that received MDSCs (Figure 4G).

Multiple reports have demonstrated the ability of MDSCs to facilitate the expansion and recruitment of Tregs.43,44,45,46 Therefore, we examined whether adoptive transfer of MDSCs could increase gingival Treg levels. Indeed, we observed significantly higher percentages of CD4+CD25+FoxP3+ Tregs in gingiva from LIP mice receiving adoptively transferred MDSCs (6.4% ± 0.6% [M-MDSCs]; p < 0.001 and 4.6% ± 0.7% [G-MDSCs]; p < 0.001) at 8 DPL compared with LIP alone (2.65% ± 0.60%) and mice without ligature (control; 0.7% ± 0.2%). Together, these findings clearly suggest that in vivo MDSCs delivery can reasonably mitigate gingival inflammation associated with PD by conferring immunoregulatory function, in part via induction of CD4+CD25+FoxP3+ Tregs (Figures 4H and 4I).

Furthermore, compared with ligature alone, gingival tissues of LIP mice treated with anti-Gr1 displayed a progressive, dose-dependent increase in transcript levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines tnfα (fold change: 13.4 ± 1.83 [Lig] vs. 17.2 ± 0.5 [0.25 mg/mL] and 22.6 ± 1.2 [0.5 mg/mL]; p < 0.001) and il-1β (fold change: 8.9 ± 1.4 [Lig] vs. 12.0 ± 1.2 [0.25 mg/mL] and 16.3 ± 1.8 [0.5 mg/mL]; p < 0.001), accompanied by a concomitant decrease in immunoregulatory genes il-10 (4.6 ± 0.7 [Lig] vs. 3.6 ± 0.7 [0.25 mg/mL] and 2.3 ± 0.4 [0.5 mg/mL]; p < 0.001) and arg1 (1.9 ± 0.2 [Lig] vs. 1.3 ± 0.2 [0.25 mg/mL] and 0.6 ± 0.2 [0.5 mg/mL]; p < 0.001) (Figure 5H). Inhibition of MDSCs results in markedly increased alveolar bone loss

Uncontrolled periodontal inflammation can induce osteoclastogenesis and lead to alveolar bone loss.51,52 To evaluate whether MDSC depletion affects osteoclast activity, we performed micro-computed tomography (microCT) to assess alveolar bone loss in our LIP model. As expected, we observed progressive alveolar bone loss from 4 to 8 days post-ligation (DPL), consistent with the development of an inflammatory microenvironment, with the most pronounced changes at 8 DPL.

We also observed a significantly lower trabecular number (3 ± 0.7) in G-MDSC-/PMN-depleted mice compared with ligature-only (15 ± 3.5) or no-ligature controls (23 ± 4.2) (Figure 6D), further supporting the conclusion that bone loss is exacerbated in the absence of MDSC activity. Increased bone resorption via osteoclastogenesis is one of the prominent features of alveolar bone loss in periodontitis.56,57 To corroborate our bone volumetric analysis, we examined the expression of osteoclast markers in animals receiving adoptive transfer of MDSCs versus IgG-treated controls. Quantitative PCR revealed significantly higher expression of cathepsin K (9.4 ± 1.0 vs. 3.7 ± 0.4; p < 0.01), tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (tracp) (23.6 ± 2.3 vs. 13.9 ± 2.1; p < 0.01), and rank receptor (15.5 ± 2.1 vs. 9.2 ± 1.5; p < 0.05) in G-MDSC-/PMN-depleted (anti-Gr1-treated) animals (Figures 6E–6G), indicative of enhanced osteoclast activity.

RANKL is a key osteoclastogenic factor produced by inflammatory Th17 cells. Adoptive transfer of MDSCs markedly reduced rankl expression (18.9 ± 3.0 vs. 5.8 ± 0.6; p < 0.05) compared with mock controls (Figure 6H). This effect correlated with changes in CD4+IL-17+ (Th17) cells abundance in murine gingiva. Compared with no-ligature controls (0.4% ± 0.1%), LIP mice showed increased Th17 cells (4.5% ± 1.3%), whereas G-MDSC-/PMN-depleted animals exhibited a further significant increase (9.9% ± 1.2%; p < 0.0001) in CD4+IL-17+ cells (Figures 6I and 6J). Overall, these observations strongly suggest that depletion of MDSCs, through loss of their immunomodulatory functions, leads to exacerbated inflammation and concomitant loss of alveolar bone volume, a hallmark of PD.

Discussion

The potent immunoregulatory properties of MDSCs make them an attractive therapeutic target for PD, although their roles in disease initiation and resolution remain incompletely understood. Several studies have shown that MDSC-based cell therapy can modulate inflammation and promote immune homeostasis, consistent with their role as early responders to tissue injury and facilitators of wound repair.58,59 In a murine model of obesity-associated periodontitis, Kwack et al. reported increased MDSC frequencies in the spleen and bone marrow of mice fed either a low-fat diet or a high-fat diet, and further showed that M-MDSCs from high-fat diet-fed mice exhibited a greater tendency to form larger osteoclasts in vitro.60 Consistent with this observation, other studies have suggested that under certain pathological conditions associated with bone destruction, MDSCs may serve as osteoclast progenitors.61,62 Calvert et al. likewise described MDSCs as osteoclast precursors in a tumor model.63 However, the osteoclastogenic potential of MDSCs does not appear to be universal. Sawant et al. examined MDSCs isolated from multiple tissues of tumor-bearing mice and found that osteoclast differentiation was restricted to bone-derived MDSCs from mice with breast cancer bone metastasis, whereas MDSCs from the lung, spleen, blood, and lymph nodes lacked such potential.61 These findings suggest that the capacity of MDSCs to acquire osteoclastogenic properties is highly dependent on their tissue origin and local microenvironment. In our study, periodontal MDSCs retained clear immunosuppressive activity and did not exhibit evidence of direct osteoclastogenic function, as reflected by reduced CEJ-ABC distance and increased alveolar bone volume and mineral density under the conditions tested. Therefore, extrapolating findings from tumor models to PD without accounting for differences in tissue context and MDSC subset composition may be overly simplistic. Collectively, our results support the view that MDSC function is highly context-dependent, and that any intrinsic potential to differentiate into osteoclasts is likely shaped by the surrounding microenvironment rather than representing a uniform property of these cells.

Before evaluating MDSCs as a potential therapeutic strategy for periodontitis, we first asked whether these cells accumulate in inflamed gingival tissues in both humans and mice during disease progression. To address this, we compared single-cell suspensions prepared from the gingiva of healthy individuals and patients with periodontitis. Notably, significantly higher proportions of both M-MDSCs (HLA-DR-CD11b+CD33+CD14+) and G-MDSCs (HLA-DR-CD11b+CD33+CD15+) were detected in inflamed gingiva, consistent with increased MDSC infiltration during periodontal inflammation. Previous studies have shown that MDSCs expand in response to proinflammatory mediators such as IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β, where they function as a negative regulatory mechanism to restrain excessive or chronic inflammation and help maintain immune homeostasis.28 In many chronic inflammatory and infectious disease models, this response is characterized predominantly by expansion of granulocytic/PMN-MDSCs. In contrast, both our human and murine periodontal datasets revealed a more balanced increase in PMN-MDSC and M-MDSC populations within inflamed gingival tissues. This pattern suggests that periodontitis does not induce a purely granulocytic-skewed suppressor response, but rather a broader myeloid regulatory signature, likely shaped by the unique features of the periodontal niche, including persistent polymicrobial challenge, barrier disruption, and chronic local inflammatory signaling. Collectively, these findings support the idea that MDSCs may play a protective immunoregulatory role in periodontal immune tolerance by limiting tissue damage driven by excessive gingival inflammation.

MDSCs are well recognized for their ability to suppress effector T cell responses through depletion of L-arginine by arginase-1 (ARG1) and through the production of nitric oxide (NO), IL-10, and TGF-β.34,35 Beyond these canonical ARG1-and iNOS-dependent pathways, MDSCs employ several additional mechanisms to regulate immune responses and promote tissue homeostasis. For example, MDSCs secrete IL-10, a potent anti-inflammatory cytokine that suppresses effector T cell proliferation and cytokine production. They also express indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase 1 (IDO-1), which depletes tryptophan in the local microenvironment, thereby promoting T cell anergy and facilitating the expansion of regulatory T cells. In addition, ROS generated by MDSCs can impair T cell receptor signaling and induce oxidative stress in activated lymphocytes.34,35 Collectively, these pathways highlight the multifaceted and context-dependent nature of MDSC-mediated immunoregulation and support their potential role in restraining excessive inflammation in pathological settings, including periodontitis. Consistent with this concept, our findings suggest that increased frequencies of gingival MDSCs exert potent immunosuppressive activity, as reflected by the marked reduction in CD4+ T cell proliferation ex vivo. Nevertheless, although MDSCs accumulate in human periodontitis tissues under conditions of persistent inflammation, their functional status and precise contribution to disease regulation remain incompletely defined. Therefore, increased MDSC abundance in diseased gingiva should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of effective immune control, since these cells may be protective, functionally inadequate, or, under some conditions, contributory to chronic inflammation. Additional studies comparing the suppressive capacity of patient-derived MDSCs from healthy and inflamed gingival biopsies will be important to determine whether these cells display distinct functional properties across disease states and whether inflammation is associated with enhanced, impaired, or otherwise altered immunosuppressive activity.

To further validate the immune-protective roles of MDSCs in periodontitis, we administered a G-MDSC-/PMN-depleting antibody (anti-Gr1) at different doses in our LIP murine model. Notably, higher doses of anti-Gr1 markedly reduced the numbers of infiltrating MDSCs (both G-MDSCs and M-MDSCs), which was associated with increased CD4+ T cell infiltration and elevated expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines. We acknowledge that anti-Gr-1 treatment may also affect neutrophils,57 since Gr-1 is expressed on these cells. However, in our model, the worsening of disease following anti-Gr-1 administration suggests that depletion of neutrophils alone is unlikely to be the dominant driver of the observed phenotype. Rather, these findings are more consistent with our central premise that loss of the Gr-1+ suppressive myeloid compartment, including MDSCs, exacerbates periodontal pathology. In the LIP model, we observed a marked expansion of the Gr-1+CD11b+ myeloid suppressor population, and complementary functional assays demonstrated that these cells possess canonical immunosuppressive activity, including dose-dependent suppression of CD4+ T cell proliferation. Within this biological context, the finding that anti-Gr-1 treatment significantly aggravated disease severity supports the interpretation that the Gr-1+ suppressive myeloid compartment plays a protective role during disease progression. Nevertheless, because anti-Gr-1 is not specific to MDSCs, we cannot attribute this effect exclusively to endogenous MDSCs. Accordingly, additional studies using more selective approaches will be necessary to define the precise contribution of endogenous MDSCs in this model.

Chronic osteolytic inflammation in periodontitis drives progressive alveolar bone loss. Because M-MDSCs, like monocytes, can serve as osteoclast precursors in some settings, they could theoretically worsen disease by contributing directly to bone resorption.19,39,64,65,66 However, the extent to which infiltrating MDSCs in periodontal tissues actually differentiate into osteoclasts remains unclear. In this context, the report by Zhou et al. that monocytic MDSCs may promote bone destruction in periodontitis appears to contrast with the well-established immunosuppressive functions of these cells.63 Rather than indicating loss of suppressive activity, however, increased MDSC accumulation may reflect a compensatory response to ongoing inflammation aimed at limiting excessive immune activation. This interpretation is supported by the known ability of MDSCs to promote regulatory T cell recruitment and expansion44,45,46 and by the increased proportion of Tregs in gingival tissues from periodontitis subjects observed in our study.

Our findings further support a predominantly immunoregulatory role for MDSCs in PD. Ex vivo, higher MDSC frequencies were associated with marked suppression of CD4+ T cell proliferation. In the murine LIP model, endogenous MDSCs were detectable in inflamed gingival tissues, indicating that their recruitment is part of the natural host response to periodontal inflammation; however, this endogenous accumulation appeared insufficient to fully restrain inflammatory cell infiltration and alveolar bone loss. Consistent with this, anti-Gr-1-mediated depletion aggravated disease, as evidenced by increased CD4+ T cell infiltration and greater alveolar bone resorption. In contrast, adoptive transfer of MDSCs reduced inflammatory cell accumulation, decreased TNF-α and IL-1β, increased ARG1 and IL-10, and was accompanied by increased CD4+CD25+ Tregs. Together, these data indicate that MDSCs exert a protective immunoregulatory function in vivo.

Thus, the apparent differences between studies likely reflect the context-dependent functional heterogeneity of MDSCs rather than a true contradiction. Their net effects may vary according to subset composition, inflammatory stage, and tissue microenvironment, including whether local conditions favor immunosuppressive or osteoclastogenic programs. In our study, the overall effect of MDSCs was anti-inflammatory and tissue-protective, consistent with our human ex vivo data showing that gingival MDSCs from inflamed tissues suppress autologous T cell proliferation. Collectively, these findings support the concept that MDSC-based therapeutic strategies may help attenuate periodontal inflammation and limit disease progression.

Importantly, G-MDSC-/PMN-depletion in this study was associated with exacerbated alveolar bone loss, as evidenced by increased CEJ-ABC distance, reduced bone volume, and fewer trabeculae, all indicative of more severe disease progression in the LIP model. This aggravated bone loss was accompanied by increased expression of osteoclast-associated markers (cathepsin K, TRAP, and RANK), elevated RANKL levels, and expansion of the Th17 cell population, linking excessive inflammation with a more pronounced osteoclastogenic response. Given the well-established pro-osteoclastogenic role of Th17-derived inflammatory signals,26 these findings suggest that loss of MDSC-mediated immunoregulation amplifies local inflammation in a manner that favors bone-destructive pathways. However, it is important to note that the anti-Gr-1 antibody (clone RB6-8C5) selectively depletes G-MDSC and PMN populations while leaving Ly6C+ monocytic MDSCs intact. Because these residual M-MDSCs can act as potent osteoclast precursors, their persistence at the inflamed site represents a confounding factor in interpreting global MDSC contributions to bone loss. Therefore, we have restricted our interpretation of these functional outcomes to the specific loss of the G-MDSC subsets. Overall, our data supports the conclusion that the immunoregulatory and osteoprotective functions of gingival G-MDSCs may be therapeutically leveraged to reduce periodontal inflammation and limit alveolar bone destruction.

MDSCs can exert both protective and potentially detrimental effects in chronic polymicrobial and infectious settings, thus acting as “double-edged sword”.67,68,69 Their immunosuppressive activity may help limit excessive inflammation, reduce collateral tissue damage, and preserve local tissue homeostasis; however, persistent expansion or activation of MDSCs may also suppress antimicrobial immune responses and thereby contribute to chronic infection or unresolved inflammation. This functional duality is also evident in cancer, where MDSCs are well recognized to promote immune evasion by suppressing antitumor T cell responses and facilitating disease progression, highlighting that the biological effects of MDSCs are strongly context-dependent.64 In addition, MDSCs have been shown to attenuate lethal sepsis in murine models and to suppress excessive T cell responses in bacterial and protozoan infections, further underscoring their broader role as regulators of inflammation in diverse infectious settings.70,71 In the context of periodontitis, which develops within a chronic polymicrobial inflammatory environment,67,68,69 elevated MDSC frequencies may therefore reflect an attempt to restrain tissue-destructive immune activation without necessarily achieving effective disease resolution. On the contrary, these cells, similar to myeloid inflammatory cells (monocytes and macrophages) may serve as precursors to osteoclasts to augment alveolar bone loss.59,63,72,73 Thus, the role of MDSCs in periodontal lesions is likely determined by the interplay among microbial burden, inflammatory stage, tissue microenvironment, and the balance between immunoregulatory, antimicrobial, and osteoclastogenic programs.

Limitations of the study

Although our findings support an immunoregulatory and osteoprotective role for MDSCs in periodontitis, several limitations warrant consideration. First, MDSCs were characterized primarily based on surface-marker expression, which may overlap with conventional neutrophil and monocyte populations under inflammatory conditions.19 Second, the anti-Gr1 depletion strategy used in vivo is not MDSC-specific and may concurrently affect other Gr1+ myeloid cells, including neutrophils,57 thereby limiting mechanistic specificity. Third, the adoptive transfer experiments were intended as proof-of-principle studies to examine the consequences of increasing suppressive myeloid populations and may therefore represent supraphysiological conditions relative to endogenous periodontal inflammation. Fourth, the ex vivo co-culture assays do not fully capture the complexity of the periodontal tissue microenvironment and host–microbial interactions present in vivo. Fifth, the divergent findings between our study and prior reports further emphasize that MDSC biology in periodontitis is likely context dependent and influenced by subset composition, inflammatory milieu, and disease chronicity.64,65,66 Finally, gene-expression analyses from whole gingival tissues may partially reflect changes in cellular infiltration patterns rather than purely transcriptional regulation within specific cell populations. Future investigations employing selective depletion systems, lineage-tracing models, and single-cell spatial analyses will be important to define the precise role and functional heterogeneity of MDSCs in PD.

Experimental model and study participant details

Human subjects

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee at the University of Illinois Chicago, College of Dentistry (IRB Protocol #2017-1064). Subjects presented to the Postgraduate Periodontics Clinic were recruited. Systemically healthy individuals exhibiting either stage III, grade B periodontitis or healthy periodontal tissues were enrolled as previously described by our group.74 Periodontitis subjects displayed probing depths ≥6 mm, clinical attachment loss ≥5 mm, bleeding on probing, and radiographic evidence of bone loss. Periodontally healthy controls exhibited probing depths ≤3 mm, no bleeding on probing, no clinical attachment loss, and no radiographic evidence of bone loss.

Inclusion criteria were male and female subjects 18–70 years of age in good systemic health. Exclusion criteria included chronic systemic disease (e.g., diabetes, hepatitis, renal failure, clotting disorders, HIV), current smoking, antibiotic therapy for any medical or dental condition within one month before screening, and use of medications known to affect periodontal status (e.g., phenytoin, calcium channel blockers, cyclosporine).

For periodontally healthy subjects (N = 12/group), a single gingival biopsy (including gingival epithelium, col area, and underlying connective tissue) was collected at the time of crown-lengthening procedures for esthetic or restorative purposes. For subjects with stage III, grade B periodontitis (N = 12/group), a similar gingival biopsy was collected at the time of periodontal flap surgery. Informed consent to publication was obtained from the human subjects. Samples were processed separately for RNA isolation and flow cytometric analysis.

Murine model of ligature-induced periodontitis

The protocol and procedures were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of Illinois Chicago (ACC #22–122). All experiments were performed in accordance with institutional and national guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals.

Ligature induced periodontitis was induced as described previously36,75 using a 6-0 silk ligature placed bilaterally between the maxillary first and second molars, around the buccal and palatal gingiva, in 8–12-week-old female mice (n = 4/group; two males and two females). Anesthesia was achieved via intraperitoneal injection of ketamine and xylazine. Animals were sacrificed at 4- and 8-day post-ligature (DPL) placement. Mice in which the ligature was displaced during the experimental period were excluded. Gingival tissues were harvested circumferentially from the maxillary first and second molars.

Method details

Gingival cell isolation

We have used our previously published protocol to isolate single cell isolation from gingiva.36 Human and murine gingiva were minced with a sterile scalpel and incubated for 1 h at 37°C in RPMI 1640 containing 1 mg/mL collagenase IV (Millipore Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) and 0.1 mg/mL DNase I (Roche Diagnostics, Indianapolis, IN, USA). Cell suspensions were passed through a 70 μm cell strainer, after which 5 mL of DNase I solution (0.1 mg/mL; Millipore Sigma) was added and the samples were centrifuged at 800 rpm for 5 min at room temperature. The supernatant was discarded, and the cells were incubated twice more with DNase I solution, each time centrifuging at 800 rpm for 5 min, and finally resuspended in PBS with 1% BSA.

RNA isolation and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was isolated from human and murine gingiva using the miRNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Approximately 250 ng of total RNA was used to synthesize first-strand cDNA using a Reverse Transcription Kit (Qiagen). For expression analysis of TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-10, ARG1, CTSK, TRAcP, RANK, and RANKL, primers were purchased from Sigma, and transcripts were quantified by real-time PCR using a StepOnePlus thermocycler (Applied Biosystems, Carlsbad, CA, USA). To ensure robust normalization, we evaluated two commonly used housekeeping genes, β-actin and GAPDH, across healthy and periodontitis samples. Importantly, neither gene showed significant variation in expression between conditions in our dataset, indicating their stability in this experimental context. Based on this validation, β-actin was selected as the reference gene for normalization.

Flow cytometry

Single-cell suspensions obtained from human gingiva and gingiva from the murine LIP model were subjected to multiparametric flow cytometry using previously published protocol from our laboratory.73,74,75,76 Briefly, the cells were washed with ice-cold PBS supplemented with 1% (v/v) BSA and 0.08% sodium azide. Typically, Fc portions of antibodies may bind to FcR-bearing cells, that result in nonspecific staining. Therefore, prior to staining, we treated single-cell suspensions derived from human and mouse gingival tissues with Human BD Fc Block Reagent and Mouse BD Fc Block Reagent (BD Pharmingen), respectively, to minimize non-specific antibody binding. Samples were stained with the following fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies (all from BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA): anti-human CD11b–Pacific Blue (clone ICRF44), anti-human CD33–APC (clone WM53), anti-human CD14–APC-700 (clone HCD14), anti-human HLA-DR–AF488 (clone L243), anti-human CD4–FITC (clone OKT4), anti-human CD25–PerCP (clone M-A251), anti-human IFN-γ–APC-Cy7 (clone 4S.B3), anti-mouse I-A/I-E–AF488 (clone M5/114.15.2), anti-mouse CD11b–PerCP (clone M1/70), anti-mouse Ly6C–PE (clone HK1.4), anti-mouse Ly6G–APC (clone 1A8), anti-mouse CD25–APC/Cyanine7 (clone 3C7), anti-mouse Ly-6G/Ly-6C (Gr-1)–Pacific Blue (clone RB6-8C5), and anti-mouse IL-17–PE (clone TC11-18H10.1). Antibody staining was performed at 4°C for 45 min in PBS with 1% BSA.

For intracellular IFN-γ staining, isolated cells were stimulated with leukocyte activation cocktail in the presence of GolgiStop (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) at 37°C for 4 h. After surface staining, cells were permeabilized using permeabilization buffer (eBioscience, San Diego, CA, USA) and stained with anti-mouse IFN-γ (clone XMG1.2). Cells were washed twice with PBS containing 1% BSA, and data were acquired on a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter Life Sciences, Indianapolis, IN, USA). Unstained and isotype control-treated cells were used as negative controls. Data was analyzed using FlowJo v10.9.0 (Ashland, OR, USA).

Human and murine MDSC sorting and co-culture with CD4+ T cells

For human co-culture experiments, HLA-DR-CD11b+CD33+ MDSCs were sorted into two subsets by FACS: M-MDSCs (HLA-DR-CD11b+CD33+CD14+) and G-MDSCs (HLA-DR-CD11b+CD33+CD14−). Similarly, MDSCs from the gingiva in the murine LIP model were sorted as I-A/I-E-CD11b+Gr-1+ cells, with M-MDSCs defined as I-A/I-E-CD11b+Ly6C+ and G-MDSCs as I-A/I-E-CD11b+Ly6G+; together, these comprised the total MDSC population.

For human MDSC co-cultures, 1 × 105 MDSCs were plated in U-bottom 96-well plates, and autologous CD4+ T cells were added at 1:1, 1:5, and 1:10 MDSC: T cell ratios, keeping the number of G-MDSC and M-MDSC cells constant at 1 × 105 per well, in the presence of P. gingivalis lysate (100 μg/mL). CD11b+HLA-DR+ myeloid cells were added in the same proportions as MDSCs for antigen presentation. To assess the immunoregulatory activity of murine gingiva-derived MDSCs, I-A/I-E-CD11b+Gr-1+ cells (1 × 105 per well) were plated in U-bottom 96-well plates, and autologous splenocytes were added at 1:1 and 1:10 ratios. Prior to culture, human CD4+ T cells and murine splenocytes were labeled with CFSE (10 μM; Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Co-cultures were harvested on day 5, stained with anti-human or anti-mouse CD4 for 45 min on ice in PBS with 1% BSA, washed twice in PBS with 1% BSA (w/v), and acquired on a CytoFLEX flow cytometer. Data were analyzed using FlowJo v10.9.0.

Adoptive transfer of MDSCs

Spleens were processed by mechanical trituration followed by red blood cell lysis. Cell suspensions were filtered through a 70 μm strainer and washed twice with PBS. Splenocytes were then stained with fluorescence-conjugated anti-mouse antibodies for surface markers, including CD11b (clone M1/70), Gr-1 (clone RB6-8C5), and Ly6G (clone S19018G) or Ly6C (clone RB6-8C5), and sorted by FACS. Using a microsyringe, 2 × 106 flow-sorted I-A/I-E-CD11b+Gr-1+ cells were resuspended in saline and infused into the retro-orbital space (behind the globe of the eye) of anesthetized mice after ligature placement. This cell number was selected based on prior adoptive transfer studies in murine models of inflammatory disease demonstrating the immunomodulatory efficacy of MDSCs at a comparable dose.77,78 Both M-MDSCs and G-MDSCs were transferred independently rather than as a mixed population in different mice groups. Control animals received saline alone. Animals were humanely euthanized at 8 DPL. In separate experiments, we tested low (0.5 mg/mL) and high (1 mg/mL) doses of the G-MDSC-/PMN-depleting antibody anti–Gr-1, administered locally to block MDSC infiltration and activity in the gingiva.

Microcomputed tomography (microCT)

Maxillae were dissected, fixed in 4% neutral-buffered paraformaldehyde for 24 h, and stored in 70% ethanol at 4°C until analysis. MicroCT scanning (Siemens Medical Solutions USA, Inc., Malvern, PA, USA) was performed with the following parameters: source voltage 60 kV, current 220 μA, and isotropic resolution 8.89 μm. Three-dimensional images were reconstructed and analyzed using Inveon Research Workplace software (Siemens Medical Solutions USA, Inc.).

For linear measurements, alveolar bone loss was quantified as the distance from the cementoenamel junction (CEJ) to the alveolar bone crest (ABC). The buccal groove, palatal groove, mesio-buccal, disto-buccal, mesio-palatal, and disto-palatal sites of the maxillary second molars were used as anatomical reference points. CEJ–ABC distances at these sites were measured independently by three blinded investigators.

We also qualitatively assessed additional alveolar bone parameters, including percent bone volume (bone volume/tissue volume, BV/TV) and trabecular number (Tb.N). For volumetric measurements, circumferential bone loss around the maxillary second molars was evaluated as previously described.63 Briefly, contours were drawn beginning just below the furcation roof and extended apically until the first appearance of the alveolar bone crest. The most distal root of the first molar and the most mesial root of the third molar served as boundaries. Contours were drawn at regular intervals (every five data planes).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism software and are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was determined using either unpaired Student’s t tests or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), as appropriate for the experimental design. Differences were considered statistically significant when p < 0.05.
