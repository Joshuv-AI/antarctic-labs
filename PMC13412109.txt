Introduction

Burn injuries represent a severe form of trauma affecting both children and adults, often resulting in an immunosuppressive state that significantly increases the risk of infection [1, 2]. By compromising the integrity of the skin barrier, burns facilitate microbial invasion, which can lead to local infections and, in severe cases, systemic sepsis and organ failure [3, 4, 5]. Post‐burn infections remain a critical concern in burn centers worldwide, contributing substantially to patient morbidity and mortality, with Acinetobacter baumannii ( A. baumannii ) increasingly recognized as a leading cause of hospital‐acquired infections and therapeutic failures [6, 7]. The genus Acinetobacter comprises over 50 nonmotile, strictly aerobic Gram‐negative species capable of surviving in diverse environmental conditions and persisting on abiotic surfaces [8]. Among these, A. baumannii is the most clinically significant, accounting for approximately 95% of hospital outbreaks, followed by Acinetobacter nosocomialis ( A. nosocomialis ) and Acinetobacter pittii ( A. pittii ). Its ability to withstand harsh hospital conditions facilitates nosocomial transmission, and infection is particularly severe in immunocompromised patients, often leading to mortality rates exceeding 50% [8, 9, 10].

The increasing prevalence of multidrug‐resistant (MDR) A. baumannii , defined as resistance to three or more antimicrobial classes—including β‐lactams, aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, and increasingly carbapenems—has rendered conventional antibiotic therapies largely ineffective, posing a substantial clinical challenge [11, 12, 13]. Current treatment options for MDR strains, such as tigecycline and colistin, face limitations due to pharmacokinetic issues, renal toxicity, and the emergence of further resistance [14, 15, 16, 17].

These challenges have intensified the search for novel and nontoxic therapeutic strategies [18]. Among emerging alternatives, bacteriophage therapy exploits viruses that specifically infect and lyse bacteria [19], offering a targeted approach against MDR pathogens, while probiotics, administered orally or topically, provide antimicrobial, immunomodulatory, and wound‐healing benefits [20]. Probiotics, as live microorganisms, represent a promising therapeutic approach that confers health benefits without the adverse effects commonly associated with antibiotics when administered in adequate amounts. These beneficial microbes exert their antimicrobial activity by disrupting the bacterial cytoplasmic membrane, forming pores in the phospholipid bilayer, and generating a proton motive force, thereby inhibiting bacterial growth and survival [21].

In addition to direct antimicrobial effects, probiotics modulate the host immune system by enhancing cellular activity, stimulating cytokine production, and preventing pathogen aggregation, providing both immunological and non‐immunological protection against infections [22]. Building on these properties, wound dressings incorporating probiotics and prebiotics have emerged as innovative tools in burn care, offering localized antimicrobial action, reducing infection risk, and promoting tissue repair and wound healing. Collectively, these findings underscore the potential of probiotics as both a preventive and therapeutic strategy in managing burn wound infections, particularly those caused by MDR pathogens [20].

The primary aim of this review is to provide a comprehensive and critical assessment of bacteriophage and probiotic therapies as emerging biological strategies for the prevention and management of MDR A. baumannii infections in burn wounds.

Probiotics

Probiotic therapy may aid in healing various skin conditions, including wounds, diabetic ulcers, dermatitis, acne, and pressure sores [20, 23]. Burn wound infections frequently involve drug‐resistant bacteria, underscoring the necessity for new treatment strategies [24]. Probiotics are live microbes that promote health when consumed appropriately, according to the World Health Organization (WHO) [25]. Probiotics protect by regulating the immune system, strengthening the intestinal barrier, competing with pathogens for nutrients, and using bacteriocins. Their role in preventing pathogen colonization is called “colonization resistance” [18]. The most prominent probiotic species explored for their wound‐healing potential include lactic acid bacteria (LAB) like Lactobacillus plantarum ( L. plantarum ), Lactobacillus acidophilus ( L. acidophilus ), Lactobacillus fermentum , Lactobacillus paracasei , Lactobacillus rhamnosus , Lactobacillus bulgaricus , Lactobacillus reuteri (L. reuteri), Lactobacillus delbrueckii , Lactobacillus salivarius , and Bifidobacterium lactis [20, 26, 27, 28, 29].

Certain probiotic bacteria on human skin, such as Lactobacillus spp. (partially reclassified as Lactiplantibacillus and Limosilactobacillus), have been studied for their role in burn wound healing and infection treatment. Lactiplantibacillus plantarum (previously known as Lactobacillus plantarum [ L. plantarum]) can reduce bacterial load and improve healing in infected burns in humans, and is linked to less scarring in rabbit models [24]. Alginate gels infused with Lactobacillus spp. effectively prevent burn wound infections in rats [30]. In mouse models infected with A. baumannii, treatment with L. acidophilus and Limosilactobacillus reuteri (previously known as Lactobacillus [L. reuteri]) has improved survival rates [24, 31].

Probiotics as Modulators of Wound Healing: Cellular and Molecular Insights

Probiotics may promote healing by inhibiting both planktonic Gram‐negative and Gram‐positive bacteria, reducing biofilm formation, disrupting quorum‐sensing pathways, and preventing bacterial adhesion to epithelial tissue [20, 26].

Mechanisms of Immune System Modulation

Enhance innate immunity by boosting macrophage activity to clear bacteria and debris, and by improving natural killer (NK) cell function to destroy infected cells [32]. Enhance acquired immunity by promoting the proliferation and activation of T lymphocytes, especially helper T cells (CD4+), and increasing immunoglobulin A (IgA) levels on mucosal surfaces to block pathogens [32, 33]. Regulate cytokine production to ensure a balanced, effective inflammatory response: raise pro‐inflammatory cytokines like IL‐1β and TNF‐α for initial wound clearance, and increase anti‐inflammatory cytokines such as IL‐10 to prevent tissue damage afterward. These cytokines also promote repair processes, including fibroblast activity, angiogenesis, and collagen synthesis (Figure 1) [32].

The direct antimicrobial actions and immunomodulatory effects of probiotics promote accelerated wound healing.

Oral and Topical Probiotics: Comparative Approaches in Wound Healing

Interest in topical and oral probiotic products has grown significantly over the past decade [20]. The gut–brain–skin axis proposes that probiotics improve skin health by modulating the microbiome [26, 38]. Bacterial compounds enhance skin barrier function through immune‐mediated pathways [39]. Shared developmental origins and disease comorbidity further support this axis [40, 41], with Stokes and Pillsbury hypothesizing Lactobacillus's potential to mitigate stress‐related gut and skin conditions [42]. Putra et al. [43] suggest that probiotic administration may improve inflammatory parameters in burn patients. Their double‐blind, randomized controlled study of 23 critically burned patients found that both monostrain ( L. acidophilus or Bifidobacterium longum , 107 CFU) and multistrain (Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium species, and Streptococcus salivarus subsp. Thermophilus) probiotics, administered orally in capsules daily for 14 days, significantly enhanced immune response by decreasing neutrophil and leucocyte levels.

Furthermore, probiotics limited microbial growth and biofilm development of burn pathogens, potentially improving wound healing [20, 44]. Topical probiotic formulations offer a promising approach to enhance wound healing by promoting tissue repair and inhibiting bacterial growth [20]. Topical probiotics exhibit varying abilities to inhibit wound pathogen growth in an in vivo‐like human plasma biofilm model (hpBIOM) [44].

Topical L. plantarum has shown promise in burn wound management. In mice, it inhibited P. aeruginosa colonization, improved tissue repair, and enhanced phagocytosis [45]. Clinical trials in second‐ and third‐degree burn patients demonstrated that L. plantarum was as effective as silver sulfadiazine in reducing bacterial load, promoting granulation tissue formation, and accelerating wound healing [46]. Given that topical probiotics may offer benefits due to localized action, further investigation into their use for skin disorders is warranted [47].

The mechanisms of topical probiotic formulations are not fully understood. Preliminary studies, including a single clinical trial with 23 burn patients and animal research, suggest topical probiotics may accelerate wound healing through immune modulation, pathogen inhibition [43], and tissue regeneration. However, human evidence remains limited and relies mainly on this small study. Thus, larger, more comprehensive trials are needed to confirm these findings and establish standard treatment protocols. Effects of Probiotics on Biofilm Inhibition and RND Efflux Pump Regulation in A. baumannii

A. baumannii biofilm formation involves virulence factors, including the csuA/BABCDE chaperone–usher system. This system mediates pili biosynthesis and is crucial for twitching motility [55]. Pili expression and twitching motility enable A. baumannii adherence to abiotic surfaces during early biofilm formation [56]. Shin et al.'s study indicates that Clostridium butyricum ( C. butyricum ) cell‐free supernatants (CFS) inhibit A. baumannii biofilm formation by suppressing motility [57]. The mechanism of A. baumannii biofilm development and motility remains unclear and requires further investigation [57].

RND efflux systems are key virulence factors in Gram‐negative bacteria, conferring antibiotic resistance, modulating quorum sensing, neutralizing intracellular metabolites, and regulating cellular homeostasis and intercellular communication [58]. In A. baumannii , the AdeABC, AdeFGH, and AdeIJK systems are identified. AdeABC, the primary antibiotic resistance mediator, comprises AdeA (inner membrane fusion protein), AdeB (transmembrane segment), and AdeC (outer membrane protein channel), facilitating drug efflux across both membranes [59, 60], RND efflux pump genes, particularly AdeABC, are implicated in A. baumannii biofilm formation [61]. AdeABC overexpression enhances biofilm development [61, 62], while downregulation reduces biofilm formation, increases antimicrobial susceptibility, and diminishes virulence [63].

Shin and Eom's [57] study suggests C. butyricum CFS inhibits A. baumannii biofilm formation by suppressing RND efflux pump genes, linking AdeABC expression to biofilm formation in A. baumannii . Al‐Shamiri et al. demonstrated that, of three Lactobacillus species tested, L. rhamnosus significantly inhibited A. baumannii biofilm maturation and adherence. This was achieved through mechanisms including cell envelope membrane disruption, protein lysis, downregulation of biofilm‐related genes, and bacterial DNA degradation, suggesting the potential of probiotics and their derivatives for preventing and treating A. baumannii infections [64].

Research shows that A. baumannii forms biofilms and gains antibiotic resistance via csu pilinization systems and RND efflux pumps like AdeABC [65]. Recent studies reveal that metabolites from probiotics such as C. butyricum and L. rhamnosus effectively inhibit these mechanisms by blocking bacterial motility, suppressing efflux pump gene expression, and disrupting cell wall structure, thereby preventing biofilm formation [57, 64]. Probiotic strains like L. rhamnosus or their metabolites (e.g., C. butyricum supernatant) could complement antibiotic therapy against resistant A. baumannii [57, 64]. This strategy may enhance treatment by breaking down biofilms and increasing bacterial antibiotic susceptibility. Further clinical trials are necessary to confirm efficacy and establish optimal dosing and formulation.

Molecular Mechanisms of Bacteriophage Action Against Acinetobacter baumannii

Bacteriophages, commonly known as phages, are viruses that specifically infect bacteria and act as obligate intracellular parasites. They depend entirely on the host bacterium's biosynthetic machinery to replicate and produce progeny viral particles. Structurally, phages consist of a nucleic acid genome—either DNA or RNA—enclosed within a protective protein capsid. This capsid provides stability and allows the virus to remain viable outside host cells for extended periods [66].

Phages are found ubiquitously in nature, intimately coexisting with bacterial populations across diverse ecological niches such as soil, water, feces, and various biological environments. They exhibit a remarkable range of structural complexity, from simple spherical virions with genomes smaller than 5 kbp to highly complex forms harboring genomes exceeding 280 kbp. Typically, their structure includes two main components: the head, which contains the viral genome; and the tail, which mediates host recognition and genome injection. The tail, together with receptor‐binding proteins, enables phages to specifically attach to bacterial surface receptors—such as peptide sequences and polysaccharide moieties—on both Gram‐positive and Gram‐negative bacteria. This specificity determines the host range of each phage [67, 68].

The discovery of bacteriophages dates back to 1915, when British bacteriologist Frederick Twort first observed an unknown agent causing clearing and morphological changes in Staphylococcus colonies. Two years later, in 1917, French‐Canadian microbiologist Félix d'Hérelle independently documented similar lytic activity against intestinal bacteria and conclusively demonstrated that the agent was a virus capable of lysing bacteria, which he named “bacteriophage.” This groundbreaking work laid the foundation for modern phage biology [66, 69, 70].

Replication Pathways of Bacteriophages: Lytic and Lysogenic Cycles

The lytic and lysogenic cycles are the two primary replication pathways of bacteriophages, each characterized by distinct processes and consequences for the host bacterial cell. In the lytic cycle, phages hijack the bacterial machinery to replicate and subsequently lyse the host cell, releasing newly formed virions. In contrast, during the lysogenic cycle, phage DNA integrates into the bacterial genome as a prophage and replicates passively with the host until induction into the lytic phase. For therapeutic applications, strictly lytic phages are preferred because of their direct bactericidal activity and reduced risk of horizontal gene transfer [71, 72].

Bacteriophage‐Based Interventions Against MDR Acinetobacter baumannii in Burn Infection Management

Today, bacteriophages are not only invaluable tools in molecular biology and genetics but also promising therapeutic agents. They serve as alternatives or adjuncts to conventional antibiotics, particularly in addressing the global challenge of MDR bacterial infections [73]. Although phage therapy was initially explored in clinical settings, the rise of antibiotics in the West largely overshadowed its use. However, in countries such as Georgia, Russia, and Poland, phage research continued uninterrupted [74]. For therapeutic purposes, lytic phages are preferred due to their ability to directly kill bacterial cells [75, 76]. Recently, artificial intelligence (AI) and machine‐learning‐based platforms have emerged as valuable tools for optimizing phage selection and characterization. These computational approaches can predict phage–host interactions, identify effective lytic phages against MDR pathogens, and accelerate the screening process for personalized phage therapy. AI‐assisted analyses may also improve the prediction of host range, phage stability, and therapeutic efficacy, thereby enhancing the precision and efficiency of phage‐based treatment strategies. Over the past two decades, the global threat of MDR bacteria has reignited interest in phage therapy as an alternative or adjunct to antibiotics—particularly against pathogens like Acinetobacter baumannii . This bacterium is notorious for causing hospital‐acquired infections, sepsis, and high mortality rates, especially in patients with severe burn injuries [77, 78].

Recent studies have demonstrated that bacteriophages can effectively target A. baumannii, even when this pathogen forms resilient biofilms on wound surfaces or medical devices [79, 80]. Notably, research by Lood et al. [81] on the phage‐derived lysin PlyF307 and by Rahimzadeh Torabi et al. [80] on the bacteriophage Pɸ‐Bw‐Ab, which targets extensively drug‐resistant (XDR) A. baumannii strains, confirmed the ability of phages to eradicate resistant biofilms and significantly improve survival outcomes in burn wound infection models. These findings underscore the substantial therapeutic potential of phage therapy, particularly via topical application or the use of phage‐embedded wound dressings, as a promising approach for managing severe burn wound infections caused by A. baumannii [80, 81]. Challenges and Limitations of Phage Therapy in Burn Wound Infections

The application of bacteriophages in treating burn wound infections, while demonstrating significant advantages against MDR bacterial strains, faces several notable challenges and limitations that must be addressed to ensure successful and widespread clinical implementation. One primary challenge is the narrow host range of many therapeutic phages [91]; most phages exhibit specificity to particular bacterial strains, which can reduce treatment efficacy in polymicrobial infections or in cases where pathogens rapidly evolve genetically. Additionally, bacteria can develop resistance to phages, especially in the context of biofilm‐associated infections or repeated phage exposure. To mitigate this, the use of phage cocktails or combination therapies with antibiotics is often recommended, although standardization and optimization of effective formulations remain an ongoing research and manufacturing challenge. Furthermore, the lack of globally accepted guidelines for phage selection, production, and quality control complicates the evaluation of safety and efficacy in clinical settings [92, 93].

Another significant limitation concerns the pharmacokinetics, delivery methods, and stability of phages at the burn wound site [84]. Studies have demonstrated that phage titer at the infection site critically influences therapeutic outcomes, and rapid phage clearance or neutralization by immune responses such as antiphage antibodies may reduce effectiveness. Some clinical trials have reported slower bacterial clearance rates with phage therapy compared to standard treatments, potentially due to formulation instability or insufficient active phage reaching the wound [94]. Additional concerns include the possibility of horizontal gene transfer of antibiotic resistance or virulence factors mediated by certain phages, challenges in developing stable pharmaceutical preparations, and the limited number of randomized controlled trials with adequate sample sizes. Collectively, these factors underscore the urgent need for further investigation, standardized production protocols, and clear regulatory frameworks to fully harness and safely integrate phage therapy into burn wound management [91, 95].

Mechanisms of Phage Action in Biofilm Disruption and Interaction With Antibiotics

Biofilms are complex microbial communities encased within an extracellular polymeric substance (EPS) matrix, which protects bacteria from environmental stressors and antimicrobial agents [96]. The emergence of biofilm‐producing A. baumannii has created significant challenges in clinical infections. A. baumannii develops biofilms on various living and nonliving surfaces, aiding its persistence on medical devices and in hospital environments [97]. Over 75% of A. baumannii strains may produce biofilm [98]. Bacteriophages exhibit unique mechanisms for targeting and degrading bacterial biofilms, making them promising agents against persistent infections caused by biofilm‐forming pathogens such as A. baumannii [99].

Soothill [100] was the first to demonstrate that bacteriophage BS46 provides robust protection against A. baumannii infection in a murine burn wound model, as no viable bacteria were recovered from any of the surviving mice. The Mechanisms by Which Bacteriophages Mediate Biofilm Degradation Include the Following

Enzymatic Degradation of Biofilm Matrix

Bacteriophages commonly encode specialized depolymerase enzymes, which are typically incorporated into tail‐spike or tail‐fiber structures, enabling targeted degradation of polysaccharide components within the extracellular polymeric substance. These enzymes catalyze the hydrolysis of key structural polymers such as poly‐β‐1,6‐N‐acetylglucosamine (PNAG), alginate, and various other exopolysaccharides, thereby disrupting the cohesive matrix that maintains biofilm integrity. The enzymatic cleavage of this scaffold results in reduced viscoelastic properties of the biofilm, destabilization of microcolonies, and facilitates the detachment of sessile bacterial cells [103]. Hughes et al. [104] were the first to demonstrate that bacteriophages can actively degrade bacterial biofilms, showing that phage‐borne polysaccharide depolymerases can disrupt the extracellular polymeric substance matrix of biofilms.

Beyond mere structural degradation, depolymerase‐mediated hydrolysis of the EPS also exposes previously shielded bacterial surface receptors, including lipopolysaccharide O‐antigens and outer membrane proteins. This exposure significantly enhances phage adsorption and infection efficiency. As depolymerase activity advances, phages penetrate progressively deeper into the biofilm architecture, accessing bacterial cells that were formerly protected by multiple polymer layers. This deeper infiltration not only promotes intrabiofilm phage replication but also facilitates the creation of new diffusion channels, thereby improving the penetration of additional antimicrobial agents and host immune effectors [105, 106, 107].

Recent research has highlighted the remarkable substrate specificity of phage depolymerases, which permits the rational selection or engineering of phages tailored to the unique polysaccharide composition of specific pathogenic biofilms. Furthermore, genetic engineering approaches have enabled the incorporation of depolymerase genes into phages originally lacking antibiofilm activity, thereby broadening their host range and enhancing their matrix‐degrading capabilities. These technological advances are instrumental in the development of “designer” phages with improved efficacy against recalcitrant biofilm‐associated infections [108, 109].

Targeted Bacterial Lysis

Upon binding to specific bacterial surface receptors, bacteriophages inject their genomes into the cytoplasm and hijack the host's transcriptional and translational machinery to produce progeny virions. In the late stage of infection, phage‐encoded holins accumulate in the inner membrane, forming pores that enable endolysins to reach and enzymatically degrade the peptidoglycan layer. This coordinated holin–endolysin system ensures temporally and spatially precise lysis of target bacterial cells [110, 111].

Within biofilms, such localized lysis events generate micron‐scale voids and channels, compromising the structural integrity of the microbial community. Successive cycles of phage adsorption, replication, and lysis propagate waves of bacterial cell death throughout the biofilm depth, ultimately leading to large‐scale biofilm disruption and collapse [112]. Importantly, confining lytic activity to infected cells minimizes collateral damage to nonpathogenic commensals outside the biofilm [113].

Moreover, the intracellular contents released during phage‐mediated lysis—including DNA, enzymes, and metabolites—can paradoxically serve as nutrients for surviving bacteria and stimulate additional depolymerase production, thereby accelerating matrix degradation [103]. Some bacteriophages encode autolysin‐like proteins that act synergistically with canonical endolysins to enhance peptidoglycan hydrolysis. Collectively, these precise lytic mechanisms both eliminate embedded bacterial cells and mechanically disrupt biofilm aggregates by physically prying apart the matrix [114].

Synergistic Interaction With Antibiotics

Phage‐induced disruption of the EPS and bacterial cell lysis significantly reduce diffusion barriers within biofilms, thereby facilitating more uniform penetration of antibiotics into the biofilm core [115]. For example, phage‐generated channels enhance the access of aminoglycosides and β‐lactam antibiotics to bacterial subpopulations typically sequestered within the matrix, overcoming steep concentration gradients that contribute to biofilm‐associated antibiotic tolerance. Consequently, improved antibiotic distribution diminishes the emergence and persistence of resistant microcolonies [116].

Conversely, exposure to subinhibitory concentrations of certain antibiotics can potentiate phage efficacy by modulating bacterial physiology. β‐Lactams may weaken peptidoglycan cross‐linking, thereby revealing new phage receptor sites on the bacterial surface, while fluoroquinolones can induce stress responses that increase expression of phage‐binding receptors. Moreover, antibiotic‐driven modifications in EPS composition—such as alterations in polysaccharide charge or density—can render the biofilm matrix more vulnerable to enzymatic depolymerization [117, 118]. This bidirectional synergy has been corroborated in various in vitro and in vivo models, wherein combined phage‐antibiotic therapies achieve superior biofilm eradication compared to monotherapies [92, 119]. These combination strategies not only expedite biofilm clearance but also mitigate the development of resistance by applying complementary selective pressures, highlighting their promise in managing chronic and device‐associated biofilm infections [117, 119].

Loehfelm et al. [120] were among the first researchers to identify proteins associated with biofilm formation in Acinetobacter baumannii , thereby laying the groundwork for subsequent studies focused on strategies to combat biofilm‐related infections. Thawal et al. (2012) conducted research that demonstrated the ability of lytic phage AB7‐IBB2 to impede A. baumannii biofilm production and dismantle established biofilm [121]. Similarly, Wintachai et al. found that phage AB1801 infected 70% of the XDR A. baumannii isolates. Also, this phage prevented biofilm formation and diminished preformed biofilms in a dose‐dependent way [122].

Interference of Bacteriophages With Quorum Sensing Pathways in A. baumannii

Quorum sensing (QS) in Gram‐negative bacteria, including A. baumannii , orchestrates the coordinated expression of virulence determinants and biofilm‐associated genes in a cell‐density–dependent manner [134, 135]. Signal molecules (autoinducers), transcriptional regulators (e.g., LuxR homologs), and QS‐controlled target operons together form a regulatory network that promotes surface adhesion, exopolysaccharide synthesis, and secretion of effectors such as OmpA, AdeFGH efflux pumps, PNAG, and type I pili [61, 136]. By enabling biofilm maturation and antibiotic tolerance, QS presents a formidable obstacle to clinical interventions: biofilm‐embedded cells exhibit up to 1000‐fold increased resistance to conventional antimicrobials, and QS‐mediated regulation of efflux pumps further diminishes intracellular drug accumulation [135, 136].

Phages exert a significant influence on QS systems of Acinetobacter, which in turn modulate bacterial pathogenicity through multiple mechanisms. These interactions can alter the pathogenic traits of Acinetobacter species by affecting biofilm formation, antibiotic resistance, and the expression levels of virulence factors [137]. For instance, temperate phages may switch between lytic and lysogenic cycles in response to bacterial population density, thereby impacting host behavior [138]. Additionally, phage‐encoded quorum‐sensing receptors can trigger lytic processes upon detecting bacterial signaling molecules, leading to host cell lysis and the subsequent release of viral progeny [139].

The expression of QS target genes is regulated by the LuxI‐LuxR type regulatory system, which binds to a specific promoter region known as the lux‐box [140, 141]. The LuxI protein synthesizes acyl homoserine lactone (AHL), which interacts with the LuxR protein to modulate the transcriptional response [142, 143]. Reports indicate that the putative lux‐box sequence (CTGTAAATTCTTACAG) in A. baumannii is located 67 base pairs upstream of the target gene [144]. To date, only a single LuxI‐type AHL synthase and its cognate receptor have been identified in A. baumannii . In 2008, the M2 strain was characterized as possessing the first identified AHL synthase, termed AbaI [35]. The AbaI gene, together with the LuxR homolog AbaR, produces N‐(3‐hydroxydodecanoic)‐L‐HSL (3‐hydroxy‐C12‐HSL), which regulates biofilm formation [142].

Homologs of AbaI and AbaR have been found in numerous Acinetobacter species, and consequently, several AHL profiles have been documented among these strains. The diversity of AHL molecules contributes to the modulation of QS‐dependent pathogenicity. For instance, AHL produced by A. baumannii strain AB14 has been shown to affect the cell wall of S. aureus and induce apoptosis in various cancer cell lines, suggesting a possible role of AHLs in mediating interactions between A. baumannii , other bacterial pathogens, and mammalian hosts [145]. New research shows that AbaM controls genes in A. baumannii 5075 that depend on QS and are independent of QS. Elevated levels of AHL enhance AbaM expression; AbaM functions as a negative autoregulator that suppresses AHL production by suppressing the expression of AbaI and AbaR [61].

Biofilm production in A. baumannii seems to be regulated by QS [146]. Various approaches aimed at interrupting quorum‐sensing (QS) signaling pathways are actively being explored to combat bacterial biofilms and associated infections. Among these, small‐molecule inhibitors such as furanones and halogenated analogs have demonstrated efficacy by competitively binding to LuxR homologs, thereby diminishing acyl homoserine lactone (AHL)‐mediated transcriptional activation [147]. Enzymatic quenching represents another promising avenue: AHL lactonases and acylases, commonly derived from soil bacteria, catalyze the degradation of signaling molecules, effectively disrupting bacterial community coordination [148]. More recently, engineered phage–enzyme hybrids that simultaneously deliver depolymerases and quorum‐quenching activities have been developed, exhibiting synergistic biofilm disruption capabilities [149].

The principal advantage of targeting QS lies in its ability to specifically attenuate bacterial virulence without imposing direct selective pressure on bacterial viability, thereby potentially reducing the emergence of resistance. Moreover, QS inhibition may restore susceptibility of resistant strains to conventional antibiotics, providing a valuable adjunct to existing therapies. However, critical limitations must be considered. Off‐target effects on beneficial microbiota raise concerns regarding ecological imbalance, and the pharmacokinetic properties of enzyme‐based treatments challenge their stability and delivery in vivo. Furthermore, the emergence of QS‐deficient “cheater” mutants may undermine long‐term therapeutic efficacy by bypassing quorum‐dependent regulation mechanisms [147, 148, 150]. Integrating QS inhibitors with bacteriophage therapy and antibiotic regimens holds substantial promise for overcoming recalcitrant biofilm‐associated infections. Nonetheless, thorough in vivo validation and careful ecological risk assessments are imperative prior to clinical application to ensure safety and efficacy [151].

The Role of Bacteriophages in Immune Homeostasis and Inflammation Control

Several studies have demonstrated that bacteriophages (phages) not only directly target bacterial pathogens but also significantly influence the host immune response. Hodyra‐Stefaniak et al. [152] showed that phages can modulate immune activity, at times enhancing it to facilitate bacterial clearance. Similarly, Sweere et al. (2019) identified phage proteins capable of interacting with immune cells, highlighting the potential development of phage‐based immunotherapies.

Phages play dual roles in treating Acinetobacter‐associated burn wound infections: bacterial eradication and immune modulation. By targeting and lysing A. baumannii , phages reduce bacterial load and disrupt biofilms, thereby rendering bacteria more susceptible to immune clearance. In large burn wounds, where systemic inflammatory responses such as sepsis can arise, phage therapy may help control infection, prevent excessive immune activation, and mitigate cytokine storms [153, 154]. Indeed, phages exhibit immunomodulatory and anti‐inflammatory properties [155]. Nevertheless, the interaction between bacteriophages and the host immune system remains complex. Because phages are recognized as foreign biological entities, they may induce innate and adaptive immune responses, including the production of neutralizing antibodies that can reduce therapeutic efficacy and accelerate phage clearance from circulation. This immunological recognition represents one of the major challenges in phage therapy, particularly during repeated or systemic administration. To overcome these limitations, several strategies have been explored, including phage encapsulation, nanoparticle‐based delivery systems, and localized administration approaches aimed at protecting phages from immune neutralization and prolonging their in vivo activity.

Phage therapy contributes to maintaining immunological homeostasis by dampening overactive immune responses [156]. Mechanistically, phages bind bacterial lipopolysaccharide (LPS), inhibiting excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production while enhancing anti‐inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin‐10 (IL‐10) [157]. Miernikiewicz et al. demonstrated that phage particles can attach to free LPS, thereby reducing LPS‐induced inflammation [158, 159]. By lowering ROS levels, phages also facilitate efficient phagocytosis of bacteria by host immune cells [160].

Clinically, patients receiving phage therapy may experience improved outcomes due to the reduction of endotoxin‐driven inflammation and mitigation of allogeneic responses, evidenced by decreases in inflammatory markers such as C‐reactive protein (CRP) [157]. Phages further influence adaptive immunity by directly interacting with immune cells. They can enhance antibody production and modulate effector cell polarization, which are critical for tailored immune responses [161]. In monocytes, certain phages induce pro‐inflammatory cytokines, including IL‐1α, IL‐1β, IL‐6, and TNFα, yet they also stimulate anti‐inflammatory mediators such as interleukin‐1 receptor antagonist (IL‐1RA) and suppressor of cytokine signaling 3 (SOCS3). IL‐1RA suppresses pro‐inflammatory cytokine expression and inhibits activation of Th1 cells and macrophages, enabling phages to finely regulate immune responses [162, 163].

Moreover, phages can downregulate Toll‐like receptor 4 (TLR4), whose activation initiates the release of pro‐inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and nitric oxide, thereby modulating leukocyte recruitment and vascular permeability [163, 164]. Phages interact with platelets, which participate in leukocyte activation and inflammation; notably, phages inhibit platelet adhesion to fibrinogen, reducing T lymphocyte binding and potentially diminishing inflammatory responses [165, 166].

Similarities and Differences With Other Therapies

Phage therapy shares immunomodulatory goals with conventional immunotherapies but differs in its targeted, dual‐action approach that combines direct bacterial killing with immune regulation. Unlike broad‐spectrum immunosuppressants, phages can specifically attenuate infection‐induced inflammation while preserving beneficial immune function. However, unlike some small‐molecule drugs, the biological complexity of phages introduces challenges related to dosing, immune recognition, and potential off‐target effects [152, 167, 168].

Targeted Phage Therapy and Mechanistic Insights Into A. baumannii Biofilm Formation and Dissemination in Burn Wounds

Extensive burn injuries predispose patients to infection due to the complete loss of the epidermal barrier, exposure of underlying tissues, and suppression of both innate and adaptive immunity. Bacterial transmission to burn wounds occurs via endogenous and exogenous routes. Endogenous sources include migration of commensal skin flora such as S. aureus and gastrointestinal organisms including Enterobacterales. Exogenous sources involve contact with contaminated dressings, surgical instruments, hydrotherapy equipment, or hospital surfaces, as well as airborne dispersal of certain opportunistic pathogens like P. aeruginosa and A. baumannii . Cross‐transmission between patients and healthcare personnel in burn units is a major contributor to pathogen spread. The unique environment of the burn eschar—warm, moist, and protein‐rich—provides ideal conditions for microbial proliferation immediately after injury [1, 169, 170, 171]. Following transmission, colonization is established through bacterial adhesion to exposed dermal and subdermal structures via specialized adhesins, such as MSCRAMMs in S. aureus and pili in P. aeruginosa . Within 24–48 h, many pathogens initiate biofilm formation, creating a physical and biochemical barrier that impedes immune clearance and reduces antibiotic penetration. Concurrently, virulence factors such as tissue‐degrading enzymes, toxins, and immune‐evasion proteins are expressed, enabling bacterial persistence and deeper tissue invasion. In the colonization phase, the microbial burden increases significantly, raising the risk of progression to infection. The avascular nature of the eschar limits immune cell infiltration, providing a protected niche for bacterial expansion and spread into viable tissues [1, 177, 178, 179].

Once bacteria invade viable tissue layers, they may enter the lymphatic or vascular systems, leading to bacteremia and, in severe cases, septicemia. Gram‐negative organisms such as P. aeruginosa , Klebsiella pneumoniae , and A. baumannii are particularly associated with systemic dissemination due to their endotoxin release, while S. aureus can cause systemic toxicity via superantigens. This transition to systemic infection triggers systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS), which can progress to sepsis and septic shock, conditions associated with high mortality rates in burn patients. Understanding this stepwise progression from contamination to sepsis underscores the necessity of early microbiological surveillance, prompt surgical debridement, and targeted antimicrobial or phage‐based interventions, alongside strict infection control measures, to prevent systemic spread and improve clinical outcomes in burn wound management [1, 170, 180].

Comparative Analysis of Phage Therapy and Antibiotics in Burn Wound Infections

In the management of burn wound infections, antibiotics remain the current standard of care, demonstrating predictable efficacy against susceptible organisms. However, their effectiveness declines markedly when faced with MDR and XDR pathogens such as P. aeruginosa , methicillin‐resistant S. aureus (MRSA), carbapenem‐resistant A. baumannii (CRAB), and carbapenem‐resistant K. pneumoniae (CRKP), which are associated with increased microbiological failure and mortality rates [1, 170]. Clinical trial data in burn patients suggest that inadequate phage titers or suboptimal formulation can diminish therapeutic outcomes; for example, in the PhagoBurn randomized trial, a low‐titer phage cocktail targeting P. aeruginosa reduced bacterial burden more slowly than the standard topical therapy, underscoring the importance of phage–host matching, adequate dosing, and optimized delivery strategies [181].

In contrast, contemporary systematic reviews and translational studies indicate that bacteriophage therapy—particularly when combined with antibiotics and leveraging phage–antibiotic synergy (PAS)—can reduce microbial burden and biofilm density in otherwise refractory infections (see Table 6), although high‐quality randomized controlled trial (RCT) evidence remains limited. From a safety and tolerability perspective, therapeutic phages have consistently demonstrated a favorable profile in modern clinical use, with adverse events typically mild and transient (e.g., low‐grade fever, localized inflammation), and with minimal disruption of the host microbiota due to their narrow host range [182]. Antibiotics, by comparison, especially when used in prolonged or combination regimens for resistant organisms, carry well‐documented risks of drug‐specific toxicities (e.g., nephrotoxicity, myelosuppression), hypersensitivity reactions, and Clostridioides difficile infection, alongside broad‐spectrum disruption of the commensal flora and selection pressure for further antimicrobial resistance [1, 170]. While immune‐mediated neutralization of phages (antiphage antibodies) can occur, it is generally manageable through appropriate regimen design, including route, dosing interval, and treatment duration adjustments [117, 182].

In economic and operational terms, the direct acquisition costs of generic antibiotics are often low; however, for resistant infections, the overall cost of care increases substantially, driven by prolonged hospital and ICU stays, use of second‐ or third‐line agents, therapeutic drug monitoring, repeated surgical debridement, and higher rates of infection recurrence [183, 184]. For phage therapy, costs vary widely depending on the production pathway (off‐the‐shelf product vs. customized magistral preparation), adherence to Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) standards, route of administration (topical vs. intravenous), and the need for isolate‐specific phagogram testing. While formal cost‐effectiveness data remain scarce, regulatory and compounding frameworks such as Belgium's magistral pathway have demonstrated feasibility, and in scenarios where phages can shorten treatment courses for otherwise intractable infections, they hold potential for downstream cost savings at the system level—provided clinical efficacy is established [117, 181, 182, 183, 184].

Manufacturing Process of Bacteriophage Therapeutics for Burn Wound Applications

The process begins with the selection of strictly lytic bacteriophages active against the most clinically relevant burn wound pathogens. Phage amplification is achieved by infecting specific bacterial host strains grown in nutrient‐rich sterile media under controlled conditions, leading to extensive replication and host cell lysis. To ensure broad coverage of pathogenic species, phage cocktails are frequently formulated based on local epidemiological patterns of burn wound infections. Throughout this stage, strict control of phage titer, infectivity, and purity is critical to guarantee therapeutic efficacy [155, 185, 186].

Following production, a rigorous purification phase is undertaken to ensure product safety, particularly for topical application to vulnerable burn tissues. This involves the removal of bacterial debris, host DNA, endotoxins—especially lipopolysaccharides derived from Gram‐negative bacterial hosts—and other contaminants. Commonly applied purification methodologies include centrifugation and filtration to remove cellular debris, ultracentrifugation for phage concentration, and chromatographic procedures to reduce endotoxin levels to meet stringent regulatory thresholds. Effective endotoxin removal remains one of the most technically challenging yet essential steps to avoid toxic or inflammatory responses. Molecular assays are also employed to confirm the absence of temperate or lysogenic phages and residual nucleic acid contaminants. All procedures must comply with GMP standards to ensure batch reproducibility, stability, and regulatory compliance [187, 188, 189].

The formulation phase focuses on maintaining phage viability and activity in the unstable environment of burn wounds, which is characterized by temperature fluctuations, dehydration, and contact with wound exudate [190, 191]. In addition to traditional liquid preparations, advanced delivery systems—such as hydrogels, semisolid gels, ointments, and nanofiber‐based wound dressings—are being developed to enable sustained, localized, and targeted release [190, 192, 193]. Stabilization techniques, including lyophilization and encapsulation, are used to extend shelf life and improve resilience under storage and application conditions [194, 195, 196]. Formulation excipients must be biocompatible, nontoxic, and non‐inactivating to the phages [197]. Controlled release systems can also mitigate immune‐mediated neutralization and mechanical loss, thereby sustaining effective therapeutic concentrations at the infection site [198].

Despite recent advances in technology, significant translational hurdles remain, including complex regulatory pathways due to the biological nature of phages [199], the absence of standardized large‐scale GMP‐compliant production platforms [189], batch‐to‐batch variability [200], limitations in phage host range [201], and stringent endotoxin and stability requirements [202]. Nevertheless, progress in purification methodologies, formulation science, and real‐time quality control is improving the prospects for scalable, safe, and effective phage therapeutics tailored to burn wound infections [200]. Moreover, the ability to customize phage cocktails according to local pathogen profiles represents a strategic advantage in managing multidrug‐resistant infections common in burn care [203].

Conclusion

Multidrug‐resistant A. baumannii continues to pose a formidable challenge in the management of burn wound infections, largely due to its remarkable ability to evade conventional antibiotic therapies. Emerging biological interventions, particularly bacteriophage therapy and probiotics, offer promising alternatives by targeting pathogenic bacteria through distinct and complementary mechanisms. Phages provide highly specific bactericidal activity against resistant strains, while probiotics contribute antimicrobial effects, immune modulation, and enhanced wound healing. Evidence from recent in vitro, in vivo, and clinical studies indicates that the integration of phage and probiotic strategies may yield synergistic benefits, including effective bacterial clearance, reduced infection risk, and accelerated tissue repair. Although further clinical trials are warranted to optimize dosing, delivery, and safety profiles, these approaches represent a paradigm shift toward next‐generation, nonantibiotic therapies for burn patients, with the potential to substantially improve outcomes in the era of escalating antimicrobial resistance.
