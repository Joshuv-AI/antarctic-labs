Logarithmic Large Deviations for Heavy-Tailed Sums
José Miguel Zapata

Abstract
We establish logarithmic large-deviation bounds for sums of independent nonnegative random variables with regularly varying tails. The normalization is chosen at the extreme-value scale and the speed is log ⁡ n \log n . In contrast with Cramér’s theorem, the resulting rate function is determined only by the tail index. The proof transfers a maximum large-deviation principle to sums in the one-big-jump region.



1
Introduction

Let
(X_{k})_{k\geq 1}
be independent and identically distributed real-valued
random variables, and set
S_{n}=X_{1}+\cdots+X_{n}
. Cramér’s theorem

[
2
]
is the classical large-deviation result for sums of
independent random variables with light tails. If the logarithmic moment
generating function
\Lambda(\lambda)=\log\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda X_{1}}]
is finite in a
neighbourhood of the origin, then the empirical mean
S_{n}/n
satisfies a
large-deviation principle with speed
n
and rate function

\Lambda^{*}(x)=\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\{\lambda x-\Lambda(\lambda)\}.

In particular, for
x>\mathbb{E}[X_{1}]
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{P}(S_{n}\geq nx)=-\Lambda^{*}(x),

(1.1)

so upper-tail probabilities are exponentially small in
n
. We refer to

[
3
]
for background on Cramér’s theorem and
large-deviation theory.

This framework is no longer appropriate for heavy-tailed random variables. If
the upper tail of
X_{1}
is regularly varying, then positive exponential moments
are infinite and the Cramér transform does not describe rare upper-tail
events. In this case, large deviations occur on a polynomial rather than an
exponential scale. The relevant mechanism is the one-big-jump principle: a
large value of the sum is typically caused by one exceptionally large summand,
rather than by a collective displacement of all summands.

The purpose of this note is to formulate this heavy-tailed polynomial regime as
a Cramér-type large-deviation principle. Let
a_{n}
be the upper
1-1/n

quantile of
X_{1}
, and consider the normalized sums

Y_{n}=\left(\frac{S_{n}}{a_{n}}\right)^{1/\log n}.

This normalization converts polynomial excesses above the extreme-value scale
into fixed levels: indeed,
\{S_{n}\geq a_{n}n^{x}\}
is equivalent to

\{Y_{n}\geq e^{x}\}
.

We prove large-deviation bounds for
(Y_{n})
in the far right tail, where the
one-big-jump mechanism governs the deviation. The speed is
\log n
, and the
rate function is
I(y)=\alpha\log y
, where
\alpha
is the tail index. The
statement is set-valued: it gives lower bounds for arbitrary open sets and
upper bounds for sets that are closed in
\mathbb{R}
, both restricted to the
right-tail region
E_{\alpha}
introduced below. Thus the result does not only
identify the decay of a single threshold probability, but describes the
logarithmic decay of probabilities
\mathbb{P}(Y_{n}\in A)
for general
right-tail deviation sets
A
.

For the particular upper-tail event
S_{n}\geq a_{n}n^{x}
, the result gives

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\log n}\log\mathbb{P}(S_{n}\geq a_{n}n^{x})=-\alpha x,\qquad x>\left(1-\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)^{+}.

Thus the exponential speed
n
in Cramér’s theorem (
1.1
) is replaced by the
logarithmic speed
\log n
, the exponential scale is replaced by the natural
polynomial scale of regularly varying tails, and the rate is determined only by
the tail index.

The proof uses the large-deviation estimate for the normalized maximum
established in
[
9
, Theorem 1]
.

Relation with the literature.

The one-big-jump principle is classical in the theory of heavy-tailed and
subexponential distributions. In its basic form, for a fixed number of summands

m
, it states that

\mathbb{P}(S_{m}>t)\sim m\bar{F}(t),\qquad t\to\infty.

(1.2)

Thus the asymptotic parameter is the threshold
t
, while the number of
summands is fixed. More refined Nagaev-type and subexponential results allow
the number of summands to vary and give uniform estimates on suitable
big-jump regions; see, for instance,

[
6
,
4
,
5
]
.
These results are mainly concerned with sharp or uniform tail equivalents of
the form (
1.2
).

The present paper uses this one-big-jump regime for a different purpose:
rather than deriving a sharp equivalent for a prescribed threshold, we organize
the polynomial deviation probabilities into large-deviation bounds for a
normalized sequence. The threshold is chosen as a function of the sample size,
namely
a_{n}n^{x}
, and the asymptotic parameter is
n\to\infty
. In this sense,
the result provides a logarithmic large-deviation substitute for Cramér’s
theorem in a setting where the classical exponential theory cannot be applied.

2
Setup and main result

Let
X,X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
be independent and identically distributed nonnegative
random variables with distribution function
F
and survival function
\bar{F}=1-F.

Throughout the paper we assume that
\bar{F}
is regularly varying with index

-\alpha
, where
\alpha>0
; that is,

\bar{F}(x)=x^{-\alpha}L(x),\qquad x>0,

where
L
is slowly varying:

\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{L(xy)}{L(x)}=1,\qquad y>0.

For background on regular variation and the facts used below, we refer to

[
7
]
.

Define the upper
1-1/n

quantile

a_{n}=F^{\leftarrow}\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right),\qquad F^{\leftarrow}(u)=\inf\{x\in\mathbb{R}:F(x)\geq u\}.

Write

S_{n}=X_{1}+\cdots+X_{n},\qquad Y_{n}=\left(\frac{S_{n}}{a_{n}}\right)^{1/\log n}.

Set

c_{\alpha}=\left(1-\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)^{+},\qquad d_{\alpha}=e^{c_{\alpha}},\qquad E_{\alpha}=(d_{\alpha},\infty).

We present the main result of the note.

Theorem 2.1
.

In the setup above, the following large-deviation bounds hold on
E_{\alpha}
,
with speed
\log n
and rate function
I(y)=\alpha\log y
.

(i)

For every open set
G\subset E_{\alpha}
,

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\log n}\log\mathbb{P}(Y_{n}\in G)\geq-\inf_{y\in G}\alpha\log y.

(ii)

For every set
F\subset E_{\alpha}
that is closed in
\mathbb{R}
,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\log n}\log\mathbb{P}(Y_{n}\in F)\leq-\inf_{y\in F}\alpha\log y.

Moreover, for every
x>c_{\alpha}
one has

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\log n}\log\mathbb{P}(S_{n}\geq a_{n}n^{x})=-\alpha x.

3
Proofs

Suppose that
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
are as in Section
2
. Throughout
this section we use the notation

S_{n}=X_{1}+\cdots+X_{n},\qquad X_{(n)}=\max\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\}.

The corresponding normalized sum and maximum are

Y_{n}=\left(\frac{S_{n}}{a_{n}}\right)^{1/\log n},\qquad Z_{n}=\left(\frac{X_{(n)}}{a_{n}}\right)^{1/\log n}.

For
x>0
, set

t_{n}(x)=a_{n}x^{\log n}.

We also write

c_{\alpha}=\left(1-\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)^{+},\qquad d_{\alpha}=e^{c_{\alpha}},\qquad E_{\alpha}=(d_{\alpha},\infty).

The proof uses the following sharp large-deviation estimate for the normalized
maximum, established in
[
9
, Theorem 1]
. The version proved there assumes a weak von Mises condition and gives a
stronger Borel-set statement, namely an exact logarithmic limit. In the present
paper, we only need the upper-tail estimate below. For completeness, we include
a short proof under the present assumptions in the Appendix.

Lemma 3.1
.

In the present setup, for every
x>1
one has

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\log n}\log\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}>x)=-\alpha\log x.

Next, we prove some auxiliary lemmas.

Lemma 3.2
.

Fix
x\in E_{\alpha}
and
h>0
. Set

b_{n}(x,h)=\frac{t_{n}(x)}{2h\log n}.

Then

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}X_{k}\mathbf{1}_{\{X_{k}\leq b_{n}(x,h)\}}\geq t_{n}(x)\right)\leq n^{-h}

for all sufficiently large
n
.

Proof.

Write
t_{n}=t_{n}(x)
and
b_{n}=b_{n}(x,h)
, and put

q=\log x+1/\alpha
. Since
x>e^{c_{\alpha}}
, we have

q>1/(\alpha\wedge 1)
. Choose
p
such that

q^{-1}<p<\alpha\wedge 1
. Then
p<1
,
p<\alpha
, and
pq>1
.

Let
T_{k}=X_{k}\mathbf{1}_{\{X_{k}\leq b_{n}\}}
. Since
0\leq T_{k}\leq b_{n}
and
p<1
, for

r=1,2
,

T_{k}^{r}\leq b_{n}^{r-p}X_{k}^{p}.

Thus, taking expectations,

\mathbb{E}[T_{k}^{r}]\leq b_{n}^{r-p}\mathbb{E}[X_{k}^{p}].

By Lemma
A.1
,
\mathbb{E}[X_{1}^{p}]<\infty
, and by
Lemma
A.2
(ii),
t_{n}=n^{q+o(1)}
. Hence, for
r=1,2
,

\displaystyle\frac{n\mathbb{E}[T_{1}^{r}]}{b_{n}^{r-1}t_{n}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[X_{1}^{p}]\frac{nb_{n}^{1-p}}{t_{n}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[X_{1}^{p}]\frac{nt_{n}^{-p}}{(2h\log n)^{1-p}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[X_{1}^{p}]\frac{n^{1-pq+o(1)}}{(2h\log n)^{1-p}}.

(3.1)

Since
pq>1
and
p<1
, the last term converges to
0
. Consequently, for all
sufficiently large
n
,

n\mathbb{E}[T_{1}]\leq\frac{t_{n}}{2},\qquad 8n\mathbb{E}[T_{1}^{2}]\leq\frac{2}{3}b_{n}t_{n}.

Then, for
n
large enough,

\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{n}T_{k}\geq t_{n}\right\}\subset\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{n}\bigl(T_{k}-\mathbb{E}[T_{1}]\bigr)\geq\frac{t_{n}}{2}\right\}.

By the one-sided Bernstein inequality for bounded independent random variables;
see
[
8
, Proposition 2.14]
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}T_{k}\geq t_{n}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{t_{n}^{2}}{8n\mathbb{E}[T_{1}^{2}]+(4/3)b_{n}t_{n}}\right).

The denominator is at most
2b_{n}t_{n}
, and therefore

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}T_{k}\geq t_{n}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{t_{n}}{2b_{n}}\right)=n^{-h}.

The proof is complete.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

For every
x\in E_{\alpha}
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\log n}\log\mathbb{P}(Y_{n}>x)=-\alpha\log x.

Proof.

Since
Y_{n}\geq Z_{n}
, applying Lemma
3.1
we get the lower bound

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\log n}\log\mathbb{P}(Y_{n}>x)\geq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\log n}\log\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}>x)=-\alpha\log x.

We now prove the upper bound. Fix
x\in E_{\alpha}
and
h>0
. We have

\displaystyle\{Y_{n}>x\}

\displaystyle=\{S_{n}>t_{n}(x)\}

\displaystyle\subset\{X_{(n)}>b_{n}(x,h)\}\cup\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{n}X_{k}\mathbf{1}_{\{X_{k}\leq b_{n}(x,h)\}}>t_{n}(x)\right\}.

By Lemma
3.2
, the second event has probability at most

n^{-h}
for all sufficiently large
n
.

As for the first event, choose
\eta>0
so small that

xe^{-\eta}>1
. Note that

b_{n}(x,h)=\frac{t_{n}(x)}{2h\log n}=\frac{a_{n}x^{\log n}}{2h\log n}=\frac{t_{n}(xe^{-\eta})n^{\eta}}{2h\log n}.

Since
n^{\eta}/\log n\to\infty
, we have
b_{n}(x,h)\geq t_{n}(xe^{-\eta})
for
all sufficiently large
n
. Hence

\{X_{(n)}>b_{n}(x,h)\}\subset\{X_{(n)}>t_{n}(xe^{-\eta})\}=\{Z_{n}>xe^{-\eta}\}.

By Lemma
3.1
, applied to
xe^{-\eta}>1
,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\log n}\log\mathbb{P}(X_{(n)}>b_{n}(x,h))\leq-\alpha\log(xe^{-\eta})=-\alpha\log x+\alpha\eta.

Combining the bounds obtained above, we obtain

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\log n}\log\mathbb{P}(Y_{n}>x)

\displaystyle\leq\bigl(-\alpha\log x+\alpha\eta\bigr)\vee(-h).

Letting first
h\to\infty
and then
\eta\downarrow 0
gives

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\log n}\log\mathbb{P}(Y_{n}>x)\leq-\alpha\log x.

This completes the proof.
∎

We now turn to the proof of the main result.

Proof of Theorem
2.1
.

Let
G\subset E_{\alpha}
be open.

Given a fixed
x_{0}\in G
, we choose
a,b\in E_{\alpha}

such that
a<x_{0}<b
and
(a,b]\subset G
.

The function
x\mapsto I(x)=\alpha\log x
is strictly increasing and
a<b
, hence
I(a)<I(b)
. Fix

\varepsilon>0
such that

2\varepsilon<I(b)-I(a).

By Lemma
3.3
, for all sufficiently large
n
,

n^{-I(a)-\varepsilon}\leq\mathbb{P}(Y_{n}>a),\qquad\mathbb{P}(Y_{n}>b)\leq n^{-I(b)+\varepsilon}.

Therefore

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(a<Y_{n}\leq b)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(Y_{n}>a)-\mathbb{P}(Y_{n}>b)

\displaystyle\geq n^{-I(a)-\varepsilon}-n^{-I(b)+\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=n^{-I(a)-\varepsilon}\left(1-n^{I(a)-I(b)+2\varepsilon}\right).

Since
I(a)-I(b)+2\varepsilon<0
, the term in parentheses converges to
1
.
Consequently,

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\log n}\log\mathbb{P}(a<Y_{n}\leq b)\geq-I(a)-\varepsilon.

Letting first
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
and then
a\uparrow x_{0}
give

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\log n}\log\mathbb{P}(Y_{n}\in G)\geq-I(x_{0}).

Since
x_{0}\in G
was arbitrary,

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\log n}\log\mathbb{P}(Y_{n}\in G)\geq-\inf_{x\in G}I(x).

Now let
F\subset E_{\alpha}
be closed in
\mathbb{R}
. If
F=\emptyset
, there
is nothing to prove. Since
F
is closed in
\mathbb{R}
and

F\subset E_{\alpha}=(d_{\alpha},\infty)
, we have

a_{F}:=\inf F\in F\qquad\text{and}\qquad a_{F}>d_{\alpha}.

For every
\delta\in(0,a_{F}-d_{\alpha})
,

F\subset(a_{F}-\delta,\infty).

Hence

\mathbb{P}(Y_{n}\in F)\leq\mathbb{P}(Y_{n}>a_{F}-\delta).

By Lemma
3.3
,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\log n}\log\mathbb{P}(Y_{n}\in F)\leq-I(a_{F}-\delta).

Letting
\delta\downarrow 0
and using the continuity of
I
, we obtain

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\log n}\log\mathbb{P}(Y_{n}\in F)\leq-I(a_{F})=-\inf_{x\in F}I(x),

where we have applied in the last equality that
I
is increasing. This completes the proof of the upper bound.

Finally, we note that the final assertion of Theorem
2.1
follows from Lemma
3.3
above.
The proof is complete.
∎

Appendix A
Auxiliary results

This appendix is devoted to some auxiliary results.

Throughout, we assume that

\bar{F}(x)=x^{-\alpha}L(x),\qquad\alpha>0,

where
L
is slowly varying. We use standard facts on regularly varying
functions, including Potter bounds, quantile asymptotics, and polynomial tail
bounds; see
[
7
, Section 0.6]
.
Recall that

a_{n}=F^{\leftarrow}\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right),\qquad t_{n}(x)=a_{n}x^{\log n}.

Lemma A.1
.

If
\bar{F}(x)=x^{-\alpha}L(x)
with
\alpha>0
, then

\mathbb{E}[X^{p}]<\infty

for every
p\in(0,\alpha)
.

Proof.

For a nonnegative random variable
X
,

\mathbb{E}[X^{p}]=p\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{p-1}\mathbb{P}(X>t)\,\mathrm{d}t.

Choose
\varepsilon>0
such that
p+\varepsilon<\alpha
. By regular variation,
there exists
t_{0}>0
such that

\bar{F}(t)\leq t^{-\alpha+\varepsilon},\qquad t\geq t_{0}.

Therefore

\int_{t_{0}}^{\infty}t^{p-1}\bar{F}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\leq\int_{t_{0}}^{\infty}t^{p-1-\alpha+\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{d}t<\infty.

This proves the claim.
∎

Lemma A.2
.

The following estimates hold.

(i)

The high quantile satisfies

\frac{\log a_{n}}{\log n}\longrightarrow\frac{1}{\alpha}.

Moreover,

\frac{\log\bar{F}(a_{n})}{\log n}\longrightarrow-1.

(ii)

For every
M>1
,

\frac{\log t_{n}(x)}{\log n}\longrightarrow\frac{1}{\alpha}+\log x

uniformly for
x\in[1,M]
.

(iii)

For every
M>1
,

\bar{F}(t_{n}(x))=n^{-1-\alpha\log x+o(1)}

uniformly for
x\in[1,M]
.

(iv)

If
(b_{n})
is a positive sequence such that
\log b_{n}=o(\log n)
, then, for
every
M>1
,

\bar{F}(b_{n}t_{n}(x))=n^{-1-\alpha\log x+o(1)}

uniformly for
x\in[1,M]
.

Proof.

By regular variation,

\frac{\log\bar{F}(t)}{\log t}\longrightarrow-\alpha,\qquad t\to\infty;

see
[
7
, Proposition 0.8(ii)]
. In particular, for every

\varepsilon\in(0,\alpha)
and all sufficiently large
t
,

t^{-(\alpha+\varepsilon)}\leq\bar{F}(t)\leq t^{-(\alpha-\varepsilon)}.

We first prove the quantile estimate. We have
a_{n}\to\infty
. Indeed, if

(a_{n})
were bounded, then
\bar{F}
would vanish eventually, contradicting
regular variation with index
-\alpha
.

Fix
\varepsilon\in(0,\alpha)
and let
\rho>0
be fixed. For all sufficiently
large
n
, both
a_{n}
and
a_{n}-\rho
are large enough for the preceding
polynomial bounds to apply. By the definition of the generalized inverse,

\bar{F}(a_{n})\leq\frac{1}{n}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{1}{n}\leq\bar{F}(a_{n}-\rho).

Therefore, for all sufficiently large
n
,

a_{n}^{-(\alpha+\varepsilon)}\leq\bar{F}(a_{n})\leq\frac{1}{n}\leq\bar{F}(a_{n}-\rho)\leq(a_{n}-\rho)^{-(\alpha-\varepsilon)}.

It follows that

n^{1/(\alpha+\varepsilon)}\leq a_{n}\leq\rho+n^{1/(\alpha-\varepsilon)}.

Hence

\frac{1}{\alpha+\varepsilon}\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log a_{n}}{\log n}\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log a_{n}}{\log n}\leq\frac{1}{\alpha-\varepsilon}.

Letting
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
proves

\frac{\log a_{n}}{\log n}\longrightarrow\frac{1}{\alpha}.

Moreover,

\frac{\log\bar{F}(a_{n})}{\log n}=\frac{\log\bar{F}(a_{n})}{\log a_{n}}\frac{\log a_{n}}{\log n}.

The first factor converges to
-\alpha
and the second one to
1/\alpha
.
Thus

\frac{\log\bar{F}(a_{n})}{\log n}\longrightarrow-1.

This proves (i).

For (ii), note that

\frac{\log t_{n}(x)}{\log n}=\frac{\log a_{n}}{\log n}+\log x.

The convergence is uniform for
x\in[1,M]
by (i).

We next prove (iii). Since
t_{n}(x)\geq a_{n}\to\infty
uniformly for

x\in[1,M]
, the convergence

\frac{\log\bar{F}(t)}{\log t}\longrightarrow-\alpha

may be applied uniformly along
t=t_{n}(x)
. Hence, uniformly for
x\in[1,M]
,

\displaystyle\frac{\log\bar{F}(t_{n}(x))}{\log n}

\displaystyle=\frac{\log\bar{F}(t_{n}(x))}{\log t_{n}(x)}\frac{\log t_{n}(x)}{\log n}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow-\alpha\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}+\log x\right)=-1-\alpha\log x.

This is equivalent to

\bar{F}(t_{n}(x))=n^{-1-\alpha\log x+o(1)}

uniformly for
x\in[1,M]
.

Finally, let
(b_{n})
be a positive sequence with
\log b_{n}=o(\log n)
. Then,
uniformly for
x\in[1,M]
,

\frac{\log(b_{n}t_{n}(x))}{\log n}=\frac{\log b_{n}}{\log n}+\frac{\log t_{n}(x)}{\log n}=\frac{1}{\alpha}+\log x+o(1).

In particular,
b_{n}t_{n}(x)\to\infty
uniformly for
x\in[1,M]
. Applying again
the convergence

\frac{\log\bar{F}(t)}{\log t}\longrightarrow-\alpha

along
t=b_{n}t_{n}(x)
gives

\frac{\log\bar{F}(b_{n}t_{n}(x))}{\log n}\longrightarrow-1-\alpha\log x

uniformly for
x\in[1,M]
. This proves (iv).
∎

Proof of Lemma
3.1
.

Let
x>1
and put

q_{n}(x)=\bar{F}(t_{n}(x)).

By Lemma
A.2
(iii),

q_{n}(x)=n^{-1-\alpha\log x+o(1)}.

Since
x>1
, we have

nq_{n}(x)=n^{-\alpha\log x+o(1)}\longrightarrow 0.

Moreover,

\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}>x)=\mathbb{P}(X_{(n)}>t_{n}(x))=1-\bigl(1-q_{n}(x)\bigr)^{n}.

Using the identity

1-u^{n}=(1-u)(1+u+\cdots+u^{n-1}),\qquad 0\leq u\leq 1,

we obtain

n(1-u)u^{n-1}\leq 1-u^{n}\leq n(1-u).

Using Bernoulli’s inequality
u^{n-1}\geq 1-(n-1)(1-u)
, we get

n(1-u)[1-(n-1)(1-u)]\leq 1-u^{n}\leq n(1-u).

Applying this with
u=1-q_{n}(x)
, we obtain

nq_{n}(x)(1-(n-1)q_{n}(x))\leq\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}>x)\leq nq_{n}(x).

Since
nq_{n}(x)\to 0
, we have

1-(n-1)q_{n}(x)\longrightarrow 1.

Thus

\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}>x)=nq_{n}(x)(1+o(1)).

Using again

q_{n}(x)=n^{-1-\alpha\log x+o(1)},

we obtain

\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}>x)=n^{-\alpha\log x+o(1)}.

Hence

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\log n}\log\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}>x)=-\alpha\log x.

The proof is complete.
∎
