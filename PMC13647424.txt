Quercetin-Driven
Redox Sensitization Enhances the
Antitumor Activity of a Thiol-Reactive Agent through Glutathione Disruption

Gabriela Carrasco-Torres, Erik Andrade-Jorge, Aldo Y. Tenorio Barajas, Roberto I. Cuevas-Hernández, Ramón Román-Doval, Jesus A. Aburto-Duarte, Jaime Santoyo-Salazar

Abstract

Targeting redox homeostasis
represents a promising strategy
to
overcome adaptive mechanisms that sustain cancer cell survival under
therapeutic stress. Here, we evaluated a pharmacological sensitization
strategy based on quercetin (Q) preconditioning followed by treatment
with 3′,5′-dimaleamylbenzoic acid (DMAB), aimed at exploiting
glutathione-dependent vulnerabilities in cervical cancer cells. Q
and DMAB exhibited synergistic interactions across a 6 × 6 concentration
matrix, with a marked leftward shift in the dose–response profile
within the IC50-proximal range. Combined treatment was
associated with significant depletion of intracellular glutathione
(GSH) and a reduction in the GSH/GSSG ratio, consistent with disruption
of redox buffering capacity. Functionally, Q + DMAB reduced HeLa cell
viability to 23% at 48 h and markedly increased apoptotic cell death
(75%), accompanied by S-phase cell cycle arrest and inhibition of
cell migration. Notably, cytotoxic effects were attenuated in nonmalignant
epithelial cells. In vivo, combined administration
significantly reduced tumor burden (69%) and improved survival in
a murine L5178-Y lymphoma model, without overt signs of systemic toxicity.
Collectively, these results demonstrate that Q enhances the antitumor
activity of DMAB through modulation of glutathione-dependent redox
homeostasis and support the potential of redox-directed combination
strategies in cancer therapy.

Introduction

Cervical cancer remains a leading cause
of cancer-related morbidity
and mortality among women worldwide, with more than half a million
new cases and approximately 350,000 deaths reported annually. This
burden is particularly high in low- and middle-income countries, where
limited access to screening programs and timely treatment directly
impacts clinical outcomes.
−

 Despite advances in precision
oncology and the development of targeted therapies, their application
in advanced disease remains limited; therefore, it highlights the
need for the development of more selective and effective methodologies
and tactics.
−

 These limitations are largely attributed to tumor
heterogeneity and the development of therapeutic resistance mechanisms, including activation of survival pathways, metabolic
reprogramming, overexpression of efflux transporters, and adaptation
of stress response systems, particularly those associated with the
regulation of cellular redox balance.
,

In this
context, modulation of the intracellular redox state has
been proposed as a promising strategy to increase tumor cell susceptibility
to anticancer treatments. In particular, the glutathione (GSH) system
plays a central role in maintaining redox homeostasis and represents
one of the major cellular antioxidant buffers. Differences in the
redox environment, especially those associated with GSH, can be exploited
to induce selective imbalances in cancer cells and promote cytotoxic
responses.
,
 Furthermore, the dependence of
tumor cells on antioxidant systems, such as GSH-associated pathways,
constitutes a critical adaptive mechanism in response to therapeutic
stress and a key determinant of cell survival.
−

 In this regard, flavonoids have
gained increasing attention in oncology due to their ability to modulate
multiple processes associated with tumor biology, including proliferation,
apoptosis, inflammation, metabolism, and responses to oxidative stress.
,−

 Among them, quercetin (Q) stands out due to its pleiotropic
profile and its capacity to modulate tumor cell behavior in a context-dependent
manner. In addition to its antioxidant properties, Q has been described
as a regulator of redox homeostasis and cellular sensitivity to subsequent
stimuli.
−

 Importantly, Q has been shown to act as a pharmacological
sensitizer when administered in combination with other bioactive compounds
or chemotherapeutic agents, enhancing cytotoxic responses in various
tumor models, including cervical cancer.
,

Complementarily, thiol-reactive compounds provide a chemical
strategy
to modulate intracellular redox systems by engaging biological sulfhydryl
groups and altering antioxidant buffering capacity. In particular,
maleamide- and maleimide-derived structures can interact with −SH
groups through Michael-type addition or related thiol-reactive mechanisms,
enabling their interaction with glutathione, N-acetylcysteine, and
cysteine-rich biomolecules, thereby affecting intracellular thiol
availability and redox-dependent cellular functions.
,
 Previous studies with aryl maleimides, including benzoic acid-derived
analogues, have shown that these compounds can reduce tumor cell viability
and induce apoptosis, effects associated with glutathione depletion,
increased oxidative stress, and disruption of thiol-dependent homeostasis. In addition, structurally related maleamide
derivatives have demonstrated anticancer activity in different tumor
models, including multitarget effects in prostate cancer and antiproliferative
activity associated with caspase-3 activation in ovarian cancer cells.
,
 Recent studies further support the therapeutic relevance of maleimide-containing
systems in oncology, showing that maleimide-functionalized nanocarriers
can enhance doxorubicin delivery and antitumor efficacy, while GSH-responsive
maleimide-bearing small molecules can deplete intracellular glutathione
and sensitize resistant cancer cells to chemotherapy.
−

In this context, 3′,5′-dimaleamylbenzoic acid
(DMAB),
a benzoic acid-derived compound belonging to the same maleamide- and
arylmaleimide-related chemical framework previously synthesized, characterized,
and evaluated for anticancer activity, represents a structurally relevant
thiol-reactive agent.
,
 DMAB bears two maleamide-type
moieties linked to a benzoic acid core, providing a chemical basis
for its potential interaction with cellular thiol pools and glutathione-dependent
buffering systems. Q, in turn, can be positioned not merely as a cytotoxic
flavonoid, but as a redox-modulating preconditioning agent capable
of reshaping the intracellular antioxidant environment before DMAB
exposure and increasing tumor cell susceptibility to subsequent thiol-dependent
stress.
−

 Based on this rationale, we hypothesized that Q-driven
redox preconditioning would enhance the antitumor activity of DMAB
by sensitizing cancer cells to GSH depletion and disruption of the
GSH/GSSG balance. Therefore, this study evaluated a sequential Q →
DMAB pharmacological strategy in cervical cancer cells and explored
its antitumor efficacy in vivo.

Materials
and Methods

Cell Culture and Treatments

The human cervical cancer
cell line HeLa (ATCC CCL-2) was used as the tumor model. Two non-tumoral
epithelial cell lines were included as controls: THLE-3 (ATCC CRL-11233),
to evaluate potential off-target and systemic redox-related effects
given the central role of the liver in xenobiotic metabolism, and
HaCaT (ATCC PCS-200-011), as a representative epithelial model.

Cells were maintained under standard culture conditions (37 °C,
5% CO2, humidified atmosphere). HeLa and HaCaT cells were
cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin,
while THLE-3 cells were cultured according to ATCC recommendations.
All cell lines were thawed from cryopreserved stocks and expanded
to obtain sufficient working cultures for the in vitro experiments. To minimize passage-related variability, HeLa and HaCaT
cells were used at passage 5, whereas THLE-3 cells were used at passage
4 after thawing. Before each experiment, cultures were routinely inspected
for morphology, adherence, and growth behavior, and only cells in
the exponential growth phase were used. For all treatments, dimethyl
sulfoxide (DMSO; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA; Cat. No. D2650)
was used as vehicle control, with a final concentration not exceeding
0.1%, which did not affect cell viability.

Quercetin (Q; Santa
Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA; Cat. No.
sc-203225-A) and 3′,5′-dimaleamylbenzoic acid (DMAB)
were initially evaluated in single-agent dose–response experiments.
Cells were exposed to increasing concentrations (0.1–240 μM),
and cell viability was assessed after 48 h to determine pharmacodynamic
profiles. DMAB was synthesized and structurally characterized as previously
described. Stock solutions were prepared
in DMSO and diluted in culture medium immediately before use.

For drug interaction studies, a reduced concentration range (0,
0.3, 1, 3, 10, and 30 μM) was selected for both compounds. Q
and DMAB were combined in a 6 × 6 factorial design (36 conditions),
and cell viability was evaluated after 48 h. The resulting interaction
matrix was used for synergy analysis.

For fixed-dose combination
experiments, the Q → DMAB sequence
was selected based on preliminary optimization assays in HeLa, HaCaT,
and THLE-3 cells, in which Q pretreatment followed by DMAB produced
a reproducible biological response across the evaluated cellular models.
DMAB was added 30 min after Q to evaluate the potential sensitizing
effect of Q on DMAB-induced cellular responses. This interval was
established based on preliminary experiments assessing short-term
redox modulation and was used in the subsequent mechanistic and in vivo studies.

Cell Viability Assay

Cell viability
was evaluated using
the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT)
assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, IL, USA; Cat. No. M6494)
as an indicator of metabolic activity. HeLa, THLE-3, and HaCaT cells
were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well in 100 μL of culture medium and allowed to
attach overnight under standard culture conditions.

After treatment,
MTT was added to each well to a final concentration of 0.5 mg/mL,
followed by incubation for 4 h at 37 °C. The resulting formazan
crystals were dissolved in 100 μL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO),
and absorbance was recorded at 590 nm using a microplate reader (iMark,
Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Background values were corrected using
blank wells containing medium and MTT without cells.

Cell viability
was expressed relative to vehicle-treated controls,
which were set to 100%. Each condition was tested in triplicate wells,
and all experiments were independently repeated three times. Data
are reported as mean ± SEM, and statistical analyses were performed
using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, USA).

and Drug Interaction Analysis

Single-agent dose–response
data were analyzed according to
the median-effect principle described by Chou and Talalay. Cell viability
values were converted into fraction affected (Fa) using

Drug interaction within the 6 × 6

First, Bliss independence

CI values were interpreted as follows:
CI < 1, synergism; CI

All pharmacodynamic analyses
were performed using custom Python

Wound Healing Migration Assay

Cell migration was evaluated
using a wound healing assay with minor modifications to the method
described by Liang et al. HeLa cells
were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 3 × 105 cells per well and allowed to reach 90–100% confluence.

A linear wound was created across the cell monolayer with a sterile
200 μL pipette tip. Non-adherent cells were carefully removed
by washing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). To exclude the contribution
of cell proliferation to wound closure, cells were treated with mitomycin
C before exposure to the indicated treatments. Cells were then exposed
to the indicated treatments, and phase-contrast images were acquired
at 0 and 48 h under identical acquisition settings.

Wound closure
was calculated as
[ao6c04893_m007.webp]Wound closure(%)=Area0⁡h−Area48⁡hArea0⁡h×100

Wound area was quantified using ImageJ
software (NIH, USA). Each
condition was evaluated in ten independent wells (technical replicates),
and experiments were performed using independent cell preparations.
Results are expressed as mean ± SEM. Statistical analyses were
performed using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, USA).

TUNEL Assay
for Apoptotic DNA Fragmentation

Apoptotic
DNA fragmentation was assessed using a TUNEL assay (In Situ Cell Death
Detection Kit, Fluorescein; Sigma-Aldrich; Cat. No. 11684795910).
HeLa cells were seeded onto glass coverslips placed in 24-well plates
at a density of 3 × 104 cells per well and allowed
to adhere overnight under standard culture conditions.

After
treatment, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at
room temperature, washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and
permeabilized using 0.1% Triton X-100 in 0.1% sodium citrate for 2
min at 4 °C. Samples were then incubated with the TUNEL reaction
mixture for 1 h at 37 °C in the dark. Negative controls were
prepared by omitting terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase, while
positive controls were obtained by DNase I treatment.

Nuclei
were counterstained with propidium iodide (PI) to visualize
total cell nuclei. Coverslips were mounted using Vectashield medium
and analyzed by confocal microscopy under identical acquisition settings.

TUNEL-positive nuclei were quantified as a percentage of total
PI-stained nuclei using ImageJ (NIH, USA). At least five random fields
per coverslip were analyzed. Each condition included technical replicates
(n = 10 coverslips), and experiments were performed using independent
cell preparations. Data are expressed as mean ± SEM, and statistical
analysis was carried out using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software,
USA).

Western Blot Analysis of Caspase-3 Activation

After
treatment, HeLa cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific)
supplemented with protease inhibitors. Protein concentration was determined
using a Bio-Rad Protein Assay (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA).

Protein extracts (30 μg per sample) were separated on 10–12%
SDS–PAGE gels and transferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes
were blocked with 3% nonfat milk in PBS-T (0.1% Tween-20) and incubated
overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against caspase-3,
1:1000 dilution (Cell Signaling Technology; Cat. No. 9662) and β-actin, 1:2000 dilution (Cell Signaling Technology;
Cat. No. 4970). After washing steps, membranes were exposed to horseradish
peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies.

Signal detection
was performed using enhanced chemiluminescence
(ECL), and images were acquired with a ChemiDoc MP Imaging System
(Bio-Rad). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ (NIH, USA)
and normalized to β-actin as the loading control.

All experiments were conducted using three independent biological
replicates. Data are expressed as mean ± SEM, and statistical
analysis was carried out using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software,
USA).

Cell Cycle Distribution Analysis

Cell cycle distribution
was analyzed by flow cytometry following propidium iodide (PI) staining.
HeLa and HaCaT cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of
3 × 105 cells per well and allowed to adhere overnight
under standard culture conditions. HeLa cells were treated with Q
(10 μM), DMAB (10 μM), or Q + DMAB (3 μM + 3 μM),
whereas HaCaT cells were exposed to vehicle or Q + DMAB (3 μM
+ 3 μM) for 48 h.

After treatment, cells were harvested,
washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and incubated with RNase
A (final concentration 0.2 mg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich; Cat. No. R6513) and
PI (final concentration 100 μg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich; Cat. No. P4170)
for 40 min at 37 °C in the dark.

DNA content was analyzed
using a FACSCalibur flow cytometer (BD
Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA), and at least 10,000 events were acquired
per sample. Cell cycle distribution (G0/G1,
S, and G2/M phases) was determined using ModFit LT software
(Verity Software House, USA).

Experiments were performed in
four independent biological replicates.
Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical analyses were conducted
using GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad Software, USA).

Intracellular
Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) Measurement

Intracellular ROS
levels were measured using 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescin
diacetate (DCFH-DA; Sigma-Aldrich; Cat. No. D6883), a cell-permeable
probe that is converted intracellularly into the fluorescent compound
dichlorofluorescein (DCF).

HeLa and HaCaT cells were seeded
in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per
well and cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium
(DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) until approximately
80% confluence. Cells were washed with Hank’s balanced salt
solution (HBSS) and subjected to serum-reduced conditions (DMEM containing
2% FBS) for 12 h prior to treatment.

Following 24 h exposure
to the indicated treatments, cells were
incubated with DCFH-DA working solution for 30 min at 37 °C in
the dark. Fluorescence intensity was measured using a Fluoroskan Ascent
fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at excitation and emission
wavelengths of 480 and 515 nm, respectively.

ROS levels were
expressed as relative fluorescence units (RFU)
and normalized to total protein content.

Experiments were performed
in three independent biological replicates.
Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical analyses were conducted
using GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad Software, USA).

Intracellular
Glutathione (GSH/GSSG) Analysis

Intracellular
reduced glutathione (GSH) and oxidized glutathione (GSSG) levels were
quantified in HeLa and HaCaT cells after 24 h of treatment using an
o-phthaldialdehyde (OPT)-based fluorescence assay. Following treatment,
cells were washed with Hank’s balanced salt solution (HBSS),
harvested, and collected by centrifugation.

Cell pellets were
resuspended in FEDTA buffer (0.1 M sodium phosphate monobasic, 5 mM
EDTA, pH 8.0) and deproteinized by the addition of 25% phosphoric
acid. Samples were centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 30 min at 4
°C, and the resulting supernatants were used for glutathione
determination.

For GSH quantification, aliquots of the supernatant
were reacted
with OPT reagent, and fluorescence was measured at excitation and
emission wavelengths of 350 and 420 nm, respectively. For selective
GSSG determination, samples were pretreated with N-ethylmaleimide
(NEM) to derivatize reduced glutathione prior to the OPT reaction.

Total glutathione content was calculated as the sum of GSH and
GSSG concentrations. All values were normalized to total protein content.
Intracellular redox status was assessed based on total glutathione
levels (GSH + GSSG) and the GSH/GSSG ratio.

Experiments were
performed in six independent biological replicates.
Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical analyses were conducted
using GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad Software, USA).

Murine L5178-Y
Lymphoma Model

The murine L5178-Y ascitic
lymphoma model was used to evaluate in vivo antitumor
efficacy and survival outcomes following treatment. The model was
established by intraperitoneal transplantation of viable tumor cells
into male BALB/c mice.

Tumor cells were obtained from donor
mice bearing established ascitic lymphoma. Male BALB/c mice (6–8
weeks of age, body weight 26–28 g) were used in all experiments.

All experimental protocols were approved by the Institutional Committee
for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (CICUAL) (protocol no.
0254-11). Animals were provided by the Unidad de Producción
y Experimentación en Animales de Laboratorio (UPEAL) at CINVESTAV,
Mexico City, Mexico. Mice were maintained under controlled environmental
conditions (12 h light/dark cycle, temperature of 21 °C, and
50% relative humidity), with unrestricted access to standard laboratory
diet (PMI Feeds Inc., Laboratory Diet) and water.

For tumor
induction, 3.2 × 106 viable L5178-Y cells
suspended in sterile 0.9% NaCl solution were injected intraperitoneally
into recipient mice. Tumor establishment was confirmed by the development
of ascitic fluid prior to treatment initiation.

Treatment
Protocol and Survival Analysis

The test compounds
were administered once daily for 10 consecutive days by intragastric
gavage (10 mL/kg body weight). A 0.9% NaCl solution was used as vehicle
control. Dose selection was based on preliminary toxicity and efficacy
studies.

Q was administered at 2 mg/kg body weight, and DMAB
at 4 mg/kg body weight. These doses were selected to achieve biologically
relevant and well-tolerated treatment conditions in vivo, while accounting for the pharmacological differences between in vitro exposure and systemic administration.

Cyclophosphamide
(6 mg/kg body weight) was included as a positive
control due to its well-established use as an alkylating agent in
the treatment of lymphoid malignancies.

For sequential treatment
groups, Q was administered first, followed
by DMAB 30 min later within the same dosing session, to mimic the in vitro pharmacological sensitization strategy.

Survival
was monitored daily, and survival time was recorded for
each animal. Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan–Meier
method and compared using the log-rank (Mantel–Cox) test. Investigators
responsible for survival monitoring and end point assessment were
blinded to treatment allocation. Humane end points were explicitly
defined as severe abdominal distension, marked reduction in mobility
or responsiveness, inability to access food or water, persistent recumbency,
signs of severe pain or distress, or body-weight loss exceeding 20%
relative to baseline. Only a limited proportion of animals exhibited
these predefined humane-end point criteria during the experimental
period. Animals meeting any of these criteria were immediately euthanized
in accordance with the institutional animal-welfare procedures of
UPEAL-CINVESTAV and the Mexican Official Standard NOM-062-ZOO-1999
for the production, care, and use of laboratory animals.

Tumor Growth
Evaluation

Tumor growth was evaluated
based on ascitic tumor development and tumor cell burden. Ascitic
fluid was aseptically collected using a sterile syringe, and tumor
cell viability and concentration were determined using the trypan
blue exclusion method with a Neubauer hemocytometer. Cell viability
was expressed as the percentage of viable (unstained) cells relative
to the total cell count.

Tumor burden was estimated from abdominal
distension using an ellipsoid-based approximation, as previously described
for murine ascitic lymphoma models. Tumor
dimensions were obtained through external measurements of the abdominal
region using a caliper. Tumor volume was calculated as

where L represents the major length and W
the perpendicular width.

Treatment efficacy was further evaluated
by calculating tumor growth
inhibition (TGI), defined as

where ΔV

the change
in tumor volume (V
final – V

Body weight was monitored throughout the experimental period as
an indicator of systemic toxicity and overall health status.

Statistical
Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed
using GraphPad Prism software (version 7.0; GraphPad Software, USA).
Data are presented as mean ± SEM unless otherwise indicated.
The number of independent biological replicates and, where applicable,
technical replicates is specified in the corresponding Methods subsection
and figure legend. Comparisons among multiple groups were performed
using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s
multiple-comparison test. Survival curves were generated using the
Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the log-rank (Mantel–Cox)
test. For the in vivo experiments, 10 animals were
included per group. Differences were considered statistically significant
at p < 0.05. Statistical significance is indicated
as ns, not significant; p < 0.05; p < 0.01; and p < 0.001.

Results

Pharmacodynamic
Analysis Demonstrates Synergistic Interaction
between Q and DMAB

To characterize the interaction between
Q and DMAB, single-agent and combination dose–response relationships
were first established and analyzed using nonlinear regression, followed
by matrix-based interaction analyses using Bliss independence and
the Chou–Talalay combination index (CI) framework ().

Q and DMAB each produced
concentration-dependent
reductions in
HeLa cell viability, with estimated IC50 values of approximately
13 and 10 μM, respectively (A). In contrast, the 1:1 Q + DMAB combination
showed a marked leftward shift in the dose–response curve,
with an estimated IC50 in the 2–3 μM range,
indicating increased potency relative to either monotherapy.

To further assess drug interaction, the Chou–Talalay method
was applied across increasing effect levels. The resulting CI–Fa
plot showed CI values below 1 over a broad range of fractional effects,
consistent with synergistic interaction (B). Based on the single-agent response profiles,
a 6 × 6 factorial concentration matrix (0, 0.3, 1, 3, 10, and
30 μM) was then designed to cover subeffective, IC50-proximal, and high-effect ranges.

Bliss independence analysis
revealed positive ΔE values across
much of the matrix, indicating that the observed combined effect exceeded
the Bliss-predicted additive response (D). This effect was most pronounced in the
IC50-proximal region (1–3 μM). Consistently,
the CI heatmap derived from the Chou–Talalay framework identified
a central region of synergistic interaction, again most evident within
the IC50-proximal range (E).

Taken together, these results show
that Q and DMAB produce a synergistic
effect within a defined concentration range, with the combination
exhibiting greater potency than either agent alone.

Combined Treatment
Enhances Selective, Time-Dependent Cytotoxicity
in Tumor Cells

Based on pharmacodynamically relevant concentrations
identified in the dose–response analysis, HeLa cervical cancer
cells and the non-tumor epithelial cell lines HaCaT and THLE-3 were
treated with Q (10 μM), DMAB (10 μM), or their combination
(3 μM + 3 μM) for 12, 24, 36, and 48 h. Cell viability
was assessed by the MTT assay.

In HeLa cells, treatment-related
changes were detectable as early as 12 h. At this time point, Q and
DMAB monotherapies reduced viability to approximately 87% and 90%,
respectively, whereas the combination did not differ significantly
from the vehicle control (A). At 24 h, both Q and the combination produced larger
decreases in viability than DMAB alone (B). At 36 h, the combination showed the lowest
viability values, decreasing the MTT-derived signal to approximately
50%, compared with about 62% for DMAB (C). By 48 h, Q monotherapy reduced viability
to approximately 54%, whereas the combination produced the strongest
effect, lowering viability to approximately 23% relative to vehicle-treated
cells (D).

In non-tumor epithelial cells, the effects were
markedly attenuated.
In HaCaT cells, Q and DMAB induced modest reductions in viability
at 48 h, whereas the combination produced little additional effect
(E). Similarly,
THLE-3 cells showed only minor changes across treatment conditions,
indicating lower sensitivity than HeLa cells (F).

Collectively, these results indicate
that Q and DMAB produce a
time-dependent reduction in HeLa cell viability, with the strongest
effect observed for the combination at 48 h, while non-tumor epithelial
models display substantially lower sensitivity under the same conditions.

Combined Treatment Inhibits Cell Migration and Promotes Apoptotic
Cell Death

To evaluate the impact of treatment on HeLa cell
behavior, wound closure and apoptosis-related end points were assessed
following exposure to Q (10 μM), DMAB (10 μM), or their
combination (3 μM + 3 μM) for 48 h. Wound closure was
first examined using a scratch assay. Representative images are shown
in A. Control
and vehicle-treated cells displayed comparable closure of the scratched
area, indicating that the vehicle (0.1% DMSO) did not affect the assay.
In contrast, the combination treatment resulted in a visibly wider
residual wound area. Quantitative analysis showed that Q and DMAB
monotherapies produced modest reductions in wound closure, whereas
the combination displayed the lowest wound closure values, reaching
50% relative to control conditions (B).

To determine whether these effects were associated
with apoptosis,
DNA fragmentation was assessed by TUNEL staining. Representative fluorescence
images are shown in C. PI-staining identified total nuclei, whereas TUNEL-positive
nuclei corresponded to apoptotic cells. Merged images indicated that
TUNEL labeling was localized to PI-counterstained nuclei. Quantitative
analysis revealed low basal apoptosis in control and vehicle-treated
cells, while Q and DMAB increased the proportion of TUNEL-positive
cells to 43% and 35%, respectively. The combination treatment produced
the highest apoptotic index, reaching 70–75%, significantly
above both monotherapies (D).

To further assess the apoptotic response,
caspase-3 activation
was evaluated by Western blot. Both procaspase-3 (32 kDa) and cleaved
caspase-3 fragments (17/19 kDa) were detected (E). Cleaved caspase-3 levels were minimal
in control and vehicle-treated cells, increased after either monotherapy,
and were highest in the combination group. Densitometric analysis
normalized to β-actin confirmed an approximately fivefold increase
for Q, a threefold increase for DMAB, and an approximately eightfold
increase for the combination relative to control (F).

Collectively, these
findings indicate that Q + DMAB markedly impairs
wound closure and promotes apoptotic cell death in HeLa cells, accompanied
by enhanced caspase-3 cleavage.

Combined Treatment Induces
Cell Cycle Arrest

To assess
whether the antiproliferative effects of Q and DMAB were linked to
changes in cell cycle progression, DNA content was evaluated by flow
cytometry using propidium iodide staining. HeLa cells were exposed
to Q (10 μM), DMAB (10 μM), or their combination (3 μM
+ 3 μM) for 48 h. Representative histograms are shown in A, and the corresponding
phase distribution is presented in B.

Control cells exhibited a typical
cell cycle distribution,
with
approximately 48.2% of cells in G0/G1, 40.8%
in S phase, and 11.0% in G2/M. Treatment with Q resulted
in a marked accumulation of cells in G0/G1 (62.3%,
***), accompanied by a reduction in S phase (32.9%) and a decrease
in G2/M (4.8%, ***), suggesting partial arrest at the G0/G1 checkpoint.

Similarly, DMAB increased
the proportion of cells in G0/G1 (57.1%, ***),
while reducing the G2/M population
(3.8%, ***). The S-phase fraction (39.1%) remained comparable to control,
indicating a moderate inhibition of cell cycle progression.

In contrast, the combination treatment produced a pronounced redistribution
of the cell cycle profile, characterized by a substantial accumulation
of cells in S phase (65.3%, **), accompanied by a marked decrease
in G0/G1 (33.8%, ***) and an almost complete
depletion of G2/M (0.9%, ***). This pattern indicates a
strong S-phase arrest, consistent with impaired DNA replication or
S-phase progression.

Overall, Q and DMAB as single agents mainly
led to partial accumulation
in the G0/G1 phase, whereas the combined treatment
markedly altered cell cycle progression, promoting S-phase accumulation.
This shift may be associated with the enhanced cytotoxic and pro-apoptotic
effects observed.

To determine whether the S-phase accumulation
induced by the combined
treatment was also observed in nonmalignant epithelial cells, cell-cycle
distribution was evaluated in HaCaT cells under the same treatment
conditions. Vehicle-treated HaCaT cells showed 49.1% of cells in G0/G1, 38.9% in S phase, and 12.0% in G2/M. Following Q + DMAB treatment, the corresponding distributions
were 48.2%, 40.8%, and 11%, respectively, with no significant redistribution
among cell-cycle phases. These findings indicate that the marked S-phase
accumulation observed in HeLa cells was not reproduced in HaCaT cells
under the same experimental conditions, supporting a tumor-selective
effect of the combined treatment.

Combined Treatment Modulates
Intracellular ROS Dynamics

Intracellular ROS-associated fluorescence
was assessed using DCFH-DA
following treatment (n = 3 independent experiments). In HeLa cells,
Q significantly reduced fluorescence intensity compared with vehicle-treated
controls (***), indicating a decrease in ROS-associated signal. In
contrast, DMAB did not significantly alter fluorescence levels.

The combination treatment also significantly reduced fluorescence
relative to control conditions (***); however, the magnitude of this
reduction was lower than that observed with Q alone (B).

In non-tumoral HaCaT cells, Q similarly reduced
fluorescence intensity
compared with vehicle-treated controls (***). DMAB produced only minor
changes, while the combination treatment resulted in fluorescence
levels comparable to control conditions (B).

Disruption of Redox Homeostasis through Glutathione Depletion

Intracellular glutathione parameters were quantified to assess
alterations in cellular redox homeostasis induced by the different
treatments (n = 6 independent experiments).

In tumor cells,
treatment with Q monotherapy significantly reduced intracellular glutathione
pools compared with vehicle-treated controls (***), as evidenced by
decreased levels of reduced glutathione (GSH) and total glutathione
(GSH + GSSG). In addition, oxidized glutathione (GSSG) levels were
lower than those observed in control cells, while the GSH/GSSG ratio
increased, indicating a shift in intracellular redox balance in HeLa
cells (C–F).

Treatment with DMAB monotherapy also altered glutathione homeostasis
in HeLa cells. GSH levels were significantly reduced (***) accompanied
by a decrease in total glutathione content (***). GSSG levels were
also significantly reduced (***), whereas the GSH/GSSG ratio showed
a modest but significant decline (***), suggesting partial disruption
of redox equilibrium in HeLa cells.

Notably, the Q + DMAB combination
treatment produced the most pronounced
disruption of intracellular glutathione homeostasis in HeLa cells.
This treatment induced a marked depletion of reduced glutathione (GSH)
(***) and total glutathione (***), together with a substantial decline
in the GSH/GSSG ratio (***) relative to control conditions. GSSG levels
were also slightly lower than those observed in control cells (C–F).

To determine whether this glutathione-dependent redox disruption
was also present in a non-tumorigenic epithelial model, the same parameters
were evaluated in HaCaT cells after 24 h of treatment. In HaCaT cells,
Q and DMAB produced moderate decreases in GSH levels, whereas Q +
DMAB induced a further reduction in GSH (C). In contrast to HeLa cells, GSSG levels
increased following treatment, particularly in the Q + DMAB group
(D). Total
glutathione showed only a moderate reduction compared with the marked
depletion observed in HeLa cells, and the GSH/GSSG ratio also decreased,
indicating redox modulation in the context of comparatively preserved
total glutathione content (E, F).

Together, these findings indicate that
Q + DMAB disrupts glutathione-dependent
redox homeostasis in both cellular contexts, but with a distinct pattern
between tumor and non-tumorigenic cells. In HeLa cells, the combination
produced marked GSH depletion, reduced total glutathione content,
and a pronounced decrease in the GSH/GSSG ratio, consistent with impaired
antioxidant buffering capacity. In HaCaT cells, the response was characterized
by moderate GSH depletion, increased GSSG levels, and a reduced GSH/GSSG
ratio, while total glutathione content was comparatively less affected.
This differential response suggests that HaCaT cells retain a greater
capacity to preserve the overall glutathione pool, whereas HeLa cells
exhibit greater vulnerability to the combined treatment.

Overall,
these results indicate that Q + DMAB primarily disrupts
glutathione-dependent redox homeostasis rather than promoting detectable
ROS accumulation. The pronounced reduction in GSH levels, total glutathione
content, and the GSH/GSSG ratio observed in HeLa cells is consistent
with compromised antioxidant buffering capacity, whereas the comparatively
preserved total glutathione pool in HaCaT cells supports a differential
redox response between tumor and non-tumorigenic cells.

Combined Treatment
Suppresses Tumor Growth and Prolongs Survival In Vivo

Tumor progression was evaluated based on
ascitic fluid accumulation and viable tumor cell counts in the murine
L5178-Y lymphoma model. Control groups (NC and vehicle) exhibited
high ascitic fluid volumes, consistent with progressive tumor growth
(B). Treatment
with DMAB and cyclophosphamide (C+) significantly reduced ascitic
accumulation, whereas Q alone produced only a modest, non-significant
effect. Notably, the combination treatment resulted in the greatest
reduction in ascitic volume, indicating a marked suppression of tumor
burden.

Consistently, analysis of viable tumor cells in
ascitic fluid showed
a significant decrease in treated groups, with the combination yielding
the lowest cell counts among all conditions (C).

Tumor growth inhibition (TGI) analysis
further supported these
findings. The combination achieved the highest inhibitory effect,
exceeding that observed with DMAB, Q, and cyclophosphamide, indicating
an enhanced antitumor response (D).

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis revealed
clear differences
among treatment groups (E). The combination treatment provided the greatest survival
benefit, maintaining approximately 50% survival up to day 60. In contrast,
control groups showed rapid mortality, while monotherapy groups displayed
intermediate survival profiles.

Body weight was monitored as
an indicator of overall health status
(F). Control
of animals exhibited increased body weight, consistent with ascitic
accumulation. In contrast, the combination-treated group showed reduced
body weight, likely reflecting decreased ascitic fluid burden. No
treatment-associated body-weight changes suggestive of overt systemic
toxicity were observed.

Collectively, these findings indicate
that the combination of Q
and DMAB enhances antitumor efficacy in vivo, as
evidenced by reduced tumor burden, decreased viable tumor cell counts,
increased tumor growth inhibition, and prolonged survival.

Discussion

Redox homeostasis has emerged as an important
factor influencing
tumor progression, therapeutic resistance, and treatment outcomes.
Elevated intracellular glutathione (GSH) levels and enhanced antioxidant
capacity have been linked to reduced sensitivity to chemotherapy in
both murine models and human cancers, including cervical cancer.
,−

 Beyond its role in detoxification, glutathione also
plays a key role in regulating tumor cell proliferation, survival,
and adaptation to oxidative stress.
,,
 Accordingly, targeting glutathione metabolism and
redox balance has gained increasing attention as a potential anticancer
strategy.

Mechanistically, the enhanced efficacy of the combined
treatment
is consistent with a sequential redox sensitization process in which
Q pre-exposure may facilitate the subsequent response to DMAB. Q may
act as a priming agent, inducing a controlled modulation of intracellular
redox balance and partially perturbing glutathione-dependent buffering
capacity, thereby lowering the cellular threshold for stress adaptation
and increasing susceptibility to subsequent insults. Following this,
DMAB exposure further disrupts thiol-dependent systems, likely through
interaction with sulfhydryl (−SH) groups, contributing to the
observed depletion of intracellular GSH and impairment of thiol-dependent
antioxidant defenses. Although the present data directly demonstrate
disruption of glutathione homeostasis, they do not establish GSH as
the exclusive intracellular target of DMAB. Given the broad thiol
reactivity reported for maleamide- and maleimide-related compounds,
DMAB may also interact with protein-bound cysteine residues and other
low-molecular-weight thiols. Therefore, the observed antitumor effects
are more appropriately interpreted as resulting from disruption of
a broader thiol-dependent redox network, with glutathione representing
a major experimentally demonstrated component. Identification of preferential
DMAB-modified proteins will require dedicated thiol-profiling or chemoproteomic
analyses. This Q → DMAB sequence establishes a state of redox
vulnerability that is not achieved by either monotherapy, underscoring
the importance of pharmacological timing in maximizing antitumor efficacy.
In agreement with this mechanism, the combined treatment significantly
enhanced cytotoxicity, impaired cell cycle progression, promoted apoptosis,
and suppressed migratory capacity, while exerting minimal effects
on non-tumoral epithelial cells (HaCaT and THLE-3), supporting a selective
antitumor response mediated by targeted disruption of glutathione
homeostasis.
,,,

At the cellular level, the combined
treatment produced a pronounced
apoptotic response in HeLa cells, as evidenced by the marked increase
in TUNEL-positive nuclei and enhanced caspase-3 cleavage. Similar
pro-apoptotic effects of Q have been reported in HeLa and other tumor
cell models.
−

 In parallel, the marked inhibition of wound closure
observed in the Q + DMAB group indicates a substantial impairment
of migratory capacity. Given the central role of cytoskeletal dynamics
and redox signaling in cell adhesion and motility, these findings
suggest that disruption of glutathione-dependent homeostasis may affect
both tumor cell survival and migration. Although several classes of
migration-inhibiting agents are currently under investigation, most
remain in preclinical development.
,

Apoptosis
induction was further validated by the activation of
caspase-3, a key executioner protease responsible for the morphological
and biochemical changes associated with programmed cell death. While all treatments induced some degree of
caspase-3 cleavage, the combined Q + DMAB treatment resulted in significantly
higher levels of caspase-3 activation, consistent with an enhanced
apoptotic response associated with disruption of glutathione-dependent
redox homeostasis. These findings align with previous studies showing
that GSH depletion sensitizes cancer cells to caspase-dependent apoptosis
by lowering the cellular threshold for oxidative and mitochondrial
stress.
,

In parallel, combined treatment exerted
a pronounced effect on
cell cycle dynamics. While Q and DMAB monotherapies induced partial
G0/G1 arrest, their combination promoted a marked
accumulation of cells in S phase, accompanied by near-complete depletion
of the G2/M population. This shift is consistent with altered
S-phase progression associated with disruption of redox homeostasis.
Notably, nuclear GSH has been implicated in the control of the G1-to-S transition and DNA replication fidelity
,
 and its depletion may compromise replication processes, leading
to S-phase arrest. The divergence from previously reported Q-induced
G2/M arrest
,
 highlights the context-dependent
nature of redox-mediated cell cycle regulation and underscores the
modulatory effect of combination strategies. Importantly, the pronounced
S-phase accumulation induced by Q + DMAB in HeLa cells was not observed
in nonmalignant HaCaT cells, which maintained a cell-cycle distribution
comparable to that of vehicle-treated controls. This differential
response supports the selectivity of the combined treatment and suggests
a greater susceptibility of HeLa cells to treatment-associated cell-cycle
disruption. Nevertheless, validation in additional nonmalignant and
tumor-derived cellular models will be required to determine the broader
relevance of this selective response.

An important finding of
this study is the dissociation between
ROS accumulation and cytotoxicity. While Q reduced steady-state ROS
levels and the combined treatment did not induce sustained ROS accumulation,
a pronounced depletion of GSH and a reduction in the GSH/GSSG ratio
were observed. These findings indicate that cytotoxicity is primarily
driven by impairment of redox buffering capacity rather than ROS overload.
Importantly, the additional analysis in non-tumorigenic HaCaT cells
showed a comparatively attenuated disruption of glutathione-dependent
redox balance, with a less pronounced effect on total glutathione
content than that observed in HeLa cells. This differential response
supports a greater vulnerability of HeLa cells to Q + DMAB-induced
disruption of glutathione homeostasis compared with HaCaT cells. This
observation aligns with emerging evidence suggesting that depletion
of antioxidant reserves is sufficient to trigger cell death in redox-dependent
tumors, even in the absence of measurable ROS accumulation.
,,

Functionally, this redox
disruption translated into inhibition
of cell migration, indicating an impairment of migratory capacity
in HeLa cells. Given the role of cytoskeletal dynamics and redox signaling
in cell motility, the observed impairment of migration suggests that
glutathione-dependent processes contribute not only to survival but
also to the regulation of tumor cell motility. This dual impact reinforces
the therapeutic relevance of targeting redox homeostasis.

Importantly,
the translational relevance of these findings was
supported by in vivo evaluation. Combined Q + DMAB
treatment significantly reduced ascitic tumor growth, decreased viable
tumor cell counts, and achieved superior tumor growth inhibition compared
with monotherapies and cyclophosphamide. Moreover, survival analysis
demonstrated a marked extension of lifespan, with 50% survival maintained
up to approximately day 60. The observed reduction in body weight
in the combination group was consistent with decreased ascitic fluid
accumulation and was not accompanied by treatment-associated body
weight changes suggestive of overt systemic toxicity. These findings
indicate that redox-targeted sensitization strategies can produce
a meaningful antitumor response in vivo.

Notably,
the lack of significant antitumor activity observed with
Q monotherapy in vivo further emphasizes that its
primary role in this system is not as a direct cytotoxic agent, but
rather as a pharmacological sensitizer that enhances the efficacy
of thiol-reactive compounds such as DMAB. This highlights the importance
of temporal and mechanistic coordination in combination therapies
targeting redox vulnerabilities. These findings position redox-directed
sensitization as a rational framework for the design of next-generation
combination therapies targeting metabolic vulnerabilities in cancer.

An important limitation of this study is the use of different tumor
models for the in vitro and in vivo evaluations. Mechanistic analyses were performed mainly in HeLa
cervical cancer cells, whereas antitumor efficacy was assessed in
the murine L5178-Y lymphoma model. Therefore, the in vivo results should be interpreted as proof-of-concept evidence rather
than direct validation in cervical cancer. Further studies in additional
cervical cancer models and other tumor systems are needed to determine
the broader applicability of this strategy.

In summary, this
study provides mechanistic and functional evidence
that sequential modulation of glutathione-dependent redox homeostasis
represents a promising strategy to enhance antitumor efficacy. By
preferentially disrupting glutathione-dependent redox homeostasis
in HeLa cells while producing a comparatively lower impact on nonmalignant
epithelial models, this approach supports further investigation of
redox-based combination strategies with potential for improved selectivity
and translational relevance.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrates
that the
combination of Q and 3′,5′-dimaleamylbenzoic
acid elicits a pronounced antitumor response associated with disruption
of glutathione-dependent redox homeostasis, as the observed effects
were linked primarily to intracellular glutathione depletion and a
reduced capacity to maintain redox balance rather than to sustained
ROS generation. Pre-exposure to Q appears to modulate the intracellular
redox environment and increase tumor cell susceptibility to the subsequent
thiol-reactive effects of DMAB, while the sequential Q→DMAB
strategy enhanced cytotoxicity, promoted S-phase accumulation and
apoptosis, and reduced cell migration, with a comparatively lower
impact on nonmalignant epithelial cells.

Importantly, these
effects were also reflected in vivo, where the combined
treatment reduced tumor-associated ascitic development,
decreased viable tumor cell burden, and extended survival without
treatment-associated body weight changes suggestive of overt systemic
toxicity. Together, these findings support the concept that targeting
glutathione-dependent vulnerabilities through sequential redox modulation
may enhance antitumor efficacy and warrants further evaluation in
additional tumor models.
