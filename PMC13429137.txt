Resting-State Brain Activity, Functional Connectivity, and Orbital Imaging Changes in Restrictive Myopathy Associated with Thyroid Eye Disease
Authors: Haiyang Zhang, Zilin Fang, Duojin Xia, Songnan Liang, Yuwei Wang, Mengda Jiang, Xiaofeng Tao, Yinwei Li, Jing Sun, Xingtong Liu, Huifang Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeRestrictive myopathy is an important subtype of thyroid eye disease (TED) characterized by ocular motility restriction. Emerging evidence has indicated brain remodeling in TED restrictive myopathy, yet the underlying mechanism remains elusive. Therefore, this study aims to investigate brain activity and extraocular muscles (EOMs) abnormalities in TED restrictive myopathy, providing a deeper insight into the relationship between ocular motility restriction and brain functional changes.Patients and MethodsThis prospective observational study recruited 45 TED patients recruited (28 with restrictive myopathy and 17 without). Brain function parameters and EOMs metrics were obtained. The two-sample t test was performed to compare intergroup differences, and correlation analysis was applied to uncover the association between brain functional activity and EOMs involvement.ResultsTED restrictive myopathy exhibited increased intraocular pressure (IOP), long diameters (LD) and short diameters (SD) of inferior rectus, and decreased visual functioning score in Graves’ orbitopathy-specific quality of life questionnaire (GO-QoL). Increased amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations (ALFF) in the left middle temporal gyrus (MTG.L) were found in TED restrictive myopathy. The functional connectivity (FC) analysis further discovered higher FC between MTG.L and the right cerebellum_8 and bilateral middle frontal gyrus (MFG). The brain imaging parameters were significantly correlated with vertical deviation, exophthalmos and EOMs metrics in TED restrictive myopathy.ConclusionThis study explored the brain alterations in TED restrictive myopathy, underscoring oculomotor restriction-associated brain functional changes. The study also serves as a preliminary exploration of the alterations in visual pathway in restrictive strabismus, represented by TED with restrictive myopathy.

Introduction

Thyroid eye disease (TED) is a common orbital inflammatory disease intimately associated with autoimmune disorder.1 TED patients frequently present with exophthalmos, upper eyelid retraction, periorbital connective tissue swelling and oculomotor dysfunction.2 Among these patients, a substantial proportion can be classified as the restrictive myopathy subtype, which is featured by extraocular muscles (EOMs) involvement.3 Epidemiological evidence suggests that approximately 43% of TED patients exhibit restrictive myopathy.4 EOMs enlargement commonly leads to strabismus, diplopia in primary gaze, and ocular motility restriction in TED restrictive myopathy.5 Orbital magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) plays a critical role in evaluating EOMs involvement and assessing disease severity in TED. Although surgeries have been applied to patients with restrictive myopathy to address the restriction of ductions, a considerable number of patients fail to regain normal binocular single vision.6 This clinical observation indicates that long-term abnormal oculomotor function and visual stimuli may induce plastic changes in the brain. The brain remodeling could in turn contribute to persistent visual dysfunction independent of EOMs involvement, suggesting the role of eye-brain crosstalk.

Restrictive myopathy in TED impairs eye movement function, primarily manifesting as strabismus and diplopia. As eye movement is modulated by sophisticated neural mechanisms integrating sensorimotor and cognitive systems, the sustained restriction of eye movement may lead to neural functional alterations.7,8 In addition, strabismus and diplopia resulting from restrictive myopathy may also contribute to brain remodeling.9,10 Studies using functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) demonstrated that strabismus patients displayed functional changes in brain areas such as precuneus, precentral gyrus, middle occipital gyrus and so on.11,12 In addition, abnormal brain activities in occipital and parietal lobes have been reported in patients with fusion dysfunction.13,14 Functional changes predominantly involve brain areas responsible for eye movement, fusion control or recognition. Collectively, existing evidence indicates the brain alterations associated with restrictive myopathy, yet there are currently no studies exploring the underlying neural mechanism.

In recent years, neuroimaging studies have shed light on TED patients as the neuropsychiatric symptoms become increasingly prominent. Multimodal MRI has documented structural and functional changes in multiple brain regions in TED, with the occipital and parietal lobes being the most affected.15–17 Additionally, distinct patterns of brain alterations have been observed in patients with dysthyroid optic neuropathy or different immunoendocrine status.17,18 However, brain alterations in restrictive myopathy, an important subtype of TED, remain unexplored. Given that oculomotor restriction and visual impairment may drive brain functional changes, this study focused on TED restrictive myopathy subtype, to delineate the associated brain functional changes.

Collectively, accumulating evidence highlights the importance of brain dysfunction in the progress of TED restrictive myopathy. Therefore, we integrated fMRI and orbital MRI, to acquire a more comprehensive understanding of brain alterations in restrictive myopathy associated with TED. Whole-brain spontaneous functional activities were firstly obtained to uncover brain regions with significant differences between TED with and without restrictive myopathy. Clinical and orbital MRI metrics were then combined to examine the relationship between brain alterations and EOMs involvement.

Materials and Methods

Participants

This prospective study has received approval from the ethics committee. The study has been performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. A total of 80 moderate-severe TED patients were prospectively recruited in this study from August 2023 to April 2024 in Shanghai Ninth People’s Hospital, based on clinical guidelines proposed by European Group On Graves’ Orbitopathy (EUGOGO) and Oculoplastic and Orbital Disease Group of Chinese Ophthalmological Society of Chinese Medical Association.19,20 Informed consent was obtained from all participants. Patients were categorized into two groups: one group with restrictive myopathy, and the other without. The restrictive myopathy subtype of TED was determined according to criteria: (1) horizontal deviation ≥ 15 prism degree (PD) or vertical deviation ≥ 10 PD; (2) presence of ocular motility restriction; (3) presence of constant diplopia.5,21,22 Patients without restrictive myopathy was characterized according to criteria: (1) horizontal deviation < 15 PD and vertical deviation < 10 PD; (2) absence of ocular motility restriction; (3) no signs of constant diplopia. Participants were excluded if they: (1) had low-quality imaging data; (2) had dysthyroid optic neuropathy, corneal ulcer or any other EOMs dysfunction; (3) lack essential clinical data or cannot be diagnosed as restrictive myopathy or non-restrictive myopathy in certainty. Finally, 28 TED patients with restrictive myopathy and 17 TED patients without restrictive myopathy were included for subsequent analyses. Two groups were well matched in sex, age and education level. Details were presented in Figure 1. All patients underwent a complete ophthalmic examination, including clinical activity score (CAS), best corrected visual acuity (BCVA), intraocular pressure (IOP), exophthalmos and near strabismus degree measured by prism test in primary gaze. A Graves’ orbitopathy-specific quality of life questionnaire (GO-QoL) was also conducted. The examiners were blind to the group information.

Imaging Data Acquisition

A combined brain and orbital imaging method was implemented on each participant. MRI examination was done using a 3.0 T scanner (Magnetom Vida, Siemens, Germany) equipped with a 64-channel phased array head coil. Subjects were instructed to close their eyes without falling asleep. Head motion and scanning noise were reduced by foam padding and earplugs. For neuroimaging, the high-resolution sagittal structural T1-weighted imaging (3D-T1WI) was applied with following parameters: repetition time = 2400 ms, echo time = 2.4 ms, thickness = 0.8 mm, gap = 0 mm, acquisition matrix = 320×320 mm2, field of view = 256×256 mm2, flip angle = 8°, number of slices = 208 and voxel size = 1 × 1×1 mm3. Functional images were obtained with following parameters: repetition time = 2000 ms, echo time = 30 ms, thickness = 2.0 mm, gap = 0 mm, acquisition matrix = 78×78 mm2, field of view = 208×208 mm2, flip angle = 90°, number of slices = 72 and voxel size = 2 × 2×2 mm3. Blood oxygen level dependent signals across 240 time points were obtained. For orbital imaging, Dixon T2-weighted images were acquired in the axial plane with parameters as follows: repetition time = 3800 ms, echo time = 94 ms, number of slices = 15, thickness = 3 mm, matrix = 256×256 mm2, field of view = 180×180 mm2 and flip angle = 160°.

Orbital Imaging Processing

The measurement of orbital imaging parameters associated with EOMs was independently conducted by two orbital radiologists with more than 10 years of experience (operator 1 with 12 years of experience in head and neck radiology; operator 2 with 10 years of experience in head and neck radiology), blinded to clinical information. EOMs were manually delineated in the coronal images. The short diameters (SD) and long diameters (LD) of inferior rectus, medial rectus, superior rectus and lateral rectus were measured at the third retrobulbar layer on coronal images. All measured imaging were reviewed by another orbital radiology expert with 20 years of experience. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was utilized to assess the consistency of measurements between the two operators and within each operator. An ICC above 0.80 indicates that inter-operator and intra-operator variability has no substantial impact on measurement results. Details are demonstrated in Figure 2. Brain Rs-fMRI Processing

All rs-fMRI data were preprocessed using DPARSFA (http://rfmri.org/DPABI) on the MATLAB platform (https://www.mathworks.com/products/matlab/).23 The first 10 functional volumes were eliminated to maintain magnetization balance and reduce interference. Slice timing and realignment for head motion correction were performed. Images were then normalized into Montreal Neurological Institute template (resampling voxel size = 3 × 3×3 mm3) by DARTEL method. A 6 mm full width at half maximum Gaussian kernel was utilized for smoothing. Detrending was applied to remove linear trends. Finally, the nuisance covariates were eliminated by linear regression, including average signals from the cerebrospinal fluid and white matter, and six head motion parameters. No participants were excluded due to their maximal head motion exceeding 3.0 mm or 3.0°. The mean framewise displacement of TED with restrictive myopathy and TED without restrictive myopathy was 0.09 and 0.083, respectively, and no significant differences were observed (p = 0.635).

The ALFF values were computed to demonstrate alterations of local brain activity. The time series of each voxel was converted to the frequency domain with a fast Fourier transform algorithm, and the averaged square root of the spectrum spanning the frequency range of 0.01–0.08 Hz was then taken as the ALFF measurement. For standardized purpose, mean ALFF was subsequently calculated as the ALFF value of each voxel divided by global mean ALFF value. On the basis of ALFF result, brain regions with significant different brain activities were identified as the seed. Then the functional connectivity (FC) analysis was conducted in a voxel-wise manner through the whole brain. Head motion scrubbing regressors [FD threshold 0.2 for “bad” time] were added in the linear regression to address the influence of motion.24,25 The correlation of time series between seed region and each voxel within the brain was computed. Fisher’s Z transformation was applied for normalization, which helps subsequent statistical analysis.

Statistical Analysis

The demographic and clinical data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10.3.1 (GraphPad, USA). We utilized Shapiro–Wilk tests to assess normality. Statistical analysis of group differences was performed as follows: for continuous variables with a Gaussian distribution, independent-samples t tests were employed. For non-Gaussian distributed continuous data, Mann–Whitney tests were employed. A p-value < 0.05 is considered as significant. Cohen’s d and its 95% confidence interval (CI) were computed using effectsize R package (version 1.0.2).

The DPABI toolbox was used to analyze mALFF and FC maps of TED patients with and without restrictive myopathy.23 Two-sample t tests were implemented to compare group differences, incorporating age and sex as covariates. The results were corrected for multiple comparison using Gaussian random field (GRF) method. The statistically significant threshold was set at voxel-level p < 0.001 and cluster-level p < 0.05, two tailed. BrainNet Viewer was used to visualize brain regions with significant difference.26

Finally, Pearson and Spearman correlation tests were performed to further examine the association of brain imaging parameters, orbital imaging metrics and clinical data, controlling for age and sex. The relationship between ALFF and FC in regions with significant group differences and parameters of orbital imaging as well as clinical characteristics was revealed. A p-value < 0.05 is considered as significant.

Correlation Analysis

In patients with restrictive myopathy, the ALFF value of MTG.L was positively correlated with vertical deviation (r = 0.455, p = 0.022). Moreover, a positive correlation was found between ALFF in MTG.L and SD of lateral rectus (r = 0.427, p = 0.042). The FC value of MTG.L with other brain areas further revealed the correlation with clinical characteristics and orbital imaging metrics. The FC value of MTG.L-CRBL8.R was negatively correlated with LD of lateral rectus (r = −0.507, p = 0.014). The FC value of MTG.L-MFG.R was positively correlated with exophthalmos (r = 0.448, p = 0.036), and negatively correlated with LD of superior rectus (r = −0.421, p = 0.046), SD of superior rectus (r = −0.522, p = 0.011), LD of lateral rectus (r = −0.437, p = 0.042). The FC value of MTG.L-MFG.L displayed a positive correlation with exophthalmos (r = 0.436, p = 0.038), and a negative correlation with SD of superior rectus (r = −0.528, p = 0.009). Discussion

Restrictive myopathy represents a highly prevalent subtype of TED, with accumulating evidence suggesting its impacts on the visual function and brain function. However, there were no studies investigating brain functional changes in patients with restrictive myopathy. Based on clinical treatment and brain research experience, our team prospectively recruited TED patients with and without restrictive myopathy for exploration, aiming to explore the abnormalities of the brain and orbit in restrictive myopathy associated with TED. The results demonstrated an increased ALFF in the MTG.L. The MTG.L was subsequently taken as the seed for FC analysis, and an increased connectivity was discovered with right cerebellum_8 and bilateral MFG. Furthermore, the correlation analyses revealed the association between ALFF value of MTG.L and vertical deviation, as well as lateral rectus metrics. The FC values of MTG.L and its functionally connected brain regions were correlated with metrics of superior rectus, lateral rectus and exophthalmos. Collectively, these findings preliminarily present abnormal spontaneous brain activities and functional connectivity associated with oculomotor restriction and fusion dysfunction.

As a common subtype of TED, restrictive myopathy reflects an autoimmune attack against the EOMs. Antibodies against EOMs were detected in restrictive myopathy patients, indicating oculomotor function impairment.2,21 Besides, localized orbital inflammation may result in EOMs fibrosis, which in turn contributes to restrictive myopathy.28 Thus, the higher-order visual function was impaired, especially the fusion function, which may further affect brain activity. Notably, restrictive strabismus can be caused by conditions such as TED, orbital fractures, and high myopia.29,30 This study focuses on TED restrictive myopathy, a representative type of restrictive strabismus, and provides preliminary evidence for future neural research in various forms of restrictive strabismus, offering additional insights into the mechanisms underlying eye-brain crosstalk.

The analyses of clinical characteristics showed TED restrictive myopathy with a higher IOP, which may be attributed to EOMs restriction.5 It’s noteworthy that for patients with strabismus, Icare tonometry may fail to align properly with the central cornea, potentially leading to unreliable IOP measurements. In this study, a non-contact air-puff measurement was implemented on patients, which provides more accurate IOP values. Besides, a significantly decreased visual functioning score in GO-QoL was demonstrated in TED restrictive myopathy, indicating impairment of visual function and decline in quality of life.

Through analysis of ALFF across the brain, an increased ALFF was identified in the MTG.L. Middle temporal neurons were found to carry binocular signals for higher-order visual processing, rendering MTG a critical brain area for encoding information about three-dimensional surface structure and retinal image velocities.31,32 A previous study revealed higher brain activation in MTG complex in intermittent exotropia when visual disparity stimuli were applied.14 Moreover, MTG exhibits an increased FC with posterior area of the right primary visual cortex in patients with strabismus, indicating a compensatory mechanism for fusional vergence.33 Besides, as a component of dorsal attention network, MTG plays a pivotal role in visuospatial attention, ocular movement and visuomotor coordination.34,35 Therefore, the increased intrinsic activities in MTG may indicate a compensatory response related to ocular motility restriction and fusion dysfunction. Besides, as MTG is implicated in anxiety and depression, the higher functional activities may be also related with emotional disorders.36,37

The cerebellum plays an important role in visual perception and sensorimotor control.38,39 It is reported that the cerebellum processes information from brain cortex, and contributes to coordination of accurate eye movement.40,41 Studies demonstrated decreased gray matter volume in strabismus patients, providing an insight into the structural underpinnings.42 Besides, abnormal functional activities were evinced in cerebellum posterior lobe, which may result from oculomotor and stereopsis dysfunction.43,44 MFG is located in frontal lobe, which integrate rich afferent signals from other parts of the brain.39,45 Moreover, MFG contains part of frontal eye field, a brain region responsible for eye movement control.46 MFG also serve as a convergence site for dorsal and ventral attention network, and the neural activities may be implicated in cognition and emotional disorders.47,48 Altered activities and abnormal microstructure in MFG were found in patients with concomitant strabismus, indicating the impairment of ocular motility and the compensation mechanism.49,50 Hence, the increased FC between MTG.L and cerebellum and bilateral MFG may suggest the compensatory activities associated with oculomotor restriction. In addition, since the cerebellum and MFG are involved in attentional regulation,51,52 spontaneous functional connectivity alterations with these brain regions may reflect changes in higher-order cognitive functions.

Our study also observed that increased LD and SD of inferior rectus in patients with restrictive myopathy, and the neuroimaging metrics were correlated with EOMs metrics primarily in lateral rectus and superior rectus. The order of EOMs involvement in TED is as follows: inferior rectus, medial rectus, superior rectus and lateral rectus.53 EOMs control ocular motility with dynamic alterations in their cross-sectional areas observed during horizontal and vertical eye movement.54–56 Previous studies revealed that patients with ocular motility restriction demonstrated higher EOMs diameters, and the diameters were correlated with motility deficiency.57,58 Thus, the increased diameters of EOMs in patients indicate a more sever restriction in ocular motility, which mainly presents as strabismus and may lead to diplopia. The correlations between EOMs diameters and brain functional metrics further illuminate the associations between restrictive myopathy and altered brain activity. We postulate that the abnormal visual stimuli might play a key role and exert persistent effects on the brain. Notably, this study includes TED restrictive myopathy patients with eye movement restriction in different directions, with a high proportion showing vertical movement restriction. While significant differences were found in the LD and SD of the inferior rectus, alterations of other EOMs necessitate further classification and investigation.

Though this study gives us more clues about the neural mechanism of TED, there are some limitations. First, as preliminary research, the study explored the brain changes in restrictive myopathy associated with TED based on a relatively small sample size, and health controls were not included. Second, given the observational design, this study cannot establish a causal relationship between restrictive myopathy and brain functional changes. Further research with longitudinal designs and multiple tests is required to validate the neural mechanism. Third, TED restrictive myopathy consists eye movement restriction in different directions, so more studies may be conducted on the basis of further classification. Besides, there are still more methods to evaluate EOMs condition, such as cross-sectional volumes, and autoantibodies against EOMs might be detected for immune-related visual dysfunction research. Last, this study provides preliminary insight into brain functional changes in restrictive myopathy associated with TED. To obtain a more comprehensive understanding of oculomotor restriction-associated brain alterations, restrictive myopathy attributed to other causes may be included in future study.

Conclusion

This exploratory study combined neuroimaging and orbital imaging to elucidate the alterations of the visual pathway in TED restrictive myopathy, providing preliminary evidence of the aberrant spontaneous brain activities and orbital MRI abnormalities. MTG.L demonstrated altered functional activities and increased connectivity with cerebellum and MFG, which correlated with oculomotor-associated metrics. These findings underscore the relationship between eye movement restriction and the abnormal brain activities, offering a deeper insight into the neural mechanism in TED. Crucially, this study also provides preliminary evidence for neural changes in restrictive strabismus, as represented by TED restrictive myopathy. Further large-scale longitudinal and controlled studies are required to validate the brain remodeling linked to oculomotor restriction.
