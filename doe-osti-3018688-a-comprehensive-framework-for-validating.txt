Executive Summary:
Accurate simulation of power-plants is essential to the planning and operation of modern
power grids. The current methods used to periodically check power-plant simulation
models have many open questions about their limitations and accuracy. The research in
this project explored using Monte-Carlo Experimentation (MCE) as a means for answering
these important questions.

MCE is the ideal process for determining the accuracy of model validation methods. A
laboratory-scale Mock Power-Plant (MPP) was designed, commissioned, and MCE was
performed on the MPP. While MCEs are practical in the laboratory setting, they are not
realistic for grid-scale power-plants that actively supply energy to consumers. This
research used the results of the MCE to aid in development of a simulation framework that
aims to mimic MCE without the need for a large set of data. The Monte-Carlo Simulation
(MCS) framework can be used to analyze active power-plant models due to the diZerences
in data requirements.

MCE requires repeatedly performing an experiment on a system, and then statistically
analyzing the combined results of each experiment. The MPP is a grid-synchronized
hardware-in-the-loop power-plant. The system is comprised of a physical generator and
prime mover along with a real-time computer that allows the system to behave like a
variety of power-plant types. The MPP also has high-speed switched load banks for
initiating repeatable transient condition experiments. This repeatability of transient events
enables successful MCE to be performed and studied. The results of MCE serve as
baseline for validating more eZicient methods, like MCS, that can be applied to actual
power-plants.

MCS is a simulation framework that attempts to recreate the results gathered from MCE
while relying on a single transient event instead of many. To make up for the reduction in
data, ambient power-plant data preceding a transient event is required. Spectral analysis
techniques are applied to the ambient data the characterize the stochastic components of
the system. Using this stochastic knowledge and the transient data, MCS bootstrap the
data into a large set of transient events, like what would be obtained through MCE.
Validating the results of MSC against the results of MCE will allow future research to study
the accuracy of modern model validation techniques and improve the reliability of power
system planning and operation. These advances are also a ﬁrst step toward verifying online

model calibration methods which could say time and money when generation owners ﬁnd
their models need to be revised.

Introduction and Background:
To maintain and operate a resilient and reliable power grid, operators perform countless
simulation studies of the grid over a continuum of operating conditions. One of the most
complex pieces of these simulations is the power plant simulation model. The model for a
single generator could be composed of models for the excitation control system, the
turbine, the governing control system, the power system stabilizer, and the electric
machine itself. All together the models can have dozens of parameters and over ten states.
The gold standard for generating these models is intensive oZline baseline testing of the
powerplant.

Once in service, the North American Electric Reliability Corporation (NERC) requires the
models to be periodically validated for accuracy [1-2]. The industry standard is to perform
model validation through play-in simulations using synchrophasor data recorded at the
output of the generator using a Phasor Measurement Unit (PMU). A play-in simulation
injects the recorded voltage and frequency data into the modeled generator and then
compares the outputs real and reactive power to the real and reactive power calculated
from the synchrophasor recording. Due to the complexity of the power plant models, the
play-in records must be large disturbance events. Small disturbances and ambient
conditions will not exercise the model enough to ensure the model is accurate for the full
range of operating conditions. Even when large disturbance data is available, relying on a
single event for model validation presents signiﬁcant scientiﬁc challenges. A single
transient event rarely excites all the dynamics of a generator to the same degree, which
leads to the problem of parameter identiﬁability.

The fundamental gap in the current standard is the inability to quantify the conﬁdence of a
validated model. When a simulation matches a set of diverse recorded events, it is often
assumed the model parameters are correct. However, due to parameter correlation and
system and measurement noise, it is possible for an incorrect set of parameters to produce
simulations that matches a speciﬁc event. Critical questions remain unanswered in
standard industry practice, such as:
 • Which parameters are adequately excited by a given event?
 • What is the quantitative accuracy of an estimated parameter?
 • What is the impact of measurement noise and PMU ﬁlter settings on validation and
 calibration?
Ideally, these questions would be answered through Monte Carlo Experimentation (MCE),
where the exact same disturbance is applied to the system repeatedly under identical
conditions. By analyzing the statistical distribution of the resulting parameter estimates,
engineers could determine the true accuracy and identiﬁability of their models.

However, conducting MCE on the actual power grid is impossible. System operators cannot
subject a commercial power plant to repeated fault conditions or line trips simply to gather
statistical data. Consequently, the industry lacks a "ground truth" dataset to verify the
eZicacy of modern automated calibration algorithms.

This research project was designed to close this gap. It hypothesized that a laboratory-
scale Mock Power-Plant (MPP) could generate the physical MCE data that is unavailable
from the grid. Furthermore, this research explores whether a measurement for the
identiﬁability of a particular model parameter could be derived with respect to a single
disturbance recording by using spectral analysis and bootstrapping techniques to create a
synthetic Monte Carlo Simulation (MCS).

Project Objectives:
Part way through the project the principal investigator changed. As part of the change the
goals and objectives of the project were reevaluated. The original research plan intended to
create the MCS framework using Bayesian inference methods. However, these methods
were found to be too computationally intensive to support practical applications in
industry. The research was pivoted to using spectral coherency methods for the MCS
framework. The following goals were set and accepted.

Goal 1.1: Obtain and catalog test data sets to include: actual system data sets, and data
from the Mock Power Plant (MPP). Speciﬁc goals for the MPP are: 1) complete construction
and commissioning of the MPP; 2) conduct IEEE-based tests to obtain/validate MPP
models; 3) develop experiments for conducting identiﬁability research.

Goal 1.2: Investigate, compare, and develop identiﬁability algorithms and approaches.

Goal 1 Status: (100% Complete) The MPP was completed and commissioned. MCEs were
performed and used in a comparative identiﬁability study.

Goal 2.1: Develop a framework and software that conducts model validation or calibration
that includes estimating the model and input parameter distributions.

Goal 2.2: Apply the software to several test cases including ambient data cases. Show how
multiple data sets may be used to complimentary validate/calibrate parameters.

Goal 2 Status: (95% Complete) A simulation framework was developed and tested. The
comparative studies between MCE, MCS, and existing methods are in progress and will be
included in R. Hruban’s Ph.D. Dissertation.

Goal 3.1: Demonstrate how extended data (e.g. exciter voltage) can be used in model
validation and its impact on the identiﬁability of model parameters.

Goal 3.2: Demonstrate how validation, calibration, and identiﬁability can be applied to
inverter-based resources (IBRs).

Goal 3 Status: (30% Complete) Extending methods to new models and data types was not
completed. Methods for incorporating extended data into the validation framework were
identiﬁed but not tested. Inverter-Based Resource (IBR) models were not tested.

Technical Approach and Results:

The Mock Power-Plant (MPP) System:

The MPP is a laboratory-sized power plant designed to test model validation concepts
along with experiments related to power system operation and controls, power system
dynamics, instrumentation and control, signal processing, and analysis of electric
machinery. One of the deﬁning features of this laboratory testbed is the ability to emulate
steam, hydro, and gas-ﬁred driven powerplants. Figure 1 is a block diagram of the system.
The generator is a 31KVA, 480VAC, synchronous generator with a rotating rectiﬁer excitation
system. A salient pole permanent magnet motor is used as the prime mover. The motor
was specially selected for its fast reaction time and ability to provide constant torque over
a wide range of speeds. These features enable the emulation of a diverse range of turbine
systems. Finally, the system is controlled by a 3.7 GHz dual core real-time computer. The
real-time computer completes the hardware-in-the-loop system by simulating the
excitation controller, the governing controller, the power system stabilizer, and turbine
model while also logging sensor data. These controllers can be implemented as the
standardized NERC models or custom models. The system runs at a 4.8kHz update rate. A
more detailed summary of the system is documented in [3] and [4].

Monte Carlo Experimentation (MCE):

Monte Carlo Experimentation (MCE) was made possible because of the MPP. The system is
grid synchronized through a series of line reactors and connected to multiple 110kW three-

 Figure 3 - One Line Diagram of MPP Circuit

phase resistive load bank (see the one line diagram in Figure 3). By programmatically
switching these load banks in and out of the circuit, repeatable transient events are
created. The disturbance events are reliably repeated, and a dataset can be created in
under an hour.

 Figure 4 - Repeated 20 kW Disturbances at Two DiBerent Power Factors

To demonstrate the capabilities of the MPP, a set of Monte Carlo Experiments (MCE) were
performed to create a baseline dataset for studying parameter identiﬁability. The
experiment consisted of repeatedly subjecting the generator to a speciﬁc transient event
while maintaining consistent operating conditions.

For one of the primary experiments, documented in [6], the MPP was synchronized to the
grid with the generator outputting 0.7 PU real power at a nominal lagging power factor of
0.99. A disturbance was created by switching a 0.65 PU resistive load at the generator
terminals for 4 cycles. This event was repeated 50 times over the course of one hour to
ensure ambient conditions remained consistent. High-speed data was recorded using an
M-class Phasor Measurement Unit (PMU) algorithm implemented on the real-time
computer.

Figure 4 shows the overlaid response of these 50 events. The tight grouping of the Real and
Reactive Power traces demonstrates the high repeatability of the MPP hardware, validating
its use as a “truth” for Monte Carlo analysis.

Once obtained, these MCE datasets allowed for the statistical analysis of model
calibration accuracy. For each of the 50 recorded events, a parameter estimation algorithm
(Levenberg-Marquardt) was used to calibrate perturbed generator model parameters
(Inertia, q-axis reactance, etc.) until the play-in simulation matched the recorded data.
Figure 5 illustrates the resulting distribution of these parameter estimates from each
dataset. A key ﬁnding from this analysis is that the parameter estimates do not always
follow a Gaussian distribution and are not the same for each dataset. This challenges the
standard assumption in many conﬁdence-interval calculation methods. Additionally, the
research explored how diZerent cost functions (ﬁtting to Complex Power vs. Current
Phasors) impacted these distributions. While both methods produced similar variances,
the choice of cost function can introduce bias into the estimated parameters.

Monte Carlo Simulation:

The core innovation of this project is the Monte-Carlo Simulation (MCS) framework,
designed to replicate the statistical insights of MCE without requiring physical repetition of
events. As detailed in the Project Objectives section, this framework was pivoted from a
Bayesian approach to a Spectral Coherency approach to reduce computational burden.

The MCS framework operates on the principle that the "noise" in a power plant's response
is not just measurement noise and load switching, but a process noise colored by the
mechanical and electrical subsystems.

The MCS framework involves three steps:

 1. Ambient Data Analysis: Spectral coherency analysis is performed on ambient PMU
 data (recorded before a transient event) to characterize the system noise.
 Coherency plots reveal which frequency components of the output are linearly
 related to the inputs and which are attributed to internal process noise.
 2. Noise Modeling: Coloring ﬁlters are designed to shape white noise so that its
 spectral properties cause the coherency of the play-in simulation to match the
 actual coherency observed in the ambient data.
 3. Monte Carlo Simulation: This colored noise is injected into the generator model
 during a play-in simulations of a single recorded transient event. By repeating this
 simulation with diZerent random seeds for the noise, a synthetic Monte Carlo
 dataset is generated.
The framework is documented in [7]. Figure 6 compares the coherency of the actual PMU
data (black) against the simulation. The red trace shows the simulation coherency with no
noise, which fails to match the system dynamics. The blue trace shows the simulation
coherency with the designed noise injection, which closely matches the spectral
characteristics of the real-world system. Validation studies to conﬁrm that the MCS
framework produces parameter estimate distributions that closely resemble those
generated by the physical MCE are being performed in R. Hruban dissertation. The results
will be able to conﬁrm that the framework can provide engineers with a quantitative
measure of parameter identiﬁability (e.g., "How much can I trust this estimate of Inertia?")
using only a single disturbance record.

 Figure 6 - Comparison of Data Coherency to Simulation Coherency (with and without injected noise)

Challenges and Deviations:
The project was markedly impacted by a fault in the input/output interface board for the
real-time computer in the MPP. In addition to a damaged channel, the record data was
dropping data samples which led to phase jumps in sinusoidal data. Figure 6 shows an
example of these phase jumps on a sinusoid reference signal that was generated by the
real-time computer and fed back into itself for measurement. Identiﬁcation of the source of

the data errors, troubleshooting the system, and repair of the real-time computer
contributed to signiﬁcant delays gathering and analyzing data. This is a major contributing
factor for the status of Goal 3.

Conclusions and Future Work:
This project successfully developed a comprehensive framework for validating power plant
models using Monte Carlo methods. The construction and commissioning of the Mock
Power-Plant (MPP) provided a unique experimental capability, allowing for the generation of
“true” Monte Carlo datasets (MCE)—a feat impossible on grid-connected commercial
units.

The research demonstrated that standard assumptions about parameter estimation,
speciﬁcally that errors are always Gaussian, are not always valid. It was also found that the
choice of cost function (ﬁtting to current phasors vs. complex power) has a measurable
impact on the bias and variance of calibrated parameters.

The Spectral Coherency MCS framework, is ready to be validated against the “true”
parameter estimate distributions generated MCE data from the MPP. This method provides
a computationally eZicient pathway for researchers to assess the reliability of model
calibration results without the need for impossible-to-get repeated physical tests.

Future Work: While the framework is being validated on synchronous machine models, the
application to Inverter-Based Resources (IBRs) remains a critical next step (Goal 3). The
theoretical basis of the spectral analysis is agnostic to the generation source, suggesting
high potential for IBR application. Future work should focus on:

 1. Testing the MCS framework on IBR models, which possess distinct noise
 characteristics compared to rotating machines.
 2. Automating the "Coloring Filter" design process, which is currently a manual tuning
 step, to allow for fully automated deployment in control centers.
 3. Quantifying the impact of using extended governor and exciter datasets on the
 identiﬁability of parameters.

 Introduction to the System
 Theoretical Background for the System
 The Montana Tech Power Plant (MTPP) was designed and created to test model
validation concepts along with power system operation and controls, power system dynamics,
instrumentation and control, signal processing, and analysis of electric machinery. The most
fundamental goal of the system was to create a laboratory testbed to emulate distinct types of
synchronous generating power plants, which include steam, hydro, and gas-fired turbines.

 Synchronous Generating Power Plant Overview

 The most basic overview of a generic synchronous power plant is shown in Figure 1.

 Figure 1: Generic Synchronous Power Plant Overview

 Figure 1 lays out the desired emulated system. A working fluid such as steam or water
enters one or more turbines via controlled valves to torque the shaft connected to the
synchronous generator. The generator typically connects to the grid or network via a step-up
transformer. An Automatic Voltage Regulator (AVR) controls the excitation current that
produces the desired voltage at the terminals. The governor controls the speed and power
according to a desired frequency-power droop characteristic.

 Hardware-in-the-Loop Overview

 To effectively emulate multiple types of synchronous generation plants, the MTPP
system implements hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) simulation methods. Hardware-in-the-loop
consists of test systems where the controller exists in the path of the feedback loop for the
control loops. For the MTPP, implementing HIL methods allow for the simulation of specific
parts of the power plant. The relation between the physical system and the real-time controller is
shown in Figure 2.

 Figure 2: HIL Implementation

 As shown in Figure 2, portions of the AVR and governor-turbine model (GTM) are
implemented within the real-time controller (RTC) along with any desired signal processing. The
benefit of representing the governer-turbine model within the RTC is that it allows the emulation
of various types of mechanical systems without changing the physical system configuration. The
physical system includes the motor, motor controller, generator, rigid shaft, power amplifier,
transducers, and grid interconnect. The RTC interacts with the physical system by performing
control and data acquisition functionality. The outputs from the RTC consist of the VRref and τmref.
The RTC samples data from the τm, ωm, VT, and IT where the voltages and currents are measured
at the terminal of the generator. The system operator has the ability to set the Vref, ωref, and Pref
from the human machine interface (HMI).

 Hardware Implementation
 The MTPP consists of several major components designed to meet the project goals. The
Major components consist of the following:

 Generator
 The carefully specified generator has characteristics of much larger units in service.
Marathon Electric manufactured the 31-kVA synchronous generator which is part of the
MAGNAPLUS series of generators with a nominal VLL of 480 VAC. The generator consists of
four salient poles with wound rotor coils. Also, the generator includes a set of 5 die-cast
amortisseur (dampening) bars per pole with end rings shorting them together in each pole to
provide the dampening. Since the motor is salient pole, it does not have iron or bars on the q-
axis. The generator connects to the PCM via a rigid shaft.

 Precision-Controlled Motor

 The PCM system consists of the motor and motor controller. An ABB variable frequency
drive (VFD) controls the 60kW permanent magnet Baldor Electric AC motor. The VFD
implements ABB’s direct torque control which controls the torque and speed directly based on
the electromagnetic state of the motor. Using the controlling variables such as motor magnetizing
flux and motor torque, the resulting drive has a fast and accurate torque response.

 Excitation System
 The generator is equipped with a field-controlled alternator-rectifier excitation system
powered by an external power source. IEEE Standard 421.5 designates this excitation system as
a Type AC1A. A controlled DC power amplifier supplies the field voltage delivered to the stator
field coils. An Acopian DC power amplifier takes an external setpoint from the RTC and delivers
the VR to the field coils.

 Transducers

 To measure the POW signals produced by the generator, Ohio Semitronics current and
voltage transducers were installed. The current transducers are hall-effect sensors with calibrated
signal conditioners to use the full input range of the RTC for the system. The voltage transducers
are calibrated high-voltage isolators which also utilize the full range of the RTC. The current and
voltage transducers respond to a change in input from 0 to 90% of the range in less than 50µs
and 80µs, respectively, with accuracies of 0.5% and 0.25% of the full-scale range. To measure
the rotary position of the system, an analog resolver with a 24-bit interface module measures the
speed of the PCM. In addition, a high-resolution 2500 pulse per revolution quadrature encoder
measures the speed of the generator. This arrangement gives speed measurements across both
sides of the physical system. The system integrates an HBM strain gauge type torque transducer.
The torque sensor is mounted between the PCM and rigid shaft. The torque sensor has an
accuracy of 0.03% with a measurement frequency range up to 6 kHz.

 System Interconnect and Disturbances

 To properly emulate a generation unit, the MTPP has the capability to synchronize with
the grid with the interconnect shown in Figure 11. The generator connects to the grid through
two three-phase series reactors which emulate transmission line impedances (𝑅 + 𝑗𝑋 and
𝑅 + 𝑗𝑋 in Figure 11).

 To create transient events, the system utilizes two programmable resistive load banks
(𝑅 and 𝑅 in Figure 11). Simplex load banks are made of statically switched resistors which
range from 135.7Ω to 5.84Ω phase-to-phase wired in a delta. This equates to 5kW to 110kW at
the rated voltage 480VLL with the ability to switch in 5kW increments. To implement high speed
switching, a set of zero-crossing solid state relays are controlled from RTC. The relays permit
half-cycle or longer faults with the ability to fault any combination of all three phases.

Typical System Operation
 The following section goes over an abbreviated version of the system operation manual
to give an overview of the typical operation of the system.

 1. Prepare system for operation by turning on power to all components, setting up
 load banks, initialize Simulink model and run RTC from HMI.
 2. Start motor from HMI interface, set both system frequency in per unit and exciter
 voltage in per unit.
 3. With generator voltage matching the grid voltage and frequency at 1 per unit, turn
 autosynching function knob to ‘Enable’.
 4. Once synching indicator shows the system is synched, change the load banks
 power setting from each one’s respective controller. Also, set the generator power
 and exciter voltage.
 5. Using the load bank controls from the HMI, switch the load banks in/out at a
 specific time interval to create transient events.
 6. Once tests are completed, turn the generator power to zero and adjust the exciter
 voltage to match the grid.
 7. After the voltage and power reach their setpoints, the system can be
 unsynchronized by turning the Autosynching function knob to the ‘Off’ position.
 8. Turn off the motor and decrease generator terminal voltage to zero before
 returning all components to pretest state.
 9. Manually save the test data from the Matlab workspace to a ‘.mat’ file.
