On the equal sum partition problem
Shlomo Hoory and Dani Kotlar

Abstract
. We consider the equal sum partition problem, motivated by distance magic graph labeling: Given n , k ∈ ℕ n,k\in\mathbb{N} such that k | ∑ i = 1 n i k\,|\sum_{i=1}^{n}i and a partition p 1 + ⋯ + p k = n p_{1}+\cdots+p_{k}=n , when is it possible to find a partition of the set { 1 , 2 , … , n } \{1,2,\ldots,n\} into k k subsets of sizes p 1 , … , p k p_{1},\dots,p_{k} , such that the element sum in each subset is the same? A known necessary condition is the slack condition , requiring that for all j j , placing the largest possible elements in the j j smallest sets yields a total sum that is at least what is needed. However, this condition is not sufficient, and known counterexamples exist. This work clarifies the boundary between solvable and unsolvable instances of the problem. We extend the list of unsolvable problem instances satisfying the slack condition by exhibiting infinite families where the n / k n/k ratio is any rational number in the interval ( 2 , 24 7 ) (2,\frac{24}{7}) , and a new criterion for unsolvability. Furthermore, we show that the slack condition is natural, as it is both necessary and sufficient for the fractional relaxation of the problem. Based on this result, we prove that the problem is solvable for the class of linear partitions, where k k is fixed, p 1 , … , p k p_{1},\ldots,p_{k} grow linearly with n n , and where the slack condition holds in a strong sense. We do this by applying a randomized rounding algorithm to a solution of the fractional relaxation of the problem and proving that the algorithm has an exponentially small failure probability.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background

A
distance magic labeling

[
12
]
(or
sigma labeling

[
5
]
) of a simple undirected graph with
n
vertices is a bijective assignment of the numbers in
[n]=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
to its vertices, such that the sum of the values assigned to the neighbors of each vertex is the same.

Challenges involving distance-magic labelings, such as deciding whether one exists, and constructing one when it does, are closely tied to questions about partitioning the set
[n]
. A classic example is the following: Given positive integers
n
and
k
with
k
dividing
\sum_{i=1}^{n}i
, can
[n]
be partitioned into
k
subsets whose element sums are all equal? Straight and Schillo
[
13
]
showed that the answer is yes. But what happens when the sizes of these subsets are prescribed? This is the subject of our work.

Problem 1.1

(
[
3
]
Problem 6.3 and
[
7
]
, Problem 1.1)
.

Let
n,k
and
p_{1},\dots,p_{k}
be positive integers such that
p_{1}+\cdots+p_{k}=n
and
k
divides
\sum_{i=1}^{n}i
. When is it possible to find a partition of the set
[n]
into
k
subsets of sizes
p_{1},\dots,p_{k}
, respectively, such that the sums of the elements in each subset are equal?

Problem
1.1
was shown to be equivalent to problems of finding a distance magic labeling of complete multipartite graphs (Miller, Rodger and Simanjuntak
[
12
]
, Cichacz and Gőrlich
[
7
]
), and of graphs obtained by replacing each vertex in
C_{k}
by a clique, with a slightly modified definition of distance magic labeling (Anholcer, Cichacz and Peterin
[
3
]
). For more results and surveys on distance magic labeling, see
[
2
]
,
[
4
]
, and
[
9
]
.

A clearly necessary condition for the existence of a partition as described in Problem
1.1
is that, for every
j
, if we place the largest available elements into the
j
smallest parts, the resulting total must be at least as large as the required sum, possibly with some additional “slack”. We refer to this requirement as
the slack condition
(see Definition
1.6
).

It was shown by Miller, Rodger and Simanjuntak
[
12
]
and Beena
[
5
]
that the slack condition is sufficient when
k=2,3
. Kotlar
[
10
]
extended the result to
k=4
and dared to conjecture (as did Arumugam, Froncek, Dalibor and Kamatchi
[
4
]
) that this holds in general. However, Ebrahem Hoory and Kotlar
[
8
]
exhaustively checked all instances of Problem
1.1
with
n\leq 200
, and identified two counterexample structures. Examples
2.8
and
2.9
illustrate these structures.

In this paper, following the approach taken in two earlier works
[
10
,
8
]
, we study Problem
1.1
independently of the graph‑labeling questions related to it, as we think the problem is intrinsically interesting.

1.2.
Definitions and notation

For a positive integer
n
a
composition
of
n
is a sequence of positive integers
P=[p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{k}]
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}=n
.
Given a composition of
n
so that
k
divides
\sum_{i=1}^{n}i
, we would like to know whether it is possible to find a partition of the set
[n]
into
k
subsets of sizes
p_{1},\dots,p_{k}
, respectively, such that the sum of the elements in each subset is the same.
There is a necessary condition for this. To introduce it, we need some more definitions:

Notation 1.2
.

Given a composition
[p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{k}]
of
n
, where
k
divides
\sum_{i=1}^{n}i
, we denote the target sum for partitioning
[n]
into
k
equal sum parts by

s^{n,k}=\frac{n(n+1)}{2k}.

Definition 1.3
.

Let
P=[p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{k}]
be a non-descending composition of a positive integer
n
. For
j=1,\ldots,k
let
P_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{j}p_{i}
. For
j=1,\ldots,k-1
let

\textrm{slack}_{j}(P)=\sum_{i=1}^{P_{j}}(n-i+1)-js^{n,k}.

We denote the slack of
P
by

\textrm{slack}(P)=\min_{1\leq j<k}\textrm{slack}_{j}(P).

Remark 1.4
.

We do not include
\textrm{slack}_{k}(P)
in the minimum expression, as it is always 0.

Observation 1.5

(
[
3
]
)
.

Let
P=[p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{k}]
be a non-descending composition of a positive integer
n
. If it is possible to partition the set
[n]
into
k
equal-sum subsets of sizes
p_{1},\dots,p_{k}
, then
\textrm{slack}(P)\geq 0
.

Definition 1.6
.

We call the condition
{\textrm{s}lack}(P)\geq 0

the slack condition
. If this condition holds for
P
, we say that
P
satisfies the slack condition.

As satisfying the slack condition is a necessary condition for the existence of a partition of the set
[n]
into
k
equal-sum subsets of sizes given by
P
, we make it part of the definition:

Definition 1.7
.

A triple
(n,k,P)
is an instance of the Equal Sum Partition Problem (ESPP instance, for short), if
n
and
k
are positive integers such that
k
divides
\frac{n(n+1)}{2}
, and
P=[p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{k}]
is a non-descending composition of
n
satisfying the slack condition.
We say that an ESPP-instance
(n,k,P)
is solvable if it is possible to partition the set
[n]
into subsets of sizes
p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{k}
respectively, so that the sum of the elements in each set is the same.

Remark 1.8
.

Since the composition
P
in an ESPP
(n,k,P)
contains the information about
n
and
k
, we shall sometimes use
P
instead of the triple
(n,k,P)
to refer to an ESPP instance. For example, we will say that
P
is solvable, meaning that the ESPP instance
(n,k,P)
is solvable.

Notation 1.9
.

Let
(n,k,P)
be an ESPP instance. The notation
P=[q_{1}^{e_{1}},q_{2}^{e_{2}},\ldots,q_{t}^{e_{t}}]
means that
q_{1}<q_{2}<\cdots<q_{t}
and for
i=1,\ldots,t
,
P
has
e_{i}
parts of size
q_{i}
. Each
q_{i}^{e_{i}}
is a block, or the
q_{i}
-block.

1.3.
Our results

In this work, we expand the boundary of what is known about ESPP instances in both directions: non-solvable and solvable.
For the non‑solvable direction, we show a new criterion for unsolvability of ESPP instances (
2.11
).
Additionally, based on a criterion from
[
8
]
, infinitely more unsolvable instances are provided by the following theorem:

Theorem 1.10
.

For any rational number
2<a<\frac{24}{7}
, there is an infinite family of ESPP instances
(n,k,P_{k})
, where
a=n/k
, that are not solvable.

For the solvable direction, we analyze linear families
\mathcal{P}_{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}}
, each consisting of instances of the form
(n,k,P_{n}=[\alpha_{1}n,\ldots,\alpha_{k}n])
, where
[\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}]
is a non-descending sequence of positive rational numbers summing to 1, and satisfying a strengthened version of the slack condition (Definition
4.1
). Using a randomized rounding algorithm (Algorithm
2
), we construct an equal‑sum partition for any such instance, and our main result is:

Theorem 1.11
.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}}
be a linear partition family.
Then, there exist
\delta>0
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
so that for every
n\geq N
, the probability that Algorithm
2
fails to solve
(n,k,P_{n})\in\mathcal{P}_{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}}
is at most
e^{-\delta n}
.

The randomized rounding algorithm is based on our solution (
Algorithm

1
) to a version of the fluid mixing problem in operations research. This problem arises as a fractional version of the equal-sum partition problem. We also define an analogue of the slack property, which coincides with the usual slack in the integral case. We do not describe this problem here, as its definition is elaborate, but we state the main result in this context:

Theorem 1.12
.

An instance of the fluid mixing problem is solvable if and only if it satisfies the fractional slack condition.

2.
Non-solvable ESPP instances

In this section, we study families of non-solvable ESPP instances. In the first part, we introduce the useful notion of an
incomplete ESPP instance
. In the second part, we introduce a new criterion for unsolvability. In the third part, we show that a criterion from a previous work (
[
8
]
, Criterion 1) defines infinite families of such instances.

2.1.
Incomplete ESPP instances

It is possible to identify the unsolvability of some ESPP instances by looking at a prefix of the corresponding nondescending composition of
n
. For this, we need some new notions.

Definition 2.1
.

An incomplete non-descending composition of a positive integer
n
is a non-descending sequence of positive integers
Q=[p_{1},\ldots,p_{l}]
such that
k<l
and

(2.1)

n-\sum_{i=1}^{l}p_{i}\geq(k-l)p_{l}

A non-descending composition
P
of
n
is a completion of an incomplete non-descending composition

if
Q
is a prefix of
P
.

Proposition 2.2

(
[
8
]
, Lemma 6.3)
.

Any incomplete non-descending composition of a positive integer
n
has a completion.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
n
and
k
be positive integers such that
s^{n,k}
is an integer, and let
Q=[p_{1},\ldots,p_{l}]
be an incomplete non-descending composition of
n
. We define
\textrm{slack}_{j}(Q)

, as in Definition
1.3
, and
\textrm{slack}(Q)=\min_{1\leq j\leq l}\textrm{slack}_{j}(Q)
.

Note that while the minimum expression for
\textrm{slack}(P)
when

is a non-descending composition of

, the minimum for
\textrm{slack}(Q)
, when

is an incomplete non-descending composition, includes

.

A slightly different version of the following lemma appears in
[
8
]
(Lemma 6.4) for a non-descending composition. We state it here for an incomplete non-descending composition. We omit the proof, as it is the same as the one in
[
8
]
.

Lemma 2.4
.

In the setup of Definition
2.3
, the value of
\textrm{slack}(Q)=\min_{1\leq j\leq l}\textrm{slack}_{j}(Q)
is obtained at

p_{j}
is the last part in

Definition 2.5
.

An
incomplete instance of an equal sum partition problem
(incomplete ESPP instance) is a triple
(n,k,Q)
, where
n
and
k
are positive integers such that
s^{n,k}
is an integer,
Q
is a non-descending incomplete composition of

, and
\textrm{slack}(Q)\geq 0
.

Definition 2.6
.

Given an incomplete ESPP instance
I=(n,k,Q)
, a completion of
I
to an ESPP instance is an ESPP instance
(n,k,\mathcal{P})
, such that
P
is a completion of
Q
.

Note that by Proposition
2.2
, any incomplete ESPP instance has a completion.

We quote
Criterion 1
from
[
8
]
:

Proposition 2.7

(
[
8
]
, Theorem 4.2)
.

Let
I=(n,k,[2^{e},d^{f}])
be an incomplete ESPP instance, where
e,f>0
and
d\geq 3
. Let
C=\{x\in[n]:x\geq s^{n,k}-n\}
,
c=s^{n,k}-n
, and
h=|C|-2e=2(n-e)-s^{n,k}+1
. If

(a)

f>h
, and

(b)

\sum_{i=c-d(f-h)}^{c-1}i<(f-h)s^{n,k}
,

then any completion of
I
to an ESPP instance is not solvable.

The idea of this criterion is that any attempt to find a partition of
[n]
with equal sums runs out of ‘big’ numbers, namely elements of the set
C
, so that it is impossible to satisfy the remaining parts because of what we loosely call the “slack without
C

and

.

Example 2.8

(
[
8
]
)
.

Any completion of the incomplete ESPP instance
I=(39,13,[2^{9},3^{2}])
is not solvable.

Reason.

It can be verified that
I
is indeed an incomplete ESPP instance and that

. Using the notation of Proposition

, we have

,

and

. So, clearly (a) holds. The sum in (b) is

be a completion of
[2^{9},3^{2}]

[39]
containing nine parts of size 2 and two parts of size 3 so that all its parts’ sums are 60. The parts of size 2 must contain the numbers

, excluding 30, in pairs of sum 60. On the other hand, any part of size

must contain at least one number greater than

∎

The unsolvability of the incomplete ESPP instance in the next example follows from
Criterion 2
in
[
8
]

Example 2.9

(
[

)

Any completion of the incomplete ESPP instance

Reason.

It can be verified that

Suppose that there is an equal sum partition of

, where

Since

However,

∎

2.2.
A criterion for unsolvability

We introduce a new criterion for the unsolvability of an ESPP instance.

Notation 2.10
.

For a set of numbers
A
and a positive integer
l\leq|A|
, we denote by
\textrm{maxSum}(l,A)
the sum of the
l
largest elements in
A
.
For two integers
a<b
we write
[a,b]
for the set
\{a,a+1,\ldots,b\}
.

Theorem 2.11
.

Let
I=(n,k,p=[2^{d},\ldots])
be an ESPP instance. Let
u=2n-s^{n,k}+1-2d
,
e=\lceil(u+1)/2\rceil
and
m=s^{n,k}-n-1
.
Then,
I
is not solvable if one of the following conditions holds for some
1\leq i\leq k-d-u
:

(I)

\textrm{maxSum}(\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{i}p_{d+u+i^{\prime}},[m])<is^{n,k}

(II)

\textrm{maxSum}(\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{i}p_{d+u+i^{\prime}},[m])=is^{n,k}

and
\textrm{maxSum}(p_{d+e}-1,[m-\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{i}p_{d+u+i^{\prime}}])<s^{n,k}/2
.

Proof.

Assume for contradiction that (I) or (II) holds for some
1\leq i\leq k-d-u
, and there is a partition
\mathcal{P}
of
[n]
such that the sum in each part is
s^{n,k}
.
Assume
\mathcal{P}=[A_{1},A_{2},\ldots,A_{k}]
, where each
A_{i}
has size
p_{i}
.
Let
C=[s^{n,k}-n,n]
and let
C^{\prime}
be the subset of
C
consisting of those elements that do not appear in the pairs. Since the
d
pairs contain only elements of
C
, we have
|C^{\prime}|=u
.
Let
J
be the set of indices in
[d+1,d+u+i]
such that
A_{j}\cap C^{\prime}=\emptyset
.
Since
|\{j\in[d+1,d+u+i]:A_{j}\cap C^{\prime}\neq\emptyset\}|\leq|C^{\prime}|=u
, we have
|J|\geq(u+i)-u=i
. Let
J_{1}\subseteq J
be any subset of size
i
.
Denoting by
S(A)
the sum of the elements in the set
A
, we have

(2.2)

is^{n,k}=\sum_{j\in J_{1}}S(A_{j})\leq\textrm{maxSum}(\sum_{j\in J_{1}}p_{j},[m])\leq\textrm{maxSum}(\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{i}p_{d+u+i^{\prime}},[m])\leq is^{n,k}

The first inequality holds since the
A_{j}
,
j\in J_{1}
, consist only of elements of
[m]
, but not necessarily the biggest ones. The second inequality follows from the fact that the
p_{t}
are non-descending. The third inequality combines (I) and (II).
If (I) holds, the third inequality in (
2.2
) is strict, and we have a contradiction.
If (II) holds, by (
2.2
), we have that all subsets of size
i
of
J
are the same, so
|J|=i
, and we may assume that
J=[d+u+1,d+u+i]
. So, the sets
A_{d+u+1},\ldots,A_{d+u+i}
contain the
\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{i}p_{d+u+i^{\prime}}
largest elements of
[m]
and each of the sets
A_{d+1},\ldots,A_{d+u}
contains exactly one element of
C^{\prime}
, and all the rest of the elements in these sets are from
[m-\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{i}p_{d+u+i^{\prime}}]
.

Since the set
C^{\prime}
consists of pairs of elements
x,s^{n,k}-x
, where
x<s^{n,k}/2
, and possibly
s^{n,k}/2
itself (if
s^{n,k}
is even), at least half the elements in
C^{\prime}
are less than or equal to
s^{n,k}/2
, and thus, at least one of the sets
A_{d+1},\ldots,A_{d+e}
contains, as its

element, a number less than
s^{n,k}/2
. Suppose it is
A_{u+l}
,
l\leq e
. Let
s_{l}
be the sum of
A_{u+l}
’s non-
C^{\prime}
elements. Then,
s_{l}>s^{n,k}/2
. On the other hand,
s_{l}\leq\textrm{maxSum}(p_{d+l}-1,[m-\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{i}p_{d+u+i^{\prime}}])\leq\textrm{maxSum}(p_{d+e}-1,[m-\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{i}p_{d+u+i^{\prime}}])<s^{n,k}/2
, by (II) - a contradiction.
∎

Case (I) of this criterion is an extension of Criterion 1 in
[
8
]
(Proposition
2.7
here) and is in fact the version that was used for the search in

, although it is not stated there in this form.

2.3.
Infinite families of non-solvable ESPP instances

In this subsection, we use Proposition
2.7
to show that for each

there are infinitely many unsolvable ESPP instances with

. These instances have the form
(ak,k,[2^{uk},d^{vk},\ldots])
, where

and

are positive integers, and

.

We restate Theorem
1.10
:

Theorem

.

For any rational number
2<a<\frac{24}{7}
, there is an infinite family of ESPP instances
(n,k,P_{k})
, where

Before delving into the question of realizing such a family, the following observation gives simple upper and lower bounds on

Observation 2.12

Any ESPP instance of the form
(ak,k,P)

.

To prove the lower bound on

.
The upper bound on

Therefore, we have

, implying the required bound.
∎

,

is an incomplete ESPP inatance, and

,

, and

.

From now on, we refer to these as “Property (1)”, “Property (2)”, etc.

The proof proceeds as follows: Lemma

3.
A fractional version of the equal sum partition problem

In this section, we explore a fractional version of the equal sum partition problem, and obtain that in this case being solvable and satisfying the slack condition are equivalent. This problem is a special case of a family of classic linear programming problems, known as
pooling problems
,
blending problems
, or
fluid mixing problems
(as we shall refer to them here).
Introductions to these problems and their different solutions can be found in many textbooks on linear programming and operations research, two of which are
[
11
]
and
[
14
]
.
One possible physical interpretation of a fluid mixing problem is as follows:
Given
n
containers full of different liquids, where container
i
has volume
u_{i}>0
and mass
a_{i}
,
is it possible to pour the liquids into
k
empty containers, where container
j
has volume
v_{j}>0
so that its mass will be
b_{j}
?
Formally:

Definition 3.1
.

The 4-tuple
\pi=(\overline{a},\overline{u},\overline{b},\overline{v})
is an instance of the Fluid Mixing Problem with
n
source containers and
k
target containers if

\overline{a}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}
,
\overline{u}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n}
,

\overline{b}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{k}
,
\overline{v}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{k}

and the following conservation equalities hold:

(3.1)

\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}b_{j}\qquad\mbox{ and }\qquad\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}v_{j}.

Furthermore, we assume that the source and target containers are ordered in descending order by density, that is,

a_{1}/u_{1}\geq\cdots\geq a_{n}/u_{n}
and
b_{1}/v_{1}\geq\cdots\geq b_{k}/v_{k}
.

Remark 3.2
.

For technical reasons we allow
u_{1}=a_{1}=0
and/or
v_{1}=b_{1}=0
.

Definition 3.3
.

We say that an instance
\pi=(\overline{a},\overline{u},\overline{b},\overline{v})
of the Fluid Mixing Problem is
solvable
, if there exist a solution
X=[x_{i,j}]_{i\in[n],j\in[k]}
such that:

(3.2)

\displaystyle x_{i,j}

\displaystyle\geq 0

\displaystyle\textrm{for all }i\in[n]\textrm{ and }j\in[k],

(3.3)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}x_{i,j}

\displaystyle=1

\displaystyle\textrm{for all }i\in[n],

(3.4)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}x_{i,j}

\displaystyle=v_{j}

\displaystyle\textrm{for all }j\in[k],

(3.5)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}x_{i,j}

\displaystyle=b_{j}

\displaystyle\textrm{for all }j\in[k].

The solution matrix
X=[x_{i,j}]_{i\in[n],j\in[k]}
is sometimes referred to in the literature as a
transfer plan
.

Adopting the physical interpretation of the problem described above, each of the variables
x_{i,j}
represents the portion of container
i
that is poured into container
j
.

The fluid mixing problem includes the fractional relaxation of the equal sum partition problem
1.1
.
Indeed, given an ESPP instance
(n,k,P=[p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}])
, we set
a_{i}=n-i+1
,
u_{i}=1
,
b_{j}=s^{n,k}
and
v_{j}=p_{j}
.
If one also adds the integrality constraint
x_{i,j}\in\{0,1\}
, this is exactly the equal sum partition problem.
Furthermore, the following definition of slack generalizes the previous definition to the more general problem.
For the equal sum partition problem, the definition coincides with Definition
1.3
of
\textrm{slack}(P)
.

Definition 3.4

(Fractional Slack)
.

Let
\pi=(\overline{a},\overline{u},\overline{b},\overline{v})
be an instance of the Fluid Mixing Problem. Define
U_{0}=V_{0}=0
, and for
1\leq i\leq n
and
1\leq j\leq k

Define
U_{i}=\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{i}u_{i^{\prime}}
and
V_{j}=\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{j}v_{j^{\prime}}
.
Then:

\displaystyle\textrm{slack}(\pi)

\displaystyle=\min_{1\leq l\leq k-1}\textrm{slack}_{l}(\pi),\;\;\;\textrm{where}

\displaystyle\textrm{slack}_{l}(\pi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{a_{i}}{u_{i}}\cdot\mu_{i,l}-\sum_{j=1}^{l}b_{j},\;\;\;\textrm{where}

\displaystyle\mu_{i,l}

\displaystyle=\max(0,\min(u_{i},V_{l}-U_{i-1})).

Furthermore, if
u_{i}=0
, then necessarily
\mu_{i,l}=0
and the summand
\frac{a_{i}}{u_{i}}\cdot\mu_{i,l}
in the expression for
\textrm{slack}_{l}(\pi)
is regarded as zero.

We shall refer to the condition
\textrm{slack}(\pi)\geq 0
as
satisfying the slack condition
. It is equivalent to the condition of
majorization
appearing in the literature (see
[
11
]
).
It is a known classical result that majorization (or satisfiability of the slack condition) guarantees that the fluid mixing problem is solvable via a greedy algorithm.
However, for the sake of the next section, we need an algorithm (Algorithm
1
) that guaranties the property detailed in Lemma
3.26
.
Theorems
3.5
and
3.6
correspond to the two directions of Theorem
1.12
. Moreover, the proof of Theorem
3.6
establishes the correctness of Algorithm
1
.

Theorem 3.5
.

A solvable instance
\pi
of the fluid mixing problem satisfies the slack condition.

Proof.

Given an instance
\pi=(\overline{a},\overline{u},\overline{b},\overline{v})
of the fluid mixing problem with the solution
x_{i,j}
,
we need to prove that
\textrm{slack}_{l}(\pi)\geq 0
for all

.
Given

M_{i,l}=\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{i}\mu_{i^{\prime},l}
.
So, if
U_{i}\leq V_{l}
then
\mu_{i^{\prime},l}=u_{i^{\prime}}
for all

.
Otherwise,

and let

Therefore:

(3.6)

Given the solution

\displaystyle A_{i,l}

Using (

\displaystyle A_{i,l}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\min\!\!\left(\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{i}u_{i^{\prime}},\sum_{j=1}^{l}v_{j}\right)=\min(U_{i},V_{l})=M_{i}.

Then, applying (

\displaystyle\textrm{slack}_{l}(\pi)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{a_{i}}{u_{i}}\cdot\mu_{i,l}-\sum_{j=1}^{l}b_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{a_{i}}{u_{i}}\cdot\mu_{i,l}-\sum_{j=1}^{l}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}x_{i,j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{a_{i}}{u_{i}}\cdot\mu_{i,l}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{a_{i}}{u_{i}}\left(u_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{l}x_{i,j}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{a_{i}}{u_{i}}\cdot(\mu_{i,l}-\alpha_{i,l}).

The next step is to define

for

Note that

for all

Then:

\displaystyle\textrm{slack}_{l}(\pi)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{a_{i}}{u_{i}}\cdot(\mu_{i,l}-\alpha_{i,l})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{i^{\prime}=i}^{n}\Delta_{i^{\prime}}\right)\cdot(\mu_{i,l}-\alpha_{i,l})

\displaystyle\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}\Delta_{i^{\prime}}\sum_{i=1}^{i^{\prime}}(\mu_{i,l}-\alpha_{i,l})=\sum_{i^{\prime}=i}^{n}\Delta_{i^{\prime}}(M_{i^{\prime},l}-A_{i^{\prime},l})\geq 0,

as claimed.

4.
The Linear Partition Problem

In this section we go back to the original partition problem and show that when the part sizes
p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}
are linear functions of

and satisfy a stronger version of the slack condition, the problem is solvable for a sufficiently large

The solution is based on applying randomized rounding to the fractional solution of the linear problem presented in the previous section and showing that the imperfection introduced by the rounding can be mended with high probability by reassigning a small number of elements.

Definition 4.1

Let

Remark 4.3

Recall that

5.
Discussion

Originally motivated by graph-labeling problems, this paper investigates Problem
1.1
: the partitioning of
[n]
into equal-sum sets of prescribed sizes.
We term an instance of this problem
ESPP-instance
(Equal Size Partition Problem).

As demonstrated in Section
3
, the
slack condition
, which is the natural necessary condition for solvability of an ESPP-instance, is also a sufficient condition for the fractional relaxation of the problem. This provides a link to diverse fields, including operations research.
While the slack condition is known to be sufficient to guarantee a solution when the number of parts is at most four
[
5
,
10
]
, recent work
[
8
]
has shown this does not hold in general; there exist instances of the problem that are not solvable, despite satisfying the slack condition
[
8
]
.

In this work, we approach this discrepancy from two different angles. First, we prove that the class of linear equal-sum partition problem families satisfying a strengthened version of the slack condition is solvable for sufficiently large n. Furthermore, we provide a randomized algorithm for finding such partitions that fails with only exponentially small probability. Second, we identify new non-solvable families of ESPP-instances, further clarifying the boundary of the problem.

Although a complete characterization remains elusive, and we conjecture the general problem to be NP-complete, our results suggest several promising paths forward. We conclude with a list of open problems and directions for future investigation:

•

All currently known examples of non-solvable ESPP instances contain (many) parts of size 2. Since our search has not produced so far non‑solvable ESPP instances such that the smallest part is of size at least 3, we propose two contrasting avenues for further investigation: either prove that ESPP instances are solvable when the partition contains no parts of size 2, or develop criteria for non‑solvability that apply to such instances, and for which the corresponding family of examples is non‑empty. The criteria introduced in
[
8
]
for partitions whose smallest parts have size 2 may offer useful insight in this direction.

•

Define conditions that would constitute a sufficient set of conditions for solvability, or

•

Demonstrate that the general version of Problem
1.1
is NP‑complete. The two criteria established in
[
8
]
, together with the additional criterion in Subsection
2.2
, indicate that the problem is more intricate than previously assumed. In particular, they highlight the inadequacy of the earlier, overly optimistic conjecture that the slack condition alone would suffice.

•

Extend the result of Section
4
to other families of partitions. For example, when
k
grows with
n
, or when the sizes of the parts are not linear in
n
but rather
o(n)
.

•

Generalize Problem
1.1
and the slack condition to an arbitrary set of numbers, instead of just
[n]
, or to the case where the sums of the parts are some given values that are not necessarily equal. For the latter case, the situation involving undetermined part sizes was treated in
[
6
]
.
