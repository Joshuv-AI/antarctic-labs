Executive Summary
Quantum computing stands at the threshold of a transformative decade, where the field will evolve
from small-scale demonstrations toward practical scientific computing at scale. This Blueprint
identifies fault-tolerant quantum computers (FTQCs) as a viable, scalable, and broadly applicable
path to achieving “quantum scientific utility,” defined as solving scientifically valuable problems
beyond the reach of conventional, classical computers. This capability is expected to show
scientific demonstrations in the late 2020s and to mature in the early-to-mid 2030s. This Blueprint
outlines a strategy to prepare the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) for FTQCs and their
integration into the U.S. national scientific computing infrastructure. Its purpose is to identify the
steps, milestones, and research directions necessary for DOE to enable initial deployment of
FTQCs in 2028 as a scientific tool for the nation and mature this capability into the 2030s.

DOE has a long history of supporting quantum information science and technology, contributing
significantly to research advancements, training a quantum-ready workforce, and providing access
to early small-scale quantum hardware. Given recent demonstrations of logical operations on error-
corrected logical qubits and the advancement of commercial hardware roadmaps, DOE should
begin preparations for large-scale, fault-tolerant quantum computing deployment for DOE science
missions.

This Blueprint proposes that DOE focus on (1) deploying first-generation scientifically relevant
quantum computers with at least 100 logical qubits and performing at least 10,000 to 100,000 hard
logical operations1 in scientifically relevant calculations; (2) developing essential FTQC
programming competencies, system software, and facility readiness; and (3) investing in cutting-
edge focused R&D that fosters breakthroughs in scientific applications, algorithms, and logical
architectures needed to accelerate the advent of scientific utility. This effort will position DOE to
transition to larger systems: production-scale quantum computers that comprise 1,000 to 10,000
logical qubits, perform 1 to 10 billion hard logical operations, and execute scientifically useful
computations at scale.

Achieving these goals will require DOE facilities to evolve with urgency to support scientific
campaigns that integrate quantum and classical computing resources into efficient workflows,
novel software and firmware environments for compiling and routing quantum programs on FTQC
machines, and suitable infrastructure for quantum hardware. It will also require further
development and optimization of scientific applications from the fields of materials science,
quantum chemistry, and high-energy and nuclear physics. The Blueprint calls for transformative
R&D and collective action to accelerate the advent of scientific quantum utility and bring it within
reach by 2028.

1. Introduction
 To position the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) for global leadership in quantum computing for scientific
 discovery, it is important to urgently develop and deploy a mission-driven quantum computing capability and
 ecosystem. This initiative requires defining and aligning DOE applications with co-designed computer architecture,
 software, facilities, and resource roadmaps that lay the foundation now for initial capability deployment by 2028.

Quantum computation promises the ability to solve certain computational problems—for example,
simulating and calculating properties of native quantum physical systems such as molecules,
materials, and quantum fields—exponentially faster than any conventional computer. Harnessing
this transformative capability aligns with DOE’s mission to lead the world in scientific discovery.
For decades, DOE and its 17 national laboratories have incubated, created, hosted, and used the
world’s most capable supercomputers and facilities to push scientific frontiers and solve science
problems beyond the reach of pure theory and observational experiments. Building on its global
leadership in deploying numerous first-of-their-kind supercomputers, DOE is poised to deliver
era-defining quantum supercomputing capabilities. This Blueprint presents the motivation, the
justification, and a high-level plan for DOE to drive an effort to establish quantum computing
capabilities in concert with its existing support for strategic quantum computing R&D.
Scientifically relevant quantum computers (SRQCs) will be unique in their requirements for
housing, operation, and programming. It is critical for DOE to prepare for their advent now with
the following actions:

 1. Accelerate national competencies for SRQCs and production-scale quantum computers
 (PSQCs), analogous to the United States’ world-leading supercomputing ecosystem:

 a. Create expertise in mapping scientific applications to SRQCs and co-design SRQC
 architectures to implement them; grow expertise and the nation’s workforce more
 generally to support and use SRQCs.

 b. Invest in toolchains to automate large-scale compilation of algorithms to fault-tolerant
 quantum computers (FTQCs).

 c. Develop standardized assessments and benchmark frameworks for FTQCs.

 2. By 2028, deploy SRQCs that implement a complete and programmable suite of fault-
 tolerant logical operations; enable at least 104 to 105 hard logical operations—such as T or
 Toffoli gates—on at least 100 logical qubits to validate the promise and viability of FTQCs;
 and demonstrate scaling2.

This set of priorities and the accompanying timeline are consistent with projections for
improvement in best-in-class quantum algorithms and logical architectures and with roadmaps of
leading quantum computing hardware companies.

2. The Landscape of Quantum Computation
 The most promising quantum computing paradigm for scientific problems is fault-tolerant quantum computing,
 which processes logical qubits encoded into quantum error correction (QEC) codes. This will be implemented on
 special-purpose computing machines that are expected to coexist and cooperate with leadership-class high-
 performance computing (HPC) systems on scientific campaigns.

A wide range of paradigms for SRQCs have been proposed. In the noisy intermediate-scale
quantum (NISQ) era, non-fault-tolerant paradigms such as adiabatic evolution, annealing, analog
simulation, variational computing, and bare physical computation were considered. Although
several of these paradigms have the potential to enable some novel domain-science discoveries
[Kim2023, Alam2025a, Alam2025b], FTQCs are the most promising approach for providing
scalable solutions to science problems in multiple domains that are well beyond the reach of

 2

classical supercomputers [DOE2024]. This report therefore focuses on the prospect of FTQC
architectures (also called logical architectures) and machines and on the algorithms that run
efficiently on them.

2.1 Fault-Tolerant Quantum Computers for Science
FTQCs are a rapidly emerging technology. The first demonstrations of (tiny) end-to-end fault-
tolerant computations, according to the rubric proposed by Gottesman almost 10 years ago
[Gottesman2016], appeared in 2024–25 [Reichardt2024a, Reichardt2024b, Bluvstein2025]. More
critically, all the key components that will enable large-scale FTQC technology—error-corrected
logical qubits [Google2025a], transversal logical gates [Egan2021, Paetznick2024], and “hard”
nontransversal logical operations [Postler2022, Dasu2025]—have been demonstrated. These point
to the feasibility of increasingly large FTQC computations in the coming years. Extensive theory
and design have led to roadmaps for how to develop hardware to a scale sufficient for PSQCs.

Two widely used metrics for an FTQC are the number of logical qubits it provides and the number
of hard logical operations that it can perform before accumulating substantial computational
errors. We provide estimates of how many logical qubits and hard logical operations are sufficient
to perform the core computational tasks for several exemplar science challenges in Section 3. In
Section 4, we assess the resource requirements for these given the projected capabilities of
quantum hardware over the next 5+ years.

2.2 Synergy Between Quantum and Classical Supercomputers
As currently envisioned, quantum computing will be a specialized technology that will provide
transformative advantages on specific classes of computational problems that impact DOE’s
mission. Scientific campaigns that resolve grand open challenges—such as “How does high-Tc
superconductivity work?”, “What is the nature of transition-metal catalysts?”, and “How do quarks
and gluons dynamically form the matter we observe?”—with the help of quantum computation
will also rely heavily on conventional HPC, a powerful resource in its own right. This report is
motivated by a vision of quantum and classical supercomputers coexisting and working together
on integrated scientific campaigns.

As large FTQCs become available, at least three modes of joint operation with HPC resources are
conceivable:

 • Independent: A quantum algorithm is executed on an FTQC. Its inputs and/or outputs may
 be pre- or postprocessed by an HPC system offline.

 • Loosely coupled: A classical control program delegates subroutines to FTQC and HPC
 resources, whose inputs are defined by conditional logic using results of prior tasks. The
 FTQC-HPC interfaces occur at relatively low latency/bandwidths. FTQC and HPC
 resources may be accessible through a common API and may only require
 latency/bandwidth consistent with cloud access.

 3

 • Tightly coupled: Hybrid algorithms run simultaneously on FTQC-HPC resources,
 interchanging data at latencies/bandwidths comparable to heterogeneous CPU-GPU
 systems and motivating specialized interconnects and APIs.

While independent and loosely coupled approaches are better represented in the current literature,
tightly coupled hybrid algorithms remain under active investigation. Useful FTQC subroutines are
expected to require many (>10,000) logical operation clock cycles at slow logical operation clock
speeds (<100 kHz) to yield minimal data (10 to 1,000 bits). Tight coupling may become more
important in future FTQC architectures if FTQC algorithmic output data bandwidths increase.

It is worth emphasizing that below the algorithmic level, an FTQC is universally expected to
require very significant embedded classical computing resources—as much as 50 petaflops at 1
Tb/s bandwidth and 20 μs latency [Camps2024, Caldwell2025]—to decode error-correction
syndromes; run the logical microarchitecture; manage layout, scheduling, and routing in a way
that is adapted for runtime failures; and perform on-the-fly calibrations. Integrating tightly coupled
computational resources in the control plane to implement FTQCs represents a significant
development challenge.

3. Solving Domain-Science Problems on Fault-
 Tolerant Quantum Computers
 Early demonstrations of quantum scientific utility will likely emerge in at least three DOE-relevant domains:
 materials science, quantum chemistry, and high-energy and nuclear physics (HENP). In each domain, critical
 scientific questions rely on simulations of complex quantum systems—simulations that are beyond the limits of
 even exascale classical computation. Although current analyses suggest that FTQCs with 1,000+ logical qubits and
 the ability to perform billions of hard logical operations might be required to outperform cutting-edge HPCs,
 focused research into algorithms, applications, and FTQC architectures could enable scientific campaigns to be
 launched with on the order of 100 logical qubits and hundreds of thousands of hard operations.

A significant portion of DOE HPC workloads is devoted to three domains—materials science,
quantum chemistry, and HENP—for which quantum computing offers a compelling promise of
benefit [Camps2025]. Many urgent questions in these domains can be related to the properties or
behaviors of an underlying complex quantum system. Efficient and accurate simulation of
quantum physical systems on quantum computers [Feynman1982, Lloyd1996] is arguably the best
near-term prospect for advancing these domains beyond the reach of post-exascale HPC.

This section presents three representative use cases for quantum computing that span these three
domains, and gauge the minimum size and quality of a quantum computer needed to demonstrate
scientific utility. We have selected these problems because they (1) have significant scientific
value, (2) are beyond the reach of exascale-class HPC, and (3) are among those likely to be within
the reach of FTQCs the soonest. Each discussed problem represents a broad class of scientifically
valuable problems [Bauer2020, Bellonzi2024, Alexeev2024, DiMeglio2024] that could all be
solved by an FTQC of comparable size. The FTQC requirements we present for these problems
are based on published analyses of state-of-the-art algorithms. However, quantum algorithms are
constantly improving, and the “first fruits” of quantum computing are a moving target
[Proctor2025].

3.1 Assessing Prospects for Utility
Resource estimation [Harrigan2024, Gheorghiu2025] involves a detailed investigation of how an
algorithm for a specific problem could be compiled and implemented on a specific proposed FTQC
architecture and, critically, what expensive resources it would require. Resource estimates for
applications based on worked-out implementations of established algorithms and architectures
provide approximate upper bounds (worst-case estimates) for the quantum computational cost of
a problem. Over the past 30 years, innovations in every part of the quantum software “stack,”
including algorithms, compilation, routing, and the underlying logical architecture, have
contributed to a steady and dramatic reduction in the estimated resource costs of flagship
computational problems. We expect this trend to continue, especially with focused investments by
the DOE. Today, FTQC resource estimates exist for many scientific problems. Some are believed
to be extremely conservative and thus likely candidates for significant improvements through
continued research [Dalzell2023]. Others, especially those for canonical “challenge problems”
expected to represent the earliest attainable examples of quantum scientific utility [Proctor2025],
are the result of extensive research and may be difficult to reduce in complexity by more than 10×–
100×. Importantly, every known application admits space-time (qubit-runtime) trade-offs that
allow algorithms to be squeezed into fewer logical qubits (space) at the cost of more logical
operations (time) or vice versa [Fowler2012b].

3.2 Materials Science
3.2.1 Description
Quantum materials are widely regarded as foundational for driving transformative advances in
energy-relevant technologies across microelectronics, information, and computing systems. A key
goal of modern physics is to develop a detailed understanding of the microscopic mechanisms that
give rise to emergent and exotic quantum states exhibiting magnetic, superconducting, and
topological order. The (Fermi-)Hubbard model is one of the simplest models that exhibits, even at
small scales, some of the phenomena observed in the high-temperature superconducting cuprates,
including Mott insulating, antiferromagnetic, strange metallic, and superconducting states.
However, classical supercomputers cannot simulate the Hubbard model at sufficient size and
energy accuracy to address grand challenges such as high-temperature superconductivity.
Simulation of the Hubbard model is therefore a promising, significant application for quantum
computing. Furthermore, it is characteristic of a broad class of valuable problems in condensed
matter physics.

3.2.2 Candidate Workflow
A promising approach to solving lattice models such as the Hubbard model is to treat a large
material system as a small cluster embedded within a large bath. The cluster is solved accurately,
including all the complex many-electron interactions, while the bath is treated approximately (e.g.,
using mean-field theory). This suggests a loosely coupled quantum-classical workflow (Figure 2)
in which the quantum computer simulates the cluster depending on the bath properties and the
classical computer simulates the bath depending on the cluster properties [Bauer2016,

 5

Vorwerk2022]. Quantum embedding approaches can also be combined with other approximations,
such as density functional theory, to yield fully ab initio materials calculations.

Figure 2. Quantum simulation of embedded Hubbard model. Starting with a nominal single-particle Green’s
function, a classical computer determines an effective cluster Hamiltonian and approximate ground state. On a
quantum computer, the approximate ground state is prepared, refined, propagated forward in time according to the
effective Hamiltonian, and finally measured in order to estimate a single-particle excitation-deexcitation amplitude.
From a series of such simulations, each repeated many times, the time-dependent single-particle Green’s function is
estimated more accurately. This estimate is used to inform the next iteration, and the loop proceeds until the estimated
Green’s function converges, indicating a self-consistent solution.

3.2.3 Target Instances and Solution Requirements
Recent proof-of-principle demonstrations of the hybrid workflow described above on NISQ
computers have targeted problems consisting of a single impurity site coupled to up to three
[Keen2020, Steckmann2023, Nie2024, Hogan2025] bath sites. The accuracy of the cluster
embedding model generally increases with both cluster size and bath size. A four-site Hubbard
model cluster with eight bath sites is at the limit of classical computing capabilities. Solving a 6 ×
6 embedded cluster model would lead to new scientific results that could potentially be validated
by experiments on twisted bilayers of transition metal dichalcogenides, which are believed to be
good realizations of the Hubbard model. Resource estimates for quantum phase estimation
(QPE)—a related computation that may be expected to have comparable costs—suggest that
simulation of such a cluster could be accomplished with 200–300 logical qubits and 108 hard
logical operations [Campbell2021, Yoshioka2024, BaySmidt2025].

3.3 Quantum Chemistry
3.3.1 Description
The field of quantum chemistry presents a vast range of important scientific and technological
challenges that offer vast potential for commercial and scientific utility. Several of these appear

 6

uniquely amenable to analysis on quantum computers, including efficient catalysts for fertilizers
(nitrogen fixation); the discovery of new drugs; and the development of advanced materials for
batteries, photovoltaics, and quantum information technologies.

Chemists simulating these problems on classical computers are forced to choose between accuracy
and efficiency in their calculations. Some computational methods are efficient but vulnerable to
systematic uncontrolled approximation errors, while others can achieve arbitrarily good accuracy
at the cost of computational infeasibility (inefficiency). Quantum simulation algorithms offer the
prospect of circumventing this dilemma, enabling accurate and efficient calculations when
executed on sufficiently powerful quantum computers [McArdle2020, Babbush2023].

It is neither possible nor sensible to place an upper bound on the number of chemical systems for
which a quantum computer could enable accurate and efficient calculations, as chemistry (and
materials science) offer countless open problems because elements from the periodic table can be
combined in combinatorially many ways. However, a few representative canonical problems have
emerged in the quantum computing literature as clear exemplars of the potential impact of FTQCs.
The best-known examples are FeMoco [Reiher2017, Low2025] and cytochrome P450
[Goings2022]. They represent important use cases for nitrogen fixation and drug metabolism,
respectively.

Strongly interacting electrons are also the key to plasma physics, especially in the context of fusion
energy [Joseph2023], a key DOE mission area where experimental data is rare and expensive, and
attributing real-world behavior to atomic-scale processes is computationally hard. High-accuracy
simulations of chemical processes in plasma-facing components could make magnetic fusion
devices more reliable. Quantum simulations of degenerate plasmas can improve our microscopic
understanding of materials for inertial fusion devices in experimentally inaccessible
thermodynamic regimes where performance-limiting instabilities form [Rubin2024]. Quantum
computations, assisted by AI and HPC, could yield more accurate models of solvation chemistry
in molten-salt breeding blankets, enhancing production of tritium fuel required for both inertial
and magnetic fusion. Quantum algorithms for fusion/plasma applications are similar to those for
chemistry and materials, except that thermal and nonequilibrium effects cannot be neglected. In
the future, quantum algorithms for solving partial differential equations describing macroscale
plasma models [Liu2021, Ameri2023] (with connections to chemistry [Kharazi2026]) may have
even greater impact for fusion science.

3.3.2 Candidate Workflow
Wholesale replacement of classical electronic structure methods with quantum computers is
unlikely for problems of chemical interest, at least until the advent of PSQC systems. Nevertheless,
it is expected that integrating quantum computing with classical HPC techniques can enable
computations on SRQC systems that provide scientific insights [Alexeev2025]. A workflow for
such a hybrid computation is shown in Figure 3. The basic idea is very similar to that described in
the previous section on materials. A classical computation is performed to determine the properties
of the overall system and determine the size of the smaller fragment to be treated more accurately
using the quantum computer. The results from the quantum computer are then combined with the

 7

classical ones to determine the property of scientific interest. A convergence criterion is used to
determine whether more calculations using the quantum computer are necessary.

Figure 3. Quantum simulation of a molecule with fragment-based treatment of the region of strong correlation
[Alexeev2025]. First, the chemical property of interest, selected molecule, level of theory, and basis set are specified.
An effective fragment Hamiltonian and approximate ground (or, if desired, excited) state are computed on a classical
computer. An approximation to the target state is then prepared and refined on a quantum computer, and the
expectation value of the Hamiltonian is estimated. This estimate is combined with prior information in a
postprocessing step on the classical computer to yield a better estimate of the chemical property of interest, which is
used to inform the next iteration. Iteration proceeds until the chemical property is obtained to the desired accuracy.

3.3.3 Target Instances and Solution Requirements
A recent report reviewing projected resource requirements for quantum chemistry systems
suggested that solving scientifically relevant, hard quantum chemistry problems such as estimating
the ground energy of FeMoco, using QPE, will require running at least 108 hard logical operations
on about 1,000 logical qubits, with estimates going up to 1012 or more hard logical operations,
depending on the specific application and algorithm under consideration [Camps2025]. For
problems such as those described in Figure 3, where insights from classical calculations are
combined with those from a quantum computer, the requirements are much more modest: in the
range of 50–100 logical qubits with 104–105 hard logical operations for a QPE calculation of a
ground- or excited-state energy [Alexeev2025].

3.4 High-Energy and Nuclear Physics
3.4.1 Description
The purpose of HENP is to discover new physics beyond our current knowledge by studying
fundamental forces and particles, the nature of space and time, and the origin and evolution of the
universe. Because HENP R&D is a core DOE mission that is not pursued by industry, in contrast

 8

to applied chemistry or material science, commercial vendors are unlikely to co-design quantum
computers for applications in HENP without proactive input from DOE.

A primary challenge that quantum computing is expected to accelerate is performing calculations
based on quantum chromodynamics (QCD), the theory of the strong force in nature. The strong
force is responsible for binding quarks and gluons into hadrons, including protons and neutrons,
the building blocks of matter, from atomic nuclei to neutron stars and supernovae. Conventional
classical supercomputing has led to remarkable success in predicting static properties of hadrons
from the underlying QCD interactions [Joó2019, Davoudi2021, Davoudi2022, Aoki2024].
However, the Monte Carlo sampling techniques that enable this success encounter a computational
wall, namely sign problems, in addressing dynamical processes or systems at finite density: to
converge to the answer, exponentially large resources are required as the system size increases.
Even static properties are beyond computational reach for highly compact particles, such as some
dark matter candidates, because the runtime of classical algorithms grows inversely with the eighth
power of the particle size [DeTar2004, Farrar2017, Farrar2020].

Quantum computers offer a new way to execute first-principles QCD-based calculations of dense
environments, nonequilibrium phenomena, and real-time processes—phenomena prevalent in the
early universe, supernovae explosions, nuclear reactions, and high-energy collisions of particles
and nuclei. The ability of quantum computers to naturally encode exponentially large Hilbert
spaces and to enable important sign-problem-free algorithms rooted in Hamiltonian evolutions
leads to expectations that such computers will have a decisive advantage over classical methods
[Carlson2018, Beck2019, Asai2023, Beck2023, Bauer2023].

3.4.2 Candidate Workflow
Workflows for simulating many such physical systems of interest have commonalities. Led by
HENP subject-matter experts (SMEs), one may start the quantum program by designing and
performing suites of physics-aware digital quantum simulations that prepare an array of initial
quantum states of interest, apply time-evolution operators derived from the Standard Model of
particle physics (including low-energy effective field theories of QCD) to them, and furnish sets
of physics-motivated measurements of the properties of these nontrivial states throughout their
evolution. Using physics-aware algorithmic optimizations, aided by quantum algorithm SMEs,
one formulates optimized subroutines that boost the performance of the state-preparation, time-
evolution, and observable measurement stages within the accuracy requirements. Next, in
conjunction with quantum architecture SMEs, one then maps and compiles the algorithm to
hardware in an optimized way using the prevailing logical (fault-tolerant) quantum instruction set
architecture (ISA). In collaboration with quantum hardware SMEs, one then executes this
compiled algorithm on the hardware and returns a suite of results that can be used for physics
analysis by HENP SMEs and integrated into science domain research programs. In the long term,
the roles of the quantum algorithm, quantum architecture, and quantum hardware SMEs—
currently AI assisted—may become more automated. In the near term and especially for SRQC
machines expected in the 2028 time frame, their inputs remain critical because co-design is
essential for deployment success. Additionally, novel mappings of gauge theories (e.g., Refs.
[Illa2024, Kürkçüoglu2025, Siew2026, Than2025]) and AI-assisted hybrid computing methods
(e.g., Refs. [Lin2024, Aktar2025]) can be prototyped. Figure 4 depicts this notional workflow.

3.4.3 Target Instances and Solution Requirements
As one example calculation, we consider digitized time evolution of a pure SU(3) lattice gauge
theory [Kogut1975, Byrnes2006, Ciavarella2021, Ciavarella2024] (in the absence of dynamical
fermions) to measure key observables, such as energy-energy correlations [Lee2025], distribution
of particles and jets [Barata2022], and the viscosity and other transport coefficients of a finite-
density medium [Cohen2021, Turro2024, Turro2025]. Perhaps surprisingly, pure SU(3) lattice
gauge theory on just a 3 × 3 lattice in even two space dimensions and one time dimension is at the
limits of what the most advanced supercomputers can simulate today. On the other hand,
simulating a 10 × 10 lattice with a single-qubit truncation can be achieved with only 100 logical
qubits and 3.2 × 106 T gates over 50 Trotter steps. Modeling more interactions can require modest
increases to 3.6 × 106 T gates [Ciavarella2025]. A more faithful 5-qubit-per-plaquette
representation increases the costs to 500 logical qubits and 4.7 × 1011 T gates [Ciavarella2025].
Complementarily, one can approach SU(3) via larger encodings using discrete subgroups
[Assi2024] or anyonic regularizations [Rhodes2026]. On a smaller 3 × 3 system, the same
simulation requires 144 logical qubits and 5 × 107 T gates using an 8-qubit approximation
[Perez2025], rising to 162 logical qubits and 4 × 1010 T gates with a 9-qubit approximation
[Perez2025]. Progressing toward fully realistic simulations of QCD—which require three spatial
dimensions, the inclusion of dynamical quarks, and increasingly precise approximations—will
demand a larger number of logical qubits and lower error rates for hard logical operations
[Kan2021, Rhodes2024]. Nonetheless, the algorithms and encodings required at these larger scales
are direct extensions of those demonstrated on 100 logical qubits, ensuring that insights gained on
early systems translate directly to the regimes necessary for full physical realism.

3.5 Common Algorithmic Motifs
The applications described above—like many applications expected to benefit from FTQCs—
revolve around the simulation of a quantum physical system. Generally, a simulation algorithm
takes a description of a quantum system’s Hamiltonian and returns an estimate of a stationary or
dynamical observable of that system. The ground-state energy is the most common stationary
observable. Dynamical properties of interest include temporal correlation functions, phase
transitions, and responses to external influences. Algorithms for simulating quantum systems on
FTQC machines have been investigated since 1996 [Lloyd1996]. Although significant room for
innovation remains, almost all simulation algorithms comprise three distinct subroutines:

 1. Preparation of the qubits in a state that encodes an initial state of the physical system, often
 an approximation to the ground state.

 2. Many (e.g., 104) repeated applications of a unitary operator that encodes the Hamiltonian.

 3. Measurement of a multiqubit operator that encodes a scientifically interesting observable.

Typically, this three-step sequence is repeated multiple times to obtain statistically accurate
estimates of targeted observables.

Although the specifics of each subroutine differ across applications, a relatively small set of
quantum computational kernels can be used to implement them all. An FTQC that can perform
each of these kernels at scale will be able to run quantum algorithms for a wide variety of scientific
applications.

A primary computational kernel is Hamiltonian evolution. This kernel applies 𝑒𝑥𝑝 (−𝑖𝐻𝑡) to a set
of qubits, where 𝐻 is a Hermitian operator (often, but not always, the system Hamiltonian
[Low2019]) and 𝑡 is the duration of evolution under 𝐻. The operator 𝐻 typically consists of a sum
of relatively simple but noncommuting terms. Various techniques exist for approximately
implementing short-time evolution with bounded error. Evolution for long times is implemented
as a sequence of short-time evolutions, such as Trotter steps. Besides simulating dynamics,
Hamiltonian evolution is also used in QPE to estimate energies.

The quantum Fourier transform performs a very efficient discrete Fourier transform on the vector
of probability amplitudes that define the state of a qubit register. It is used, for example, within
QPE to transform time-domain signals into frequency information.

QPE is a routine used to isolate eigenvalues and eigenstates of an operator. It is often employed to
find the ground state and/or ground-state energy of a system’s Hamiltonian. Traditional QPE
applies a series of ancilla-controlled Hamiltonian evolutions to an initial state and then performs a
quantum Fourier transform on the ancilla register. Reading out the ancillae yields an energy
(eigenvalue) and leaves the system qubits in the corresponding eigenstate. The particular eigenstate
obtained is generally nondeterministic. It depends on the energy distribution of the initial state;
preparing a “good” (energy-localized) initial state can be harder than phase estimation itself.

 11

Amplitude amplification is a method commonly used to boost the success probability of another
quantum subroutine. It involves repeated application of that subroutine, interspersed with calls to
two quantum “oracles” (black-box subroutines) that mark (i) the initial state and (ii) the target state
or subspace whose amplitude is to be amplified. If the success probability of the original subroutine
is p, amplitude amplification boosts the success probability to approximately 1 by calling the
subroutine only p−½ times (fewer than the 1/p calls necessary for classical probability
amplification). Amplitude amplification is used to prepare initial states for phase estimation and
for many other uses, including function inversion (or “search”) and integration.

The quantum singular value transform is a meta-algorithm that unifies and generalizes many of
the kernels above. Given a function 𝑓 and the ability to implement an operator 𝐴, it provides an
efficient way to implement 𝑓(𝐴). This is a general and powerful technique that can be used for
many tasks, including Hamiltonian evolution, imaginary-time evolution (simulated cooling),
inversion, and spectral filtering.

Many other quantum algorithms and kernels are used within end-to-end quantum applications. The
performance and resource requirements of the kernels above have been analyzed in a host of
different contexts, leading to plausible cost and performance projections for many quantum tasks.
Although the discovery of new quantum kernels has been historically infrequent, research in
quantum algorithms continues to yield steady improvements in algorithmic efficiency.

3.6 Capability Requirements for Achieving Quantum Scientific
 Utility
This survey of applications indicates that scientifically significant quantum computations involve
roughly a billion or more hard logical operations on several hundred or more logical qubits.
Proposed FTQC architectures use 100–1,000 elementary quantum operations, or quops
[Gidney2021], per hard logical operation, as well as additional logical qubits for data movement
and implementation of hard operations. Thus, some current estimates for broad scientific utility
require a teraquop machine that can accurately perform 1012 quops on 1,000+ logical qubits. We
believe and expect that advances in algorithms and architectures, outlined in Section 6, will bring
this estimate down over the next 4–8 years.

4. Capability Roadmap and Projections for Fault-
 Tolerant Quantum Computers
 Achieving general scientific utility will require FTQCs that greatly exceed today’s capabilities. Today, ~5,000
 operations can be performed on ~100 physical qubits. Roadmaps suggest the availability of megaquop quantum
 computers using advanced architectures that can perform 10,000–100,000 hard logical operations on 100 logical
 qubits circa 2028–29, followed by teraquop systems in the early 2030s. These milestones define a clear path forward
 and will require fault-tolerant architectures using QEC.

State-of-the-art quantum computers based on superconducting transmons, trapped ions, and
neutral atoms can execute ~5,000 operations on ~100 physical qubits. They have demonstrated
QEC [Egan2021, Google2025a, Bluvstein2025], quantum simulation of Ising [Kim2023] and
Hubbard [Alam2025a, Alam2025b] models, and other accomplishments [Arute2019]. However,
existing machines are orders of magnitude less performant than necessary to execute scientifically
relevant computations that require 105 to 109 hard logical operations on 100 to 1,000 logical qubits.

The performance of such machines is often quantified by the error rate per elementary quantum
operation (quop). However, algorithms are usually specified in terms of hard logical operations
(e.g., T or Toffoli gates), and each hard logical operation typically requires 100–1,000 quops. Thus,
an FTQC that can execute 109 hard logical operations is a teraquop (not gigaquop) machine.
Logical architectures proposed for achieving teraquop performance use as many as 584 physical
qubits per logical qubit [Gidney2025] or as few as 72 [Yoder2025], with extremely sensitive
dependence on the quality of the physical qubits. Although improvement can be expected, the goal
of 109 hard logical operations on 1,000+ logical qubits is likely to require at least 100,000 high-
fidelity physical qubits.

Quantum computers of rapidly increasing size and scale are being demonstrated by U.S.
commercial companies. (Academic groups, U.S. national labs, and non-U.S. companies have
demonstrated impressive but less capable systems.) Several industrial roadmaps are published and
indicate a consistent trend over the next decade. Progress by any individual quantum hardware
producer is unpredictable, but we judge it plausible that at least one platform will, at any given
time, be able to produce a machine consistent with the following expectations:

    •   2026: 1–10 logical qubits, 104 quops
 • 2027: 5–50 logical qubits, 103–104 hard logical operations
 • 2028: 25–100 logical qubits, 104–105 hard logical operations
 • 2029: 60–200 logical qubits, 105–107 hard logical operations
    •   2031–33: 1,000–2,000 logical qubits, 109–1012 hard logical operations

These moderately optimistic best-guess estimates are consistent with Figure 5 from a 2025
National Energy Research Scientific Computing Center (NERSC) study [Camps2025] that
compared capability requirements for quantum scientific utility to vendor roadmap predictions for
2025, 2030, and 2035.

Barring transformative breakthroughs, best estimates from quantum algorithms (Section 3) and
vendor roadmap projections suggest the following:

 • Multi-megaquop FTQCs offering ~100 logical qubits and 104–105 hard logical operations
 may become available at the end of this decade. These first-generation FTQCs will enable
 testing of the principles, scalability, logical ISAs, and software stack of FTQCs. They will
 function as scientific instruments, allowing for increasingly complex scientific

 • A teraquop FTQC may become available in the early 2030s. Such a PSQC would increase
 the capability and capacity of scientific campaigns.

These projections motivate priorities for achieving scientifically relevant quantum computing as
rapidly as possible by pursuing three activities urgently and simultaneously:

 1. Co-design with vendors to deploy a quantum computer that can perform at least 104–105
 hard logical operations on ~100 logical qubits targeting the 2028 time frame.

 2. Begin preparing facilities, user bases, expert operators, and software/theory
 infrastructure necessary to make immediate, effective use of such a quantum computer
 by 2028.

 3. Scope and fund urgent, breakthrough R&D focused on achieving transformative leaps
 that could (individually or collectively) enable quantum scientific utility to be achieved
 early on an FTQC.

5. Software, Systems, and Facilities Preparations
 Over the next five years, DOE should fully develop a system software environment that makes SRQC accessible to
 a range of users as a managed resource. DOE should adopt and/or create logical instruction schedulers,
 compiling/routing software, and intermediate representations that facilitate the flexible use of diverse FTQC
 architectures across all application regimes without assuming a particular underlying quantum computing
 technology.

 By 2028, to successfully host the first SRQCs, DOE facilities should evolve from experimental hosts to operational
 environments for quantum systems through investments in facility infrastructure, environmental control, and
 integrated management. These facility approaches may vary depending on the requirements of specific FTQC
 technologies and/or deployment models. Establishing unified operational procedures, secure access frameworks,
 and vendor coordination will make quantum computers reliable, serviceable components of the national
 supercomputing infrastructure.

DOE should lead the integration of quantum computing into the national scientific infrastructure,
leveraging its unique scale, coordination capacity, and long-term stewardship across the U.S.
national laboratory complex. Building on its existing ecosystem of supercomputers, specialized AI
systems, high-throughput data platforms, and large scientific instruments, DOE should develop a
comprehensive software and systems environment that will allow FTQCs to be managed as
production-class resources. DOE should complete foundational work in unifying hardware control,
scheduling, compilation, and user-access interfaces inspired by similar HPC facility processes.
When SRQC deployment models require coupling to HPC resources, the DOE should establish
integrated approaches to HPC facility workflows, access models, and governance. This enables
high-level scientific applications to delegate computations to HPC resources when SRQC
capabilities are not required—for example, in pre- and post-processing of data sent to and received
from SRQC machines. Community-driven development of schedulers, compilers, and
intermediate representations can facilitate portability across architectures. The 2028 SRQC(s) will
serve as the first proving grounds for scientific discovery and full-stack integration.

5.1 Quantum-Classical Interaction Layers
The optimal configuration of how classical computing can best enable emerging application and
operational needs for FTQCs is an area of active research. In general, across a range of HPC-FTQC
coupling scenarios, the following three layers of classical computing could enable a scientific
campaign running with and around an FTQC system:

 1. QEC and Fault-Tolerance Control Layer: This layer provides a real-time (low latency
 and high-bandwidth state processing) exchange between classical compute and quantum
 hardware, performing continuous data processing, error decoding, and device
 configuration to maintain overall system stability. It is expected to be procured with the
 quantum system and may employ application-specific integrated circuits (ASICs) or field-
 programmable gate arrays (FPGAs) to manage I/O operations and low-level hardware
 setup specific to the underlying qubit technology. To achieve error correction or fault
 tolerance, more than one computational sub-layer may be needed. To enable the DOE to
 make best use of this layer, it would be valuable for the DOE to co-design the QEC and
 FTQC interfaces for applications it is interested in, much as it has historically done in

 15

 conventional HPCs. For example, error-corrected logical fermionic data types and methods
 for processing them fault-tolerantly could support the exemplar applications listed in
 Section 3.

 2. Quantum-Classical Orchestration Layer: This layer controls execution by utilizing
 classical computing capabilities to manage compilation, scheduling, and coordination of
 SRQC resources, linking user workflows to the hybrid compute environment (Figure 6).
 In some cases, this tier may be procured with the quantum system; in others, it may reside
 within a larger HPC complex. In all cases, it must include sufficient compute resources and
 libraries to perform compilation, scheduling and hybrid resource management, telemetry
 aggregation and response, and overall orchestration of the job workflow through the SRQC
 system. This will become especially important as we scale the number of logical qubits up,
 because algorithms for optimal compilation often demand computational resources that
 scale as high-degree polynomials as a function of the number of qubits involved.

 3. Scientific Ecosystem Workflow Layer: The quantum supercomputers procured and
 deployed by DOE will be a part of the broader DOE Genesis Mission’s infrastructure. This
 science-facing infrastructure will support end-to-end research workflows and will deliver
 cutting-edge scientific outcomes. It will execute the classical components of scientific
 discovery workflows, encompassing data preprocessing, model calibration, and post-
 quantum analysis. This layer connects quantum execution outputs with large-scale HPC
 and AI resources for data assimilation, parameter optimization, and uncertainty
 quantification. The Genesis Mission infrastructure is expected to enable integration with
 DOE user facilities and experimental instruments, allowing direct ingestion of
 observational or experimental data to drive workflows encompassing hybrid simulation,
 training, and validation loops.

The examples in Section 3 illustrate DOE scientific missions that integrate quantum and classical
components and require advancements across all layers. The quantum portion executes on the
FTQC subsystem, whose architecture, error rates, and runtimes may vary across technologies
[Buchs2025] and circuit/system architectures [Bravyi2022]. A low-latency, high-bandwidth
classical control layer is critical to sustain QEC and fault tolerance. Quantum-derived results can
be exchanged with classical supercomputers within outer workflow loops that coordinate iterative
quantum-HPC execution cycles.

5.2 Software Implementation
A software ecosystem for scalable quantum supercomputing should address the layers described
in Section 5.1. Although a complete solution is several years away, ecosystem building efforts will
be a part of the SRQC pathfinding and help bolster success in the PSQC phase.

The primary areas of R&D required to develop this coordinated software stack are:

 1. Hardware Abstraction and Device Management: Although vendors may initially aim
 to develop their own hardware interfaces and device management technologies, it is in
 DOE’s interest to co-design and jointly develop a cross-vendor solution and interface
 through R&D. This is essential for portability across vendors and would help to move the

 16

 ecosystem toward standardized and consistent functions for initialization, calibration,
 execution, telemetry, connectivity, and error rates integrated into DOE’s facility-
 monitoring and maintenance systems. Establishing the right level of standardization across
 different logical architectures will accelerate the deployment and adoption across the
 scientific user base.
 2. Compilation and Optimization Frameworks: Development is needed in hybrid compiler
 pipelines to map high-level quantum algorithms into portable intermediate representations
 and dynamically optimize them for multiple back ends. Co-designing logical ISAs using
 innovations in codes, heterogeneous processing and memory elements, and other
 technologies would enable DOE to deploy logical ISAs for their applications onto fixed
 physical hardware with fixed physical ISAs developed by vendors. Advanced compilers
 will likely use telemetry and error-correction data to adapt scheduling and resource
 optimization on the fly, giving users visibility into achievable precision, depth, and
 runtimes. AI-assisted optimization could accelerate circuit mapping and performance
 tuning. Ongoing efforts such as the Munich Quantum Software Stack [Burgholzer2025],
 RIKEN’s integration of quantum systems with the Fugaku supercomputer [Caldwell2025],
 and work led by Oak Ridge National Laboratory [Beck2024, Shehata2025, Shehata2026]
 demonstrate the value of effective modular design, open interfaces, and shared telemetry.
 DOE will build on this experience, particularly in portability, orchestration APIs, and
 standardized metrics, while leading efforts to develop an industry-wide solution that
 encompasses the needs of the DOE mission.
 3. User Access and Application Interfaces: Unified portals, APIs, and workflow systems
 should be delivered through existing DOE supercomputing environments, where
 applicable. Support for standardized quantum frameworks and domain-specific libraries
 will allow researchers to launch hybrid jobs using familiar authentication, storage, and
 scheduling tools. These quantum frameworks should be designed so that they can utilize
 DOE’s existing HPC infrastructure. For workflows that require only independent or loosely
 coupled HPC support, such integration is less imperative, whereas other workflows might
 rely on it more substantially.

hand-crafted sequence of steps as illustrated in Figure 6 (and earlier in the HENP application

effectively representing distinct firmware variants with different choices of QEC code and/or

5.3 Facilities Integration
Connecting quantum computers with DOE’s HPC centers may require targeted preparation across
facilities, infrastructure, and operations. Quantum systems can impose unique environmental,
power, and cooling requirements compared to classical systems [Mansfield2025, Slysz2025], and
the quantum technologies themselves vary in their environmental requirements. For example,
quantum computers based on superconducting transmons and trapped ions demand vibration
isolation, electromagnetic shielding, and temperature stability, whereas photonic systems have
strict requirements for optical alignment, dust control, and humidity ranges. If co-located in HPC
centers, these requirements must be satisfied without compromising the reliability of existing
supercomputing operations. It is expected that redundant environmental infrastructure systems,
such as power, chilled water, HVAC, vacuum, and cryosystems will help mitigate prolonged
system downtimes. When HPC and SRQC machines are co-located, these environmental controls
may be integrated into the existing facility management and monitoring systems used for DOE
supercomputers, ensuring that both classical and quantum resources can be managed under a
unified operational framework.

Operational readiness will require new procedures and workforce competencies. Quantum systems
must undergo periodic calibration and maintenance that differ from typical HPC systems. This will
require coordination among facility operators, system administrators, the user community, and
hardware vendors. Establishing automated recalibration pipelines, telemetry feedback loops with
responsive scheduling, and scheduled maintenance windows will help maximize uptime consistent
with DOE service-level expectations. The identification of replaceable modular components in
SRQC-scale machines is a research problem in its own right at the moment that the DOE should
work with vendors to resolve.

The integration of quantum computers into DOE’s computing infrastructure must include secure
and transparent access mechanisms. Quantum systems should appear as managed resources within
existing job schedulers and authentication frameworks, allowing users to submit and monitor
hybrid workflows in terms of metadata, system context, and workflow tracking. Developing and
standardizing these interfaces across facilities will reduce operational complexity and accelerate
the transition from experimental hosting to production-class hybrid computing environments.

Finally, to realize this capability in its fullest, DOE should deploy one or more SRQCs (and follow-
on PSQC machines), which can be installed in the facilities described above. This procurement
will necessitate deep, ongoing public-private partnerships that culminate in a deployment. The
process could begin with a request for information (RFI) in the first half of 2026 to determine
vendor requirements and to gather the necessary information to begin any required facilities work.
The RFI could be followed by a solicitation issued later in 2026. Pursuing multiple machines with

 19

distinct underlying technologies would reduce risks and bottlenecks associated with reliance on a
single technology.

6. Transformative Acceleration of Scientific
 Quantum Utility
 DOE can change the trajectory and landscape of quantum computing—accelerating the advent of unambiguous,
 broad-based quantum scientific utility from the 2030s to as soon as 2028—by supporting focused, goal-driven,
 high-priority R&D in three critical limiting areas: scientific applications, quantum algorithms, and logical
 architectures. All three are highly technical challenges that offer clear potential for transformative breakthroughs
 that could make scientific utility attainable on an SRQC. Goal-driven investment in applications, algorithms, and
 architectures represents a moon-shot opportunity for DOE to grasp scientific and technological leadership in
 quantum computing while accelerating scientific discovery across missions.

A multi-megaquop FTQC with an advanced architecture capable of performing 104–105 hard
logical operations on 100+ logical qubits will be able to perform high-fidelity quantum
computations that vastly surpass simulatability by any known (or imaginable) classical computer;
the earliest claims of such “quantum supremacy” date back to 2019 [Arute2019]. But it is not yet
known whether a megaquop machine can be used to solve useful scientific problems. The gap
between simulatability and scientific usefulness is not fully understood. Bridging this divide
represents a primary pathway to achieving true quantum scientific utility with an SRQC as early
as 2028–29. An opportunity exists for focused research efforts to close this gap by 2028. Success
in this endeavor would be transformative, changing the landscape of quantum computing. In this
section, we describe high-impact research directions that could enable breakthroughs and
massively increase the impact of the SRQC deployment.

Three areas of research provide opportunities for transformative breakthroughs:

 1. Discovery of new scientific applications amenable to FTQCs and aligned with DOE’s
 mission.

 2. Invention of new quantum algorithms that use logical resources more efficiently.

 3. Creation of new logical architectures that use physical qubits more efficiently.

Each of these topics has a long history and is recognized as an essential pillar of quantum
computing. However, each remains the specialized province of relatively few experts. Just as
historical surges of focused effort in, for example, nuclear physics or rocketry supported by U.S.
government science agencies produced rapid, transformative advances, these areas contain
opportunities for breakthroughs that would emerge from focused research efforts. DOE is well
positioned to assemble its leading experts and establish initiatives to advance these critical
priorities.

 20

6.1 Opportunities for Transformative Scientific Applications
Outperforming current or future HPC systems on known impactful scientific applications—for
example, finding energies of FeMoco or the Hubbard model—is expected to require millions or
billions of hard logical operations [Campbell2021, Babbush2023, Low2025]. In contrast, random
circuit sampling (almost universally regarded as a contrived and unuseful problem) requires only
thousands [Arute2019]. It is possible that this gap is not fundamental, but represents classical
habits of thought in the definition of “impactful scientific applications.” Historically,
transformative scientific instruments such as the microscope, x-ray imaging, or radio telescopes
have revealed new questions that were not previously imagined. It is reasonable to expect that once
utility-scale quantum computers become available, they will catalyze the discovery or articulation
of new scientific questions that are better adapted to quantum computing—and that, in retrospect,
might have been solved with an SRQC.

There is, in principle, no reason why these questions could not be articulated before a DOE SRQC
becomes available, just as microorganisms could, in principle, have been conjectured before the
microscope. One suggestive, although insufficient, example is the dynamics of chemical or
material systems. With classical methods, dynamics are harder to compute than energies, so
scientists have focused on energies as representative properties. In contrast, on a quantum
computer, dynamics may actually be easier than energy estimation, but fewer applications of
dynamical simulation have been conceived and articulated because decades of pre-quantum-
computing research focused on energies. We note the recent investigation of out-of-time-order
correlation functions [Google2025b] as a promising example of the sort of speculative search for
novel applications that might yield problems with radically lower resource costs.

6.2 Opportunities for Transformative Quantum Algorithms
Historically, the efficiency of classical algorithms (e.g., those for eigensystems) has improved
steadily, producing efficiency gains in computation that have at times outpaced the improvement
in hardware due to Moore’s Law. Quantum algorithms are a far younger field but have thus far
shown even more rapid improvement. It is taken for granted among experts that current quantum
algorithms are far from optimal. Since resource costs have already been reduced by several orders
of magnitude over the past 10 years, it is widely expected that additional orders of magnitude can
be extracted from quantum algorithms for a range of problems—possibly even for Hamiltonian
simulation (discussed in Section 3).

However, quantum algorithms for FTQCs—and the techniques and advancements that achieved
order-of-magnitude speedups over the past 10–20 years—are extraordinarily technical and
challenging. Relatively few scientists are experts in these techniques, and relatively little funding
has supported their work. Extensive investment in the related field of NISQ and variational
quantum algorithms laid the groundwork for the discovery of new FTQC algorithms and produced
a trained workforce that can now be brought to bear on the harder problem of improving FTQC
algorithms. Algorithm advancements are more likely to be solved by hard, well-informed
teamwork among top experts in classical algorithms for DOE science problems and experts in the
unique structural properties of quantum algorithms.

 21

Opportunities for transformative advances include research that could (1) identify new algorithms
or modifications to known algorithms that are particularly well suited to resource-constrained
FTQCs, (2) discover or create novel connections between quantum algorithms and specific domain
applications, (3) discover completely new quantum algorithms for known or innovative DOE
science applications, (4) create and leverage novel and highly efficient quantum data types (e.g.,
logical fermions), (5) discover highly efficient replacements for ubiquitous but inefficient
encodings (e.g., as block encodings of Hamiltonians improved on Trotterization), and (6) replace
common subroutines with more efficient quantum-specific implementations (e.g., residue
arithmetic).

6.3 Opportunities for Transformative Logical Architectures
For almost 15 years, most roadmaps to large-scale FTQCs have been based on a QEC code called
the surface code [Dennis2002, Fowler2012a]. Although the surface code is conceptually
straightforward and has a high error threshold, it is resource inefficient: several hundred physical
qubits will be needed to encode a single logical qubit with sufficiently low error [Gidney2025].
Recent work in quantum low-density parity check (qLDPC) codes has led to the first explicit
practical fault-tolerant architecture with a significantly smaller overhead (24:1) [Yoder2025] at the
cost of more complex logical operations. In principle, much more efficient fault-tolerant protocols
are possible, but the discovery and implementation of such protocols are complex, labor-intensive
processes that require coordinated research by multidisciplinary teams of experts.

Logical architecture breakthroughs offer an opportunity to drastically accelerate the timetable for
quantum utility, multiplying advances in applications and algorithms. Whereas better algorithms
enable users to extract more powerful computations out of the same number of logical qubits and
operations, better architectures enable a quantum computer to provide more logical qubits and
logical operations with the same physical hardware. A 2028 quantum computer with 10,000
physical qubits and a physical error of 3 × 10−4 per gate using the surface code would yield an
SRQC offering 100 logical qubits and 107 quops. With better QEC codes, routing, and
implementations of hard gates, the same machine might provide 600 logical qubits and 1010 quops,
transforming it into a PSQC that could solve the Hubbard model at a scientifically groundbreaking
scale.

Examples of (hypothetical) transformative logical architecture breakthroughs include (1)
discovery of a scalable family of qLDPC codes whose logical/physical encoding rate is greater
than 1/12; (2) systematic creation of block codes that enable easy manipulation of block-encoded
logical qubits; (3) significant reductions in the size of factories for magic-state distillation and
cultivation; (4) invention of pairs of codes that enable simple, low-overhead code switching to
implement universal logical gates; (5) creation of QEC codes whose structure enables reduced
control and decoding bandwidth (e.g., because they are partially self-correcting); and (6) efficient
logical operations co-designed specifically for certain qubit hardware (e.g., movable atomic
lattices or photonic resource states).

7. Criteria for Success
 By 2028, DOE should deploy SRQCs that demonstrate fault-tolerant quantum computing capable of performing
 science-relevant calculations and benchmarks; demonstrate progress toward PSQCs; have a workforce, staff, and
 facilities fully prepared to enable the use of SRQCs for scientific campaigns; and have made focused efforts to
 make utility achievable on an SRQC through transformative research.

7.1 What a Successful SRQC Would Look Like
At a minimum, SRQCs must be able to support compilation and execution of mini-applications
relevant to users from each of the three main domain sciences described in Section 3. Programs
that reach the maximum capacity of the SRQC’s capability (both logical qubits and logical
operations) represent the intended use of these systems and should be prioritized in design.

The SRQCs must perform scientific computations that, without necessarily exceeding the
capabilities of a conventional computer, (1) demonstrate the viability of FTQCs for solving
scientific problems and (2) clearly demonstrate a problem-size-dependent scaling that, when
extrapolated to larger problems, predicts superclassical performance for next-generation PSQCs.

 • Example 1: Computations of the ground-state energy density (to some precision, not
 necessarily impressive) for Fermi-Hubbard models on lattices of size 2 × 2, 4 × 4, and
 6 × 6.

 • Example 2: Performance of one, two, and four Trotter iterations for QPE on some encoding
 of a transition-metal molecule (e.g., FeS).

 • Example 3: Computation of energy-energy correlations in pure SU(3) lattice gauge theory
 in two space and one time dimension on spatial lattices of size 3 × 3, 4 × 4, and 5 × 5.

The SRQCs will also be used to begin or accelerate the development of the software ecosystem
necessary for FTQC systems, as described in Section 5. At a minimum, a compiler and associated
tools will be required to make the machine minimally functional. Additionally, interfaces to the
hardware devices to manage and interrogate the system will be needed to ensure smooth
operations.

The software and applications that leverage SRQCs will evolve throughout the lifetime of the
machine, potentially intersecting with several upgrades of the SRQC on the path to the final
deployment of the PSQC machines. Where possible and reasonable, the system software stack will
develop abstractions that are common to all the FTQC architectures deployed by DOE to enable a
common, standard set of interfaces.

7.2 What Success in Transformative Research Would Look Like
The goal of the transformative research program outlined in Section 6 is to accelerate the advent
of quantum scientific utility by making it achievable on SRQCs. Any of the following three
accomplishments would constitute clear and compelling success of the program:

 23

 • A new application (scientific problem) of interest is discovered or invented that can be
 solved on an SRQC-class machine and is widely agreed to constitute useful domain science.

 • A new quantum (or hybrid quantum-classical) algorithm is created that can solve a
 recognized problem of scientific interest that exceeds the capability of HPC and can be run
 on an SRQC-class machine.

 • New logical architecture elements are invented that increase the quantum computational
 capability of an SRQC-class machine (defined now by physical hardware properties) to
 enable a quantum algorithm that achieves quantum scientific utility to be executed on the
 same hardware.

Enabling the SRQC to outperform exascale HPC on a recognized science problem by combining
partial success of these goals would also constitute success.

Although the research described in Section 6 is high risk in nature, it would establish DOE’s
leadership role in quantum computing applications, algorithms, and architectures, paving the way
for successful FTQC deployments in later years.

8. Next Steps and Roadmap
To enable demonstration of a scientific-scale FTQC in 2028, DOE must follow an aggressive
schedule to organize the quantum computing and domain-science experts in the national laboratory
complex, select vendors, and begin a robust co-design process. This process will include joint
projects with the vendor community for R&D in hardware and runtime innovations, intermediate
representations, and compiler interfaces. Co-design efforts aimed at deployment in the DOE
ecosystem will also explore uptime metrics and facility targets for vendors and DOE centers.

The primary objectives of this project include the following:

 • Deploy SRQCs that can execute at least 104–105 hard logical operations on at least 100
 logical qubits.

 • Develop a portable, end-to-end software stack for execution, including compilation, circuit

 ecosystem.

 • Advance foundational research in FTQC technology for improved algorithms and
 applications, error-correcting codes, FTQC protocols, and logical architecture designs.

 • Establish long-term quantum industry partnerships for co-design and software
 standardization.

 • Strengthen partnerships with U.S. industry and academia to promote joint problem-

 • Prepare DOE facilities for operational hosting of quantum systems, including

 • Demonstrate quantum viability on key DOE applications (e.g., energy, materials,

9. Glossary
AI: Artificial intelligence
A branch of computer science focused on systems capable of learning, reasoning, and pattern
recognition. In quantum and quantum-HPC contexts, AI currently supports optimization, error
correction, and exploration of large configuration spaces in hybrid workflows.

API: Application programming interface
A defined set of commands, data structures, and protocols enabling software components, such as
quantum compilers, schedulers, or workflow managers, to interoperate across classical and
quantum computing environments.

Campaign (scientific campaign)
A coordinated, multistep computational or experimental effort to address a defined scientific
objective, often combining HPC simulations, quantum subroutines, data analysis, and AI-driven
exploration under a unified workflow. Such workflows may operate in timescales that may go up
to hours or days.

Classical computing
Conventional digital computation performed on information carriers that cannot exist in
superposition states. It is usually implemented with binary logic and transistor-based architectures.
Classical computers execute deterministic or probabilistic operations on bits, whereas quantum
computers operate on qubits, which can support superposition and entanglement.

DOE Office of Science computing facilities: ALCF, NERSC, OLCF
DOE Office of Science user facilities that provide open, peer-reviewed access to some of the
world’s most powerful supercomputers for large-scale scientific research. The three DOE
computing facilities—the Argonne Leadership Computing Facility (ALCF), the Oak Ridge
Leadership Computing Facility (OLCF), and the National Energy Research Scientific Computing
Center (NERSC)—enable computational campaigns that address grand scientific challenges
across energy, materials, climate, and fundamental physics. In the context of this Blueprint, DOE’s
Office of Science computing facilities represent the operational and architectural model for
integrating future quantum supercomputers into the national high-performance computing
ecosystem.

Fault tolerance
A system’s ability to tolerate faults in its operation by preventing any errors that occur from
propagating catastrophically. This is typically achieved through redundancy. Everything from
bridges to computers to governmental systems can be designed in a fault-tolerant manner. A
system can be either small or large and still be fault tolerant.

FTQC: Fault-tolerant quantum computer
A quantum computer that performs computations on logical qubits that are protected from noise
and errors by being encoded within a larger quantum substrate (usually many physical qubits) and
periodically checked and corrected to eliminate errors.

 26

Hard logical operation
A logical operation such as a T or Toffoli gate on 1–3 logical qubits that, because of how quantum
error correction digitizes information and restricts the performance of arbitrary gates, can only be
performed using a large number of elementary quops. Hard logical operations are the “long pole”
of quantum algorithms, and their number is used to quantify the resource cost of a given algorithm
instance. Innovations in logical architecture could change which operations are considered hard
and which are considered easy. In this document, hard logical operations are usually assumed to
be reliable.

HENP: High-energy and nuclear physics
The field of physics that investigates elementary particles and fields and their interactions.

HPC: High-performance computing
The use of massively parallel computing systems composed of thousands to millions of processing
elements to perform large-scale numerical simulations, data analysis, and modeling tasks. In the
DOE context, HPC refers to leadership-class supercomputing resources that provide the
computational backbone for scientific discovery. Within this Blueprint, HPC also denotes the
classical computing tier that complements quantum systems through orchestration, pre- and
postprocessing, and hybrid quantum-classical workflows.

ISA: Instruction set architecture
The abstract machine-level interface that defines the set of logical operations and programming
conventions supported by a quantum processor. This is analogous to classical ISAs but designed
for quantum gates, qubit connectivity, and error-corrected operations.

Logical qubit
An effective qubit whose state is distributed across multiple subsystems. QEC codes exploit
redundancies in logical qubit encodings to suppress subsystem errors through active feedback. In
this document, logical qubits are usually assumed to be reliable.

NISQ: Noisy intermediate-scale quantum
The paradigm of performing quantum algorithms on logical qubits that are not encoded into QEC
codes but are instead implemented at a 1:1 rate directly in physical qubit systems.

PSQC: Production-scale quantum computer
A large-scale, fault-tolerant quantum supercomputer (≥1,000 logical qubits, ≥109 hard logical
operations) capable of performing scientifically meaningful calculations beyond classical reach
and targeted for DOE deployment in the early-to-mid 2030s.

QEC: Quantum error correction
A method for correcting errors in quantum systems. The most common form is syndrome-based
QEC, in which one first measures an error syndrome on one or more logical qubits. The syndrome
reveals information only about errors that may have occurred and nothing about the information
stored in the logical qubits. A decoder then processes the syndrome and identifies a recovery
operation intended to undo the error’s effects. The recovery itself need not be acted upon
immediately but merely tracked until it is necessary to deploy a corrective action, such as adapting
future computational actions on logical qubits as needed.

 27

Quop: Quantum operation
An elementary operation on logical qubit(s). What is considered elementary depends on the logical
ISA. For example, a single-control, arbitrary-target controlled-NOT (CNOT) gate (also called a
fanout-CNOT, or FCNOT) is considered elementary in lattice-surgery-based surface-code ISAs.
The execution time of elementary gates scales only as a small multiple of the code distance of the
logical qubit(s). Hard operations, such as logical T or Toffoli gates, might require 100–1,000 quops
when the time to distill special quantum states that realize them, called magic states, is considered.
That said, hard logical operations can often be pipelined in logical ISAs so that many hard logical
operations can occur in the time of one or a few quops via logical teleportation from logical magic
states.

Reliable
A component or operation within a computational system is said to be reliable if it performs
correctly with sufficiently high probability that the overall system or computation can perform its
task successfully. The quantitative threshold for reliability is context dependent. For example, in
a computation containing 106 individual operations, “reliable” operations can fail with probability
~10−6, but in a computation containing 109 operations, “reliable” demands that each operation fail
with a probability no greater than 10−9.

SRQC: Scientifically relevant quantum computer
A mid-scale fault-tolerant quantum system (~100 logical qubits) capable of executing tens to
hundreds of thousands of reliable operations. SRQCs validate system integration, early software
frameworks, and early scientific applications ahead of full utility-scale systems.

Teraquop
One trillion elementary quops, generally indicating a quantum computer capable of executing 1
trillion fault-tolerant logical idles (multiple cycles of fault-tolerant syndrome extraction) on
individual logical qubits. For example, 1 billion cycles of fault-tolerant syndrome extraction on
1,000 logical qubits.

 Appendix A. Quantum Computing R&D Roundtable
 Findings
 March 4–5, 2026, Rockville, MD

A.1 Context
The National Laboratory members of the DOE Laboratory Quantum Supercomputing Blueprint
Team invited leading researchers to a roundtable meeting in March 2026 to identify critical
technical gaps, recommend near-term R&D priorities, and align the community on actions needed
to maximize the scientific value of the systems described in the Blueprint Report. This appendix
is the synthesis of these discussions by the National Laboratory Organizing Committee (NLOC);
it is not a direct reporting of attendee contributions.

Roundtable participants were charged with answering a central question:

How can the community deliver a clear win for domain-science problems of DOE interest on a
scientifically relevant quantum computer (SRQC), namely one possessing approximately 100
logical qubits and able to execute approximately 100 million quops, by 2028?

As noted in the main Blueprint Report, the NLOC acknowledged to roundtable participants that
while SRQCs are well beyond the reach of what classical computers can simulate, the path to a
scientific win with such machines is unclear and would benefit from input from the roundtable as
to what investment choices, informed by judgment about prioritization, would be the best.

Participants considered several related questions:

 • What priority R&D directions in logical architectures, algorithms, and their co-design will
 best facilitate this win?

 • What priority R&D directions in materials science, quantum chemistry, and High Energy
 and Nuclear Physics (HENP) applications will best facilitate this win?

 • What priority capability investments will best ensure that the outcomes of these R&D
 directions are deployed for a win?

After an introduction and a reading of the charge, DOE Under Secretary for Science Dr. Darío Gil
made opening remarks and responded to a moderated Q&A session. A selected group of nine
attendees then presented five-minute lightning talks conveying their initial thoughts. Over two full
days, attendees then participated in breakouts and developed specific recommendations in five
primary areas: algorithms, architectures, co-design, chemistry and materials applications, and
HENP applications. They also came together collectively and identified cross-cutting themes and
enabling infrastructure that would best support these recommendations. The remainder of this
appendix summarizes the findings of this meeting.

 A-1

A.2 Cross-Cutting Themes
All five primary area breakout teams agreed that achieving a scientifically meaningful result on
SRQCs will depend on close coordination across the full quantum computational stack. At the
scale of ~100 logical qubits, they noted that performance will remain dominated by architectural
constraints and execution overheads. Many argued that isolated advances will have limited
significance within the 2028 timeframe. They suggested that, instead, tightly integrated workflows
linking algorithm design, fault-tolerant execution, and hardware capabilities will define the
primary path to progress.

Key themes include the following:

 • Co-design across algorithms and architectures has great potential. Algorithms,
 compilation, resource states, and architectural constraints should be developed and
 evaluated together. This approach provides a strong possibility for resource reductions in
 certain scenarios.

 • Common representations are needed to enable comparison. The lack of shared
 abstractions for architectures and workflows limits coordination and convergence.

 • Benchmarks and reference problems are important to drive progress. Some domains
 have representative problems, whereas others lack well-defined benchmarks with clear
 observables and targets.

 • Progress depends on problem-driven, interdisciplinary workflows. Advancing toward
 a 2028 demonstration will require highly committed and coordinated efforts across domain
 scientists, algorithm developers, software developers, and system experts. This should be
 balanced with efforts to build a robust, sustainable quantum computing ecosystem.

 • Investigating new approaches to fault tolerance could be a valuable area of focus to
 produce sought-after FTQC performance and resource gains. Alternative approaches
 to error correction and detection, such as dynamical FTQC, may improve efficiency under
 early FTQC constraints.

A.3 Primary Areas
A-3.1 Quantum Logical Architectures
Participants identified four key challenges faced by quantum logical architecture R&D:

 1. R&D for these architectures is highly fragmented and lacks common frameworks,
 languages, and software tools with which to innovate and explore. Researchers often work
 in isolation between institutions and comparing approaches can be difficult, obstructing
 hybridization of ideas and cross-platform adoption.

 A-2

 2. Verifying correctness, emulating behaviors, and benchmarking performances of logical
 architectures are hindered by a lack of sufficiently flexible and modular shared software
 tools. Vendors are not motivated to develop cross-platform solutions.

 3. Slow hardware design cycles and bespoke near-term architectures limit the speed with
 which one can iteratively explore and deploy new logical architectures, such as those co-
 designed on a faster cycle with algorithms for DOE science applications.

 4. The workforce size may be inadequate to innovate the breakthroughs required to achieve a
 scientific win by 2028 SRQCs.

This breakout group identified the following research priorities to address these challenges:

 • Cultivate a shared logical architecture R&D environment by fostering the creation of
 common frameworks, languages, and software tools. Some potential building blocks
 discussed included Gottesman’s dynamical FTQC framework [arXiv:2403.04163], the ZX
 calculus [arXiv:2012.13966], and various logical quantum intermediate representation (IR)
 languages. Shared libraries of published architectures expressed with these paradigms
 could foster hybridization of ideas with more rapid innovation cycles and enable
 reproducible comparison, interoperability, and reuse.

 • Coordinate the development of shared methods and tools for verifying, validating,
 and benchmarking FTQC architectures. Some ideas suggested included developing a
 quantum “digital twin” framework, hosting a shared library of validated reference
 architectures, augmenting something like Stim [arXiv:2103.02202] to be a more
 comprehensive analysis tool, and creating better cross-platform resource estimation tools.

 • Ensure that DOE, in partnership with vendors, can deploy newly innovated logical
 architectures as firmware upgrades that obviate full hardware upgrades. Noted
 examples of firmware upgradability include QEC codes, logical gate protocols, decoder
 algorithms, and logical data types, especially for any of these co-designed with DOE
 science applications in mind.

 • Invest in the growth of the workforce. Ideas generated included working with
 universities on faculty hiring support and curriculum development, funding cross-training
 initiatives to draw in experts in related areas, and establishing scholarships and fellowships
 for students working in this area.

A-3.2 Quantum Algorithms
Over the past 20+ years, steady incremental progress in quantum algorithms has reduced the
complexity of scientifically relevant quantum computations by orders of magnitude. Nevertheless,
there was agreement among participants that existing algorithmic approaches are likely insufficient
to enable scientifically relevant calculations on quantum computers in 2028–29. Participants noted
a dearth of algorithms tailored for early FTQCs, the need for better methods and tools for
prototyping and evaluating algorithms, and the value of having agreed-upon reference applications
and algorithms to spur and assess progress.

 A-3

 • Algorithms suited for SRQCs. Research in new methods for implementing quantum
 simulation, novel approaches to computing quantities of interest in quantum physical
 systems, and problems outside quantum simulation for which SRQCs may provide an
 advantage.

 • A living suite of benchmark problems. The community would benefit from a
 collaborative public repository containing a range of well-defined target computations,
 covering multiple scientific domains, for SRQCs. The content for each computational
 target would ideally include (1) a concrete problem specification, including details of the
 Hamiltonians, initial states, observables to be measured, evolution times, and desired
 precision, for example; (2) an assessment of the best known classical approaches; (3) state-
 of-the-art quantum implementations and/or resource estimates for representative
 architectures.

 • Experiments to evaluate critical algorithmic techniques. The arrival of SRQCs will
 enable quantum computational experiments that can inform and accelerate the
 development of algorithmic techniques in ways that theory and classical simulation cannot.
 The design of such computational experiments is nontrivial and should begin before the
 arrival of the hardware.

A-3.3 Co-Design and Compilation
Quantum algorithms and logical architectures are often treated as distinct. Algorithms and
applications are conceived abstractly and then compiled, routed, and implemented for specific
universal architectures, whereas QEC codes and FTQC protocols are designed and optimized
independently. However, exceptions to this rule, such as Gidney’s designs for factoring
[arXiv:2505.15917] or the STAR architecture [arXiv:2303.13181, arXiv:2509.18294], show that
co-design of architectures and algorithms can reduce physical resource requirements. Many
roundtable participants suggested that co-designing algorithms, QEC, and logical operations,
especially for SRQC hardware, could accelerate the timeline for scientific utility. The promise of
co-design will likely depend on physical architecture, but almost all participants agreed that co-
optimizing gate-synthesis techniques on SRQCs with algorithms—often incorporated into
compilation—could accelerate progress.

The roundtable participants discussed investigating whether, where, and how effectively
algorithms and architectures can be co-optimized. Demonstrations that co-design works and proofs
limiting its utility will each help direct future research. Concrete items include the following:

 • Quantum computational primitives of SRQCs: Investigate methods for extra-efficient
 logical operations in specific near-term architectures. Investigate catalytic resource states
 that, once prepared, can reduce the cost of computation by, for example, enabling efficient
 single-qubit rotations [arXiv:2305.07720] or replacing costly operations like division with
 easier operations like multiplication [arXiv:2306.08585].

 • Resource estimation and compilation tools: Create and disseminate better tools for
 resource estimation, especially compilation of complex logical operations into elementary
 ones.

 A-4

 • Partial fault tolerance: Investigate and systematize techniques for performing efficient
 logical operations in partially fault-tolerant ways, e.g., fault-tolerant compilation that
 improves the accuracy of specific observables or error mitigation designed for SRQC use.

 • Inherently fault-tolerant operations: Investigate and develop complex (beyond
 Clifford + T) computational primitives with intrinsic error-detecting and error-correcting
 properties (as in [arXiv:1606.01904], for example) by synthesizing a CCZ gate using eight
 T gates while performing error detection. Methods agnostic to the underlying code would
 be the most powerful.

A-3.4 Chemistry and Materials
Quantum chemistry and materials science applications have been at the forefront of demonstrating
the benefits of quantum computing. The roundtable discussed challenge problems of interest, but
emphasized that the field is broad with many problems of interest. Effort is needed by the
community to accelerate workflows to enable rapid feedback with domain-science experts to
investigate applications and algorithms. Domain problems benefit from horizontal integration
between SMEs and quantum computing experts who can deploy quantum algorithms, QEC, and
hardware-aware optimizations to significantly accelerate problem components on quantum
computing systems. For example, bringing QEC approaches into algorithm design can result in a
vast reduction of resources needed for the problem (e.g., [arXiv:2511.09124]). Similarly, bringing
physical and chemical awareness and intuition into algorithm design has already been shown to
reduce the resources required, and we can expect this to continue. This integration will also enable
a proper resource estimation for chemistry and materials problems that account for the constraints.

The roundtable discussed application constraints and candidate applications in chemistry and
material science.

Chemistry
Essential information about entanglement in quantum systems can be extracted directly during
quantum simulations. Strong correlation effects in both ground and excited states relevant to
catalysis, photocatalysis, and various spectroscopic processes will benefit from more accurate
calculations on a 100 logical-qubit platform. One specific candidate application is the design of
next-generation, energy-efficient metal-sulfide-based catalysis for fuel production and emission
mitigation. Concrete developments include the following:

 • Develop an improved description of strong correlation effects in the ground and excited
 states relevant to catalysis, photocatalysis, and spectroscopies in various energy regimes.

 • Develop downfolding and embedding methods that encode missing correlation effects
 (dynamical correlation) that cannot be directly captured today in 100-qubit simulations.

 • Devise improved downfolding and embedding methods by leveraging machine learning
 techniques.

 A-5

Material Science
Fermionic lattice models are a promising domain area in which acceleration with quantum
computing can bring improvements in accuracy and time-to-solution. Specifically, FTQCs can
bring early scientific wins in the calculation of spectroscopic signatures of fermionic lattice models
of quantum materials. These calculations are necessary to use experimental data to establish an
underlying theory by determining the microscopic interactions and mechanisms that lead to
materials behavior. An important takeaway is that it is important to identify problems that
emphasize dynamics over ground states.

Microscopic interactions encoded in model Hamiltonians can be inferred from experimental
spectroscopic data (e.g., inelastic neutron scattering, angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy,
and scanning tunneling microscopy). However, this requires accurate calculations of spectroscopic
signatures in model Hamiltonians. Quantum embedding offers a scalable, physically meaningful
pathway toward calculating these spectroscopic signatures. It reduces the complexity to something
manageable on a 100-logical-qubit quantum computer by mapping the lattice problem onto an
impurity problem embedded in a bath designed to represent the rest of the system. Impurity models
have the advantage of being weighted toward the dynamical problem—the ground state is easier
to prepare than it is for a straightforward lattice model, and this can be tuned as the size of the
impurity is increased. Such embedding approaches enable a meaningful scientific problem to be
run on a 100-qubit system, and readily extrapolate to larger systems, if and when they become
available. Application domain targets of DOE relevance include Moiré superlattices and
superconductivity in nickelates and cuprates. Concrete developments include the following:

 • Develop embedding algorithms that use the FTQC infrastructure selectively and
 judiciously. What error rates are really needed? (For example, is 10−8 needed everywhere?
 Can we use low-precision operations for certain operations?)

 • Develop methods to obtain the simplest Hamiltonians that qualitatively match experiments,
 for example, relative pole heights and locations (50% accuracy).

 • Explore strategies to improve the quality of AI models by fine-tuning them with training
 data obtained on a quantum computer. Use this training data to improve models beyond
 the capabilities of classical computers. An advantage of this approach is that it can be
 developed and tested at present through coarse training with approximate methods (e.g.,
 density functional theory) and fine-tuning with more accurate methods (e.g., tensor
 networks or coupled cluster).

A-3.5 High Energy Physics and Nuclear Physics
Simulating the dynamics of matter under extreme conditions and at scale is required to optimize
the scientific impact of the experimental, theoretical, and computational programs in HENP in the
United States. Many such simulations lie beyond the capabilities of classical computation and
quantum simulations are currently the only foreseeable technology capable of illuminating these
phenomena. In addition to observation of complex dynamics, quantum simulations of these
systems open the possibility of identifying new quantum signatures that will enhance the discovery
potential of current and future experiments.

 A-6

The roundtable breakout team noted that over the past decade, quantum simulations of (1+1)D
systems have advanced significantly, constrained primarily by the limitations of current NISQ
devices. However, dynamics in one spatial dimension differ qualitatively from those in higher
dimensions. The advent of SRQCs will enable the exploration of gauge dynamics in (2+1)D—and
ultimately (3+1)D—on modest volumes, establishing a foundation for scalable quantum
simulations. Importantly, this would not be about building a “femtoscope” for a single problem
but developing a capability to advance the HENP discovery program in multiple directions. Real-
time simulations of strongly interacting, strongly correlated matter at subatomic scales will
eventually impact our understanding of hadronization and hydrodynamics at the Large Hadron
Collider (LHC), neutrino-nucleus interactions at the Deep Underground Neutrino Experiment
(DUNE), and nuclear reactions relevant to energy and national security. These targets provide
concrete opportunities to establish quantum advantage with first-generation SRQCs using
simplified and approximate approaches.

Research Directions:
We anticipate measuring real-time observables relevant to hadronization and jet fragmentation,
correlation functions relevant to transport coefficients, with finite-density systems as a longer-term
target. Recent estimates for the FTQC resources needed to simulate the time evolution of SU(3)
quantum fields reveal a broad landscape that is strongly dependent on the truncation strategy and
system size. As a fiducial benchmark example, consider 50 Trotter steps of time evolution in an
SU(3) pure gauge theory. In this case, results at modest system sizes that are nonetheless classically
infeasible to simulate show that digitization choices drive resource requirements, making optimal
representation a central challenge for near-term quantum simulation of non-Abelian gauge
theories. At the same time, significant headroom remains for algorithmic improvements, including
specialized distillation and catalysis techniques. Full-stack resource estimation remains
underdeveloped, underscoring the need for closer collaboration with experts in resource modeling
and fault-tolerant architecture.

Wide variations in resource estimates make clear that encoding and QEC strategies are central to
practical quantum simulations. By tailoring QEC to the symmetries of the target theory, gauge
redundancy becomes a feature rather than overhead, reducing costs and improving performance.
Extending these approaches to non-Abelian theories offers a promising path toward scalable,
hardware-aligned simulations of the Standard Model.

The success and acceleration of such efforts will depend on expanding and sustaining a skilled
workforce spanning HENP, algorithms, software, systems integration, and QEC. The field faces a
clear imbalance: the number of high-impact problems far exceeds the number of researchers
prepared to tackle them, and continued progress will only widen this gap. Addressing it requires
broad recruitment, deliberate cross-disciplinary training, and critically sustainable career paths,
particularly in computational and quantum information fields. Creating stable, long-term
opportunities is essential to attract, retain, and empower the talent needed to realize the program’s
scientific goals.

Specific Research Priorities:

 • Dynamics of confinement: Develop and benchmark quantum algorithms for real-time
 evolution, emphasizing observables that probe the dynamics of confinement.

 A-7

 • (2+1)D models: Develop a systematic SRQC roadmap for confining non-Abelian gauge
 theories in going from (2+1)D to (3+1)D.

 • Cross-domain program in HENP: Define a first set of SRQC-computable observables that
 are relevant to hadronization (LHC), neutrino-nucleus interactions (DUNE), and nuclear
 reaction dynamics.

 • Quantum advantage pathway: Develop a resource optimization plan including encodings,
 truncation schemes, and circuit constructions in order to achieve FTQC advantage.

 • HEP-aware QC: Investigate error correction and fault tolerance strategies specific to HENP
 examples and quantify reductions in resource requirements relative to generic approaches.

 • Workforce development: Establish a sustained program at the HENP-QIS interface to train
 early career researchers and create long-term positions targeted to enhancing and
 maintaining domain-specific expertise.

A.4 Enabling Infrastructure
In addition to the five primary topic areas, Roundtable participants highlighted infrastructural
needs in software, data, hardware, and workforce coordination. These were identified as key
enablers for the priority research directions outlined above and vital to achieving meaningful
results on SRQCs.

 1. Integrated, Modular Software Stack: A composable software stack is required to
 connect algorithms, compilation, QEC, and execution. Toolchains must be modular,
 allowing components such as encodings, compilers, and decoders to be developed,
 evaluated, and exchanged without rebuilding full systems. Programming environments
 should support rapid prototyping, implementation, and benchmarking with low user
 burden. The stack must support end-to-end workflows linking application definition,
 compilation, execution, and analysis, while integrating simulation and resource estimation
 capabilities.

 2. Shared Repositories and Standardized Artifacts: Shared repositories are required to
 enable reproducible evaluation and comparison. These include device models, benchmark
 problems, reference implementations, validated FTQC architectures, and protocol libraries.
 Curated application libraries, benchmark circuits, and syndrome datasets support
 consistent evaluation across architectures and algorithms, whereas domain-specific
 libraries provide reusable components for common computational tasks.

 3. Resource Estimation, Simulation, and Validation Tools: Tools are required to evaluate
 resource requirements and performance under realistic conditions. Resource estimation
 must be linked to compilation and execution to assess end-to-end costs. Classical
 simulation frameworks must support noise modeling, large-scale studies, and validation
 against experimental data. QEC decoders and fast simulators are needed to model system
 behavior.

 A-8

4. Scalable Compute and Hardware Access: Access to HPC systems is required for
 simulation, optimization, and design-space exploration. Access to SRQC systems and
 emulators is required for testing and validation. These resources must support iterative
 workflows across simulation and execution within a hybrid computing environment.

5. Coordinated, Cross-Disciplinary R&D: Progress requires coordinated efforts across
 applications, algorithms, QEC, compilation, and hardware. Cross-disciplinary
 collaboration is necessary to address system-level challenges, including structured
 collaboration across institutions, sustained workforce development, and continued support
 for theory and simulation.
