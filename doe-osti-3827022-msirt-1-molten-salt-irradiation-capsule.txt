 SUMMARY
The Molten Salt Irradiation Research Testbed (MSIRT) is being developed at Oak
Ridge National Laboratory to establish a capability for molten fuel salt irradiation
testing in the High Flux Isotope Reactor (HFIR) through a phased development
approach. MSIRT-1 is the first implementation of this capability and the first
molten salt irradiation experiment designed specifically for HFIR. MSIRT-1
utilizes a passive, sealed, nonfueled molten salt capsule to generate separate-
effects data on irradiation-assisted corrosion and salt chemistry evolution before
the added complexity of fuel bearing salts is introduced. The capsule incorporates
a welded molybdenum holder, miniature corrosion specimens, passive silicon
carbide thermometry, and a dedicated plenum within a 6061 aluminum alloy
housing. Neutronics analyses were performed for NaCl–MgCl2 and NaCl–CeCl3
configurations, activation analyses were completed to support handling and post-
irradiation examination planning, and a parameterized ANSYS thermal model was
developed for NaCl–MgCl2 to evaluate irradiation position, gas gap geometry, and
helium–argon fill gas composition for achieving the target specimen temperature
of 600°C. The resulting design establishes a practical experimental platform that
can be used to screen different salt–alloy combinations and develop the fabrication,
safety, thermal control, and post-irradiation examination workflows needed to
support subsequent MSIRT experiments with fuel-bearing salts.

 MSIRT-1 Molten Salt Irradiation Capsule Design
 1. INTRODUCTION
 Molten fuel salt irradiation data are needed to determine how fuel salt chemistry and structural material
performance evolve under the combined effects of neutron irradiation, elevated temperature, and salt
exposure. Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) is developing the Molten Salt Irradiation Research
Testbed (MSIRT) as an experimental capability for conducting these irradiations in the High Flux Isotope
Reactor (HFIR) through a phased development approach. MSIRT-1 is the first implementation of this
capability and the first molten salt irradiation experiment designed specifically for HFIR. It uses a compact,
passive capsule that contains nonfueled molten salt and candidate structural material specimens. MSIRT‑1
is intended to generate separate-effects data on irradiation-assisted corrosion and salt chemistry evolution
as well as establish the experimental procedures and infrastructure needed for later fueled salt irradiations.
Subsequent MSIRT experiments will introduce fuel-bearing salts and progressively increase experimental
complexity through instrumentation, active control, and eventually flowing configurations. This report
documents the MSIRT-1 design requirements, capsule configuration, neutronics and activation analyses,
thermal performance, design parameters, engineering drawings, and post-irradiation examination (PIE)
strategy.

 1.1 BACKGROUND
 The Aircraft Reactor Experiment (ARE) and Molten Salt Reactor Experiment (MSRE) demonstrated
the operation of circulating molten fuel salts and produced foundational information on fuel salt behavior,
materials compatibility, fission product chemistry, and irradiation-related degradation [1, 2].
Complementary in-pile experiments were conducted in the Low Intensity Test Reactor (LITR) [3],
Materials Testing Reactor (MTR) [4-11], and Oak Ridge Research Reactor (ORR) [12-14] to isolate
specific chemical and materials phenomena using forced circulation loops, natural convection loops, and
static capsule experiments. These experiments contributed to understanding corrosion and salt-chemistry
behavior under irradiation, including neutron-driven radiolysis and coupled phenomena such as noble-metal
transport and tellurium-induced embrittlement. The reactors and specialized experimental infrastructure
that supported these programs have since been decommissioned.
 More recent molten salt irradiation experiments have been conducted at the Massachusetts Institute of
Technology Reactor (MITR) [15, 16], the Ohio State University Research Reactor (OSURR) [17, 18], the
High Flux Reactor (HFR) in Petten [19-22], and the Neutron Radiography Reactor (NRAD) at Idaho
National Laboratory [23, 24]. These programs have renewed in-pile molten salt testing and extended the
experimental basis to additional fluoride and chloride salt systems. However, the data produced by these
facilities are distributed across different salt compositions, structural materials, neutron environments,
temperatures, exposure durations, and experimental objectives. Therefore, a systematic high-flux
irradiation capability is needed to generate directly comparable data for modern structural materials and
salt systems using controlled exposure conditions and a coordinated PIE strategy.
 Structural material performance and fuel salt chemistry are directly coupled in molten salt reactor
(MSR) systems. Alloying elements released through corrosion enter the salt and can change its impurity
inventory, redox condition, chemical speciation, and phase behavior. These changes can influence
subsequent corrosion, fission product behavior, and the long-term chemical stability of the fuel salt. Neutron
irradiation simultaneously produces displacement damage and transmutation within structural materials and
contributes to chemical changes within the salt. Therefore, neutron irradiation alters the corrosion response
relative to an otherwise equivalent out-of-pile exposure, although the magnitude, mechanisms, and
dependence on material and salt chemistry remain insufficiently understood.

 1

 Out-of-pile corrosion experiments provide the reference data needed to understand salt–alloy
compatibility under controlled thermal and chemical conditions. MSIRT-1 is designed to build on these
out-of-pile corrosion experiments by adding concurrent neutron irradiation and material damage to this
experimental basis. Comparisons between MSIRT-1 specimens and matched out-of-pile controls will
support a separate-effects evaluation of the influence of irradiation on corrosion performance.
Measurements of salt composition, corrosion products, gas generation, microstructure, and mechanical
properties will be used to relate changes in the material to corresponding changes in the salt environment.
 The long-term objective of MSIRT is to support irradiation testing of molten fuel salts in contact with
representative molten salt reactor structural materials. MSIRT-1 is intentionally configured as a passive,
nonfueled salt experiment. The absence of fissile material limits the initial complexity associated with
fission heat, fission product generation, safety requirements for fueled capsules, fuel handling, and PIE on
irradiated fuel. This simplified configuration is intended to provide a relatively rapid and cost-effective
method for screening candidate structural materials and salt systems under high-flux irradiation. It also
provides clearer interpretation of irradiation–corrosion interactions before fuel-bearing salts introduce the
additional chemical complexity associated with fission product generation, actinide transmutation, evolving
redox conditions, and fission-product partitioning and precipitation.
 Moreover, MSIRT-1 is designed to establish the experimental infrastructure required for later fuel salt
irradiations. The experiment will also provide a basis for developing procedures for salt preparation, capsule
assembly, containment welding, passive thermal control, HFIR safety qualification, and plenum gas, salt,
and specimen retrieval during PIE. The resulting data will support material down-selection and identify
experimental conditions that warrant evaluation in fueled systems. Subsequent MSIRT experiments will
build on this foundation by introducing fissile-bearing fuel salts, fission product generation, in-pile
instrumentation, active gas and temperature control, and higher-fidelity experiment configurations. Thus,
MSIRT-1 will serve both as a separate-effects irradiation experiment and as a capability development step
toward qualification-relevant molten fuel salt testing.
 The previous MSIRT milestone report established the phased program strategy and the conceptual basis
for the first irradiation experiment [25]. The work documented in this report advances MSIRT-1 from
conceptual development to a detailed mechanical design and engineering analysis. HFIRCON calculations
were used to determine neutron flux and component-specific nuclear heating. To estimate activation
products and activities relevant to capsule handling, transportation, and PIE, the HFIR Activity Workbook
Generator (HAWK) [26], a Python-based interface to the ORIGEN depletion and decay code within
SCALE 6.3.2 [27], was used. The calculated nuclear heating rates were incorporated into finite-element
thermal analyses that were used to select the capsule geometry, gas gap configuration, fill gas composition,
and irradiation position. Together, analyses provide the predicted neutron, heating, activation, and thermal
conditions used to develop the MSIRT-1 design.

 1.2 HIGH FLUX ISOTOPE REACTOR
 HFIR is an 85 MW, light-water–cooled, beryllium-reflected research reactor located at ORNL. HFIR
provides several irradiation facilities with different neutron spectra, flux levels, and operating capabilities.
The goal for the design of MSIRT-1 is to accommodate the three flux trap facility types shown in Figure 1:
target rod rabbit holders (TRRHs), peripheral target positions (PTPs), and the hydraulic tube (HT).
 The flux trap facilities contain targets with irradiation capsules, commonly referred to as rabbits,
stacked at discrete axial positions. The neutron flux experienced by each capsule depends on its radial
location in the facility and its axial position within the stack. As shown in Figure 1, the axial flux peaks
near the HFIR fuel centerline and decreases above and below the core midplane. Capsule placement within
the rabbit stack is therefore selected to provide the desired irradiation conditions.

 2

 The radial neutron flux profiles in Figure 2 show the differences between the flux trap positions. The
PTPs are located closest to the fuel elements and provide the highest fast neutron flux of any irradiation
position in HFIR, making them well suited for accelerated displacement damage studies. The more centrally
located TRRHs provide the highest thermal neutron flux in HFIR. Selection of the irradiation position can
thus be used to adjust the relative fast and thermal neutron exposure. The maximum fast and thermal neutron
fluxes achievable in the HFIR flux trap can reach approximately 6.5 and 11 times, respectively, those
available at other domestic test reactor facilities [28].
 Experiments in the TRRH and PTP facilities are inserted and removed during reactor outages between
irradiation cycles. The hydraulic tube allows capsules to be inserted and removed during reactor operation,
enabling controlled irradiations shorter than a full HFIR cycle. Compatibility with all three facility types
provides flexibility in neutron spectrum, flux, accumulated dose, and irradiation duration.

 2. EXPERIMENT DESIGN

 2.1 OBJECTIVES AND REQUIREMENTS
 As previously stated, MSIRT-1 will be the first molten salt irradiation experiment designed specifically
for HFIR and the first implementation of the MSIRT. It uses passive, sealed capsules to study irradiation
and corrosion for different combinations of nonfueled molten salts and candidate structural alloys. The high
neutron flux available in HFIR enables highly accelerated separate effects testing relative to other domestic
irradiation facilities and provides a cost-effective method for evaluating a broad matrix of salt–alloy
combinations. These experiments will generate foundational data for material and salt down-selection
before proceeding to more complex and expensive irradiation of fueled salt.
 The primary scientific objective of MSIRT-1 is to determine how neutron irradiation affects molten
salt chemistry and the corrosion response of candidate structural alloys under MSR-relevant temperature
conditions and accelerated irradiation damage accumulation. Irradiated salts and specimens will be
compared with matched out-of-pile controls to distinguish irradiation effects from high-temperature
corrosion. PIE will characterize changes in salt composition, gas generation, specimen mass,
microstructure, and mechanical properties. These measurements will help identify promising salt–alloy
combinations and inform the selection of experiment parameters for future fueled salt studies.
 MSIRT-1 will also establish the baseline capability for conducting molten salt irradiation experiments
in HFIR. The experiment will provide operational experience with capsule design, fabrication, salt loading,
assembly, safety basis acceptance, irradiation, disassembly, sample recovery, and performance evaluation.
The procedures and infrastructure developed for MSIRT-1, along with lessons learned during its execution,
will provide the basis for later fueled and instrumented MSIRT experiments.
 The capsule must safely contain the molten salt throughout fabrication, handling, irradiation, and post-
irradiation transfer. The design and associated handling procedures must be developed to minimize
exposure of the salt and specimens to moisture, oxygen, and unintended impurities that could alter the salt
chemistry or affect interpretation of the results. The design must account for the HFIR safety constraints
applicable to irradiation experiments in each intended flux trap facility, and the required safety case
evaluations must be completed before approval for reactor insertion. The capsule must also maintain the
specimens within the required temperature range through passive thermal control and provide

 4

representative irradiation and salt exposure conditions. Sufficient volume must be provided for salt
expansion and gas generation, and the gas, salt, corrosion specimens, and thermometry specimens must be
recoverable for PIE.

 2.2 CAPSULE DESIGN
 The design adapts previous HFIR molten metal capsules [31-33] for molten salt containment,
contamination control, specimen exposure, temperature measurement, and post-irradiation recovery. The
capsule was designed with a common configuration that can accommodate testing of different salt
compositions and structural alloys without requiring a new experiment design for every salt–alloy
combination. The overall capsule configuration is shown in Figure 3.
 The experiment consists of a welded molybdenum holder enclosed within an aluminum alloy 6061
capsule housing. The molybdenum holder contains the molten salt, corrosion specimens, and plenum gas.
Molybdenum was selected for its high-temperature strength, weldability, chemical compatibility with the
candidate chloride and fluoride salts, and its resilience to galvanic corrosion with the corrosion specimens.
The aluminum housing provides the structural interface with the HFIR irradiation facility and separates the
internal experiment assembly from the reactor coolant.
 The molybdenum holder contains separate salt and plenum regions. The salt volume is sized to fully
immerse the corrosion specimens while maintaining a salt volume-to-specimen surface area ratio greater
than 2.5 cm [34]. The plenum above the salt accommodates thermal expansion and gases generated during
irradiation. The holder is loaded and sealed in a controlled, dry environment to minimize exposure of the
salt to moisture, oxygen, and other impurities. These controls are necessary because changes in the initial
salt chemistry could affect corrosion behavior and complicate interpretation of the results.
 Two miniature corrosion specimens are positioned within the salt volume on either side of a central
thermometry well. The miniature tensile geometry supports both corrosion evaluation and post-irradiation
mechanical testing [35]. Molybdenum retaining wires maintain the specimen positions during assembly,
handling, and irradiation. Different candidate alloys can be installed without changing the overall capsule
architecture.
 Passive silicon carbide (SiC) thermometry specimens are located within the central well near the
corrosion specimens. The well isolates the thermometry from direct contact with the molten salt, preventing
chemical interaction and impurity introduction as well as maintaining representative thermal coupling to
the specimen region. Following irradiation, dilatometry will be used to estimate the irradiation temperature
and compare it with the predictions from thermal analysis [36].
 Titanium centering thimbles maintain the concentric alignment of the molybdenum holder within the
aluminum housing while preventing direct contact between the two components. A quartz insulator at the
bottom of the capsule limits axial heat transfer and prevents direct contact between the molybdenum holder
and aluminum housing.
 The gas gap between the molybdenum holder and aluminum housing provides the primary means of
controlling the experiment temperature. Nuclear heating generated within the salt and capsule components
is conducted through the holder, across the gas gap, and through the housing to the HFIR coolant. Gas gap
conductance is controlled through a combination of the gap dimension and the helium–argon fill gas
composition. Increasing the helium concentration increases thermal conductance and lowers the experiment
temperature, whereas increasing the argon concentration reduces thermal conductance and raises the
temperature. The gap dimension and fill-gas composition can therefore be selected to achieve irradiation
temperatures representative of molten salt reactor applications, approximately 500–750°C.

 5

 The holder geometry also permits the gas gap dimension to vary along the axial length of the capsule.
These variations allow local control of heat transfer to compensate for axial differences in nuclear heat load
associated with variations in material distribution along the capsule. In addition, the bottom end cap extends
upward through the central thermometry well and into the salt region, displacing salt from the center of the
holder and creating an annular salt geometry. The shorter conduction distance through the relatively low-
conductivity salt reduces radial temperature gradients. Together, the annular salt geometry and variable
axial gas gap improve temperature uniformity across the salt region. The detailed thermal model, gas gap
design, fill gas selection, and predicted temperature distributions are discussed in Section 2.4.
 The capsule outer geometry follows the typical sealed capsule configuration used in the HFIR flux trap
facilities. For each facility, safety analyses are performed to evaluate capsule performance under the
applicable normal operating and off-normal conditions, including conditions such as reactor overpower and
constricted coolant flow. These analyses define a facility-specific safety envelope within which an
experiment can be designed. The envelope establishes limits on parameters such as total material loading,
nuclear heat generation, component temperatures, internal pressure, and minimum and maximum gas gap
dimensions. Each MSIRT-1 configuration must remain within this design space before it can be approved
for reactor insertion.
 The capsule is also designed with post-irradiation recovery in mind. Following transfer to a hot cell
facility, the aluminum housing can be removed to access the molybdenum holder and recover the
thermometry specimens. Within an inert enclosure, the molybdenum holder can then be opened and the
plenum gas can be collected before the salt and corrosion specimens are recovered. This sequence will
support chemical, microstructural, and mechanical characterization, and it will provide experience needed
to develop the more complex post-irradiation handling methods required for future fueled MSIRT
experiments.

 2.3 NEUTRONICS ANALYSIS
 Neutronics analyses were performed to determine component-specific nuclear heating for the MSIRT-
1 capsule. These heating rates provide inputs to the thermal analysis documented in Section 2.4 and support
the HFIR safety evaluation. Calculations were performed using the HFIRCON analysis package [37], which
accounts for prompt neutron, prompt photon, delayed photon, and alpha and beta decay heating.
 Two MSIRT-1 capsule configurations were modeled within a rabbit stack in peripheral target position
A1. A capsule containing eutectic NaCl–MgCl2 was placed at axial position 5, and the capsule containing
eutectic NaCl–CeCl3 was placed directly above it at axial position 6. These two salts were chosen for
analysis because NaCl–MgCl2 is a representative fuel carrier salt with very well understood thermophysical
properties, whereas NaCl–CeCl3 represents a chemically similar surrogate for NaCl–UCl3 [38]. Two empty
aluminum rabbits were modeled above the MSIRT-1 capsules, and four were modeled below them. The
complete rabbit stack was included to provide a representative model of the materials surrounding the
experiments and their effects on the local neutron and photon fields.
 The beginning-of-cycle HFIR MCNP model with homogeneous fuel and a representative experiment
loading was used as the basis for the calculations [39]. The MSIRT-1 geometries were developed from the
capsule engineering drawings and modeled in PTP-A1 for one complete HFIR irradiation cycle. Figure 4
shows axial and radial views of the rabbit stack within the HFIR flux trap, and Figure 5 provides detailed
axial and radial views of the MSIRT-1 capsule model.

 2.4 THERMAL ANALYSIS
 The CAD model shown in Figure 3 was imported into the Ansys finite element analysis software. A
one-half model using 180° symmetry was used to reduce computational requirements. Conductive and
radiative heat transfer were included in the model. Heat transfer within the molten salt was represented by
solid body conduction; natural convection of the salt was not explicitly modeled.
 The component-specific heat generation rates calculated in Section 2.3 are scaled to the flux distribution
of the flux trap facilities and were applied to the corresponding capsule components depending on axial
position. Heat generated within the salt and internal components is transferred through the molybdenum
holder, across the gas gap, and through the aluminum housing. A convective boundary condition was
applied to the external surface of the housing to represent heat rejection to the HFIR coolant.
 The ORNL Irradiation Engineering Group maintains a database of design and analysis calculations
(DACs) containing temperature-dependent thermophysical properties used in irradiation experiment
thermal analyses in HFIR. These DACs are available upon request. A temperature-dependent material

 9

property DAC for eutectic NaCl–MgCl2 was developed to define the salt properties in the Ansys thermal
model. The NaCl–MgCl2 DAC consolidates evaluated thermophysical property data and correlations from
the Molten Salt Database (MSD) and additional literature sources [40-44].
 The model was parameterized to evaluate the effects of gas gap geometry, fill gas composition, and
internal capsule geometry on the predicted temperature distribution. Two annular gas gap regions are used
between the molybdenum holder and aluminum housing: an upper gas gap and a lower gas gap. The upper
gas gap dimension is varied directly, while the lower gas gap dimension is adjusted independently by
changing the axial extent of the lower holder geometry. This allows the thermal resistance in the upper and
lower portions of the capsule to be controlled separately and provides a means of reducing axial temperature
gradients within the salt. The helium fraction in the He–Ar fill gas was also parameterized to control the
overall gap conductance. Additional geometric parameters included salt height and central pin geometry,
with dependent dimensions calculated from the primary design variables to maintain geometric consistency.
 Ansys Mechanical and Mechanical APDL scripts were used for automated post-processing to extract
minimum, maximum, and volume-averaged temperatures for the corrosion specimens, salt, holder, housing,
thermometry, and other internal components. The specimen gauge regions were evaluated separately
because they define the primary irradiation target temperature. The molten salt temperature distribution was
also evaluated using element volume–weighted axial averaging to account for the nonuniform finite-
element mesh and provide an axial salt temperature profile for assessing temperature gradients.
 These design variables were incorporated into a parameterized thermal model in Ansys Workbench.
The model was then coupled to Ansys optiSLang using a surrogate-based optimization workflow. An
Adaptive Metamodel of Optimal Prognosis (AMOP) analysis was first used to explore the design space and
construct surrogate models relating the design variables to the calculated thermal responses. Global
adaptive refinement was used with a maximum of 50 Ansys design samples, and polynomial regression,
Moving Least Squares, and isotropic Kriging models were evaluated during metamodel construction. The
resulting metamodels were then passed to the optiSLang One-Click Optimization workflow, which
evaluated up to 2,000 candidate designs.
 For the baseline NaCl–MgCl2 experiment, the optimization objective was to minimize the axial
temperature difference within the molten salt while constraining the average corrosion specimen gauge
temperature to 595–605°C. A minimum salt volume-to-specimen surface area ratio of 2.5 cm was also
imposed. The optimization therefore identified combinations of upper and lower gas gap dimensions, fill
gas composition, and internal geometry that maintained the nominal 600°C specimen irradiation condition
while reducing axial temperature variation within the salt.
 This thermal modeling and optimization workflow provides a reusable thermal design framework for
evaluating additional salt configurations within MSIRT-1. Once nuclear heating rates and thermophysical
properties are determined for another salt composition, the same model can be used to evaluate the
corresponding operating temperature and determine an appropriate capsule configuration. Thermal analysis
of the NaCl–CeCl3 capsule has not been completed as of this writing.

2.4.1 Thermal Results
 The AMOP analysis was first used to identify the design parameters with the greatest influence on the
principal thermal responses. Figure 6 presents the Coefficient of Prognosis (CoP) matrix for the selected
input parameters and responses. The average specimen gauge temperature was governed primarily by the
helium fraction and the dimension of the upper gas gap region, and the molten salt minimum, maximum,
and average temperatures showed similar dependence on these parameters. The salt axial temperature
difference and axial gradient were influenced by several geometric parameters, with the upper gas gap and
lower gas gap geometry providing the strongest contributions. The total CoP values indicate that the

metamodels provided strong predictive capability for the principal temperature responses used in the

 Figure 7 illustrates the corresponding response surfaces for the two primary thermal design responses.
The predicted average specimen gauge temperature varies strongly with both helium fraction and upper gas
gap dimension, demonstrating that these parameters provide the primary control of the nominal irradiation
temperature. The salt axial temperature difference also depends strongly on the upper gas gap and the
geometry controlling the lower gas-gap region, demonstrating the importance of independently controlling
the thermal resistance along the capsule length. These response surfaces were used to define the
combinations of geometric and fill gas parameters capable of maintaining the specimen near the 600°C
target while reducing axial temperature variation within the salt.
 The AMOP metamodels were subsequently used in the One-Click Optimization workflow to identify
a feasible design that minimized the molten salt axial temperature difference. Figure 8 shows the
optimization history for the objective function. Early design evaluations included a large number of
infeasible configurations that violated the constraints on either the specimen temperature or salt volume-to-
surface-area. As the optimization progressed, the search converged toward a region containing a greater
proportion of feasible designs and substantially lower salt axial temperature differences. The selected
design is a feasible solution that satisfies the specified specimen gauge temperature range of 595–605°C
and minimum salt volume-to-specimen surface-area ratio of 2.5 cm, and it minimizes the axial salt
temperature difference within the explored design space. The resulting design provides a salt volume of
1.67 cm3 and a salt volume-to-specimen surface-area ratio of 2.9 cm.

 The selected design parameters were transferred back to the full Ansys finite element model for direct
evaluation. The resulting component temperatures are summarized in Table 3, and the corresponding
temperature distributions are shown in Figure 9.
 The specimen gauge regions have a predicted average temperature of approximately 602°C, near the
nominal 600°C irradiation target, while the passive SiC thermometry specimens are predicted to operate
near 598°C. The molten salt exhibits a broader local temperature distribution, with an overall average
temperature of approximately 578°C. Because the salt is modeled using solid-body conduction, thermally
driven natural convection within the molten salt is not represented. In the physical experiment, temperature
gradients within the molten salt are expected to produce density-driven natural convection, which will
redistribute heat and reduce radial temperature nonuniformity. The largest predicted salt-temperature
gradients and localized extrema occur primarily in the upper portion of the salt column, whereas the lower
region containing the corrosion specimens and adjacent SiC thermometry is substantially more uniform.
The conduction-only model therefore predicts a conservative local minimum salt temperature, including a
localized region below the NaCl–MgCl2 melting temperature, as shown in Table 3, that may not persist
once the salt is molten and convective heat transfer is established. The axial salt-temperature distribution is
therefore evaluated using element-volume-weighted averaging, as shown in Figure 10, to characterize the
bulk temperature variation along the salt column without emphasizing localized radial extrema from the
conduction-only model. Minimizing the axial salt-temperature variation is a primary design objective
because a flatter axial temperature profile reduces the potential for natural convection between axially
separated hotter and cooler salt regions to alter the local thermal environment around the corrosion
specimens and thereby reduce confidence in the conduction-only specimen temperature predictions.

 2.5 ACTIVATION ANALYSIS
 Activation calculations were performed to estimate the radionuclide inventories and the activities of
the MSIRT-1 capsules following irradiation. The results provide information needed for capsule transfer,
hot cell handling, disassembly, and PIE. They also identify transmutation products generated within the
salts that could affect salt chemistry and interpretation of the corrosion results. As previously stated,
calculations were performed using HAWK [26], a Python-based interface to the ORIGEN depletion and
decay code within SCALE 6.3.2 [27]. The activation models used a representative 23.5-day HFIR
irradiation cycle. The NaCl–MgCl2 capsule was modeled in PTP-A1 axial position 5, and the NaCl–CeCl3
capsule was modeled in axial position 6, consistent with the neutronics model described in Section 2.3.
Activities were evaluated after an approximately 31-day post-irradiation decay period.
 The calculations were performed during an earlier stage of the capsule design, when molybdenum and
Nickel 200 were both being considered as holder materials. The NaCl–MgCl2 model included an aluminum
housing and molybdenum holder, whereas the NaCl–CeCl3 model included an aluminum housing and
Nickel 200 holder. These calculations provided an initial comparison of the activation inventories
associated with the two candidate holder materials. Activation behavior was not the primary basis for the
final holder selection; molybdenum was selected principally because of its superior chemical compatibility
with the candidate salts, and resilience to galvanic corrosion effects on the corrosion coupons, which could
otherwise confound post-irradiation examination results.

2.5.1 Activation Results
 Tables 4 and 5 present the activation products with the highest calculated activities for the NaCl–MgCl2
and NaCl–CeCl3 configurations, respectively. The tables report isotope mass, activity at the assay date, and
percentage of the applicable Category 3 nuclear facility threshold quantity.
 For the NaCl–MgCl2 configuration, sulfur-35 was the dominant activation product, with a calculated
activity of 85.36 Ci and 377.695% of its Category 3 threshold quantity. Phosphorus-32 was the next largest

 15

contributor, with an activity of 2.425 Ci. For the NaCl–CeCl3 configuration, cerium-141 had the highest
calculated activity at 58.30 Ci. Sulfur-35 had an activity of 30.95 Ci and the largest contribution relative to
its Category 3 threshold quantity at 136.960%.
 The results also provide an indication of the transmutation products expected within the irradiated salts.
Sulfur-35 and phosphorus-32 are produced through neutron reactions with chlorine, while cerium‑141 is
produced through activation of cerium in the NaCl–CeCl3 salt. In addition to their importance for
radiological handling, these products represent irradiation-generated changes in the salt composition. Their
formation may influence salt chemistry and corrosion behavior and must therefore be considered when
interpreting post-irradiation results. This distinction is important when separating corrosion changes
associated with salt chemistry evolution from effects directly associated with displacement damage in the
structural specimens.

 3. STATUS AND REMAINING WORK

 3.1 CURRENT STATUS
 The baseline MSIRT-1 capsule design and scoping analyses for the NaCl–MgCl2 configuration have
been completed. Neutronics results have been produced for the NaCl–MgCl2 and NaCl–CeCl3
configurations. The thermal model has been completed for NaCl–MgCl2, and analysis of NaCl–CeCl3 is

 16

underway. Both models are parameterized to support additional salt compositions and specimen materials
as funding and programmatic interest permit.
 A draft set of engineering drawings has been prepared. The HFIR safety analysis framework has been
configured, but the calculations have not yet been performed, and the safety envelope has not been
established. Final approval of the drawings is therefore pending confirmation of safety-significant
dimensions and tolerances.
 A conceptual inert atmosphere fixture and procedure for recovering the plenum gas, salt, specimens,
and thermometry have been discussed with hot cell personnel and determined to be feasible. However, hot
cell work controls for beryllium-bearing salts have not been established. This remains a barrier to using
FLiBe and was a primary reason for selecting NaCl–MgCl2 for the initial MSIRT-1 demonstration. Existing
hot cell capabilities can support fuel-bearing salts, although future MSIRT designs may require modified
disassembly fixtures should the capsule geometry change for later MSIRT experiments.

 3.2 FUTURE WORK
 The next major activity is completion of the HFIR safety evaluation. This analysis will establish limits
on material loading, nuclear heat generation, temperatures, internal pressure, gas gap dimensions, and other
safety-significant parameters. The engineering drawings and HFIR insertion approval documentation can
then be finalized.
 Out-of-pile testing is needed to demonstrate capsule assembly, leak tightness, and performance during
representative thermal cycling. Previous HFIR molten-metal experiments provide confidence that the inner
molybdenum holder can maintain a hermetic seal under irradiation conditions. A disassembly fixture must
also be designed and tested to demonstrate controlled recovery of the gas, salt, corrosion specimens, and
thermometry without introducing contamination.
 Following safety approval and out-of-pile testing, the remaining activities include procurement,
fabrication, capsule assembly, and preparation for irradiation. Additional salt configurations can be
analyzed using the existing neutronics and thermal models, and conceptual design work can begin for the
fueled MSIRT-2 experiment. Subject to funding and completion of the remaining safety, testing, and
insertion-approval activities, MSIRT-1 can proceed to irradiation readiness.

 4. CONCLUSIONS
 MSIRT-1 establishes a practical design basis for the first molten salt irradiation experiment developed
specifically for HFIR. The passive capsule enables high-flux, separate-effects testing of multiple salt–alloy
combinations while providing containment, contamination control, passive temperature control, and
recovery of experiment materials for post-irradiation examination. The completed capsule design and
supporting neutronics, activation, and baseline NaCl–MgCl2 thermal analyses demonstrate the technical
feasibility of the experiment and provide adaptable models for evaluating additional chloride and fluoride
salt systems.
 The remaining work includes completing the HFIR safety evaluation, finalizing engineering drawings
and insertion documentation, and conducting out-of-pile assembly, thermal cycling, and disassembly
testing. Subject to funding and successful completion of these activities, MSIRT-1 can proceed to
fabrication and irradiation. The experience and infrastructure developed through MSIRT-1 will support
future fueled and instrumented MSIRT experiments in HFIR.
