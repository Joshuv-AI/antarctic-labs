Healthcare-associated post-neurosurgical meningitis caused by extended-spectrum beta-lactamase-producing Klebsiella pneumoniae in an Infant: A case report
Authors: Shinsuke Mizuno, Junji Koyama, Hiroshi Kurosawa, Naoko Imuta, Junichiro Nishi, Masashi Kasai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Healthcare-associated meningitis caused by extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL)-producing Klebsiella pneumoniae is a well-recognized condition after neurosurgical procedures in adults; however, it remains uncommon in children, particularly when detailed microbiological characterization is available. Here we report a case of ESBL-producing K. pneumoniae meningitis with hypervirulent features following neurosurgical intervention in an infant. A previously healthy male infant developed fever and seizures on day of life 58, 2 days after external ventricular drain (EVD) placement and tumor resection. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis revealed pleocytosis and gram-negative bacilli. Intravenous meropenem was initiated, and EVD was removed. Cultures from CSF and the catheter yielded ESBL-producing K. pneumoniae (ST37) harboring blaCTX-M-15, blaSHV-11, and blaTEM-1. The isolate demonstrated hypermucoviscosity and carried virulence-associated genes. CSF sterilization was achieved within 5 days; however, the clinical course was complicated by cranial nerve deficits. The patient completed 8 weeks of therapy and was discharged with persistent neurological sequelae. The findings from this case suggest that ESBL-producing Gram-negative bacilli should be considered for infants with post-neurosurgical meningitis when risk factors such as EVD placement, CSF leakage, and healthcare exposure are present. Prompt source control and empiric antimicrobial therapy guided by local resistance epidemiology are critical. Although resistance and virulence-associated traits were identified in our case, their contribution to the neurological sequelae remains uncertain.

Introduction

Healthcare-associated ventriculitis and meningitis are serious complications of neurosurgical procedures and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) diversion devices in children. An external ventricular drains (EVD) is an established risk factor because it allows direct communication between the ventricular system and the external environment. Although Gram-positive organisms remain the most common pathogens, Gram-negative bacilli account for a substantial proportion of device-associated infections and are associated with higher morbidity, particularly when appropriate antimicrobial therapy is delayed [1], [2].

Among Gram-negative pathogens, Klebsiella pneumoniae is a well-recognized cause of post-neurosurgical meningitis in adults; however, it remains an uncommon causative pathogen for pediatric healthcare-associated meningitis, with relatively few infant cases reported in the literature [2], [3], [4]. Pediatric healthcare-associated meningitis caused by multidrug-resistant Gram-negative bacilli remains poorly characterized with respect to microbiological features, antimicrobial resistance mechanisms, and factors associated with clinical outcomes, despite the increasing prevalence of extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL)-producing Enterobacterales in pediatric healthcare settings [5], [6]. As the prevalence of ESBL-producing Enterobacterales continues to increase worldwide, delayed recognition of resistant Gram-negative bacilli may compromise empirical antimicrobial therapy in high-risk postoperative patients [5], [6].

To our knowledge, few pediatric reports have combined comprehensive molecular characterization of ESBL-producing K. pneumoniae isolates with detailed clinical descriptions of healthcare-associated post-neurosurgical meningitis. Here, we report a case involving an infant with healthcare-associated post-neurosurgical meningitis caused by ESBL-producing K. pneumoniae following EVD placement and CSF leakage. In addition to describing the clinical course, we characterized the isolate by antimicrobial susceptibility testing and molecular analysis. The findings from this case highlight the clinical circumstances in which ESBL-producing Gram-negative bacilli should be considered during empirical antimicrobial selection for the treatment of pediatric post-neurosurgical meningitis.

Case

A boy was born via an uncomplicated spontaneous vaginal delivery at the gestational age of 38 weeks and 3 days (birth weight, 2818 g; head circumference, 32.5 cm). Cultures of vaginal and stool samples from the mother at the gestational age of 34 weeks were negative for pathogenic organisms. On day of life (DOL) 51, the infant developed difficulty in breastfeeding with a bulging anterior frontanelle. Brain computed tomography revealed a fourth ventricular mass with hydrocephalus. Consequently, EVD was inserted using a subcutaneous tunnel technique on the same day. On DOL 56, tumor removal was performed, and pathological examination showed brainstem cavernous malformations.

On DOL 58, the infant developed fever and convulsions. His body temperature was 3 9.8°C with an unstable pulse and respiratory rate and a Glasgow Coma Scale for infants of E4V1M4. Physical examination was normal; however, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leakage at the EVD exit site, alongside the catheter, was observed. White blood cell count was normal; however, elevated C-reactive protein concentration was detected (Fig. 1). CSF examination revealed a high cell count and gram-negative bacilli. Intravenous meropenem was initiated, and the EVD tube was removed. Blood cultures obtained on DOL 58 tested negative, but CSF and the removed EVD tube cultures tested positive for K. pneumoniae, specifically the ST37 complex detected by multi-locus sequence typing. Antibiotic susceptibility tests indicated an ESBL-producing strain. Study of antibiotic-resistance genes detected blaCTX-M−15, blaSHV−11, and blaTEM−1. The “string test” result was positive (Fig. 1). After 5 days of treatment, CSF culture tested negative. However, on DOL 63, the patient presented with dysphagia, and on DOL 64, ophthalmological, fundus, and laryngoscope examinations indicated the development of left facial and vagus nerve palsy. On DOL 84, brain magnetic resonance imaging and abdominal ultrasonography showed normal findings. Automated auditory brainstem response testing and electroencephalography revealed no abnormalities during hospitalization. Antibiotic treatment was continued for 8 weeks. The patient was discharged on DOL 136 with left facial nerve and vagus nerve palsy.

Discussion

Healthcare-associated meningitis remains one of the most serious complications following neurosurgical procedures in children, particularly those requiring CSF diversion devices. EVD is a well-established risk factor because it disrupts the barrier of the central nervous system and provides a potential entry route of infectious organisms. Current guidelines identify Gram-positive cocci as the predominant pathogens; however, Gram-negative bacilli, including K. pneumoniae, account for a substantial proportion of healthcare-associated ventriculitis and meningitis cases, particularly in patients with prolonged hospitalization or those receiving repeated neurosurgical procedures [1]. In our patient, meningitis developed shortly after tumor resection with EVD placement, and CSF leakage was observed at the catheter exit site. Isolation of the same organism from both CSF and the removed EVD strongly supported device-associated infection rather than hematogenous spread. Prompt initiation of meropenem together with EVD removal resulted in microbiological cure, although cranial nerve deficits remained.

Although K. pneumoniae is a well-recognized pathogen in adult post-neurosurgical meningitis, pediatric reports remain uncommon. In large pediatric case series of healthcare-associated central nervous system infections, Gram-positive organisms, particularly coagulase-negative Staphylococci and Staphylococcus aureus, predominated, whereas Gram-negative bacilli account for a smaller proportion of cases [1], [2]. Among these Gram-negative pathogens, K. pneumoniae has been reported only sporadically in infants, and detailed microbiological characterization has been rarely performed [3]. Carrie et al. described the epidemiological and clinical features of Klebsiella meningitis in infants; however, most reported cases involved community-acquired infections, and molecular analyses of antimicrobial resistance or determinants of virulence were limited [3]. Thus, the present case expands the limited pediatric literature by providing detailed clinical, microbiological, and molecular characterization of healthcare-associated ESBL-producing K. pneumoniae meningitis following neurosurgery.

The principal clinical message of this report is not the identification of K. pneumoniae itself, but the recognition of clinical situations in which ESBL-producing Gram-negative bacilli should be considered during empirical antimicrobial selection. Previous hospitalization, recent neurosurgical intervention, indwelling ventricular devices, prolonged antimicrobial exposure, and CSF leakage have all been associated with healthcare-associated Gram-negative meningitis and infection caused by resistant Enterobacterales [1], [5], [6]. Our patient simultaneously exhibited several of these risk factors, including recent EVD placement, postoperative CSF leakage, and healthcare exposure. In such circumstances, empirical meropenem may be appropriate, particularly in institutions with a high prevalence of infections due to ESBL-producing Enterobacterales. Conversely, routine empirical carbapenem therapy cannot be recommended for all cases of postoperative meningitis because excessive carbapenem use may further promote antimicrobial resistance. Therefore, empirical therapy should continue to be guided by local epidemiology, institutional susceptibility patterns, and individual patient risk factors, as recommended by current guidelines [1].

The isolate belonged to ST37 and harbored blaCTX-M−15, blaSHV−11, and blaTEM−1, together with hypermucoviscosity and virulence-associated genes. Although the convergence of antimicrobial resistance and virulence has been increasingly recognized for K. pneumoniae worldwide [7], [8], the clinical significance of these microbiological findings in pediatric healthcare-associated meningitis remains uncertain. Current evidence does not demonstrate that these bacterial characteristics independently predict neurological complications in children. In the present patient, the persistent cranial nerve deficits were likely multifactorial, reflecting the combined effects of the underlying posterior fossa lesion, neurosurgical manipulation, CSF leakage, and inflammatory damage rather than the effect of the bacterial genotype alone. Accordingly, the findings should not be interpreted as evidence supporting poorer neurological outcomes as a consequence of resistant or virulence-associated clones.

In conclusion, the findings from this case suggest that ESBL-producing K. pneumoniae should be considered when multiple risk factors for resistant Gram-negative infection are present in cases involving infants with healthcare-associated post-neurosurgical meningitis. Early recognition, prompt source control through device removal, and empirical antimicrobial therapy guided by local resistance epidemiology remain the most important clinical lessons from this case. Although comprehensive molecular characterization contributes to an understanding of the microbiology of pediatric healthcare-associated meningitis, further multicenter pediatric studies are needed to clarify the epidemiology, microbiological characteristics, and prognostic significance of resistant Gram-negative pathogens in this population.
