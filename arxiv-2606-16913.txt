Optimal Multiscale Learning of Linear Operators
Jiaheng Chen and Daniel Sanz-Alonso

Abstract
We study the statistical and computational limits of learning bounded linear operators between Sobolev spaces from noisy input–output data. In wavelet coordinates, the problem is recast as an infinite-dimensional matrix regression problem with a heterogeneous two-sided multiscale structure. We establish minimax rates under Sobolev operator-norm loss and construct a finite-resolution blockwise least-squares estimator attaining these rates. The analysis reveals a nonuniform local estimation difficulty across scales, which can be exploited algorithmically: by assigning scale-adaptive sample sizes, the estimator achieves the optimal computational cost among dense least-squares implementations.

Introduction

Operator learning is an emerging paradigm at the intersection of scientific computing and machine learning
[

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

]
. Its central objective is to learn maps between function spaces from data, a setting that arises, for instance, in the approximation of solution operators, parameter-to-observable maps, and time-evolution maps for dynamical systems. In a supervised learning formulation, one observes noisy input–output pairs
\{(u_{i},f_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
satisfying

(1.1)

\displaystyle f_{i}=\mathcal{A}u_{i}+w_{i},\qquad 1\leq i\leq N,

where
\mathcal{A}
is an unknown operator and
\{w_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
models observational noise. The goal is to design an estimator of
\mathcal{A}
that is statistically accurate and computationally efficient.

This paper studies this problem for bounded linear operators between Sobolev
spaces on a
d
-dimensional domain. For fixed Sobolev indices
s,s^{\prime}
and radius
R>0
, we consider the operator class

\mathcal{O}(s,s^{\prime},R):=\bigl\{\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{L}(H^{s},H^{-s^{\prime}}):\|\mathcal{A}\|_{H^{s}\to H^{-s^{\prime}}}\leq R\bigr\}.

The inputs and noise are Gaussian fields,

u_{i}\sim\mathcal{GP}(0,(I-\Delta)^{-r_{1}})
and

w_{i}\sim\mathcal{GP}(0,(I-\Delta)^{-r_{2}})
. The estimation error is
measured in the Sobolev operator norm
H^{t}\to H^{-t^{\prime}}
, where
t>s
and

t^{\prime}>s^{\prime}
. This model imposes no PDE-specific, geometric, or sparsity structure on the target operator. Instead, the problem is governed by the interaction between the Sobolev scales of the operator class, the input distribution, the noise model, and the loss norm; this structure alone is sufficient to obtain polynomial statistical and computational rates.

Our first main result, Theorem
2.1
, establishes the minimax rate up
to logarithmic factors:

\displaystyle\inf_{\widehat{\mathcal{A}}}\sup_{\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{O}(s,s^{\prime},R)}\mathbb{E}\,\|\widehat{\mathcal{A}}-\mathcal{A}\|_{H^{t}\to H^{-t^{\prime}}}\,\asymp_{\log}\,N^{-\min\big\{\frac{1}{2},\frac{t-s}{2(r_{1}-s)+d},\frac{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}{(2(-r_{2}-s^{\prime})+d)_{+}}\big\}}.

The three terms in the exponent correspond to a coarse-scale parametric
contribution, an input-side obstruction caused by weak excitation of
high-frequency input directions, and an output-side obstruction caused by estimating rough output components from noisy observations.
Thus the rate reflects a
two-sided multiscale bias–variance geometry.

The upper bound is constructive. In wavelet coordinates, the problem becomes an
infinite-dimensional matrix regression problem with a diagonally weighted
operator-norm loss.
We construct a finite-resolution blockwise least-squares estimator that attains the minimax rate while estimating a minimal collection of wavelet blocks. A nested-support regression step controls the omitted-variable bias arising from interactions between
retained and discarded input scales.
The estimator requires only finite-resolution observations of the training input–output pairs, with the required resolution determined by the sample size. The minimax lower bound is obtained by applying Assouad’s lemma to three thin-strip
perturbation families in the wavelet matrix model. These perturbations isolate
the coarse-scale, input-side, and output-side obstructions, respectively, and
show that each term in the minimax exponent is unavoidable.

Our second main contribution is computational. The local estimation difficulty
is highly nonuniform across scales: some blocks determine the global minimax
rate, while others can be estimated to the same target accuracy using fewer
samples. We exploit this inhomogeneity through scale-adaptive sample sizes.
Proposition
5.1
shows that, if the target accuracy is

\varepsilon
, then the cost of our estimator satisfies

\mathsf{Cost}(\varepsilon)\lesssim_{\log}\varepsilon^{-\max\big\{2+\frac{2d}{\min\{r_{1},t\}-s}+\frac{(2(r_{1}-t)+d)_{+}}{t-s},\ \frac{d+(2(-r_{2}-s^{\prime})+d)_{+}}{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}\big\}}.

The two terms in this maximum correspond to input-side and output-side
computational contributions. We further explain why these contributions are
unavoidable within the dense least-squares framework. In addition, we show that the statistical and computational bottlenecks need not coincide, highlighting that minimax rates and computational cost provide complementary perspectives on the operator learning problem.

introduction reviews related work and sets notation. Section
2

3

bias–variance geometry, the identification of a minimal collection of wavelet blocks sufficient for attaining the minimax rate, and scale-adaptive
sample usage. Section
4
constructs the estimator and
proves the minimax upper and lower bounds. Section
5

analyzes the computational cost and discusses its optimality. Appendix
A
contains auxiliary estimates used in the
proofs.

A growing body of work studies approximation, statistical, and computational

,

,

. Our results identify a
complementary polynomial-rate regime: bounded linear operators between Sobolev
spaces form a broad infinite-dimensional nonparametric class, but still admit
polynomial minimax rates under Sobolev operator-norm loss. These rates are
obtained from linearity and Sobolev-scale boundedness, without holomorphic,
source-type, or sparsity assumptions on the target operator.

Closer to our setting are works on learning linear operators and related
infinite-dimensional regression problems. Linear operator learning has been
studied for diagonal or spectrally structured operators

[
17
]
, through an infinite-dimensional regression
formulation as a non-compact inverse problem
[

in online settings
[

[

,

,

. In particular,

[

studies minimax optimal kernel operator learning for
Hilbert–Schmidt operators between Sobolev reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces,
under capacity, embedding, and source-type assumptions. In contrast, we study
the operator-norm ball
\mathcal{O}(s,s^{\prime},R)
under the Sobolev operator norm

H^{t}\to H^{-t^{\prime}}
. This worst-case loss leads to a two-sided multiscale
geometry in wavelet coordinates. Moreover, our estimator uses finite-resolution
coefficient data and blockwise least squares with scale-adaptive sample usage.
We derive an explicit computational cost for this estimator and show its optimality within the dense least-squares framework. This finite-resolution
computational viewpoint is absent from
[
21

Another closely related line of work exploits PDE or geometric structure in

parabolic, and hyperbolic PDEs

[

,

,

[

model. Instead, we analyze the statistical and computational limits generated
by linearity and Sobolev-scale boundedness. A central feature of the resulting theory is
that the minimax statistical rate and the computational cost needed to attain
it may be governed by different multiscale bottlenecks.

Throughout the paper, we work on the

\mathbb{T}^{d}=(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})^{d}
to avoid boundary effects. Similar
formulations on domains with boundary are possible after choosing appropriate
boundary conditions. For

, let

denote the usual

\mathbb{T}^{d}

the dual of
H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})

through the

pairing. Thus, if
f\in H^{-s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})

\langle f,g\rangle:=\langle f,g\rangle_{H^{-s}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}.

This agrees with the usual

and

belong to
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
. For notational simplicity, we write
H^{s}
in place of
H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
throughout the paper, and we omit the subscript in the duality pairing whenever no ambiguity can arise.

For a vector

\|x\|_{2}
its Euclidean
norm. For a matrix

, we write
\|B\|

\|B\|_{\mathrm{F}}

generally, for a matrix
B\in\mathbb{R}^{\Lambda_{1}\times\Lambda_{2}}

\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}

\ell^{2}(\Lambda_{1})

\|B\|

For

, we write

, and we use the convention

a/0=+\infty
for
a>0
. Given two positive sequences

a_{k}\leq c\,b_{k}

Main results

For
s,s^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
, let
\mathcal{L}(H^{s},H^{-s^{\prime}})
denote the space of bounded
linear operators from
H^{s}
to
H^{-s^{\prime}}
, equipped with the operator norm

\|\mathcal{A}\|_{H^{s}\to H^{-s^{\prime}}}:=\sup_{\|w\|_{H^{s}}\leq 1,\ \|v\|_{H^{s^{\prime}}}\leq 1}|\langle\mathcal{A}w,v\rangle|,

where the duality pairing is between
H^{-s^{\prime}}
and
H^{s^{\prime}}
. For
R>0
,
define

\mathcal{O}(s,s^{\prime},R):=\bigl\{\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{L}(H^{s},H^{-s^{\prime}}):\|\mathcal{A}\|_{H^{s}\to H^{-s^{\prime}}}\leq R\bigr\}.

Throughout the paper, the radius
R>0
is fixed, and implicit constants may
depend on
R
and on the fixed regularity parameters
s
and
s^{\prime}
.

We observe input–output data pairs
\{(u_{i},f_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
generated by

(2.1)

f_{i}=\mathcal{A}u_{i}+w_{i},\qquad 1\leq i\leq N,

where
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{O}(s,s^{\prime},R)
is unknown,

u_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{GP}(0,(I-\Delta)^{-r_{1}}),\qquad w_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{GP}(0,(I-\Delta)^{-r_{2}}),

for some
r_{1},r_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
, and the input and noise sequences
\{u_{i}\}
and
\{w_{i}\}
are independent. The Gaussian fields above are understood through the standard spectral construction associated with the Laplacian; in particular, a field with covariance
(I-\Delta)^{-r}
belongs almost surely to
H^{\alpha}
for every
\alpha<r-d/2

[
16
,
19
]
. The equality in (
2.1
) is understood in the distributional sense. To ensure that
\mathcal{A}u_{i}
is well-defined, we assume
r_{1}-d/2>s
. Then
u_{i}\in H^{r_{1}-d/2-\varepsilon}\hookrightarrow H^{s}
almost surely for some
\varepsilon>0
, and hence
\mathcal{A}u_{i}
is a well-defined element of
H^{-s^{\prime}}
.

Given the data
\{(u_{i},f_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
, the goal is to estimate the unknown operator
\mathcal{A}
. We measure estimation error in the Sobolev operator norm
\|\cdot\|_{H^{t}\to H^{-t^{\prime}}}
, where
t>s
and
t^{\prime}>s^{\prime}
. The embeddings
H^{t}\hookrightarrow H^{s}
and

Theorem 2.1

(Minimax risk)
.

Let
s,s^{\prime},r_{1},r_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
with
r_{1}-d/2>s
, and let
t>s
and
t^{\prime}>s^{\prime}

\displaystyle\inf_{\widehat{\mathcal{A}}}\sup_{\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{O}(s,s^{\prime},R)}\mathbb{E}\,\|\widehat{\mathcal{A}}-\mathcal{A}\|_{H^{t}\to H^{-t^{\prime}}}\,\asymp_{\log}\,N^{-\min\big\{\frac{1}{2},\frac{t-s}{2(r_{1}-s)+d},\frac{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}{(2(-r_{2}-s^{\prime})+d)_{+}}\big\}}.

Remark 2.2
(Computational cost).

The upper bound in Theorem
2.1
is achieved by an estimator that
does not require full functional data, but rather only a finite-dimensional
wavelet representation at resolutions chosen according to the sample size; see
Subsection
4.1
. Specifically, if the target error is

\varepsilon
, then the output data are only needed up to scale

\big\lceil\frac{\log_{2}(1/\varepsilon)}{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}\big\rceil
, while the input data
are used up to scale
\big\lceil\frac{\log_{2}(1/\varepsilon)}{\min\{r_{1},t\}-s}\big\rceil
.
Section
5
gives a detailed analysis of the resulting
computational cost when the induced finite-dimensional regressions are solved by
dense direct least-squares methods. In particular, Proposition
5.1
shows that the estimator attains the minimax rate
with cost

\mathsf{Cost}(\varepsilon)\lesssim_{\log}\varepsilon^{-\max\big\{2+\frac{2d}{\min\{r_{1},t\}-s}+\frac{(2(r_{1}-t)+d)_{+}}{t-s},\ \frac{d+(2(-r_{2}-s^{\prime})+d)_{+}}{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}\big\}},

where the two terms in the maximum correspond to input-side and output-side

explains

Remark 2.3
(Discussion on the convergence rate).

The minimax rate in Theorem
2.1
depends on the parameters
summarized in Table
1
. The exponent is the minimum of
three quantities,

\frac{1}{2},\qquad\frac{t-s}{2(r_{1}-s)+d},\qquad\frac{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}{\bigl(2(-r_{2}-s^{\prime})+d\bigr)_{+}}.

The first term is the coarse-scale parametric contribution. The second term is
an input-side contribution: it reflects the difficulty of learning operator
components that act on increasingly oscillatory input directions and produce
low-regularity output information. Since smoother input distributions excite
high-frequency input directions less strongly, increasing
r_{1}
slows down
this part of the rate. The third term is an output-side contribution: it
reflects the difficulty of recovering output components at increasingly low
regularity in the presence of rough observation noise. As
r_{2}
increases,
the noise becomes smoother and this output-side obstruction weakens.

This should be compared with the standard minimax rate in nonparametric
regression
[
40
,
22
]
. In a typical model

y_{i}=h(x_{i})+\xi_{i},\qquad\xi_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(0,1),

estimating a real-valued function
h
on
[0,1]^{d}
with Sobolev smoothness
\beta
under an
L^{2}

loss gives a rate of order

N^{-\frac{\beta}{2\beta+d}}.

In the present
problem, the unknown object is a linear operator rather than a function. The
rate therefore has a two-sided structure: one side is associated with how the
operator acts on input directions, and the other with the regularity of the
output components that must be recovered. This two-sided structure gives rise to
the multiscale geometry and computational considerations developed in the following
sections.
∎

Remark 2.4
(Data and noise distributions).

For simplicity of exposition, we take the input and noise distributions to be

\mathcal{GP}(0,(I-\Delta)^{-r_{1}})
and

\mathcal{GP}(0,(I-\Delta)^{-r_{2}})
, respectively. The analysis only uses the
corresponding multiscale covariance equivalences: in a suitable wavelet basis,
the input and noise covariance operators are diagonally preconditioned by the
Sobolev weights associated with
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
; see Lemma
3.1
. Thus the same result holds for more general Gaussian inputs and noises satisfying
analogous elliptic preconditioning bounds, for instance strongly elliptic
pseudo-differential covariance operators; see

[
18
,
14
]
.

In Theorem
2.1
, we assume only
r_{1}-d/2>s
, which ensures that

\mathcal{A}u_{i}
is well defined, and impose no regularity condition on

r_{2}
. The observation model (
2.1
) is therefore
understood in the distributional sense: the noise may be a generalized
Gaussian field rather than a function-valued process. In wavelet coordinates,
this gives a heteroscedastic infinite-dimensional regression model with
scale-dependent input and noise variances.

3
Main ideas

This section discusses four key ideas underlying the construction and analysis of our estimator developed in Section
4
. First, we recast the operator learning problem as the problem of learning the wavelet matrix representation of the operator. Second, we propose a local bias-variance decision rule to determine which blocks of this matrix representation are estimated and which are discarded. Third, we show that the same estimation rate can be achieved by more aggressively discarding blocks outside a smaller estimation region.
Fourth, we describe how to adapt the sample size across scales in order to balance local estimation errors. The third and fourth ideas reduce the computational cost without degrading the estimation error. We describe these ideas in turn in the following subsections.

3.1
Wavelet representation and infinite-dimensional regression

Here, we recast the operator learning problem as an infinite-dimensional matrix regression problem in wavelet coordinates. This coordinate representation is the starting point for the multiscale bias–variance and computational analysis below.

Fix an orthonormal wavelet basis

\{\varphi_{j,k}:j\geq 0,\ k\in\nabla_{j}\}
of
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, where
|\nabla_{j}|:=\mathrm{Card}(\nabla_{j})\asymp 2^{jd}.

The index
\lambda=(j,k)
records the scale
j
and location
k
. We define the wavelet index set

\Lambda:=\{\lambda=(j,k):j\geq 0,\ k\in\nabla_{j}\}.

For
\lambda=(j,k)
, we set
|\lambda|:=j
and write
\varphi_{\lambda}:=\varphi_{j,k}
.

For a function or distribution
h
, write its wavelet coefficients as

h_{\lambda}:=\langle h,\varphi_{\lambda}\rangle,\qquad\mathbf{h}:=(h_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\Lambda},

where the pairing is understood in the distributional sense when needed. We assume that the wavelets have sufficiently high regularity and sufficiently many vanishing moments so that, for every Sobolev index appearing below,

\|h\|_{H^{a}}^{2}\asymp\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}2^{2a|\lambda|}|\langle h,\varphi_{\lambda}\rangle|^{2}.

For
a\in\mathbb{R}
, define the diagonal operator
\mathbf{D}^{a}
on
\ell^{2}(\Lambda)
by

(3.1)

(\mathbf{D}^{a})_{\lambda,\lambda}=2^{a|\lambda|},\qquad\lambda\in\Lambda.

Then

\|h\|_{H^{a}}\asymp\|\mathbf{D}^{a}\mathbf{h}\|_{\ell^{2}}.

For a linear operator
\mathcal{A}
, define its wavelet matrix representation
\mathbf{A}=(A_{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}})_{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}\in\Lambda}
by

\mathbf{A}_{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}:=\langle\mathcal{A}\varphi_{\lambda^{\prime}},\varphi_{\lambda}\rangle.

With this convention, the first index corresponds to the output coordinate and the second to the input coordinate.
Formally,
\mathcal{A}
admits the wavelet expansion

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}=\sum_{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}\in\Lambda}\mathbf{A}_{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}\,\varphi_{\lambda}\otimes\varphi_{\lambda^{\prime}}=\sum_{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}\in\Lambda}\langle\mathcal{A}\varphi_{\lambda^{\prime}},\varphi_{\lambda}\rangle\varphi_{\lambda}\otimes\varphi_{\lambda^{\prime}}.

By the norm equivalence, for any
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{O}(s,s^{\prime},R)
and
t\geq s
,
t^{\prime}\geq s^{\prime}
,

(3.2)

\|\mathcal{A}\|_{H^{t}\to H^{-t^{\prime}}}\asymp\|\mathbf{D}^{-t^{\prime}}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{D}^{-t}\|.

In wavelet coordinates, the observation model
f_{i}=\mathcal{A}u_{i}+w_{i}
becomes

\mathbf{f}_{i}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{u}_{i}+\mathbf{w}_{i},\qquad 1\leq i\leq N.

Stacking these coefficient vectors row-wise gives

(3.3)

\mathbf{F}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{A}^{\top}+\mathbf{W},

where

\mathbf{F}:=[\mathbf{f}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{f}_{N}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times\Lambda},\qquad\mathbf{U}:=[\mathbf{u}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{u}_{N}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times\Lambda},\qquad\mathbf{W}:=[\mathbf{w}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{w}_{N}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times\Lambda}.

Thus, given
\mathbf{U}
and
\mathbf{F}
, the operator learning problem reduces to estimating the infinite-dimensional matrix
\mathbf{A}
under the norm
\|\mathbf{D}^{-t^{\prime}}\cdot\mathbf{D}^{-t}\|
in (
3.2
).

The following lemma summarizes the structural properties of the wavelet-coordinate model used throughout the analysis.

Lemma 3.1

(Properties of
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{U},\mathbf{W}
)
.

For
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{O}(s,s^{\prime},R)
,
u_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{GP}(0,(I-\Delta)^{-r_{1}})
, and
w_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{GP}(0,(I-\Delta)^{-r_{2}})
, the corresponding wavelet coefficient matrices
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{U},\mathbf{W}
satisfy the following properties.

(I)

Boundedness of
\mathbf{A}
:
There exists a constant
R^{\prime}>0
such that
\|\mathbf{D}^{-s^{\prime}}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{D}^{-s}\|\leq R^{\prime}
.

(II)

Diagonal preconditioning:

There exist constants
0<c_{-}<c_{+}<\infty
such that:

(i)

The population covariance matrix of the input data
\mathbf{C}_{u}:=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{U}/N}}\right]
satisfies

c_{-}\leq\sigma_{\min}(\mathbf{D}^{r_{1}}\mathbf{C}_{u}\mathbf{D}^{r_{1}})\leq\sigma_{\max}(\mathbf{D}^{r_{1}}\mathbf{C}_{u}\mathbf{D}^{r_{1}})\leq c_{+}.

(ii)

The population covariance matrix of the noise
\mathbf{C}_{w}:=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{W}/N}}\right]
satisfies

c_{-}\leq\sigma_{\min}(\mathbf{D}^{r_{2}}\mathbf{C}_{w}\mathbf{D}^{r_{2}})\leq\sigma_{\max}(\mathbf{D}^{r_{2}}\mathbf{C}_{w}\mathbf{D}^{r_{2}})\leq c_{+}.

Proof.

The bound in
(I)
follows from the assumed
operator bound on
\mathcal{A}
and the norm equivalence (
3.2
). The
preconditioning bounds in
(II)
are standard
consequences of wavelet diagonal preconditioning for Sobolev-scale
elliptic covariance operators, see

[
14
, Proposition 3.2]
and

[
18
, Propositions 3 and 13]
.
∎

The operator class
\mathcal{O}(s,s^{\prime},R)
is represented, up to constants in the wavelet norm equivalence, by the weighted matrix class

\mathcal{M}(s,s^{\prime},R^{\prime}):=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{\Lambda\times\Lambda}:\|\mathbf{D}^{-s^{\prime}}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{D}^{-s}\|\leq R^{\prime}}}\right\}.

Therefore, under the matrix model (
3.3
), we study the minimax risk

\inf_{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}\sup_{\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{M}(s,s^{\prime},R^{\prime})}\mathbb{E}\,\|\mathbf{D}^{-t^{\prime}}(\widehat{\mathbf{A}}-\mathbf{A})\mathbf{D}^{-t}\|,

where the infimum is over all measurable estimators based on
\mathbf{U}
and
\mathbf{F}
.

We summarize the first idea for learning linear operators as follows:

Idea I.

Wavelet coordinates reduce learning linear operators between Sobolev spaces to infinite-dimensional matrix estimation under diagonally weighted operator norms.

3.2
Bias–variance geometry

Recall the infinite-dimensional matrix model

(3.4)

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{A}^{\top}+\mathbf{W},

where the unknown matrix
\mathbf{A}
belongs to
\mathcal{M}(s,s^{\prime},R^{\prime})
. Given
\mathbf{U}

and
\mathbf{F}
, our goal is to estimate
\mathbf{A}
under the weighted operator
norm
\|\mathbf{D}^{-t^{\prime}}\cdot\mathbf{D}^{-t}\|
. In what follows, it is more
convenient to work with
\mathbf{A}^{\top}
, equipped with the equivalent
transposed loss
\|\mathbf{D}^{-t}\cdot\mathbf{D}^{-t^{\prime}}\|
.

The rows and columns of
\mathbf{A}^{\top}
are indexed by the multiscale wavelet
index set
\Lambda=\{\lambda=(j,k):j\geq 0,\ k\in\nabla_{j}\}
. It is
therefore useful to view
\mathbf{A}^{\top}
blockwise. We denote by

(\mathbf{A}^{\top})_{j,j^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\nabla_{j}|\times|\nabla_{j^{\prime}}|}

the
(j,j^{\prime})
-th block of
\mathbf{A}^{\top}
, where

|\nabla_{j}|\asymp 2^{jd}
and
|\nabla_{j^{\prime}}|\asymp 2^{j^{\prime}d}
.

From the perspective of statistical estimation, it is natural to begin with a
blockwise bias–variance analysis. We describe this heuristic for estimating

\mathbf{A}^{\top}
in the model (
3.4
); rigorous blockwise bias
and variance estimates are given in Appendices
A.1

and
A.3
, respectively.
Since the target loss for

\mathbf{A}^{\top}
is
\|\mathbf{D}^{-t}\cdot\mathbf{D}^{-t^{\prime}}\|
, discarding the

(j,j^{\prime})
-th block contributes, after Sobolev weighting, an error of order

2^{-jt-j^{\prime}t^{\prime}}\|(\mathbf{A}^{\top})_{j,j^{\prime}}\|.

The assumption

\|\mathbf{D}^{-s^{\prime}}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{D}^{-s}\|\leq R^{\prime}
implies, at the block level,

\|(\mathbf{A}^{\top})_{j,j^{\prime}}\|\lesssim R^{\prime}2^{js+j^{\prime}s^{\prime}}.

Hence the bias caused by discarding the
(j,j^{\prime})
-th block is of order

\mathsf{Bias}_{j,j^{\prime}}\asymp 2^{-j(t-s)-j^{\prime}(t^{\prime}-s^{\prime})}.

We next estimate the corresponding variance scale. The input covariance has
regularity
r_{1}
, so the typical size of the design in the
j
-th input
block is
2^{-jr_{1}}
. The noise covariance has regularity
r_{2}
, so the
typical size of the noise in the
j^{\prime}
-th output block is
2^{-j^{\prime}r_{2}}
.
Inverting the design therefore contributes a factor
2^{jr_{1}}
, while the
noise contributes a factor
2^{-j^{\prime}r_{2}}
. After converting the resulting block error to the
\|\mathbf{D}^{-t}\cdot\mathbf{D}^{-t^{\prime}}\|
loss
and using Chevet’s inequality to control the operator norm of a random matrix
of size
2^{jd}\times 2^{j^{\prime}d}
, the square root of the variance contribution
to the blockwise operator-norm error is heuristically

\sqrt{\mathsf{Var}_{j,j^{\prime}}(N)}\asymp\frac{2^{-jt-j^{\prime}t^{\prime}+jr_{1}-j^{\prime}r_{2}+\frac{d}{2}\max\{j,j^{\prime}\}}}{\sqrt{N}}.

The factor
2^{\frac{d}{2}\max\{j,j^{\prime}\}}
is the operator-norm fluctuation
scale of the corresponding random block. Indeed, a Gaussian random matrix of
size
2^{jd}\times 2^{j^{\prime}d}
with independent
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
entries has
operator norm of order

2^{jd/2}+2^{j^{\prime}d/2}\asymp 2^{\frac{d}{2}\max\{j,j^{\prime}\}}.

Therefore, the local bias–variance balance for deciding whether the

(j,j^{\prime})
-th block should be estimated is

(3.5)

\displaystyle 2^{-j(t-s)-j^{\prime}(t^{\prime}-s^{\prime})}\asymp\frac{2^{-jt-j^{\prime}t^{\prime}+jr_{1}-j^{\prime}r_{2}+\frac{d}{2}\max\{j,j^{\prime}\}}}{\sqrt{N}}.

Equivalently,

(3.6)

\displaystyle j(r_{1}-s)+j^{\prime}(-r_{2}-s^{\prime})+\frac{d}{2}\max\{j,j^{\prime}\}=\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}N.

Notice that this boundary is independent of the loss exponents
t,t^{\prime}
.

The boundary (
3.6
) consists of two linear pieces. In the region

j\geq j^{\prime}
, it is given by

j\Bigl(r_{1}-s+\frac{d}{2}\Bigr)+j^{\prime}(-r_{2}-s^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}N,

whereas in the region
j^{\prime}\geq j
, it is given by

j(r_{1}-s)+j^{\prime}\Bigl(-r_{2}-s^{\prime}+\frac{d}{2}\Bigr)=\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}N.

Thus the boundary passes through the points

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\log_{2}N}{2(r_{1}-s)+d},\ 0}}\right),\hskip 8.19447pt\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,\ \frac{\log_{2}N}{2(-r_{2}-s^{\prime})+d}}}\right),\hskip 8.19447pt\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\log_{2}N}{2(r_{1}-r_{2}-s-s^{\prime})+d},\ \frac{\log_{2}N}{2(r_{1}-r_{2}-s-s^{\prime})+d}}}\right),

as illustrated in Figure
1
.

Recall that the assumption
u\in H^{(r_{1}-d/2)_{-}}\hookrightarrow H^{s}
, which ensures
that
\mathcal{A}u
is well defined, is equivalent to
r_{1}>s+d/2
. In
particular, the coefficient of
j
in (
3.6
) is positive. For
simplicity, let us focus on the case
-r_{2}-s^{\prime}>0
. Then the blocks selected by the local bias–variance tradeoff rule
(
3.5

j(r_{1}-s)+j^{\prime}(-r_{2}-s^{\prime})+\frac{d}{2}\max\{j,j^{\prime}\}\leq\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}N

in the
(j,j^{\prime})
-plane. Since the
(j,j^{\prime})
-block has size
2^{jd}\times 2^{j^{\prime}d}
, the piecewise
linear cutoff in the scale indices
(j,j^{\prime})
corresponds, when viewed in the
full wavelet-index coordinates
\lambda=(j,k)
and
\lambda^{\prime}=(j^{\prime},k^{\prime})
, to
a piecewise hyperbolic cutoff boundary.

The resulting convergence rate can be read off from the extremal points of
this selected region. These are

(0,0),\hskip 8.19447pt\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\log_{2}N}{2(r_{1}-s)+d},0}}\right),\hskip 8.19447pt\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,\frac{\log_{2}N}{2(-r_{2}-s^{\prime})+d}}}\right),\hskip 8.19447pt\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\log_{2}N}{2(r_{1}-r_{2}-s-s^{\prime})+d},\frac{\log_{2}N}{2(r_{1}-r_{2}-s-s^{\prime})+d}}}\right).

Evaluating the discarded-block bias
2^{-j(t-s)-j^{\prime}(t^{\prime}-s^{\prime})}
at these four points yields the four dominant error scales

N^{-1/2},\qquad N^{-\frac{t-s}{2(r_{1}-s)+d}},\qquad N^{-\frac{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}{2(-r_{2}-s^{\prime})+d}},\qquad N^{-\frac{t+t^{\prime}-s-s^{\prime}}{2(r_{1}-r_{2}-s-s^{\prime})+d}}.

These correspond respectively to the low-frequency block, the input-side
high-frequency boundary, the output-side high-frequency boundary, and the
diagonal transition point
j=j^{\prime}
. Therefore the final convergence rate is
given by the slowest of these four rates, namely

(3.7)

N^{-\min\big\{\frac{1}{2},\,\frac{t-s}{2(r_{1}-s)+d},\,\frac{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}{(2(-r_{2}-s^{\prime})+d)_{+}},\,\frac{t+t^{\prime}-s-s^{\prime}}{(2(r_{1}-r_{2}-s-s^{\prime})+d)_{+}}\big\}}.

Notice that the diagonal transition point is never the bottleneck. Indeed,
set

a=t-s>0,\qquad b=t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}>0,\qquad c=r_{1}-s>0,\qquad e=-r_{2}-s^{\prime}.

Then

\frac{a+b}{(2(c+e)+d)_{+}}\geq\min\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\frac{a}{2c+d},\frac{b}{(2e+d)_{+}}}}\right\}.

Consequently,

\frac{t+t^{\prime}-s-s^{\prime}}{(2(r_{1}-r_{2}-s-s^{\prime})+d)_{+}}\geq\min\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\frac{t-s}{2(r_{1}-s)+d},\frac{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}{(2(-r_{2}-s^{\prime})+d)_{+}}}}\right\}.

Thus the diagonal transition point does not affect the final minimum, and the
convergence rate (
3.7
) simplifies to

N^{-\min\big\{\frac{1}{2},\,\frac{t-s}{2(r_{1}-s)+d},\,\frac{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}{(2(-r_{2}-s^{\prime})+d)_{+}}\big\}}.

Our first main result, Theorem
2.1
, shows that this heuristic
bias–variance calculation indeed gives the minimax rate, up to logarithmic
factors. A natural estimator attaining this rate is obtained by performing
linear regressions over the blocks selected by the local bias–variance
tradeoff:

(3.8)

\displaystyle j(r_{1}-s)+j^{\prime}(-r_{2}-s^{\prime})+\frac{d}{2}\max\{j,j^{\prime}\}\leq\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}N.

We henceforth refer to the set of blocks satisfying
(
3.8
) as the bias–variance region

\mathcal{BV}_{N}
. This is the quadrilateral region in the

(j,j^{\prime})
-plane illustrated in Figure
1
.

3.3
Minimal estimation region

From a computational perspective, one may ask whether it is necessary to estimate every block in the bias-variance region
\mathcal{BV}_{N}
.
It turns out that the same minimax rate can be achieved using a smaller, in fact minimal, estimation region, thereby reducing the computational cost. To illustrate the idea, suppose for simplicity that the minimax rate is
dominated by the
j
-side boundary, namely

(3.9)

\displaystyle N^{-\min\big\{\frac{1}{2},\,\frac{t-s}{2(r_{1}-s)+d},\,\frac{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}{(2(-r_{2}-s^{\prime})+d)_{+}}\big\}}=N^{-\frac{t-s}{2(r_{1}-s)+d}}.

Equivalently, the rate is determined by the block near

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\log_{2}N}{2(r_{1}-s)+d},0}}\right).

In this case, it is not necessary to estimate all blocks satisfying
(
3.8
). To preserve the rate

N^{-\frac{t-s}{2(r_{1}-s)+d}},

we can discard additional blocks if the bias incurred by doing so is below this target accuracy.
Thus we may discard all
blocks satisfying

2^{-j(t-s)-j^{\prime}(t^{\prime}-s^{\prime})}\leq N^{-\frac{t-s}{2(r_{1}-s)+d}}.

Equivalently, it suffices to estimate only those blocks satisfying

(3.10)

\displaystyle j(t-s)+j^{\prime}(t^{\prime}-s^{\prime})\leq\frac{t-s}{2(r_{1}-s)+d}\log_{2}N.

The corresponding estimation region is the shaded triangle in
Figure
1
, with vertices

(0,0),\qquad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\log_{2}N}{2(r_{1}-s)+d},0}}\right),\qquad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,\frac{(t-s)\log_{2}N}{(t^{\prime}-s^{\prime})(2(r_{1}-s)+d)}}}\right).

By the assumption
(
3.9
), we have

\frac{(t-s)\log_{2}N}{(t^{\prime}-s^{\prime})(2(r_{1}-s)+d)}<\frac{\log_{2}N}{2(-r_{2}-s^{\prime})+d}.

Therefore, the region (
3.10
) is strictly contained in the
bias–variance region (
3.8
). Hence estimating every
block selected by the local bias–variance rule is not computationally
optimal. To achieve the minimax rate

N^{-\frac{t-s}{2(r_{1}-s)+d}}
, it is enough to estimate the smaller region
(
3.10
); this region is the minimal possible estimation
region at the level of this blockwise bias criterion. This shows that the
local bias–variance rule, while statistically natural, does not by itself
lead to a computationally optimal estimator.

We summarize the third key idea for learning linear operators as follows:

Idea III.
The
local bias–variance rule does not yield the minimal minimax-optimal region, and hence does not lead to computationally optimal estimation.

3.4
Scale-adaptive sample usage

We have now identified the minimal estimation region. Thus, to achieve the
minimax rate in (
3.9
)

N^{-\frac{t-s}{2(r_{1}-s)+d}}=:N^{-\gamma},

it suffices to estimate all blocks whose scale indices satisfy
(
3.10
).

Starting from the matrix model

\mathbf{F}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{A}^{\top}+\mathbf{W},

we observe that it can be written block-columnwise. For any
j^{\prime}\geq 0
, the

j^{\prime}
-th column block satisfies

\mathbf{F}_{j^{\prime}}=\mathbf{U}(\mathbf{A}^{\top})_{\cdot,j^{\prime}}+\mathbf{W}_{j^{\prime}}.

Here

\mathbf{F}_{j^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times|\nabla_{j^{\prime}}|},\qquad(\mathbf{A}^{\top})_{\cdot,j^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\Lambda\times|\nabla_{j^{\prime}}|},\qquad\mathbf{W}_{j^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times|\nabla_{j^{\prime}}|}

denote the
j^{\prime}
-th column blocks of
\mathbf{F}
,
\mathbf{A}^{\top}
, and
\mathbf{W}
,
respectively.

A first natural estimator is to use all
N
samples and perform least
squares regression for all blocks in the minimal estimation region
(
3.10
). However, this is still not computationally
optimal. The reason is that the local statistical difficulty is not uniform
throughout the region.

Indeed, under the simplifying assumption (
3.9
), the
minimax rate is determined by the input-side boundary point

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\log_{2}N}{2(r_{1}-s)+d},0}}\right),

and equals
N^{-\gamma}=N^{-\frac{t-s}{2(r_{1}-s)+d}}
. Now consider instead
the output-side endpoint of the minimal estimation region,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,\frac{(t-s)\log_{2}N}{(t^{\prime}-s^{\prime})(2(r_{1}-s)+d)}}}\right).

This point lies on the boundary of (
3.10
), so its
discarded-block bias is exactly of the target order
N^{-\gamma}
. However,
by (
3.9
), this point lies strictly inside the larger
bias–variance region (
3.8
). Therefore, at this block,
the square root of the variance using all
N
samples is strictly smaller than its bias:

\sqrt{\mathsf{Var}_{0,\frac{(t-s)\log_{2}N}{(t^{\prime}-s^{\prime})(2(r_{1}-s)+d)}}(N)}\ll\mathsf{Bias}_{0,\frac{(t-s)\log_{2}N}{(t^{\prime}-s^{\prime})(2(r_{1}-s)+d)}}.

Since the block lies on the boundary (
3.10
), the bias is
of order
N^{-\gamma}
. Hence

\sqrt{\mathsf{Var}_{0,\frac{(t-s)\log_{2}N}{(t^{\prime}-s^{\prime})(2(r_{1}-s)+d)}}(N)}\ll\mathsf{Bias}_{0,\frac{(t-s)\log_{2}N}{(t^{\prime}-s^{\prime})(2(r_{1}-s)+d)}}\asymp N^{-\gamma}.

Thus, if all
N
samples are used, the estimation error for this block is
much smaller than the global target rate. In other words, the block near

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\log_{2}N}{2(r_{1}-s)+d},0}}\right)

is statistically the hardest one, which contributes to the minimax rate
N^{-\frac{t-s}{2(r_{1}-s)+d}}
, whereas the block near

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,\frac{(t-s)\log_{2}N}{(t^{\prime}-s^{\prime})(2(r_{1}-s)+d)}}}\right)

is easier to estimate.

This observation creates room for computational improvement. Since the square root of the
variance at this easier block is already much smaller than
N^{-\gamma}
,
we do not need to use all
N
samples to estimate it. Instead, for each
block
(j,j^{\prime})
, one may in principle use only
N_{j,j^{\prime}}\leq N
samples,
chosen so that the variance of the
(j,j^{\prime})
-block is raised to the global
target level
N^{-\gamma}
. In other words, the guiding principle is

\sqrt{\mathsf{Var}_{j,j^{\prime}}(N_{j,j^{\prime}})}\asymp N^{-\gamma},

whenever the block
(j,j^{\prime})
belongs to the minimal estimation region (
3.10
).
Thus the number of samples used for each block should depend on its local
statistical difficulty, rather than being fixed uniformly at the full sample
size
N
throughout the entire region.

In the actual estimator constructed in Subsection
4.1
, we use an even simpler
column-adaptive allocation. Namely, for each column scale
j^{\prime}
, we choose a
sample size
N_{j^{\prime}}
depending only on
j^{\prime}
, and use these
N_{j^{\prime}}

samples to estimate all relevant blocks in the
j^{\prime}
-th column. This
columnwise allocation already achieves the optimal computational cost; a
fully blockwise allocation
N_{j,j^{\prime}}
would not improve the leading-order
complexity.

We finally summarize the fourth key idea for learning linear operators as follows:

Idea IV.
Region-adaptive sample sizes balance local estimation errors,
preserve the global minimax rate, and achieve optimal computational complexity.

4
Minimax rate

4.1
Minimax upper bound

We first set up some matrix notation. Recall that

\Lambda=\{\lambda=(j,k):j\geq 0,\ k\in\nabla_{j}\},\qquad|\nabla_{j}|\asymp 2^{jd},

where
\nabla_{j}
is the set of location indices at scale
j
. For a set of
scales
I\subseteq\mathbb{N}_{0}

\Lambda_{I}:=\{(j,k):j\in I,\ k\in\nabla_{j}\}.

In particular, we write

\Lambda_{j}:=\Lambda_{\{j\}}=\{j\}\times\nabla_{j}.

For a matrix
\mathbf{B}
indexed by
\Lambda\times\Lambda

scale-level notation

\mathbf{B}_{I,K}:=\mathbf{B}_{\Lambda_{I},\Lambda_{K}}.

In particular,

\mathbf{B}_{I,j}:=\mathbf{B}_{\Lambda_{I},\Lambda_{j}},\qquad\mathbf{B}_{j,I}:=\mathbf{B}_{\Lambda_{j},\Lambda_{I}}.

We use “
\cdot

for instance,

\mathbf{B}_{\cdot,j}:=\mathbf{B}_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{j}},\qquad\mathbf{B}_{I,\cdot}:=\mathbf{B}_{\Lambda_{I},\Lambda}.

\mathbf{U}_{I}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times|\Lambda_{I}|}

2.1

The complete definitions of
\widehat{\mathbf{A}}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}
are given below.

Algorithm: Construction of the Estimator
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}

Input:
Confidence level
\delta>0
, input data matrix
\mathbf{U}_{\widetilde{I}_{0}}
, output data blocks
\mathbf{F}_{j^{\prime}}
for
j^{\prime}\leq\big\lceil\frac{\gamma\log_{2}N}{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}\big\rceil
.

Output:
Estimator
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}
.

Step 1:
Columnwise regression.

For each
j^{\prime}=0,1,\dots,\big\lceil\frac{\gamma\log_{2}N}{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}\big\rceil
, choose
N_{j^{\prime}}
satisfying

(4.5)

\displaystyle N_{j^{\prime}}\asymp\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{2^{\widetilde{J}(j^{\prime})d}+\log((\log N)/\delta),\ N^{2\gamma}\sup_{0\leq j\leq J(j^{\prime})}2^{-2jt-2j^{\prime}t^{\prime}+2jr_{1}-2j^{\prime}r_{2}+d\max\{j,j^{\prime}\}}}}\right\}.

Let
\mathbf{U}^{(j^{\prime})}_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}}
and
\mathbf{F}^{(j^{\prime})}_{j^{\prime}}
denote the
submatrices of
\mathbf{U}_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}}
and
\mathbf{F}_{j^{\prime}}
formed by
selecting the same
N_{j^{\prime}}
sample rows. Define the
j^{\prime}
-th column block of
\widehat{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}
:

(4.6)

\displaystyle(\widehat{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}},j^{\prime}}:=\big((\mathbf{U}^{(j^{\prime})}_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}})^{\top}\mathbf{U}^{(j^{\prime})}_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}}\big)^{-1}(\mathbf{U}^{(j^{\prime})}_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}})^{\top}\mathbf{F}^{(j^{\prime})}_{j^{\prime}}.

All other entries of
\widehat{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}
are set to zero.

Step 2:
Final restriction and operator construction.

Define the operator estimator
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}
by retaining only the entries of
\widehat{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}
indexed by
\mathcal{E}_{N}
:

(4.7)

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{A}}:=\sum_{(\lambda,\lambda^{\prime})\in\mathcal{E}_{N}}(\widehat{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})_{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}\,\varphi_{\lambda^{\prime}}\otimes\varphi_{\lambda}.

Remark 4.3
(Choice of
N_{j^{\prime}}
).

The two terms in the definition of
N_{j^{\prime}}
play different roles. The term

2^{\widetilde{J}(j^{\prime})d}+\log((\log N)/\delta)
ensures that the columnwise least-squares problem over the enlarged regression support is well-conditioned uniformly over the relevant column scales. The logarithmic factor comes from taking a union bound over
\mathcal{O}(\log N)
possible values of
j^{\prime}
.

The second term controls the stochastic error: for every retained block
(j,j^{\prime})\in\mathcal{E}_{N}
, it ensures that the weighted blockwise standard deviation is at most of order
N^{-\gamma}
. Since the exponent inside the supremum is a convex piecewise-affine function of
j
, the supremum is attained at one of the endpoints
j=0
or
j=J(j^{\prime})
.

Finally, the choice is feasible: the definition of
\gamma
implies that the right-hand side of (
4.5
) is
\lesssim N
uniformly over all relevant
j^{\prime}
. Thus, after adjusting constants, the estimator uses at most the available sample size; in the borderline case, it simply uses all
N
samples.
∎

The following proposition shows that the estimator constructed above attains the minimax rate
N^{-\gamma}
, up to logarithmic factors, with high probability. An in-expectation bound is established in
Appendix
A.4
for a stability-truncated version of the same estimator.

Proposition 4.4

(Minimax upper bound)
.

Under the setting of Theorem
2.1
, the following holds. For any
\delta\in(0,1)
, if
N\gtrsim\log(1/\delta)
, then with probability at least
1-\delta
, the estimator
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}
defined in (
4.7
) satisfies

\|\widehat{\mathcal{A}}-\mathcal{A}\|_{H^{t}\to H^{-t^{\prime}}}\lesssim N^{-\gamma}\,\sqrt{\log\!\Big(\frac{N}{\delta}\Big)}\,\log N.

Proof.

For a matrix
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{\Lambda\times\Lambda}
and an index set

S\subseteq\Lambda\times\Lambda
, we denote by
\mathbf{B}_{S}
the matrix obtained
by retaining the entries of
\mathbf{B}
indexed by
S
and setting all remaining
entries to zero. We write
\mathbf{B}_{S^{c}}
for the analogous restriction to

S^{c}:=(\Lambda\times\Lambda)\setminus S
. Thus,

\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{B}_{S}+\mathbf{B}_{S^{c}}.

We begin with the operator-norm equivalence

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\mathcal{A}}-\mathcal{A}\|_{H^{t}\to H^{-t^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle\asymp\|\mathbf{D}^{-t}((\widehat{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})_{\mathcal{E}_{N}}-\mathbf{A}^{\top})\mathbf{D}^{-t^{\prime}}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbf{D}^{-t}(\mathbf{A}^{\top})_{\mathcal{E}_{N}^{c}}\mathbf{D}^{-t^{\prime}}\|+\|\mathbf{D}^{-t}((\widehat{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})_{\mathcal{E}_{N}}-(\mathbf{A}^{\top})_{\mathcal{E}_{N}})\mathbf{D}^{-t^{\prime}}\|

(4.8)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\|\mathbf{D}^{-t}(\mathbf{A}^{\top})_{\mathcal{E}_{N}^{c}}\mathbf{D}^{-t^{\prime}}\|}_{=:\mathsf{Error\text{-}Bias}}+\underbrace{\|\mathbf{D}^{-t}(\widehat{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}-\mathbf{A}^{\top})_{\mathcal{E}_{N}}\mathbf{D}^{-t^{\prime}}\|}_{=:\mathsf{Error\text{-}Estimation}}.

Recall that, for each column scale
j^{\prime}\leq\lceil\frac{\gamma\log_{2}N}{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}\rceil
, the estimator in
(
4.6
) is defined by

(\widehat{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}},j^{\prime}}:=\big((\mathbf{U}^{(j^{\prime})}_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}})^{\top}\mathbf{U}^{(j^{\prime})}_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}}\big)^{-1}(\mathbf{U}^{(j^{\prime})}_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}})^{\top}\mathbf{F}^{(j^{\prime})}_{j^{\prime}}.

Moreover, the
j^{\prime}
-th block-column equation in (
4.2
),
restricted to the corresponding sample rows, can be written as

\mathbf{F}^{(j^{\prime})}_{j^{\prime}}=\mathbf{U}^{(j^{\prime})}(\mathbf{A}^{\top})_{\cdot,j^{\prime}}+\mathbf{W}^{(j^{\prime})}_{j^{\prime}}=\mathbf{U}^{(j^{\prime})}_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{A}^{\top})_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}},j^{\prime}}+\mathbf{U}^{(j^{\prime})}_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}^{c}}(\mathbf{A}^{\top})_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}^{c},j^{\prime}}+\mathbf{W}^{(j^{\prime})}_{j^{\prime}}.

Here
(\cdot)^{(j^{\prime})}
denotes restriction to the same set of

\min\{N_{j^{\prime}},N\}
sample rows. Substituting this block-column equation into the definition of the estimator yields

(\widehat{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}-\mathbf{A}^{\top})_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}},j^{\prime}}=\underbrace{\big((\mathbf{U}^{(j^{\prime})}_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}})^{\top}\mathbf{U}^{(j^{\prime})}_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}}\big)^{-1}(\mathbf{U}^{(j^{\prime})}_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}})^{\top}\mathbf{U}^{(j^{\prime})}_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}^{c}}(\mathbf{A}^{\top})_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}^{c},j^{\prime}}}_{=:\mathsf{OVB}_{j^{\prime}}}+\underbrace{\big((\mathbf{U}^{(j^{\prime})}_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}})^{\top}\mathbf{U}^{(j^{\prime})}_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}}\big)^{-1}(\mathbf{U}^{(j^{\prime})}_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}})^{\top}\mathbf{W}^{(j^{\prime})}_{j^{\prime}}}_{=:\mathsf{Var}_{j^{\prime}}}.

Let
\mathbf{B}^{\mathrm{ovb}}
and
\mathbf{B}^{\mathrm{var}}
be the matrices defined block-columnwise by

(\mathbf{B}^{\mathrm{ovb}})_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}},j^{\prime}}:=\mathsf{OVB}_{j^{\prime}},\qquad(\mathbf{B}^{\mathrm{var}})_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}},j^{\prime}}:=\mathsf{Var}_{j^{\prime}},

for all relevant
j^{\prime}
, with all remaining entries set to zero. Therefore,

(\widehat{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}-\mathbf{A}^{\top})_{\mathcal{E}_{N}}=(\mathbf{B}^{\mathrm{ovb}})_{\mathcal{E}_{N}}+(\mathbf{B}^{\mathrm{var}})_{\mathcal{E}_{N}}.

Hence,

\mathsf{Error\text{-}Estimation}\leq\underbrace{\|\mathbf{D}^{-t}(\mathbf{B}^{\mathrm{ovb}})_{\mathcal{E}_{N}}\mathbf{D}^{-t^{\prime}}\|}_{=:\mathsf{Error\text{-}OVB}}+\underbrace{\|\mathbf{D}^{-t}(\mathbf{B}^{\mathrm{var}})_{\mathcal{E}_{N}}\mathbf{D}^{-t^{\prime}}\|}_{=:\mathsf{Error\text{-}Var}}.

Consequently,

\|\widehat{\mathcal{A}}-\mathcal{A}\|_{H^{t}\to H^{-t^{\prime}}}\lesssim\mathsf{Error\text{-}Bias}+\mathsf{Error\text{-}OVB}+\mathsf{Error\text{-}Var}.

Lemma
4.5
gives the deterministic bound

\mathsf{Error\text{-}Bias}=\|\mathbf{D}^{-t}(\mathbf{A}^{\top})_{\mathcal{E}_{N}^{c}}\mathbf{D}^{-t^{\prime}}\|\lesssim R^{\prime}N^{-\gamma}.

Moreover, Lemmas
4.6
and
4.7
show that, for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, if

(4.9)

\displaystyle N\gtrsim\log(1/\delta),

then with probability at least
1-\delta

\mathsf{Error\text{-}OVB}=\|\mathbf{D}^{-t}(\mathbf{B}^{\mathrm{ovb}})_{\mathcal{E}_{N}}\mathbf{D}^{-t^{\prime}}\|\lesssim N^{-\gamma}\log N,

and

\mathsf{Error\text{-}Var}=\|\mathbf{D}^{-t}(\mathbf{B}^{\mathrm{var}})_{\mathcal{E}_{N}}\mathbf{D}^{-t^{\prime}}\|\lesssim N^{-\gamma}\sqrt{\log\!\Big(\frac{N}{\delta}\Big)}\log N.

Combining these estimates yields

\|\widehat{\mathcal{A}}-\mathcal{A}\|_{H^{t}\to H^{-t^{\prime}}}\lesssim R^{\prime}N^{-\gamma}+N^{-\gamma}\log N+N^{-\gamma}\sqrt{\log\!\Big(\frac{N}{\delta}\Big)}\log N,

with probability at least
1-\delta
. This proves the claim.
∎

Remark 4.8
(Direct regression versus nested-support regression).

We briefly explain, through a finite-dimensional toy model, why the
nested-support regression step reduces the omitted-variable bias without
changing the variance order.

Fix a column scale
j^{\prime}
. The
j^{\prime}
-th block-column of the matrix model is

\mathbf{F}_{j^{\prime}}=\mathbf{U}(\mathbf{A}^{\top})_{\cdot,j^{\prime}}+\mathbf{W}_{j^{\prime}}.

Let
I_{j^{\prime}}
denote the set of input scales retained in the final estimation
region
\mathcal{E}_{N}
, and let
\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}\supseteq I_{j^{\prime}}
denote the
larger set of input scales used in the regression region
\mathcal{R}_{N}
.

There are two natural procedures.

Direct regression.

One may regress only on the variables in
I_{j^{\prime}}
:

(\widehat{\mathbf{A}}^{\top,\mathrm{dir}})_{I_{j^{\prime}},j^{\prime}}:=(\mathbf{U}_{I_{j^{\prime}}}^{\top}\mathbf{U}_{I_{j^{\prime}}})^{-1}\mathbf{U}_{I_{j^{\prime}}}^{\top}\mathbf{F}_{j^{\prime}}.

Substituting the block-column model gives

(\widehat{\mathbf{A}}^{\top,\mathrm{dir}}-\mathbf{A}^{\top})_{I_{j^{\prime}},j^{\prime}}=(\mathbf{U}_{I_{j^{\prime}}}^{\top}\mathbf{U}_{I_{j^{\prime}}})^{-1}\mathbf{U}_{I_{j^{\prime}}}^{\top}\mathbf{U}_{I_{j^{\prime}}^{c}}(\mathbf{A}^{\top})_{I_{j^{\prime}}^{c},j^{\prime}}+(\mathbf{U}_{I_{j^{\prime}}}^{\top}\mathbf{U}_{I_{j^{\prime}}})^{-1}\mathbf{U}_{I_{j^{\prime}}}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{j^{\prime}}.

Thus the omitted-variable bias is generated by all coefficients outside

I_{j^{\prime}}
.

Nested-support regression.

Instead, our estimator regresses on the enlarged set

\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}
, and then keeps only the entries in
I_{j^{\prime}}
:

(\widehat{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}},j^{\prime}}:=(\mathbf{U}_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}}^{\top}\mathbf{U}_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}})^{-1}\mathbf{U}_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}}^{\top}\mathbf{F}_{j^{\prime}}.

Then

(\widehat{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}-\mathbf{A}^{\top})_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}},j^{\prime}}=(\mathbf{U}_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}}^{\top}\mathbf{U}_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}})^{-1}\mathbf{U}_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}}^{\top}\mathbf{U}_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}^{c}}(\mathbf{A}^{\top})_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}^{c},j^{\prime}}+(\mathbf{U}_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}}^{\top}\mathbf{U}_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}})^{-1}\mathbf{U}_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{j^{\prime}}.

After restricting to
I_{j^{\prime}}
, the omitted-variable bias now only involves
the coefficients outside the larger set
\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}
, rather than
outside
I_{j^{\prime}}
. Since
\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}\supseteq I_{j^{\prime}}
, this removes
the contribution of the intermediate scales

\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}\setminus I_{j^{\prime}}
, which is precisely the gain used in the omitted-variable bias estimate.

It remains to explain why the enlarged regression does not change the variance
order. Write

G_{1}:=\mathbf{U}_{I_{j^{\prime}}}^{\top}\mathbf{U}_{I_{j^{\prime}}},\qquad G_{2}:=\mathbf{U}_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}}^{\top}\mathbf{U}_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}}.

Conditionally on
\mathbf{U}
, the covariance of the direct-regression noise is
controlled by
G_{1}^{-1}
, while the covariance of the nested-regression
noise, restricted back to
I_{j^{\prime}}
, is controlled by

(G_{2}^{-1})_{I_{j^{\prime}},I_{j^{\prime}}}.

Writing
\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}=I_{j^{\prime}}\cup K_{j^{\prime}}
, the Schur complement formula
gives

(4.10)

\displaystyle(G_{2}^{-1})_{I_{j^{\prime}},I_{j^{\prime}}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(G_{1}-G_{12}G_{22}^{-1}G_{21}}}\right)^{-1}\succeq G_{1}^{-1}.

Thus the nested regression indeed has larger variance in Loewner order.

The preceding comparison is deterministic. To see that the variance increase is only by a constant factor, we use the good design event obtained by combining the population diagonal preconditioning in Lemma
3.1
with Gaussian sample covariance concentration. Namely, on this high probability event,

cN\mathbf{D}_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}}^{-2r_{1}}\preceq G_{2}\preceq CN\mathbf{D}_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}}^{-2r_{1}}.

Since
\mathbf{D}^{r_{1}}
is diagonal, coordinate restriction commutes with the
preconditioner: for any
I\subseteq\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}
,

\bigl(\mathbf{D}_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}}^{r_{1}}M\mathbf{D}_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}}^{r_{1}}\bigr)_{I,I}=\mathbf{D}_{I}^{r_{1}}M_{I,I}\mathbf{D}_{I}^{r_{1}}.

Therefore,

(4.11)

\displaystyle(G_{2}^{-1})_{I_{j^{\prime}},I_{j^{\prime}}}\preceq\frac{1}{cN}\mathbf{D}_{I_{j^{\prime}}}^{2r_{1}}.

The same good design event, restricted to the coordinates
I_{j^{\prime}}
, gives

cN\mathbf{D}_{I_{j^{\prime}}}^{-2r_{1}}\preceq G_{1}\preceq CN\mathbf{D}_{I_{j^{\prime}}}^{-2r_{1}}.

Consequently,

(4.12)

\displaystyle G_{1}^{-1}\succeq\frac{1}{CN}\mathbf{D}_{I_{j^{\prime}}}^{2r_{1}}.

Combining (
4.11
) and (
4.12
) yields

(G_{2}^{-1})_{I_{j^{\prime}},I_{j^{\prime}}}\preceq\frac{C}{c}G_{1}^{-1}.

Together with (
4.10
), we obtain

G_{1}^{-1}\preceq(G_{2}^{-1})_{I_{j^{\prime}},I_{j^{\prime}}}\preceq\frac{C}{c}G_{1}^{-1}.

Hence nested regression may increase the variance, but only by a universal
constant factor. The variance order is unchanged, while the omitted-variable
bias is reduced from the contribution of
I_{j^{\prime}}^{c}
to that of

\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}^{c}
.

In short, nested-support regression improves the bias because it moves scales in

\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}\setminus I_{j^{\prime}}
from the omitted part into the fitted
model. It does not change the variance order because the inverse Gram matrix is
controlled by a restriction-compatible diagonal preconditioner. The key point
is not that enlarging a regression support is harmless in general; rather, it
is harmless here up to constants because the covariance geometry is diagonal in
the multiscale coordinates.

In Appendix
A.5
, we show that if the input covariance
matrix
\mathbf{C}_{u}
is exactly diagonal in the wavelet basis, then the
nested-support regression step is unnecessary. In that case, the same estimator
defined in (
4.6
) and (
4.7
),
with the choice
\widetilde{J}(j^{\prime})\equiv J(j^{\prime})
, also achieves the minimax
rate
N^{-\gamma}
up to logarithmic factors.
∎

4.2
Minimax lower bound

The following proposition establishes the minimax lower bound for learning
linear operators under the operator norm
\|\cdot\|_{H^{t}\to H^{-t^{\prime}}}
.

Proposition 4.9

(Minimax lower bound)
.

Under the setting of Theorem
2.1
,

\displaystyle\inf_{\widehat{\mathcal{A}}}\sup_{\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{O}(s,s^{\prime},R)}\mathbb{E}\,\|\widehat{\mathcal{A}}-\mathcal{A}\|_{H^{t}\to H^{-t^{\prime}}}\,\gtrsim\,N^{-\min\bigl\{\frac{1}{2},\,\frac{t-s}{2(r_{1}-s)+d},\,\frac{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}{(2(-r_{2}-s^{\prime})+d)_{+}}\bigr\}}.

The proof proceeds by reducing the problem to the equivalent matrix model and then applying Assouad’s lemma via a unified thin-strip construction. The latter is used in three dyadic configurations —coarse-to-coarse, fine-to-coarse, coarse-to-fine— which produce the three contributions appearing in the lower bound. We begin by recalling Assouad’s lemma.

Lemma 4.10

(Assouad’s lemma
[
41
, Lemma 24.3]
,
[
40
, Theorem 2.12]
)
.

Let
\Theta=\{0,1\}^{m}
, and let
T
be an estimator based on an observation whose distribution belongs to the family
\{P_{\theta}:\theta\in\Theta\}
. Let
\psi:\Theta\to\mathcal{X}
be a parameter map into a metric space
(\mathcal{X},\mathsf{d})
, and let

H(\theta,\theta^{\prime}):=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbf{1}_{\{\theta_{j}\neq\theta_{j}^{\prime}\}}

denote the Hamming distance on
\Theta
. Then

\max_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\mathsf{d}\bigl(T,\psi(\theta)\bigr)\geq\min_{H(\theta,\theta^{\prime})\geq 1}\frac{\mathsf{d}\bigl(\psi(\theta),\psi(\theta^{\prime})\bigr)}{H(\theta,\theta^{\prime})}\cdot\frac{m}{4}\cdot\min_{H(\theta,\theta^{\prime})=1}\bigg(1-\sqrt{\frac{D_{\mbox{\tiny{\rm KL}}}(P_{\theta}\|P_{\theta^{\prime}})}{2}}\bigg),

where
D_{\mbox{\tiny{\rm KL}}}(P\|Q)
denotes the Kullback-Leibler divergence between two probability measures
P
and
Q
.

We next explain how Assouad’s lemma will be used in our setting.
By the equivalent matrix formulation of the infinite-dimensional learning
problem, it suffices to prove a lower bound in the matrix model

\mathbf{f}_{i}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{u}_{i}+\mathbf{w}_{i},\qquad 1\leq i\leq N,

under the loss

\mathsf{d}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}):=\|\mathbf{D}^{-t^{\prime}}(\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{B})\mathbf{D}^{-t}\|.

We will construct finite families

\{\mathbf{A}_{\theta}:\theta\in\Theta\}\subseteq\mathcal{M}(s,s^{\prime},R^{\prime}),\qquad\Theta=\{0,1\}^{m},

where the value of
m
and the support of the perturbations will be chosen
according to the relevant dyadic regime. The parameter map in Assouad’s lemma is
simply

\psi(\theta)=\mathbf{A}_{\theta}.

The perturbations will be supported on thin strips inside dyadic blocks. This
choice keeps the operator-norm separation transparent while making the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between neighboring hypotheses easy to control. For each
\mathbf{A}
,
we write
P_{\mathbf{A}}
for the joint law of the data under the matrix model; the
next lemma gives the KL calculation used throughout.

Lemma 4.11

(KL divergence in the matrix model)
.

For
\mathbf{A}_{0},\mathbf{A}_{1}\in\mathcal{M}(s,s^{\prime},R^{\prime})
, let
P_{\mathbf{A}_{0}}
and

P_{\mathbf{A}_{1}}
denote the joint laws of

\{(\mathbf{u}_{i},\mathbf{f}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
under the matrix model. Then

D_{\mbox{\tiny{\rm KL}}}(P_{\mathbf{A}_{1}}\|P_{\mathbf{A}_{0}})\asymp\frac{N}{2}\|\mathbf{D}^{r_{2}}(\mathbf{A}_{1}-\mathbf{A}_{0})\mathbf{D}^{-r_{1}}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2},

whenever the right-hand side is finite.

Proof.

For
\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{M}(s,s^{\prime},R^{\prime})
, write

P_{\mathbf{A}}:=\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{A}}\bigl(\{(\mathbf{u}_{i},\mathbf{f}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}\bigr).

Conditionally on
\mathbf{u}_{i}
,

\mathbf{f}_{i}\mid\mathbf{u}_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{u}_{i},\mathbf{C}_{w}),

and hence

P_{\mathbf{A}}=\prod_{i=1}^{N}P_{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{u}_{i})\,P_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{f}_{i}\mid\mathbf{u}_{i}).

Therefore,

\displaystyle D_{\mbox{\tiny{\rm KL}}}(P_{\mathbf{A}_{1}}\|P_{\mathbf{A}_{0}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{U}}\Bigl[D_{\mbox{\tiny{\rm KL}}}(P^{\mathbf{F}\mid\mathbf{U}}_{\mathbf{A}_{1}}\|P^{\mathbf{F}\mid\mathbf{U}}_{\mathbf{A}_{0}})\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\mathbf{A}_{1}\mathbf{u}_{i}-\mathbf{A}_{0}\mathbf{u}_{i})^{\top}\mathbf{C}_{w}^{-1}(\mathbf{A}_{1}\mathbf{u}_{i}-\mathbf{A}_{0}\mathbf{u}_{i})}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{N}{2}\mathrm{Tr}\Bigl(\mathbf{C}_{w}^{-1}(\mathbf{A}_{1}-\mathbf{A}_{0})\mathbf{C}_{u}(\mathbf{A}_{1}-\mathbf{A}_{0})^{\top}\Bigr).

Using the covariance equivalences in Lemma
3.1
,

\mathbf{C}_{u}\asymp\mathbf{D}^{-2r_{1}},\qquad\mathbf{C}_{w}^{-1}\asymp\mathbf{D}^{2r_{2}},

we obtain

D_{\mbox{\tiny{\rm KL}}}(P_{\mathbf{A}_{1}}\|P_{\mathbf{A}_{0}})\asymp\frac{N}{2}\mathrm{Tr}\Bigl(\mathbf{D}^{r_{2}}(\mathbf{A}_{1}-\mathbf{A}_{0})\mathbf{D}^{-2r_{1}}(\mathbf{A}_{1}-\mathbf{A}_{0})^{\top}\mathbf{D}^{r_{2}}\Bigr)=\frac{N}{2}\|\mathbf{D}^{r_{2}}(\mathbf{A}_{1}-\mathbf{A}_{0})\mathbf{D}^{-r_{1}}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}.

In the last comparison we used the inequality

\sigma_{\min}(\mathbf{A})\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbf{B})\leq\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})=\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A})\leq\sigma_{\max}(\mathbf{A})\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbf{B}),

valid for symmetric positive semidefinite matrices
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
.
This proves the claim.
∎

The next lemma implements the preceding Assouad strategy on a thin strip inside
one dyadic block. It simultaneously covers row-type and column-type
perturbations.

Lemma 4.12

(Thin-strip Assouad construction)
.

Let
J_{\mathrm{out}},J_{\mathrm{in}}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, and let

\mathcal{I}\subseteq\Lambda_{J_{\mathrm{out}}},\qquad\mathcal{J}\subseteq\Lambda_{J_{\mathrm{in}}}

be finite sets satisfying

\min\{|\mathcal{I}|,|\mathcal{J}|\}=1.

Set

m:=|\mathcal{I}|\,|\mathcal{J}|=\max\{|\mathcal{I}|,|\mathcal{J}|\}.

For
\theta=(\theta_{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}})_{(\lambda,\lambda^{\prime})\in\mathcal{I}\times\mathcal{J}}\in\{0,1\}^{m}
and
(\lambda,\lambda^{\prime})\in\Lambda\times\Lambda
, define

(\mathbf{A}_{\theta})_{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}=\delta\,\theta_{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}\,\mathbf{1}_{\{(\lambda,\lambda^{\prime})\in\mathcal{I}\times\mathcal{J}\}},

where
\delta>0
.

Assume that

(4.13)

\delta\leq c_{0}\min\Bigl\{2^{J_{\mathrm{out}}s^{\prime}}2^{J_{\mathrm{in}}s}m^{-1/2},\;N^{-1/2}2^{-J_{\mathrm{out}}r_{2}}2^{J_{\mathrm{in}}r_{1}}\Bigr\},

for a sufficiently small constant
c_{0}>0
. Then:

1.

For every
\theta\in\{0,1\}^{m}
, one has
\mathbf{A}_{\theta}\in\mathcal{M}(s,s^{\prime},R^{\prime})
.

2.

For every
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{m}
with
H(\theta,\theta^{\prime})=1
,

D_{\mbox{\tiny{\rm KL}}}(P_{\mathbf{A}_{\theta}}\|P_{\mathbf{A}_{\theta^{\prime}}})\lesssim 1.

3.

For every
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{m}
,

\|\mathbf{D}^{-t^{\prime}}(\mathbf{A}_{\theta}-\mathbf{A}_{\theta^{\prime}})\mathbf{D}^{-t}\|\asymp\delta\,2^{-J_{\mathrm{out}}t^{\prime}}2^{-J_{\mathrm{in}}t}\sqrt{H(\theta,\theta^{\prime})}.

4.

Consequently,

(4.14)

\displaystyle\inf_{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}\sup_{\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{M}(s,s^{\prime},R^{\prime})}\mathbb{E}\,\|\mathbf{D}^{-t^{\prime}}(\widehat{\mathbf{A}}-\mathbf{A})\mathbf{D}^{-t}\|\,\gtrsim\,\delta\,2^{-J_{\mathrm{out}}t^{\prime}}2^{-J_{\mathrm{in}}t}\sqrt{m}.

In particular, after optimizing over
\delta
subject to (
4.13
),

(4.15)

\displaystyle\inf_{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}\sup_{\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{M}(s,s^{\prime},R^{\prime})}\mathbb{E}\,\|\mathbf{D}^{-t^{\prime}}(\widehat{\mathbf{A}}-\mathbf{A})\mathbf{D}^{-t}\|\,\gtrsim\,\min\Bigl\{N^{-1/2}2^{-J_{\mathrm{out}}(r_{2}+t^{\prime})}2^{J_{\mathrm{in}}(r_{1}-t)}\sqrt{m},\;2^{-J_{\mathrm{out}}(t^{\prime}-s^{\prime})}2^{-J_{\mathrm{in}}(t-s)}\Bigr\}.

Proof.

We verify the four claims in turn.

Step 1: parameter space constraint.

Since one of
\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}
is a singleton, the matrix
\mathbf{A}_{\theta}
is either a row vector or a column vector supported inside the block
\mathcal{I}\times\mathcal{J}
. Hence

\|\mathbf{A}_{\theta}\|\leq\delta\sqrt{m}.

After inserting the Sobolev weights, every active entry acquires the same factor
2^{-J_{\mathrm{out}}s^{\prime}}2^{-J_{\mathrm{in}}s}
, so

\|\mathbf{D}^{-s^{\prime}}\mathbf{A}_{\theta}\mathbf{D}^{-s}\|\lesssim\delta\,2^{-J_{\mathrm{out}}s^{\prime}}2^{-J_{\mathrm{in}}s}\sqrt{m}.

Thus the first condition in (
4.13
) guarantees that
\mathbf{A}_{\theta}\in\mathcal{M}(s,s^{\prime},R^{\prime})
.

Step 2: KL control.

If
H(\theta,\theta^{\prime})=1
, then
\mathbf{A}_{\theta}-\mathbf{A}_{\theta^{\prime}}
has exactly one nonzero entry, equal to
\pm\delta
, at some

(\lambda_{0},\lambda_{0}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{I}\times\mathcal{J}\subseteq\Lambda_{J_{\mathrm{out}}}\times\Lambda_{J_{\mathrm{in}}}.

By Lemma
4.11
,

D_{\mbox{\tiny{\rm KL}}}(P_{\mathbf{A}_{\theta}}\|P_{\mathbf{A}_{\theta^{\prime}}})\asymp\frac{N}{2}\|\mathbf{D}^{r_{2}}(\mathbf{A}_{\theta}-\mathbf{A}_{\theta^{\prime}})\mathbf{D}^{-r_{1}}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}.

Since only one entry is active,

\|\mathbf{D}^{r_{2}}(\mathbf{A}_{\theta}-\mathbf{A}_{\theta^{\prime}})\mathbf{D}^{-r_{1}}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}\asymp\delta^{2}\,2^{2J_{\mathrm{out}}r_{2}}2^{-2J_{\mathrm{in}}r_{1}},

and therefore

D_{\mbox{\tiny{\rm KL}}}(P_{\mathbf{A}_{\theta}}\|P_{\mathbf{A}_{\theta^{\prime}}})\asymp N\delta^{2}\,2^{2J_{\mathrm{out}}r_{2}}2^{-2J_{\mathrm{in}}r_{1}}.

Hence the second condition in (
4.13
) implies

\max_{H(\theta,\theta^{\prime})=1}D_{\mbox{\tiny{\rm KL}}}(P_{\mathbf{A}_{\theta}}\|P_{\mathbf{A}_{\theta^{\prime}}})\lesssim 1.

Step 3: separation in the loss metric.

For arbitrary
\theta,\theta^{\prime}
, the difference
\mathbf{A}_{\theta}-\mathbf{A}_{\theta^{\prime}}
is supported on the same strip and has exactly
H(\theta,\theta^{\prime})
nonzero entries, each equal to
\pm\delta
. Since it is again either a row vector or a column vector,

\|\mathbf{A}_{\theta}-\mathbf{A}_{\theta^{\prime}}\|=\delta\sqrt{H(\theta,\theta^{\prime})}.

Applying the weights
\mathbf{D}^{-t^{\prime}}
and
\mathbf{D}^{-t}
gives

\|\mathbf{D}^{-t^{\prime}}(\mathbf{A}_{\theta}-\mathbf{A}_{\theta^{\prime}})\mathbf{D}^{-t}\|\asymp\delta\,2^{-J_{\mathrm{out}}t^{\prime}}2^{-J_{\mathrm{in}}t}\sqrt{H(\theta,\theta^{\prime})}.

This proves part (3). Since
H(\theta,\theta^{\prime})\leq m
,

\sqrt{H(\theta,\theta^{\prime})}\geq m^{-1/2}H(\theta,\theta^{\prime}),

so

\frac{\|\mathbf{D}^{-t^{\prime}}(\mathbf{A}_{\theta}-\mathbf{A}_{\theta^{\prime}})\mathbf{D}^{-t}\|}{H(\theta,\theta^{\prime})}\gtrsim\delta\,2^{-J_{\mathrm{out}}t^{\prime}}2^{-J_{\mathrm{in}}t}m^{-1/2}.

Step 4: Assouad lower bound.

Applying Lemma
4.10
with

\psi(\theta)=\mathbf{A}_{\theta},\qquad\mathsf{d}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})=\|\mathbf{D}^{-t^{\prime}}(\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{B})\mathbf{D}^{-t}\|,

and using Steps 2 and 3, we obtain

\inf_{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}\sup_{\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{M}(s,s^{\prime},R^{\prime})}\mathbb{E}\,\|\mathbf{D}^{-t^{\prime}}(\widehat{\mathbf{A}}-\mathbf{A})\mathbf{D}^{-t}\|\gtrsim\delta\,2^{-J_{\rm out}t^{\prime}}2^{-J_{\rm in}t}\sqrt{m}.

Substituting the largest admissible
\delta
in
(
4.13
) gives (
4.15
).
∎

We now prove Proposition
4.9
by applying the thin-strip
lemma in three dyadic regimes.

Proof of Proposition
4.9
.

As discussed above, it suffices to prove the
corresponding lower bound in the matrix model under the loss

\mathsf{d}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})=\|\mathbf{D}^{-t^{\prime}}(\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{B})\mathbf{D}^{-t}\|.

We apply Lemma
4.12
in three dyadic
configurations.

Case 1: coarse-to-coarse.

Take

J_{\mathrm{out}}=0,\qquad J_{\mathrm{in}}=0,\qquad m=1.

Then (
4.15
) gives

\inf_{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}\sup_{\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{M}(s,s^{\prime},R^{\prime})}\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{D}^{-t^{\prime}}(\widehat{\mathbf{A}}-\mathbf{A})\mathbf{D}^{-t}\|\,\gtrsim\,\min\{N^{-1/2},1\}\asymp N^{-1/2}.

Case 2: fine-to-coarse.

Fix
J_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
and choose a thin strip inside the block
(0,J_{1})
. Then

J_{\mathrm{out}}=0,\qquad J_{\mathrm{in}}=J_{1},\qquad m\asymp 2^{J_{1}d}.

Hence

\inf_{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}\sup_{\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{M}(s,s^{\prime},R^{\prime})}\mathbb{E}\,\|\mathbf{D}^{-t^{\prime}}(\widehat{\mathbf{A}}-\mathbf{A})\mathbf{D}^{-t}\|\,\gtrsim\,\min\Bigl\{N^{-1/2}2^{J_{1}(r_{1}-t+d/2)},\,2^{-J_{1}(t-s)}\Bigr\}.

Since
r_{1}-s+d/2>0
, choose

J_{1}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lceil\frac{\log_{2}N}{2(r_{1}-s+d/2)}}}\right\rceil.

Then the two terms are balanced, and

N^{-1/2}2^{J_{1}(r_{1}-t+d/2)}\asymp 2^{-J_{1}(t-s)}\asymp N^{-\frac{t-s}{2(r_{1}-s)+d}}.

Case 3: coarse-to-fine.

Fix
J_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
and choose a thin strip inside the block
(J_{2},0)
. Then

J_{\mathrm{out}}=J_{2},\qquad J_{\mathrm{in}}=0,\qquad m\asymp 2^{J_{2}d}.

Thus

\inf_{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}\sup_{\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{M}(s,s^{\prime},R^{\prime})}\mathbb{E}\,\|\mathbf{D}^{-t^{\prime}}(\widehat{\mathbf{A}}-\mathbf{A})\mathbf{D}^{-t}\|\,\gtrsim\,\min\Bigl\{N^{-1/2}2^{J_{2}(-r_{2}-t^{\prime}+d/2)},\,2^{-J_{2}(t^{\prime}-s^{\prime})}\Bigr\}.

If
-r_{2}-s^{\prime}+d/2>0
, choose

J_{2}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lceil\frac{\log_{2}N}{2(-r_{2}-s^{\prime}+d/2)}}}\right\rceil,

which yields

N^{-1/2}2^{J_{2}(-r_{2}-t^{\prime}+d/2)}\asymp 2^{-J_{2}(t^{\prime}-s^{\prime})}\asymp N^{-\frac{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}{2(-r_{2}-s^{\prime})+d}}.

Collecting the three cases, we conclude

\displaystyle\inf_{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}\sup_{\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{M}(s,s^{\prime},R^{\prime})}\mathbb{E}\,\|\mathbf{D}^{-t^{\prime}}(\widehat{\mathbf{A}}-\mathbf{A})\mathbf{D}^{-t}\|\,\gtrsim\,\max\bigg\{N^{-1/2},\;N^{-\frac{t-s}{2(r_{1}-s)+d}},\;N^{-\frac{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}{2(-r_{2}-s^{\prime})+d}}\bigg\},

where the third term is included only when the corresponding denominator
2(-r_{2}-s^{\prime})+d
is positive. Equivalently,

\displaystyle\inf_{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}\sup_{\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{M}(s,s^{\prime},R^{\prime})}\mathbb{E}\,\|\mathbf{D}^{-t^{\prime}}(\widehat{\mathbf{A}}-\mathbf{A})\mathbf{D}^{-t}\|\,\gtrsim\,N^{-\min\bigl\{\frac{1}{2},\,\frac{t-s}{2(r_{1}-s)+d},\,\frac{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}{(2(-r_{2}-s^{\prime})+d)_{+}}\bigr\}}.

This proves the proposition.
∎

5
Computational cost

Proposition
4.4
shows that the estimator constructed in
Subsection
4.1
achieves the minimax rate

N^{-\gamma}
, up to logarithmic factors. We now study the computational
cost required to attain this statistical accuracy. To express the cost as a
function of a target error, we write

\varepsilon=N^{-\gamma}.

Throughout this section,
\mathsf{Cost}(\varepsilon)
denotes the number of arithmetic
operations required to construct the estimator from the finite coefficient data
used in Subsection
4.1
, when the columnwise regressions
are solved by dense direct least-squares methods. We suppress polylogarithmic
factors in
\varepsilon^{-1}
and
\delta^{-1}
.

This section has two parts. In Subsection
5.1
, we derive the
cost exponent for the scale-adaptive estimator. In
Subsection
5.2
, we explain why the two components of
this exponent are unavoidable within the present blockwise
dense-regression framework.

5.1
Cost of the scale-adaptive estimator

The following proposition gives the computational cost of the estimator in
(
4.7
).

Proposition 5.1

(Cost of the scale-adaptive estimator)
.

Under the setting of Theorem
2.1
, fix a target accuracy

\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, and choose
N
so that

(5.1)

\displaystyle\varepsilon=N^{-\gamma}.

Then the estimator in (
4.7
), implemented by
columnwise dense least squares, satisfies

\mathsf{Cost}(\varepsilon)\lesssim_{\log}\varepsilon^{-\kappa},

where

\kappa=\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\kappa_{\rm in},\kappa_{\rm out}}}\right\},

with

\kappa_{\rm in}:=2+\frac{2d}{\min\{r_{1},t\}-s}+\frac{\bigl(2(r_{1}-t)+d\bigr)_{+}}{t-s},

and

\kappa_{\rm out}:=\frac{d+\bigl(2(-r_{2}-s^{\prime})+d\bigr)_{+}}{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}=\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\frac{d}{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}},\ \frac{2(-r_{2}-s^{\prime})+2d}{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}}}\right\}.

The exponent in Proposition
5.1
has two components. The term

\kappa_{\rm in}
reflects the input-side cost of solving regressions over
many input coordinates, including the enlarged regression support needed to
control omitted-variable bias. The term
\kappa_{\rm out}
reflects the
output-side cost of processing many response coordinates at high output
resolutions. The overall cost exponent is the larger of these two contributions.

The heterogeneous multiscale structure of the problem also implies that the
statistical and computational bottlenecks need not coincide. The following
remark gives a concrete regime where the minimax rate is input-side dominated,
while the computational cost is output-side dominated.

Remark 5.2
(Statistical and computational bottlenecks may differ).

Intuitively, a large value of
r_{1}
makes
high-frequency input directions poorly excited. This can make the input side
statistically hardest: even recovering a single output component as a function
of many input frequencies requires many samples. Computationally, however, this
is still essentially one large single-response regression. By contrast, the
output side may be statistically easier but computationally more expensive:
recovering one low-frequency input component across a high output scale amounts
to estimating many response coordinates, or equivalently many scalar
regressions, since
|\nabla_{j^{\prime}}|\asymp 2^{j^{\prime}d}
.

For example, take

d=1,\qquad s=s^{\prime}=0,\qquad r_{1}=t=2,\qquad r_{2}=0,\qquad t^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}.

Then the minimax exponent in Theorem
2.1
is

\gamma=\min\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\frac{1}{2},\,\frac{2}{2\cdot 2+1},\,\frac{1/2}{1}}}\right\}=\frac{2}{5},

so the statistical rate is dominated by the input-side contribution. On the
other hand, Proposition
5.1
gives

\kappa_{\rm in}=2+\frac{2}{2}+\frac{1}{2}=\frac{7}{2},\qquad\kappa_{\rm out}=\frac{1+1}{1/2}=4.

Therefore the arithmetic cost is dominated by the output-side exponent, even
though the minimax rate is dominated by the input side.
∎

Proof of Proposition
5.1
.

Recall from Definition
4.1
that the minimal estimation region is

j(t-s)+j^{\prime}(t^{\prime}-s^{\prime})\leq\gamma\log_{2}N=\log_{2}(1/\varepsilon),

and for
j^{\prime}=0,1,\dots,\big\lceil\frac{\gamma\log_{2}N}{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}\big\rceil
,

J(j^{\prime})=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lceil\frac{\log_{2}(1/\varepsilon)-j^{\prime}(t^{\prime}-s^{\prime})}{t-s}}}\right\rceil,\qquad\widetilde{J}(j^{\prime})=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lceil\frac{\log_{2}(1/\varepsilon)-j^{\prime}(t^{\prime}-s^{\prime})}{\min\{r_{1},t\}-s}}}\right\rceil.

For the
j^{\prime}
-th column-block regression, we use
N_{j^{\prime}}
samples, where

\displaystyle N_{j^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\asymp\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{2^{\widetilde{J}(j^{\prime})d}+\log((\log N)/\delta),\ N^{2\gamma}\sup_{0\leq j\leq J(j^{\prime})}2^{-2jt-2j^{\prime}t^{\prime}+2jr_{1}-2j^{\prime}r_{2}+d\max\{j,j^{\prime}\}}}}\right\}

(5.2)

\displaystyle\asymp 2^{\widetilde{J}(j^{\prime})d}+\log(\log(1/\varepsilon)/\delta)+\varepsilon^{-2}\sup_{0\leq j\leq J(j^{\prime})}2^{-2jt-2j^{\prime}t^{\prime}+2jr_{1}-2j^{\prime}r_{2}+d\max\{j,j^{\prime}\}}.

Here we used the change of variables
\varepsilon=N^{-\gamma}
, and the
equivalence between the maximum and the sum for nonnegative quantities. We solve the corresponding problem by dense least squares, computing

(\widehat{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}},j^{\prime}}=\big((\mathbf{U}^{(j^{\prime})}_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}})^{\top}\mathbf{U}^{(j^{\prime})}_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}}\big)^{-1}(\mathbf{U}^{(j^{\prime})}_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}})^{\top}\mathbf{F}^{(j^{\prime})}_{j^{\prime}}.

The Gram-matrix computation and the multi-response multiplication cost

\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N_{j^{\prime}}2^{2\widetilde{J}(j^{\prime})d}+N_{j^{\prime}}2^{\widetilde{J}(j^{\prime})d+j^{\prime}d}}}\right),

with the matrix inversion cost absorbed since

N_{j^{\prime}}\gtrsim 2^{\widetilde{J}(j^{\prime})d}
.
Hence the total computational cost satisfies

\displaystyle\mathsf{Cost}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\asymp\sum_{j^{\prime}=0}^{\frac{\log_{2}(1/\varepsilon)}{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}}N_{j^{\prime}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2^{2\widetilde{J}(j^{\prime})d}+2^{\widetilde{J}(j^{\prime})d+j^{\prime}d}}}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\log}\underbrace{\sum_{j^{\prime}=0}^{\frac{\log_{2}(1/\varepsilon)}{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}}2^{3\widetilde{J}(j^{\prime})d}}_{=:\raisebox{0.5pt}{\textcircled{\raisebox{-.2pt}{\scriptsize 1}}}}+\underbrace{\sum_{j^{\prime}=0}^{\frac{\log_{2}(1/\varepsilon)}{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}}2^{2\widetilde{J}(j^{\prime})d+j^{\prime}d}}_{=:\raisebox{0.5pt}{\textcircled{\raisebox{-.2pt}{\scriptsize 2}}}}

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{\varepsilon^{-2}\sum_{j^{\prime}=0}^{\frac{\log_{2}(1/\varepsilon)}{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}}2^{2\widetilde{J}(j^{\prime})d}\sup_{0\leq j\leq J(j^{\prime})}2^{-2jt-2j^{\prime}t^{\prime}+2jr_{1}-2j^{\prime}r_{2}+d\max\{j,j^{\prime}\}}}_{=:\raisebox{0.5pt}{\textcircled{\raisebox{-.2pt}{\scriptsize 3}}}}

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{\varepsilon^{-2}\sum_{j^{\prime}=0}^{\frac{\log_{2}(1/\varepsilon)}{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}}2^{\widetilde{J}(j^{\prime})d+j^{\prime}d}\sup_{0\leq j\leq J(j^{\prime})}2^{-2jt-2j^{\prime}t^{\prime}+2jr_{1}-2j^{\prime}r_{2}+d\max\{j,j^{\prime}\}}}_{=:\raisebox{0.5pt}{\textcircled{\raisebox{-.2pt}{\scriptsize 4}}}}.

For the first term, substituting the definition of
\widetilde{J}(j^{\prime})
gives

\raisebox{0.5pt}{\textcircled{\raisebox{-.2pt}{\scriptsize 1}}}=\sum_{j^{\prime}=0}^{\frac{\log_{2}(1/\varepsilon)}{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}}2^{3d\frac{\log_{2}(1/\varepsilon)-j^{\prime}(t^{\prime}-s^{\prime})}{\min\{r_{1},t\}-s}}\lesssim\varepsilon^{-\frac{3d}{\min\{r_{1},t\}-s}}.

Similarly, for the second term,

\raisebox{0.5pt}{\textcircled{\raisebox{-.2pt}{\scriptsize 2}}}=\sum_{j^{\prime}=0}^{\frac{\log_{2}(1/\varepsilon)}{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}}2^{2d\frac{\log_{2}(1/\varepsilon)-j^{\prime}(t^{\prime}-s^{\prime})}{\min\{r_{1},t\}-s}+j^{\prime}d}\lesssim_{\log}\varepsilon^{-\frac{2d}{\min\{r_{1},t\}-s}}+\varepsilon^{-\frac{d}{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}}.

For the third term,

\raisebox{-.2pt}{\scriptsize3}⃝

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{-2}\sum_{j^{\prime}=0}^{\frac{\log_{2}(1/\varepsilon)}{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}}2^{2\widetilde{J}(j^{\prime})d}\sup_{0\leq j\leq J(j^{\prime})}2^{-2jt-2j^{\prime}t^{\prime}+2jr_{1}-2j^{\prime}r_{2}+d\max\{j,j^{\prime}\}}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{-2}\sum_{j^{\prime}=0}^{\frac{\log_{2}(1/\varepsilon)}{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}}\sup_{0\leq j\leq J(j^{\prime})}2^{2d\frac{\log_{2}(1/\varepsilon)-j^{\prime}(t^{\prime}-s^{\prime})}{\min\{r_{1},t\}-s}+2j(r_{1}-t)-2j^{\prime}(t^{\prime}+r_{2})+d\max\{j,j^{\prime}\}}

To obtain an estimate up to logarithmic factors, it suffices to maximize the exponent

2d\frac{\log_{2}(1/\varepsilon)-j^{\prime}(t^{\prime}-s^{\prime})}{\min\{r_{1},t\}-s}+2j(r_{1}-t)-2j^{\prime}(t^{\prime}+r_{2})+d\max\{j,j^{\prime}\}

over the triangular region

j\geq 0,\qquad j^{\prime}\geq 0,\qquad j(t-s)+j^{\prime}(t^{\prime}-s^{\prime})\leq\log_{2}(1/\varepsilon).

The exponent is piecewise affine, with the only change of affine form occurring
across the line
j=j^{\prime}
. Hence it suffices to check the vertices of the
triangular region together with the possible kink point on
j=j^{\prime}
. The kink
point lies on the edge

j(t-s)+j^{\prime}(t^{\prime}-s^{\prime})=\log_{2}(1/\varepsilon).

Along this edge, the term
d\max\{j,j^{\prime}\}
is a convex piecewise-affine
function of one variable, so its maximum is attained at one of the two endpoints
of the edge. Thus the kink point is dominated by one of the endpoints. It
therefore suffices to check the three vertices

(0,0),\qquad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\log_{2}(1/\varepsilon)}{t-s},0}}\right),\qquad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,\frac{\log_{2}(1/\varepsilon)}{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}}}\right).

At the vertex
(0,0)
, the exponent equals

\frac{2d\log_{2}(1/\varepsilon)}{\min\{r_{1},t\}-s}.

After multiplying by
\varepsilon^{-2}
, this gives the contribution

\varepsilon^{-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2+\frac{2d}{\min\{r_{1},t\}-s}}}\right)}.

At the vertex
\big(\frac{\log_{2}(1/\varepsilon)}{t-s},0\big)
,
the exponent equals

\frac{2d\log_{2}(1/\varepsilon)}{\min\{r_{1},t\}-s}+\frac{\bigl(2(r_{1}-t)+d\bigr)\log_{2}(1/\varepsilon)}{t-s}.

If
2(r_{1}-t)+d<0
, this vertex is smaller than the previous one. Hence the
two input-side vertices together give

\varepsilon^{-\big(2+\frac{2d}{\min\{r_{1},t\}-s}+\frac{(2(r_{1}-t)+d)_{+}}{t-s}\big)}.

At the vertex
\big(0,\frac{\log_{2}(1/\varepsilon)}{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}\big)
, the exponent becomes

\frac{\bigl(-2t^{\prime}-2r_{2}+d\bigr)\log_{2}(1/\varepsilon)}{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}.

After multiplying by
\varepsilon^{-2}
, this gives

\varepsilon^{-\big(2+\frac{-2t^{\prime}-2r_{2}+d}{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}\big)}=\varepsilon^{-\frac{2(-r_{2}-s^{\prime})+d}{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}}.

Therefore,

\raisebox{0.5pt}{\textcircled{\raisebox{-.2pt}{\scriptsize 3}}}\lesssim\varepsilon^{-\big(2+\frac{2d}{\min\{r_{1},t\}-s}+\frac{(2(r_{1}-t)+d)_{+}}{t-s}\big)}+\varepsilon^{-\frac{2(-r_{2}-s^{\prime})+d}{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}}.

It remains to estimate
\raisebox{-.2pt}{\scriptsize4}⃝
. Using the definition of

\widetilde{J}(j^{\prime})
, we have

\displaystyle\raisebox{0.5pt}{\textcircled{\raisebox{-.2pt}{\scriptsize 4}}}=\varepsilon^{-2}\sum_{j^{\prime}=0}^{\frac{\log_{2}(1/\varepsilon)}{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}}\sup_{0\leq j\leq J(j^{\prime})}2^{d\frac{\log_{2}(1/\varepsilon)-j^{\prime}(t^{\prime}-s^{\prime})}{\min\{r_{1},t\}-s}+j^{\prime}d+2j(r_{1}-t)-2j^{\prime}(t^{\prime}+r_{2})+d\max\{j,j^{\prime}\}}.

The exponent has the same piecewise-affine structure as in the estimate of

\raisebox{-.2pt}{\scriptsize3}⃝
. Therefore the same vertex argument over the triangular region gives

\raisebox{0.5pt}{\textcircled{\raisebox{-.2pt}{\scriptsize 4}}}\lesssim_{\log}\varepsilon^{-\big(2+\frac{d}{\min\{r_{1},t\}-s}+\frac{(2(r_{1}-t)+d)_{+}}{t-s}\big)}+\varepsilon^{-\frac{2(-r_{2}-s^{\prime})+2d}{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}}.

Combining the above estimates, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathsf{Cost}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\log}\raisebox{0.5pt}{\textcircled{\raisebox{-.2pt}{\scriptsize 1}}}+\raisebox{0.5pt}{\textcircled{\raisebox{-.2pt}{\scriptsize 2}}}+\raisebox{0.5pt}{\textcircled{\raisebox{-.2pt}{\scriptsize 3}}}+\raisebox{0.5pt}{\textcircled{\raisebox{-.2pt}{\scriptsize 4}}}

(5.3)

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\log}\varepsilon^{-\frac{3d}{\min\{r_{1},t\}-s}}+\varepsilon^{-\frac{d}{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}}+\varepsilon^{-\big(2+\frac{2d}{\min\{r_{1},t\}-s}+\frac{(2(r_{1}-t)+d)_{+}}{t-s}\big)}+\varepsilon^{-\frac{2(-r_{2}-s^{\prime})+2d}{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}}.

Since
0<\varepsilon<1
, only the largest exponent matters. The first term
in (
5.1
) is absorbed by the third term. Indeed, this follows
from

2+\frac{2d}{\min\{r_{1},t\}-s}+\frac{(2(r_{1}-t)+d)_{+}}{t-s}>\frac{3d}{\min\{r_{1},t\}-s},

or equivalently,

2+\frac{(2(r_{1}-t)+d)_{+}}{t-s}>\frac{d}{\min\{r_{1},t\}-s}.

If
r_{1}\geq t
, then

2+\frac{(2(r_{1}-t)+d)_{+}}{t-s}=2+\frac{2(r_{1}-t)+d}{t-s}>\frac{d}{t-s}=\frac{d}{\min\{r_{1},t\}-s}.

If
r_{1}<t
, then, by the assumption
r_{1}-s>d/2
,

2+\frac{(2(r_{1}-t)+d)_{+}}{t-s}\geq 2>\frac{d}{r_{1}-s}=\frac{d}{\min\{r_{1},t\}-s}.

Consequently, after absorbing the first term into the third and combining the
two output-side exponents, (
5.1
) simplifies to

\mathsf{Cost}(\varepsilon)\lesssim_{\log}\varepsilon^{-\kappa},

where

\kappa=\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\kappa_{\rm in},\kappa_{\rm out}}}\right\},

with

\kappa_{\rm in}:=2+\frac{2d}{\min\{r_{1},t\}-s}+\frac{\bigl(2(r_{1}-t)+d\bigr)_{+}}{t-s},

and

\kappa_{\rm out}:=\frac{d+\bigl(2(-r_{2}-s^{\prime})+d\bigr)_{+}}{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}=\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\frac{d}{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}},\ \frac{2(-r_{2}-s^{\prime})+2d}{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}}}\right\}.

This completes the proof.
∎

5.2
Optimality within the dense-regression framework

We next explain why the two exponents in Proposition
5.1
are
unavoidable within the present blockwise dense-regression framework.
The input-side barrier appears already in the single-column problem for

\mathbf{A}^{\top}
, which reduces to an infinite-dimensional single-response
regression problem; see §
5.2.1
. The output-side barrier
appears already in the single-row problem for
\mathbf{A}^{\top}
, which reduces to
estimating many response coordinates sharing a single input coordinate; see
§
5.2.2
.

5.2.1
Input-side regression barrier

Recall that, in the transposed representation
\mathbf{A}^{\top}
, the row index is
an input wavelet index and the column index is an output wavelet index. To isolate the input-side obstruction, fix a single low-frequency output index

\lambda^{\prime}_{0}\in\Lambda_{0}=\{(j,k):j=0,k\in\nabla_{0}\}
, and consider the subclass of matrices satisfying

(\mathbf{A}^{\top})_{\lambda,\mu}=0\qquad\text{unless }\mu=\lambda^{\prime}_{0}.

Thus the only unknown coefficients form the infinite column vector

\mathbf{x}:=\bigl((\mathbf{A}^{\top})_{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}_{0}}\bigr)_{\lambda\in\Lambda}.

On this subclass, the model reduces to the infinite-dimensional
single-response regression problem

\mathbf{F}_{\lambda^{\prime}_{0}}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{W}_{\lambda^{\prime}_{0}}.

For this restricted problem, since
|\lambda^{\prime}_{0}|=0
, the contribution to the
weighted operator norm is the weighted
\ell^{2}
-error of the column vector:

\|\mathbf{D}^{-t}(\widehat{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}-\mathbf{A}^{\top})\mathbf{D}^{-t^{\prime}}\|=\bigg(\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}2^{-2|\lambda|t}|\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{\lambda}-\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}.

On the other hand, the operator-class constraint gives

\bigg(\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}2^{-2|\lambda|s}|\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}\lesssim 1.

Thus, at input scale
j
, an admissible vector
\mathbf{x}_{j}
may have Euclidean
size

\|\mathbf{x}_{j}\|_{2}\lesssim 2^{js},

and its contribution to the loss is at most

2^{-jt}\|\mathbf{x}_{j}\|_{2}\lesssim 2^{-j(t-s)}.

Therefore, input scales satisfying

2^{-j(t-s)}\lesssim\varepsilon

can be discarded without affecting the target accuracy. The finest input scale
that must be retained satisfies

J_{0}\asymp\frac{\log_{2}(1/\varepsilon)}{t-s}.

We therefore set

J_{0}:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lceil\frac{\log_{2}(1/\varepsilon)}{t-s}}}\right\rceil,\qquad I_{0}:=\{0,1,\ldots,J_{0}\}.

However, direct regression only on
\Lambda_{I_{0}}
is not uniformly stable
over the full operator class. The coefficients outside
I_{0}
still enter the
response through the empirical design and create omitted-variable bias. To
control this effect, one must regress over an enlarged support. Namely, set

\widetilde{J}_{0}:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lceil\frac{\log_{2}(1/\varepsilon)}{\min\{r_{1},t\}-s}}}\right\rceil,\qquad\widetilde{I}_{0}:=\{0,1,\ldots,\widetilde{J}_{0}\}.

Then the single-column model can be written as

\mathbf{F}_{\lambda^{\prime}_{0}}=\mathbf{U}_{\widetilde{I}_{0}}\mathbf{x}_{\widetilde{I}_{0}}+\mathbf{U}_{\widetilde{I}_{0}^{c}}\mathbf{x}_{\widetilde{I}_{0}^{c}}+\mathbf{W}_{\lambda^{\prime}_{0}}.

The estimator solves the least-squares problem over

\Lambda_{\widetilde{I}_{0}}
and then retains only the coordinates in

\Lambda_{I_{0}}
.

The enlarged regression support has dimension

m_{0}:=|\Lambda_{\widetilde{I}_{0}}|\asymp 2^{\widetilde{J}_{0}d}\asymp\varepsilon^{-\frac{d}{\min\{r_{1},t\}-s}}.

Therefore, in the dense direct least-squares model, forming the Gram matrix
already costs
N_{0}m_{0}^{2}
.

It remains to identify the necessary sample size
N_{0}
. Since the output
scale is fixed at
j^{\prime}=0
, the blockwise stochastic error at input scale
j

has size

\frac{2^{-jt+jr_{1}+jd/2}}{\sqrt{N_{0}}}.

To make this no larger than
\varepsilon
uniformly over the retained input
scales
0\leq j\leq J_{0}
, one needs

N_{0}\gtrsim\varepsilon^{-2}\sup_{0\leq j\leq J_{0}}2^{2j(r_{1}-t)+dj}\asymp\varepsilon^{-2}\varepsilon^{-\frac{(2(r_{1}-t)+d)_{+}}{t-s}}.

Consequently, even this single-column regression problem forces the dense
least-squares cost

N_{0}m_{0}^{2}\gtrsim\varepsilon^{-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2+\frac{2d}{\min\{r_{1},t\}-s}+\frac{(2(r_{1}-t)+d)_{+}}{t-s}}}\right)}.

This is precisely the input-side exponent
\kappa_{\rm in}
. The term

\min\{r_{1},t\}-s
reflects the cost of enlarging the regression support to
control omitted-variable bias; when
r_{1}\geq t
, no enlargement is needed, but
when
r_{1}<t
, the nuisance coordinates between
I_{0}
and

\widetilde{I}_{0}
must be included in the regression even though they are not
retained in the final estimator.

The enlargement scale in the nested-support regression is not an artifact of the upper bound. To see this,
consider the following two-block population regression example inside the
single-column model. Let
X_{0}
denote one low-frequency input coordinate and
let
X_{L}
denote one input coordinate at scale
L
. Suppose that

\mathsf{Var}(X_{0})\asymp 1,\qquad\mathsf{Var}(X_{L})\asymp 2^{-2Lr_{1}},\qquad\mathsf{Cov}(X_{0},X_{L})\asymp 2^{-Lr_{1}}.

Equivalently, the preconditioned variables
X_{0}
and
2^{Lr_{1}}X_{L}
have a
nonzero correlation of order one. This covariance structure is compatible with
the diagonal preconditioning bounds in Lemma
3.1
.

Now consider the noiseless single-column regression model

Y=X_{0}\beta+X_{L}\theta.

If one regresses
Y
only on
X_{0}
, omitting
X_{L}
, the population least
squares coefficient is

\beta_{\rm pop}=\beta+\frac{\mathsf{Cov}(X_{0},X_{L})}{\mathsf{Var}(X_{0})}\theta.

Hence the omitted-variable bias is

|\beta_{\rm pop}-\beta|\asymp 2^{-Lr_{1}}|\theta|.

The operator-class constraint allows a coefficient at input scale
L
of size

|\theta|\asymp 2^{Ls}.

Therefore the omitted-variable bias can be as large as

2^{-Lr_{1}}2^{Ls}=2^{-L(r_{1}-s)}.

Since this bias appears in a low-frequency retained coordinate, the

H^{t}
-loss weight does not reduce it. Thus, to make the omitted-variable bias
no larger than the target accuracy
\varepsilon
, it is necessary that

2^{-L(r_{1}-s)}\lesssim\varepsilon,

or equivalently

L\gtrsim\frac{\log_{2}(1/\varepsilon)}{r_{1}-s}.

In the regime
r_{1}<t
, this is precisely

\widetilde{J}_{0}\asymp\frac{\log_{2}(1/\varepsilon)}{\min\{r_{1},t\}-s}=\frac{\log_{2}(1/\varepsilon)}{r_{1}-s}.

Thus the enlarged regression support has the optimal order: using a smaller
cutoff would allow high-frequency nuisance coefficients, already negligible in
the final
H^{t}
-loss, to leak into low-frequency retained coordinates and
produce an error larger than
\varepsilon
.

5.2.2
Output-side multi-response barrier

To isolate the output-side obstruction, fix a single low-frequency input index

\lambda_{0}\in\Lambda_{0}
, and fix an output scale
j^{\prime}
. Consider the
subclass of matrices satisfying

(\mathbf{A}^{\top})_{\mu,\lambda^{\prime}}=0\qquad\text{unless }\mu=\lambda_{0}\quad\text{and}\quad\lambda^{\prime}\in\Lambda_{j^{\prime}}.

Thus the only unknown coefficients form the row vector

\mathbf{y}:=\bigl((\mathbf{A}^{\top})_{\lambda_{0},\lambda^{\prime}}\bigr)_{\lambda^{\prime}\in\Lambda_{j^{\prime}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\Lambda_{j^{\prime}}|}.

On this subclass, the block-column model reduces to a one-predictor,
multi-response regression problem

\mathbf{F}_{j^{\prime}}=\mathbf{U}_{:,\lambda_{0}}\mathbf{y}^{\top}+\mathbf{W}_{j^{\prime}},

where

\mathbf{U}_{:,\lambda_{0}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 1},\qquad\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\Lambda_{j^{\prime}}|},\qquad\mathbf{F}_{j^{\prime}},\mathbf{W}_{j^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times|\Lambda_{j^{\prime}}|}.

For this restricted problem, the contribution to the weighted operator norm is
the Euclidean error of the row vector, with the output Sobolev weight:

\|\mathbf{D}^{-t}(\widehat{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}-\mathbf{A}^{\top})\mathbf{D}^{-t^{\prime}}\|=2^{-j^{\prime}t^{\prime}}\|\widehat{\mathbf{y}}-\mathbf{y}\|_{2}.

The noise at output scale
j^{\prime}
has coordinate size
2^{-j^{\prime}r_{2}}
, and

|\Lambda_{j^{\prime}}|=|\nabla_{j^{\prime}}|\asymp 2^{j^{\prime}d}.

Hence, if all coordinates of
\mathbf{y}
are estimated using
N_{j^{\prime}}
samples,
the least-squares error has the scale

\|\widehat{\mathbf{y}}-\mathbf{y}\|_{2}\asymp\frac{2^{-j^{\prime}r_{2}}|\Lambda_{j^{\prime}}|^{1/2}}{\sqrt{N_{j^{\prime}}}}\asymp\frac{2^{-j^{\prime}r_{2}+j^{\prime}d/2}}{\sqrt{N_{j^{\prime}}}}.

Therefore, to make the weighted error at this output scale at most

\varepsilon
, one needs

2^{-j^{\prime}t^{\prime}}\frac{2^{-j^{\prime}r_{2}+j^{\prime}d/2}}{\sqrt{N_{j^{\prime}}}}\lesssim\varepsilon,

or equivalently

N_{j^{\prime}}\gtrsim\varepsilon^{-2}2^{-2j^{\prime}t^{\prime}-2j^{\prime}r_{2}+j^{\prime}d}.

Since processing the responses at this scale costs at least

N_{j^{\prime}}|\Lambda_{j^{\prime}}|
, the cost at the fixed output scale
j^{\prime}
is bounded
below by

N_{j^{\prime}}|\Lambda_{j^{\prime}}|\gtrsim\varepsilon^{-2}2^{-2j^{\prime}t^{\prime}-2j^{\prime}r_{2}+2j^{\prime}d}.

This lower bound cannot be improved by assigning different sample sizes to
different response coordinates. Indeed, if
N_{\lambda^{\prime}}
samples are used
for the coordinate
\lambda^{\prime}\in\Lambda_{j^{\prime}}
, then the weighted squared error
constraint gives

\sum_{\lambda^{\prime}\in\Lambda_{j^{\prime}}}\frac{2^{-2j^{\prime}t^{\prime}}2^{-2j^{\prime}r_{2}}}{N_{\lambda^{\prime}}}\lesssim\varepsilon^{2},

whereas the response-processing cost is at least

\sum_{\lambda^{\prime}\in\Lambda_{j^{\prime}}}N_{\lambda^{\prime}}.

By Cauchy’s inequality,

\bigg(\sum_{\lambda^{\prime}\in\Lambda_{j^{\prime}}}N_{\lambda^{\prime}}\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{\lambda^{\prime}\in\Lambda_{j^{\prime}}}\frac{1}{N_{\lambda^{\prime}}}\bigg)\geq|\Lambda_{j^{\prime}}|^{2}.

Consequently,

\sum_{\lambda^{\prime}\in\Lambda_{j^{\prime}}}N_{\lambda^{\prime}}\gtrsim\varepsilon^{-2}2^{-2j^{\prime}t^{\prime}-2j^{\prime}r_{2}}|\Lambda_{j^{\prime}}|^{2}\asymp\varepsilon^{-2}2^{-2j^{\prime}t^{\prime}-2j^{\prime}r_{2}+2j^{\prime}d}.

Thus the fixed-scale multi-response cost is optimal even under coordinatewise
sample allocation.

It remains to choose the largest output scale that is statistically relevant
at accuracy
\varepsilon
. Since the row vector is admissible with

\|\mathbf{y}\|_{2}\lesssim 2^{j^{\prime}s^{\prime}},

its weighted size is

2^{-j^{\prime}t^{\prime}}\|\mathbf{y}\|_{2}\lesssim 2^{-j^{\prime}(t^{\prime}-s^{\prime})}.

Therefore, output scales satisfying

2^{-j^{\prime}(t^{\prime}-s^{\prime})}\lesssim\varepsilon

can be discarded without affecting the target accuracy, while the finest
relevant scale satisfies

j^{\prime}\asymp\frac{\log_{2}(1/\varepsilon)}{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}.

At this scale, the number of output coefficients is

|\Lambda_{j^{\prime}}|\asymp 2^{j^{\prime}d}\asymp\varepsilon^{-\frac{d}{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}},

so explicit recovery already has the output-resolution floor

\varepsilon^{-\frac{d}{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}}.

Moreover, the fixed-scale multi-response lower bound becomes

\varepsilon^{-2}2^{-2j^{\prime}t^{\prime}-2j^{\prime}r_{2}+2j^{\prime}d}\asymp\varepsilon^{-\frac{2(-r_{2}-s^{\prime})+2d}{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}}.

Consequently, this restricted row-vector recovery problem already forces the
two output-side barriers

\frac{d}{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{2(-r_{2}-s^{\prime})+2d}{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}.

This explains the output-side exponent

\kappa_{\rm out}=\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\frac{d}{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}},\frac{2(-r_{2}-s^{\prime})+2d}{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}}}\right\}

within the blockwise dense-regression framework.

A.1
Bias

Proof of Lemma
4.5
.

Since
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{O}(s,s^{\prime},R)
, the norm equivalence implies that
\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{M}(s,s^{\prime},R^{\prime})
, namely,

\|\mathbf{D}^{-s^{\prime}}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{D}^{-s}\|\leq R^{\prime}.

Recall that
\mathbf{D}^{s}
is the diagonal matrix whose
j
-th block is
2^{js}I
, see (
3.1
). It follows that for each
j,j^{\prime}\geq 0
,

\bigl\|2^{-js^{\prime}}\mathbf{A}_{j,j^{\prime}}2^{-j^{\prime}s}\bigr\|\leq R^{\prime},

and hence

\|\mathbf{A}_{j,j^{\prime}}\|\leq R^{\prime}2^{js^{\prime}+j^{\prime}s}.

Therefore,

\|(\mathbf{A}^{\top})_{j,j^{\prime}}\|=\|\mathbf{A}_{j^{\prime},j}\|\leq R^{\prime}2^{js+j^{\prime}s^{\prime}}.

By the block Schur test,

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{D}^{-t}(\mathbf{A}^{\top})_{\mathcal{E}_{N}^{c}}\mathbf{D}^{-t^{\prime}}\|

\displaystyle\leq\bigg(\sup_{j}\sum_{j^{\prime}:(j,j^{\prime})\in\mathcal{E}_{N}^{c}}2^{-jt-j^{\prime}t^{\prime}}\|(\mathbf{A}^{\top})_{j,j^{\prime}}\|\bigg)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\bigg(\sup_{j^{\prime}}\sum_{j:(j,j^{\prime})\in\mathcal{E}_{N}^{c}}2^{-jt-j^{\prime}t^{\prime}}\|(\mathbf{A}^{\top})_{j,j^{\prime}}\|\bigg)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq R^{\prime}\bigg(\sup_{j}\sum_{j^{\prime}:(j,j^{\prime})\in\mathcal{E}_{N}^{c}}2^{-j(t-s)-j^{\prime}(t^{\prime}-s^{\prime})}\bigg)^{1/2}\bigg(\sup_{j^{\prime}}\sum_{j:(j,j^{\prime})\in\mathcal{E}_{N}^{c}}2^{-j(t-s)-j^{\prime}(t^{\prime}-s^{\prime})}\bigg)^{1/2}.

Recall that

\mathcal{E}_{N}=\Big\{(\lambda,\lambda^{\prime})\in\Lambda\times\Lambda:j(t-s)+j^{\prime}(t^{\prime}-s^{\prime})\leq\gamma\log_{2}N\Big\}.

Fix
j\geq 0
. If

j\leq\frac{\gamma\log_{2}N}{t-s},

then
(j,j^{\prime})\in\mathcal{E}_{N}^{c}
means

j^{\prime}>\frac{\gamma\log_{2}N-j(t-s)}{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\sum_{j^{\prime}:(j,j^{\prime})\in\mathcal{E}_{N}^{c}}2^{-j(t-s)-j^{\prime}(t^{\prime}-s^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=2^{-j(t-s)}\sum_{j^{\prime}>\frac{\gamma\log_{2}N-j(t-s)}{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}}2^{-j^{\prime}(t^{\prime}-s^{\prime})}

\displaystyle\lesssim 2^{-j(t-s)}\,2^{-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\gamma\log_{2}N-j(t-s)}{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}}}\right)(t^{\prime}-s^{\prime})}=N^{-\gamma}.

If instead

j>\frac{\gamma\log_{2}N}{t-s},

then automatically
(j,j^{\prime})\in\mathcal{E}_{N}^{c}
for all
j^{\prime}\geq 0
, so

\sum_{j^{\prime}:(j,j^{\prime})\in\mathcal{E}_{N}^{c}}2^{-j(t-s)-j^{\prime}(t^{\prime}-s^{\prime})}=2^{-j(t-s)}\sum_{j^{\prime}\geq 0}2^{-j^{\prime}(t^{\prime}-s^{\prime})}\lesssim 2^{-j(t-s)}\leq N^{-\gamma}.

Thus,

\sup_{j}\sum_{j^{\prime}:(j,j^{\prime})\in\mathcal{E}_{N}^{c}}2^{-j(t-s)-j^{\prime}(t^{\prime}-s^{\prime})}\lesssim N^{-\gamma}.

By the same argument,

\sup_{j^{\prime}}\sum_{j:(j,j^{\prime})\in\mathcal{E}_{N}^{c}}2^{-j(t-s)-j^{\prime}(t^{\prime}-s^{\prime})}\lesssim N^{-\gamma}.

Substituting these bounds into the Schur estimate yields

\mathsf{Error\text{-}Bias}=\|\mathbf{D}^{-t}(\mathbf{A}^{\top})_{\mathcal{E}_{N}^{c}}\mathbf{D}^{-t^{\prime}}\|\lesssim N^{-\gamma}.

∎

A.2
Omitted-variable bias

Proof of Lemma
4.6
.

Recall that
\mathbf{B}^{\mathrm{ovb}}
is defined block-columnwise by

(\mathbf{B}^{\mathrm{ovb}})_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}},j^{\prime}}:=\big((\mathbf{U}^{(j^{\prime})}_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}})^{\top}\mathbf{U}^{(j^{\prime})}_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}}\big)^{-1}(\mathbf{U}^{(j^{\prime})}_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}})^{\top}\mathbf{U}^{(j^{\prime})}_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}^{c}}(\mathbf{A}^{\top})_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}^{c},j^{\prime}},

for all

j^{\prime}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lceil\frac{\gamma\log_{2}N}{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}}}\right\rceil,

where

\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}=\{0,1,\ldots,\widetilde{J}(j^{\prime})\},\qquad\widetilde{J}(j^{\prime})=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lceil\frac{\gamma\log_{2}N-j^{\prime}(t^{\prime}-s^{\prime})}{\min\{r_{1},t\}-s}}}\right\rceil.

We first prove the following blockwise bound: with probability at least

1-\delta
, uniformly over all relevant
j^{\prime}
and all
j\in I_{j^{\prime}}
,

(A.1)

\displaystyle\|(\mathbf{B}^{\mathrm{ovb}})_{j,j^{\prime}}\|\lesssim R^{\prime}2^{jr_{1}+j^{\prime}s^{\prime}-\widetilde{J}(j^{\prime})(r_{1}-s)}.

Write

\widehat{\Sigma}:=\frac{1}{N_{j^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{U}^{(j^{\prime})})^{\top}\mathbf{U}^{(j^{\prime})},\qquad\widehat{\bar{\Sigma}}:=\mathbf{D}^{r_{1}}\widehat{\Sigma}\mathbf{D}^{r_{1}},

and let

\Sigma:=\mathbb{E}\widehat{\Sigma},\qquad\bar{\Sigma}:=\mathbf{D}^{r_{1}}\Sigma\mathbf{D}^{r_{1}}.

For notational simplicity, we suppress the dependence of these matrices on

j^{\prime}
. However, throughout the analysis of the
j^{\prime}
-th column block,

\widehat{\Sigma}
is formed using only the corresponding
N_{j^{\prime}}
samples.

By Lemma
3.1
,
\bar{\Sigma}
is uniformly
well-conditioned:

c_{-}\leq\sigma_{\min}(\bar{\Sigma})\leq\sigma_{\max}(\bar{\Sigma})\leq c_{+}.

Moreover,

(\mathbf{B}^{\mathrm{ovb}})_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}},j^{\prime}}=\mathbf{D}^{r_{1}}(\widehat{\bar{\Sigma}}_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}},\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}})^{-1}\widehat{\bar{\Sigma}}_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}},\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}^{c}}\mathbf{D}^{-r_{1}}(\mathbf{A}^{\top})_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}^{c},j^{\prime}}.

Equivalently,

(A.2)

(\mathbf{B}^{\mathrm{ovb}})_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}},j^{\prime}}=\mathbf{D}^{r_{1}}(\widehat{\bar{\Sigma}}_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}},\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}})^{-1}\widehat{\bar{\Sigma}}_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}},\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}^{c}}\mathbf{D}^{-(r_{1}-s)}\mathbf{D}^{-s}(\mathbf{A}^{\top})_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}^{c},j^{\prime}}.

We now control the two empirical factors. First, since

|\Lambda_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}}|\lesssim 2^{\widetilde{J}(j^{\prime})d}\lesssim N_{j^{\prime}},

standard Gaussian sample covariance concentration gives that, with probability at
least
1-\delta/2
,

\|(\widehat{\bar{\Sigma}}_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}},\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}})^{-1}\|\lesssim 1,

provided that
N_{j^{\prime}}\gtrsim 2^{\widetilde{J}(j^{\prime})d}+\log(1/\delta)
. After a union bound
over the
O(\log N)
relevant values of
j^{\prime}
, we have that with probability at
least
1-\delta/2
,

(A.3)

\displaystyle\sup_{j^{\prime}}\|(\widehat{\bar{\Sigma}}_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}},\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}})^{-1}\|\lesssim 1,

provided
N_{j^{\prime}}\gtrsim 2^{\widetilde{J}(j^{\prime})d}+\log((\log N)/\delta)
.

Second, we claim that, with probability at least
1-\delta/2
,

(A.4)

\displaystyle\sup_{j^{\prime}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\widehat{\bar{\Sigma}}_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}},\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}^{c}}\mathbf{D}^{-(r_{1}-s)}}}\right\|\lesssim 2^{-\widetilde{J}(j^{\prime})(r_{1}-s)}.

Indeed, decompose

\widehat{\bar{\Sigma}}_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}},\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}^{c}}\mathbf{D}^{-(r_{1}-s)}=\bar{\Sigma}_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}},\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}^{c}}\mathbf{D}^{-(r_{1}-s)}+(\widehat{\bar{\Sigma}}-\bar{\Sigma})_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}},\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}^{c}}\mathbf{D}^{-(r_{1}-s)}.

The deterministic part is bounded by

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\bar{\Sigma}_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}},\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}^{c}}\mathbf{D}^{-(r_{1}-s)}}}\right\|\leq\|\bar{\Sigma}\|\,\|\mathbf{D}^{-(r_{1}-s)}_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}^{c}}\|\lesssim 2^{-\widetilde{J}(j^{\prime})(r_{1}-s)}.

We next control the stochastic part by writing it explicitly as a sample
cross-covariance. For fixed
j^{\prime}
, define the coordinate projections

P_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}}:\ell^{2}(\Lambda)\to\ell^{2}(\Lambda_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}}),\qquad P_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}^{c}}:\ell^{2}(\Lambda)\to\ell^{2}(\Lambda_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}^{c}}).

Let

X_{i}:=P_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}}\mathbf{D}^{r_{1}}\mathbf{u}_{i},\qquad Y_{i}:=\mathbf{D}^{-(r_{1}-s)}P_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}^{c}}\mathbf{D}^{r_{1}}\mathbf{u}_{i}.

Then
X_{i}
and
Y_{i}
are centered jointly Gaussian random variables taking
values in

\ell^{2}(\Lambda_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}})
and

\ell^{2}(\Lambda_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}^{c}})
, respectively. Moreover,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N_{j^{\prime}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{j^{\prime}}}X_{i}Y^{\top}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{j^{\prime}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{j^{\prime}}}\big(P_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}}\mathbf{D}^{r_{1}}\mathbf{u}_{i}\big)\big(\mathbf{D}^{-(r_{1}-s)}P_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}^{c}}\mathbf{D}^{r_{1}}\mathbf{u}_{i}\big)^{\top}

\displaystyle=P_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}}\bigg(\frac{1}{N_{j^{\prime}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{j^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{D}^{r_{1}}\mathbf{u}_{i})(\mathbf{D}^{r_{1}}\mathbf{u}_{i})^{\top}\bigg)P_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}^{c}}\mathbf{D}^{-(r_{1}-s)}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\bar{\Sigma}}_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}},\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}^{c}}\mathbf{D}^{-(r_{1}-s)}.

Similarly,

\mathbb{E}XY^{\top}=\bar{\Sigma}_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}},\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}^{c}}\mathbf{D}^{-(r_{1}-s)}.

Therefore,

\frac{1}{N_{j^{\prime}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{j^{\prime}}}X_{i}Y^{\top}_{i}-\mathbb{E}XY^{\top}=(\widehat{\bar{\Sigma}}-\bar{\Sigma})_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}},\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}^{c}}\mathbf{D}^{-(r_{1}-s)}.

We now estimate the covariance quantities in
Lemma
A.1
. Since

\bar{\Sigma}=\mathbf{D}^{r_{1}}\Sigma\mathbf{D}^{r_{1}}
is uniformly bounded and bounded
below on
\ell^{2}(\Lambda)

X
is

\Sigma_{X}=P_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}}\bar{\Sigma}P_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}}.

Thus

\|\Sigma_{X}\|\lesssim 1,\qquad\mathrm{Tr}(\Sigma_{X})\lesssim\operatorname{rank}(P_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}})\lesssim 2^{\widetilde{J}(j^{\prime})d}.

Similarly, the covariance operator of
Y
is

\Sigma_{Y}=\mathbf{D}^{-(r_{1}-s)}P_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}^{c}}\bar{\Sigma}P_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}^{c}}\mathbf{D}^{-(r_{1}-s)}.

,

\|\Sigma_{Y}\|\lesssim\|\mathbf{D}^{-(r_{1}-s)}P_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}^{c}}\|^{2}\lesssim 2^{-2\widetilde{J}(j^{\prime})(r_{1}-s)}.

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}(\Sigma_{Y})\lesssim\mathrm{Tr}\Big(\mathbf{D}^{-2(r_{1}-s)}P_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}^{c}}\Big)\lesssim\sum_{k>\widetilde{J}(j^{\prime})}2^{kd}2^{-2k(r_{1}-s)}\lesssim 2^{-2\widetilde{J}(j^{\prime})(r_{1}-s)}2^{\widetilde{J}(j^{\prime})d}.

Applying Lemma
A.1
with

t\asymp\log((\log N)/\delta)
, and taking a union bound over the

O(\log N)
possible values of
j^{\prime}
, we obtain, with probability at least

1-\delta
, uniformly over all relevant
j^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|(\widehat{\bar{\Sigma}}-\bar{\Sigma})_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}},\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}^{c}}\mathbf{D}^{-(r_{1}-s)}}}\right\|

\displaystyle\lesssim 2^{-\widetilde{J}(j^{\prime})(r_{1}-s)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{\frac{2^{\widetilde{J}(j^{\prime})d}+\log((\log N)/\delta)}{N_{j^{\prime}}}}+\frac{2^{\widetilde{J}(j^{\prime})d}+\log((\log N)/\delta)}{N_{j^{\prime}}}}}\right).

In particular, since
N_{j^{\prime}}\gtrsim 2^{\widetilde{J}(j^{\prime})d}+\log((\log N)/\delta)
, we have

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|(\widehat{\bar{\Sigma}}-\bar{\Sigma})_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}},\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}^{c}}\mathbf{D}^{-(r_{1}-s)}}}\right\|\lesssim 2^{-\widetilde{J}(j^{\prime})(r_{1}-s)}.

\mathcal{S}_{j^{\prime}}\cap\mathcal{B}_{N}
fails, the corresponding block is set to
zero. Since the number of retained scale blocks and the Sobolev weights on
those blocks are polynomial in
N
, it follows that

\|\widehat{\mathcal{A}}^{\rm tr}\|_{H^{t}\to H^{-t^{\prime}}}\leq N^{K}.

Moreover, because
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{O}(s,s^{\prime},R)
and
t>s
,
t^{\prime}>s^{\prime}
,

\|\mathcal{A}\|_{H^{t}\to H^{-t^{\prime}}}\lesssim R.

This proves (
A.13
), after increasing
K

if necessary.

We now combine the preceding estimates:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\widehat{\mathcal{A}}^{\rm tr}-\mathcal{A}\|_{H^{t}\to H^{-t^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{q}N^{-\gamma}(\log N)^{3/2}+N^{K}\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\big((\mathcal{G}_{N}\cap\mathcal{B}_{N})^{c}\big)

\displaystyle\leq C_{q}N^{-\gamma}(\log N)^{3/2}+N^{K}\bigl(N^{-q}+e^{-cN}\bigr).

Choosing
q>K+\gamma+1
, the second term is absorbed into the first, and
therefore

\mathbb{E}\|\widehat{\mathcal{A}}^{\rm tr}-\mathcal{A}\|_{H^{t}\to H^{-t^{\prime}}}\lesssim N^{-\gamma}(\log N)^{3/2}.

This completes the proof.
∎

We finally note that the stability truncation does not change the computational
cost exponent. The sample sizes
N_{j^{\prime}}
are defined with

\delta_{N}=N^{-q}
, so the confidence term

\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\log N}{\delta_{N}}}}\right)=\log\bigl(N^{q}\log N\bigr)

is still only logarithmic in
N
. Therefore the estimates in
Proposition
5.1
are unchanged up to polylogarithmic factors.

Moreover, for each
j^{\prime}
, the Gram matrix
\mathbf{G}_{j^{\prime}}
is already formed in
the dense least-squares computation. Checking the stability cutoff

\mathcal{S}_{j^{\prime}}
by Cholesky factorization or eigendecomposition costs

O(m_{j^{\prime}}^{3})
, where

m_{j^{\prime}}=|\Lambda_{\widetilde{I}_{j^{\prime}}}|\asymp 2^{\widetilde{J}(j^{\prime})d}.

Since
N_{j^{\prime}}\gtrsim m_{j^{\prime}}
, this is bounded by

O(N_{j^{\prime}}m_{j^{\prime}}^{2})
, the same order as the Gram-matrix computation. The
data-size cutoff requires computing Frobenius norms of the matrices already
used in the regressions, with total cost

\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{j^{\prime}=0}^{J^{\prime}_{\max}}N_{j^{\prime}}(m_{j^{\prime}}+n_{j^{\prime}})}}\right),

which is dominated by

\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{j^{\prime}=0}^{J^{\prime}_{\max}}N_{j^{\prime}}(m_{j^{\prime}}^{2}+m_{j^{\prime}}n_{j^{\prime}})}}\right),

the dense least-squares cost analyzed in Proposition
5.1
. Hence

\widehat{\mathcal{A}}^{\rm tr}
has the same computational cost as

\widehat{\mathcal{A}}
, up to polylogarithmic factors.

A.5
Diagonal input covariance

In this subsection, we show that, when
\mathbf{C}_{u}
is exactly diagonal, the standard
estimator without nested-support regression, namely the estimator with

J(j^{\prime})\equiv\widetilde{J}(j^{\prime})
, also achieves the minimax rate

N^{-\gamma}
. The bias and variance analyses are unchanged; it therefore
remains only to revisit the omitted-variable bias term.

Since
\mathbf{C}_{u}
is diagonal, the population cross-covariance between disjoint
coordinate blocks vanishes. Therefore the population omitted-variable bias is zero, and only
the empirical cross-covariance fluctuation needs to be controlled.

We first prove the following blockwise bound: with probability at least

1-\delta
, uniformly over all
(j,j^{\prime})\in\mathcal{E}_{N}
,

(A.14)

\displaystyle\|(\mathbf{B}^{\mathrm{ovb}})_{j,j^{\prime}}\|\lesssim R^{\prime}2^{jr_{1}+j^{\prime}s^{\prime}-J(j^{\prime})(r_{1}-s)}\sqrt{\frac{2^{J(j^{\prime})d}+\log((\log N)/\delta)}{N}}.

Indeed, since the population cross-covariance vanishes, the OVB contribution is
controlled by the empirical fluctuation

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|(\widehat{\bar{\Sigma}}-\bar{\Sigma})_{I_{j^{\prime}},I_{j^{\prime}}^{c}}\mathbf{D}^{-(r_{1}-s)}}}\right\|=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|(\widehat{\bar{\Sigma}})_{I_{j^{\prime}},I_{j^{\prime}}^{c}}\mathbf{D}^{-(r_{1}-s)}}}\right\|.

By the sample cross-covariance bound, uniformly over
j^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|(\widehat{\bar{\Sigma}}-\bar{\Sigma})_{I_{j^{\prime}},I_{j^{\prime}}^{c}}\mathbf{D}^{-(r_{1}-s)}}}\right\|

\displaystyle\lesssim 2^{-J(j^{\prime})(r_{1}-s)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{\frac{2^{J(j^{\prime})d}+\log((\log N)/\delta)}{N}}+\frac{2^{J(j^{\prime})d}+\log((\log N)/\delta)}{N}}}\right).

Since
2^{J(j^{\prime})d}\lesssim N
on
\mathcal{E}_{N}
, the linear term is dominated by
the square-root term, and hence

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|(\widehat{\bar{\Sigma}})_{I_{j^{\prime}},I_{j^{\prime}}^{c}}\mathbf{D}^{-(r_{1}-s)}}}\right\|\lesssim 2^{-J(j^{\prime})(r_{1}-s)}\sqrt{\frac{2^{J(j^{\prime})d}+\log((\log N)/\delta)}{N}}.

Combining this estimate with the blockwise regularity bound for
\mathbf{A}

gives (
A.14
).

It remains to check that, after applying the weights
2^{-jt-j^{\prime}t^{\prime}}
, the
leading deterministic factor in (
A.14
) is of
order
N^{-\gamma}
. Ignoring logarithmic factors, it suffices to prove

2^{-jt-j^{\prime}t^{\prime}}2^{jr_{1}+j^{\prime}s^{\prime}-J(j^{\prime})(r_{1}-s)}\sqrt{\frac{2^{J(j^{\prime})d}}{N}}\lesssim N^{-\gamma},

or equivalently,

2^{-J(j^{\prime})(r_{1}-s)}\sqrt{\frac{2^{J(j^{\prime})d}}{N}}\,2^{-jt-j^{\prime}t^{\prime}+jr_{1}+j^{\prime}s^{\prime}}\lesssim N^{-\gamma}.

Equivalently, it suffices to show

2^{-J(j^{\prime})(r_{1}-s-d/2)+j(r_{1}-t)-j^{\prime}(t^{\prime}-s^{\prime})}\lesssim N^{1/2-\gamma}.

For
(j,j^{\prime})\in\mathcal{E}_{N}
, we have

j\leq J(j^{\prime})=\frac{\gamma\log_{2}N-j^{\prime}(t^{\prime}-s^{\prime})}{t-s}.

Therefore

\displaystyle-J(j^{\prime})\Bigl(r_{1}-s-\frac{d}{2}\Bigr)+j(r_{1}-t)-j^{\prime}(t^{\prime}-s^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq-J(j^{\prime})\Bigl(r_{1}-s-\frac{d}{2}\Bigr)+J(j^{\prime})(r_{1}-t)_{+}-j^{\prime}(t^{\prime}-s^{\prime})

\displaystyle=J(j^{\prime})\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((r_{1}-t)_{+}-r_{1}+s+\frac{d}{2}}}\right)-j^{\prime}(t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}).

If
r_{1}<t
, then

(r_{1}-t)_{+}-r_{1}+s+\frac{d}{2}=-r_{1}+s+\frac{d}{2}<0,

by the assumption
r_{1}-s>d/2
. Hence the exponent is nonpositive.

It remains to consider
r_{1}\geq t
. In this case,

(r_{1}-t)_{+}-r_{1}+s+\frac{d}{2}=\frac{d}{2}-(t-s).

If
t-s\geq d/2
, the exponent is again nonpositive. If
t-s<d/2
, then

\displaystyle-J(j^{\prime})\Bigl(r_{1}-s-\frac{d}{2}\Bigr)+j(r_{1}-t)-j^{\prime}(t^{\prime}-s^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq J(j^{\prime})\Bigl(\frac{d}{2}-(t-s)\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\log_{2}N\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{d}{2(t-s)}-1}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{2}-\gamma}}\right)\log_{2}N,

where the last inequality follows from

\gamma\leq\frac{t-s}{2(r_{1}-s)+d}\leq\frac{t-s}{d}.

This proves

2^{-J(j^{\prime})(r_{1}-s-d/2)+j(r_{1}-t)-j^{\prime}(t^{\prime}-s^{\prime})}\lesssim N^{1/2-\gamma},

and hence the OVB contribution is bounded by
N^{-\gamma}
, up to logarithmic
factors.
