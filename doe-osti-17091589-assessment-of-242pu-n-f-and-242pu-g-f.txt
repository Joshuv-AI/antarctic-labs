1 Introduction
This report presents the result of an assessment of the prompt neutron and prompt photon emission in the
242
 Pu(nth. ,f) and 242 Pu(γ,f) reactions. Three different models were used: the fission event generator FETA
[1] based on the Hauser-Feshbach statistical reaction theory and the YAHFC code [2]; the phenomenological
GEF code [3]; the fission event generator FREYA based on the Weisskopf-Ewing statistical reaction theory
[4]. Results obtained with FREYA were not included in the determination of the final numbers because they
were based on GEF 2.2 input data, which contained an incorrect fit of the total kinetic energy of the fission
fragments. The updated inputs were received too late to be included here. However, the FREYA analysis
gives an estimate of the statistical uncertainties of typical model calculations and is reported for this reason.
The FREYA result will be updated shortly. Section 2 summarizes the result of the calculations. Specific
sections are dedicated to each of the models. Section 3 for FETA results, Section 4 for GEF results and
Section 5 for FREYA results.

3 FETA
3.1 Introduction to FETA
FETA (Fission Evaluation Tools and Analytics) is a fission event generator that computes fission observables
by deexciting fission fragments within the Hauser-Feshbach (HF) statistical reaction theory [1]. The HF
deexcitation is handled by the LLNL-developed code YAHFC (Yet Another HAuser-Feshbach Code) [2].
FETA itself is a Python wrapper around YAHFC that supplies all the necessary inputs and extracts from
YAHFC outputs the relevant fission observables.
 FETA requires two classes of inputs. The initial distribution of fission fragments Ypre (Z, N ) prior to any
emission of particles, the excitation energy E ∗ (Z, N ) of each fragment and the spin distribution P(J|Z, N )
of all fragments are provided by fission models. In this assessment, we use Ypre (Z, N ) provided by GEF.
The excitation energy of the fragments is obtained by invoking a sharing mechanism of the total excitation
energy between the heavy and light fragment. The total excitation energy (TXE) of the reaction is itself
obtained from energy conservation and the total kinetic energy (TKE) as a function of the mass of the heavy
fragment TKE(AH ). For a given pair of fragments, the TXE is shared among the fragments according to the
prescription of the CGMF code [6]. The sharing is driven by a single parameter RT (AH ) that gives the ratio
of temperatures of the two fragments and is obtained by empirical fit to data. The spin distributions for the
fragment (Z, N ) are taken from the statistical model with the spin cutoff parameter given by the CGMF
as αIrig. (A, β2 )T with β2 the ground-state deformation of the nucleus (Z, N ), Irig. (A, β2 ) the rigid-body
moment of inertia, and T the temperature, given by E ∗ /a(E ∗ ) with E ∗ the excitation energy and a(E ∗ ) the

 4

level-density parameter.
 The second class of inputs is provided by nuclear structure models that determine how the statistical
decay proceeds. These include nuclear level densities, γ-strength functions, transmission coefficients, and
low-lying discrete excited states, electromagnetic transitions and branching ratios. For lack of a better
prescription, we used simple standard models for all these inputs: the Gilbert-Cameron parameterization
of the Fermi-Gas model for level densities with the same default parameterization as the TALYS code [7],
the Kopecky-Uhl fit of γ-strength functions [8] and the Koning-Delaroche optical model for the transmission
coefficients [9]. The low-lying spectra are provided by the RIPL-3 files [10].

3.2 FETA Calculations in 242 Pu(nth. ,f )
For the description of 242 Pu(nth. ,f), the multiplicative factor α for the spin cutoff parameter is provided by
the CGMF code. However, CGMF does not provide the function TKE(AH ) nor the RT (AH ) function for
energy sharing. For TKE, we thus considered three different options: (i) a copy of the TKE(AH ) CGMF

4 GEF
4.1 Introduction to GEF
GEF (GEneral description of Fission observables) is a semi-empirical model designed to provide a com-
prehensive description of the fission process [3]. It uses phenomenological theoretical concepts rather than
microscopic calculations, making it computationally efficient and well suited for Monte Carlo simulations.
GEF uses about 50 parameters that have been adjusted to fit a large number of fission systems. Several
parameters and their related uncertainties – position and width of fission channels, shell effects and fragment
deformation, etc. – are described in [3]. Once model parameters are set, the GEF code is able to predict the
fission quantities of other systems with an accuracy comparable with the uncertainties of the experimental
data used for the parameter fit, if these systems are not too far from the nuclei that were used for determining
the model parameters. GEF code determines the yields of different fission products through Monte-Carlo
sampling.

5 FREYA
FETA is a fission event generator that computes fission observables by deexciting fission fragments within
the Weisskopf-Ewing theory. At the time of this report, the FREYA calculations were run with version
2.2 of the GEF data, which produces a non-physical tend of the TKE near symmetric fission. As a result,
FREYA results were not used in the global assessment. They are reported here both for completeness and
to highlight the dependency of prompt fission observables on model inputs.

5.1 Introduction to FREYA
FREYA employs five physics-based parameters, e0 , cS , x, c, and dTKE [4].
 e0 The scission temperature, Esc = aTsc , is determined by the level density parameter a, which is based
 on an asymptotic value A/e0 modified by the Ignatyuk factor depending on the energy and shell
 corrections.
 cS The total excitation energy is divided between rotational energy and intrinsic statistical excitation
 energy. The rotational energy includes the overall rigid rotation of the system prior to scission, giving
 the average angular momentum, and fluctuations around this average value, attributed to the wriggling
 and bending modes. The degree of these fluctuations is given by TS = cS Tsc . Because cS is related to
 fluctuations in the angular momentum of the fragment, it directly affects photon emission.
 x The intrinsic excitation energy of the fragments is divided between the two fragments, first based on
 the level density parameters and then an additional excitation energy is given to the light fragment
 because, at low excitation energies, it tends to emit more neutrons on average than the heavy fragment.
 The light fragment excitation energy is multiplied by a factor x, which is assumed to be unity or above.
 This parameter primarily influences the neutron multiplicity as a function of fragment mass, ν(A).

 10

 c Thermal fluctuations in the statistical excitation energy are also included by sampling an energy
 fluctuation, δEn from a normal distribution with variance σi2 = 2c2 E i Ti . The parameter c controls
 the truncation of the normal distribution at the maximum available excitation. Energy conservation
 is maintained by making a compensating opposite fluctuation in the total kinetic energy, thus directly
 affects the neutron multiplicity distribution and its moments.
dTKE This parameter adjusts the TKE to better agree with the average neutron multiplicity.
 The five FREYA parameters, e0 , cS , x, c, and dTKE, were originally fixed for spontaneous fission based
 on simulated annealing [11]. Those results were revisited and the parameters for thermal-neutron induced
 fission were also optimized using a genetic algorithm in Ref. [12]. The optimized parameters for 239 Pu(nth. ,f)
 and 241 Pu(nth. ,f) are given in Table 6. These results produced significant improvements over the “by eye”
 parameter values used initially in FREYA.

scale on the y axis with the outgoing neutron energy, on the x axis, on a log scale. The density of the
points changes with outgoing neutron energy, with a higher density for En < 1 MeV. This is because of the
point-like density of the ENDF distribution. The calculated points have larger uncertainties at low energy,
even though they are close to the peak of the distribution, because the bins are smaller.
 The uncertainties are large for En < 1 MeV and above 7 MeV. The large uncertainties at low neutron
energy reflect the fact that the FREYA energy bins are very narrow, to match the ENDF/B-VIII.0 bins,
resulting in low statistics for these energies. While bin size is not an issue at higher neutron energies, the
statistics drop off rapidly in the tail of the distributions, leading to large calculated to experimental, C/E,
ratios in this region in particular.

5.3 Photon Observables in 242 Pu(nth. ,f )
The effect of changing the input data can be expected to be weaker for photon observables. Indeed, the
primary difference in the neutron observables arises from differences in the input TKE(AH ) which affects the
total available excitation energy at a given AH . This excitation energy is split between rotational (related
to the cS parameter) and statistical/intrinsic excitation energy (related to x). In FREYA, neutron emission
is first, followed by photon emission once the remaining excitation energy is below the neutron separation
energy, meaning that no more neutrons can be emitted. This is clearly reflected in the photon multiplicity
as a function of fragment mass in Fig. 18. The results with different yields for 239 Pu and 241 Pu, on the
left and center parts of the figure, are effectively identical. The only difference is apparent in the result for
242
 Pu, on the right-hand side of the figure. This differences arises only because of the different values of the
cS parameter in the two optimized parameter sets. This parameter is greater than unity for 241 Pu and less
than unity for 239 Pu.
 Because the cS parameter influences the total photon multiplicity from fission, it also influences the shape
of the photon multiplicity distribution, P (Nγ ), shown in Fig. 19. The peaks of P (νγ ) for 239,241 Pu agree
relatively well with those of the distributions measured by Chyzh et al. [17]. However, the shapes of the
calculated distributions are narrower than the measurement. It is unclear how important this discrepancy
is for two reasons. First, there is no FREYA parameter for photon emission that has the same effect
on this distribution that the parameter c has on the neutron multiplicity distributions shown in Fig. 16.
(The thermal fluctuations governed by the c parameter influence the width of P (ν).) Second, there are
