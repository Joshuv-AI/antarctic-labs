Tuning Derivatives for Causal Fairness in Machine Learning
Filip Edström, Guilherme W. F. Barros, Tetiana Gorbach, Xavier de Luna

Abstract
Artificial-intelligence systems are becoming ubiquitous in society, yet their predictions typically inherit biases with respect to protected attributes such as race, gender, or age. Classical fairness notions, most notably Statistical Parity (SP), demand that predictions be independent of the protected attributes, but are overly restrictive when these attributes influence mediating variables that are considered business necessities. Recent causal formulations relax SP by distinguishing allowed from not-allowed causal paths and by complementing SP with Predictive Parity (PP), requiring the predictor to replicate the legitimate influence of business-necessities. Existing path-based definitions are mainly practical when applied to categorical attributes. This paper introduces a new framework for fairness in structural causal models that is tailored to continuous protected attributes. We formalize SP and PP through path-specific partial derivatives, establish conditions under which these criteria coincide with prior causal definitions, and characterize when a fair predictor, one that satisfies SP along not-allowed paths while achieving PP along allowed paths, exists. Building on this theory, we propose a fair tuning algorithm that either constructs such a predictor or, when not possible, allows for a trade-off between SP and PP. We present experiments on simulated and real data to evaluate our proposal, compare it with previously proposed methods, and show that it performs better when PP is considered.

Definition 1

(Structural Causal Model, Def. 7.1.1 in
15
)
.

A Structural Causal Model (SCM) is a triple

\mathcal{M}=\langle\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V},\mathbf{F}\rangle

where:

i.

\mathbf{U}
is a set of background variables, that are determined by factors outside the model;

ii.

\mathbf{V}
is a set
\{V_{1},V_{2},...,V_{n}\}
of variables, called endogenous, that are determined by variables in the model—that is, variables in
\mathbf{U}\cup\mathbf{V}
; and

iii.

\mathbf{F}
is a set of functions (mechanisms)
\{f_{1},f_{2},...,f_{n}\}
such that each
f_{i}
is a mapping from (the respective domains of)
U_{i}\cup\mathbf{PA}_{i}
to
V_{i}
, where
U_{i}\subseteq\mathbf{U}
and
\mathbf{PA}_{i}\subseteq\mathbf{V}\setminus V_{i}
, called parents of
V_{i}
, and the entire set
\mathbf{F}
forms a mapping from
\mathbf{U}
to
\mathbf{V}
. In other words, each
f_{i}
in

v_{i}=f_{i}(\mathbf{pa}_{i},u_{i}),\quad i=1,...,n

assigns a value to
V_{i}
that depends on (the values of) a subset of variables in
\mathbf{V}\cup\mathbf{U}
, and the entire set
\mathbf{F}
has a unique solution
\mathbf{V}(\mathbf{u})
.

We consider probabilistic SCMs, with a probability distribution assigned to
\mathbf{U}
. An SCM
\mathcal{M}
can be attributed with a causal diagram,
\mathcal{G}
, that describes the relationships between the variables in
\mathcal{M}
such that, if
V_{i}\in\mathbf{PA}_{j}
, then the arrow
V_{i}\to V_{j}
is in
\mathcal{G}
.
V_{i}\to V_{j}
is an example of a directed path of length
|\pi|=1
. Formally, a directed path
\pi
is an ordered sequence of variables,
\pi=(V_{1},...,V_{|\pi|})
, where every variable is a parent of the next variable in the sequence. We say that
V\in\pi
if
V
appears on the path
\pi
, and if there is a directed path from
V_{i}
to
V_{k}
then
V_{i}
is called an ancestor of
V_{k}
, and conversely
V_{k}
is called a descendant of
V_{i}
. When considering multiple paths, we use
\Pi
to denote a set of paths. Additionally, if the background variables related to two endogenous variables
V_{i},V_{j}
are correlated, that is,
U_{i}
and
U_{j}
are not independent, this may be illustrated with a dashed, double-headed arrow,
V_{i}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{$\dashleftarrow$}}\mkern-18.0mu\vbox{\hbox{$\dashrightarrow$}}}V_{j}
.

SCMs allow us to model interventions, where an intervention on a variable
V_{i}
replaces the mechanism
f_{i}
according to the intervention
(
15
)
. For example, the intervention of assigning the value
v_{i}
to the variable
V_{i}
replaces
V_{i}=f_{i}(\mathbf{PA}_{i},U_{i})
with
V_{i}=v_{i}
. We use the potential outcome notation
(
19
)
,
V_{j}(v_{i})
, to describe the counterpart of
V_{j}
under the intervention
V_{i}=v_{i}
.

Using the potential outcome notation and paths, we can now define a path-specific effect of a change in the root node of a path,
V_{1}
, from the value
v_{1}
to the value
v_{1}^{*}
on the target of the path,
V_{|\pi|}
. The path-specific effect is then the difference in
V_{|\pi|}
when the change in
V_{1}
is allowed to propagate only over the path
\pi
. This is formalized using the potential outcome notation and nested counterfactuals in
20
. For instance, for an SCM with diagram
X\to Y,X\to W\to Y
we can write
Y(W(x),X)
to represent the intervention of setting
X=x
only along the path
\pi:X\to W\to Y
, not affecting the value of
X
along
X\to Y
. For convenience, we use the notation
Y(\pi(x)):=Y(W(x),X)
. A path-specific effect in a setting
\mathbf{Z}
is equivalent to a counterfactual contrast with respect to a specific path and a change from
v_{1}
to
v_{1}^{*}

(
16
)
, that is,

P(V_{|\pi|}(\pi(v_{1}^{*}))\mid\mathbf{Z})-P(V_{|\pi|}(\pi(v_{1}))\mid\mathbf{Z}).

A special case of path-specific effect is the direct effect, where the path only consists of one edge, for example,
V_{i}\to V_{j}
.

Causal Fairness Notions

17

Definition 2

(Causal SP, Def. 4 in

Let

\displaystyle\underbrace{P(\widehat{y}(x_{1},\Pi_{\text{IE}}(x_{0}))\mid x_{0})-P(\widehat{y}(x_{0},\Pi_{\text{IE}}(x_{0}))\mid x_{0})}_{\text{Ctf-DE}}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{P(\widehat{y}(x_{1},\Pi_{\text{IE}}(x_{0}))\mid x_{0})-P(\widehat{y}(x_{1},\Pi_{\text{IE}}(x_{1}))\mid x_{0})}_{\text{Ctf-IE}}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{P(\widehat{y}(x_{1})\mid x_{0})-P(\widehat{y}(x_{1})\mid x_{1})}_{\text{Ctf-SE}}=0.

17
also defines Causal PP with respect to a counterfactual contrast.

Definition 3

(Causal PP, Def. 5 in
17
)
.

Let
\widehat{Y}
be a predictor of the outcome

be a protected attribute. Then,

is said to satisfy causal predictive parity with respect to a counterfactual contrast along the path

to

\text{E}\left[Y(\pi(x_{1}))\mid\mathbf{Z}\right]-\text{E}\left[Y(\pi(x_{0}))\mid\mathbf{Z}\right]=\text{E}\left[\widehat{Y}(\pi(x_{1}))\mid\mathbf{Z}\right]-\text{E}\left[\widehat{Y}(\pi(x_{0}))\mid\mathbf{Z}\right].

The fairness of a predictive algorithm can then be defined using these two definitions, as we discuss in Section

is continuous there is an infinite number of possible contrasts. In practice, one needs to consider the definitions above for a possibly large set of points

for each

2.2
Derivative Fairness Notions

To obtain a more practical solution for continuous features, we define SP and PP in terms of derivatives along paths. This is a natural extension to contrasts as they can be regarded as discrete derivatives, up to a division by an increment. We consider an SCM
\mathcal{M}_{\widehat{Y}}
that describes the prediction algorithm, that is, the causal structure of the algorithm that outputs predictions
\widehat{Y}
and the features used as its input. Using this SCM, we define SP in terms of derivatives by applying the chain rule of calculus along the structural equations of a path.

Definition 4

(Statistical Parity in Derivatives, SPD)
.

Let
\mathbf{V}
be a set of variables, where
X\in\mathbf{V}
is a protected attribute,
\widehat{Y}\in\mathbf{V}
is an outcome predictor. For a given SCM
\mathcal{M}_{\widehat{Y}}
=\{\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V},\mathbf{F}\}
, consider a directed path
\pi:V_{1}=X\to\dots\to\widehat{Y}
where all variables along the path have compact support and each mechanism
f_{k}
along the path is assumed to be differentiable, then
\widehat{Y}
satisfies statistical parity in derivatives along path
\pi
if

\prod_{k=2}^{|\pi|}\frac{\partial f_{V_{k}}(\mathbf{PA}_{k},u_{k})}{\partial v_{k-1}}=0\quad\forall x,\mathbf{u},

where
|\pi|
is the length of the path,
V_{|\pi|}=\widehat{Y}
,
f_{V_{k}}
is the mechanism assigning values to
V_{k}\in\mathbf{V}
.

If SPD holds,
\widehat{Y}
is constant in the path-specific effect along
\pi
and at least one arrow is missing along the considered path in the diagram describing
\mathcal{M}_{\widehat{Y}}
. Thus, SPD along a path

can be achieved for a prediction algorithm that generates predictions
\widehat{Y}
, by simply excluding the parent of
Y
along
\pi

, in the literature called “Fairness Through Unawareness” (FTU)
(
13

f_{\widehat{Y}}
with respect to its parent along
\pi
to be zero. Another option is to generate an ancestor of
\widehat{Y}
along
\pi
, such that it has zero derivative with respect to its parent along
\pi
.

In order to give a definition of PP along paths in terms of derivatives, we want to compare derivatives between the prediction algorithm and the Data-Generating Process (DGP). To formalize the comparison, we consider two SCMs simultaneously: one describing the DGP,
\mathcal{M}=\langle\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V},\mathbf{F}\rangle
, and one describing the prediction algorithm,
\mathcal{M}_{\widehat{Y}}=\langle\widetilde{\mathbf{U}},\widetilde{\mathbf{V}},\widetilde{\mathbf{F}}\rangle
, including the prediction
\widehat{Y}
and the features used to generate it. We assume that the variables in
\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}
correspond to the variables in
\mathbf{V}
, that is, for each variable
V_{k}
in
\mathbf{V}
there exists a
\widetilde{V}_{k}
in
\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}
. A variable

can be equivalent to the corresponding variable
V_{k}
,
\widetilde{V}_{k}\equiv V_{k}
, or generated as a part of a sequential prediction. If the variable is generated, the structural equations differ such that
f_{\widetilde{V}_{k}}\not=f_{V_{k}}
and possibly the parents differ as well,
\mathbf{PA}_{\widetilde{V}_{k}}\not\equiv\mathbf{PA}_{V_{k}}

, in the unfair process in Figure
1
,

in

in

and part of the sequential prediction

and

differ and so do their parents, with

in
\mathbf{PA}_{P}
but not in
\mathbf{PA}_{\widehat{P}}

, is generated as

in

and denote all variables in

Definition 5

(Predictive Parity in Derivatives, PPD)
.

Let
\mathbf{V}
be a set of variables, including outcome
Y
, and
\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}
be a set of partially generated variables corresponding to
\mathbf{V}
, including
\widehat{Y}
. For given SCMs
\mathcal{M}=\langle\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V},\mathbf{F}\rangle
and
\mathcal{M}_{\widehat{Y}}=\langle\widetilde{\mathbf{U}},\widetilde{\mathbf{V}},\widetilde{\mathbf{F}}\rangle
and a path
\pi:V_{1}\to\dots\to V_{k}\to\dots\to Y
, we have that
\widehat{Y}
satisfies path-specific predictive parity in derivatives along
\pi

\pi

\mathcal{M}_{\widehat{Y}}

\frac{\partial}{\partial\widetilde{v}_{k-1}}\text{E}\left[\widetilde{V}_{k}\mid\widetilde{\mathbf{PA}}_{V_{k}}=\mathbf{p}\right]=\frac{\partial}{\partial v_{k-1}}\text{E}\left[V_{k}\mid\mathbf{PA}_{V_{k}}=\mathbf{p}\right]\quad\forall k\in\{2,\dots,|\pi|\}\text{ and }\forall\mathbf{p},

where
\widetilde{\mathbf{PA}}_{V_{k}}
are the variables in
\mathcal{M}_{\widehat{Y}}
that correspond to
\mathbf{PA}_{V_{k}}
in
\mathcal{M}
and
\text{E}[\widetilde{V}_{k}\mid\widetilde{\mathbf{PA}}_{V_{k}}=\mathbf{p}]

Remark 1
.

If
\widetilde{V}_{k}
is generated in a deterministic way, that is,
\widetilde{U}_{k}=\emptyset
,
and
\mathbf{PA}_{\widetilde{V}_{k}}\subseteq\widetilde{\mathbf{PA}}_{V_{k}}

then

\text{E}[\widetilde{V}_{k}\mid\widetilde{\mathbf{PA}}_{V_{k}}=\mathbf{p}]=f_{\widetilde{V}_{k}}(\mathbf{p})

Moreover, if only a part of the path is modeled, then only the modeled part needs to be considered. In particular, if only direct effects on Y are modeled, that is, only
\widehat{Y}
is generated and hence
\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}\setminus\widehat{Y}\equiv\mathbf{V}\setminus Y
,
then
\widetilde{\mathbf{PA}}_{Y}\equiv\mathbf{PA}_{Y}
and predictive parity reduces to

\frac{\partial}{\partial v_{|\pi|-1}}f_{\widehat{Y}}(\mathbf{p})=\frac{\partial}{\partial v_{|\pi|-1}}\text{E}\left[Y\mid\mathbf{PA}_{Y}=\mathbf{p}\right].

U_{i}\mbox{$\perp\!\!\!\!\perp$}U_{j}\ \forall i,j\in\mathbf{U}
, we have
Y(\pi^{*}(w))\mbox{$\perp\!\!\!\!\perp$}W\mid\mathbf{PA}_{Y}\setminus W
, and

\text{E}\left[Y\mid\mathbf{PA}_{Y}=\mathbf{p}\right]=\text{E}\left[Y\mid\mathbf{PA}_{Y}\setminus w=\mathbf{p}\setminus w,W=w\right]=\text{E}\left[Y(\pi^{*}(w))\mid\mathbf{PA}_{Y}=\mathbf{p}\right].

Further, since
\widehat{Y}
is deterministically generated,
\widetilde{U}_{\widehat{Y}}=\emptyset
, and since
\widehat{Y}
is the only generated variable in
\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}
,
\mathbf{U}\setminus U_{Y}=\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}
, it follows that the same applies to
\widehat{Y}
. Therefore,

\text{E}\left[\widehat{Y}(\pi^{*}(w))\mid\mathbf{PA}_{Y}=\mathbf{p}\right]+c(\mathbf{p}\setminus w)=\text{E}\left[Y(\pi^{*}(w))\mid\mathbf{PA}_{Y}=\mathbf{p}\right],

and for a contrast in
W
along
\pi^{*}
we have

\begin{split}&\text{E}\left[\widehat{Y}(\pi^{*}(w_{1}))\mid\mathbf{PA}_{Y}=\mathbf{p}\right]-\text{E}\left[\widehat{Y}(\pi^{*}(w_{0}))\mid\mathbf{PA}_{Y}=\mathbf{p}\right]=\\
&\text{E}\left[Y(\pi^{*}(w_{1}))\mid\mathbf{PA}_{Y}=\mathbf{p}\right]-\text{E}\left[Y(\pi^{*}(w_{0}))\mid\mathbf{PA}_{Y}=\mathbf{p}\right].\end{split}

This corresponds to Definition
3
when
\mathbf{Z}=\mathbf{PA}_{Y}
.
∎

The assumption
U_{i}\mbox{$\perp\!\!\!\!\perp$}U_{j}\ \forall i,j\in\mathbf{U}
is an assumption of no unmeasured confounding and ensures, for example, that the direct effect of
W
on
Y
is identified and can thus be estimated using the observed data
(
2
)
. This assumption is not necessary for a predictor to satisfy our definition of PPD.

In this section, we have defined SP and PP in terms of derivatives tailored to continuous features. However, when features are discrete, we suggest relying on contrasts, as in
17
instead of derivatives.

2.3
Fair Predictor

With the concepts of SPD and PPD defined above, we can describe a fair predictor in terms of not-allowed and allowed paths in an SCM.

Definition 6

(Fair predictor)
.

Let
\Pi_{N}
and
\Pi_{A}
be sets of not-allowed and allowed paths ending in
Y
respectively. Then, a prediction algorithm
\widehat{Y}
is called a fair predictor with respect to
Y
if

i.

\widehat{Y}
satisfies SPD with respect to all paths in
\Pi_{\text{N}}
, and

ii.

\widehat{Y}
satisfies PPD with respect to all paths in
\Pi_{\text{A}}
.

Note that a corresponding definition of a fair predictor in terms of Definition
2
(for Ctf-DE, Ctf-IE) and Definition
3
is obtained by
\widehat{Y}
satisfying Causal SP and Causal PP in all contrasts along
\Pi_{\text{N}}
and
\Pi_{\text{A}}
, respectively.

The existence of a fair predictor as defined in Definition
6
depends on the DGP, as the following result shows for the case where a not-allowed path represents a direct effect.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\mathcal{M}=\langle\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V},\mathbf{F}\rangle
be an SCM,
\pi_{\text{N}}:X\to Y
be a not-allowed path, and
\pi_{\text{A}}:W\to Y
be an allowed path, where
X,W,Y\in\mathbf{V}
. Then, there exists a predictor
\widehat{Y}
that satisfies SPD with respect to
\pi_{\text{N}}
and PPD with respect to
\pi_{\text{A}}
iff

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial w\partial x}\text{E}\left[Y\mid\mathbf{PA}_{Y}=\mathbf{p}\right]=0.

(1)

Proof.

Assume that
\widehat{Y}
is a deterministic predictor with
\mathbf{PA}_{\widehat{Y}}\subseteq\widetilde{\mathbf{PA}}_{Y}
, as in Remark
1
,
and that
\widehat{Y}
satisfies SPD and PPD with respect to
\pi_{\text{N}}:X\to Y
and
\pi_{\text{A}}:W\to Y
, respectively. Then, from SPD we have that
\partial f_{\widehat{Y}}/\partial x=0
, and therefore
\partial^{2}f_{\widehat{Y}}/\partial x\partial w=0
. Further, from PPD we have that

\frac{\partial}{\partial w}f_{\widehat{Y}}(\mathbf{p})=\frac{\partial}{\partial w}\text{E}\left[Y\mid\mathbf{PA}_{Y}=\mathbf{p}\right],

and hence we must have

0=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial w\partial x}f_{\widehat{Y}}(\mathbf{p})=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial w\partial x}\text{E}\left[Y\mid\mathbf{PA}_{Y}=\mathbf{p}\right],

where the first equality follows from SPD, while the second follows from PPD.

Conversely, if

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial w\partial x}\text{E}\left[Y\mid\mathbf{PA}_{Y}=\mathbf{p}\right]=0

we can write

\text{E}\left[Y\mid\mathbf{PA}_{Y}=\mathbf{p}\right]=g(w,\mathbf{z})+h(x,\mathbf{z}).

where
\mathbf{Z}=\mathbf{PA}_{Y}\setminus\{X,W\}
.
Then, we can choose the predictor
\widehat{Y}=f_{\widehat{Y}}(w,\mathbf{z})=g(w,\mathbf{z})+h(c,\mathbf{z})
, which satisfies SPD along
\pi_{\text{N}}
since

\frac{\partial}{\partial x}f_{\widehat{Y}}(w,\mathbf{z})=0

and PPD along
\pi_{\text{A}}
since

\frac{\partial}{\partial w}f_{\widehat{Y}}(w,\mathbf{z})=\frac{\partial}{\partial w}g(w,\mathbf{z})=\frac{\partial}{\partial w}\text{E}\left[Y\mid\mathbf{PA}_{Y}=\mathbf{p}\right].

∎

This result tells us that SPD and PPD are not always compatible, and that Equation (
1
) must hold for a fair predictor to exist. In Appendix
A
, we give an outline of a proof of a more general result, where indirect not-allowed paths are considered. However, in the sequel we focus on predictors modeling only direct effects for which Theorem
1
is relevant. In practice, Equation (
1
) is a strong assumption, and if it does not hold, a compromise between SPD and PPD needs to be made.

3
Method for Fair Prediction

We now propose a method to achieve a fair predictor
\widehat{Y}
, if it exists according to Theorem
1
. We focus here on deterministic predictors modeling direct effects on
Y
, and on cases where the not-allowed paths
\Pi_{\text{N}}
are of length one, that is, represent direct effects. We also assume that
\widetilde{\mathbf{PA}}_{\widehat{Y}}\subseteq\mathbf{PA}_{Y}
, such that Remark
1
applies. Note that, in the more general case where not-allowed paths of length greater than one are of interest, the proposed method can be applied sequentially along these paths to construct a fair predictor, as illustrated in Appendix
C
. If a fair predictor does not exist, the following method allows the user to tune predictors to navigate the trade-off between SPD and PPD. For that purpose, the following constrained optimization problem is posited:

\begin{split}\underset{\theta}{\operatorname{argmin}}\;\quad&\mathcal{L}(Y,\widehat{Y}_{\theta})\\
\text{subject to:}\quad&\nabla_{\Pi_{N}}f_{\widehat{Y}_{\theta}}=\mathbf{0},\\
&\nabla_{\Pi_{A}}f_{\widehat{Y}_{\theta}}(\mathbf{p})=\nabla_{\Pi_{A}}\text{E}[Y\mid\mathbf{PA}_{Y}=\mathbf{p}],\end{split}

(2)

where
\theta
are the parameters of
\widehat{Y}_{\theta}
,
\mathcal{L}(Y,\widehat{Y}_{\theta})
is a prediction loss,
\mathbf{0}
is a vector of zeros, and
\nabla_{\Pi}f_{\widehat{Y}_{\theta}}
is a vector of partial derivatives of
f_{\widehat{Y}_{\theta}}
with respect to the parents of
\widehat{Y}_{\theta}
along the paths in
\Pi
; see Appendix
B
for definition. The resulting predictor satisfies both SPD with respect to the not-allowed paths and PPD with respect to the allowed paths if Equation (
1
) holds in the DGP. Note that no assumptions about the structural equations in the DGP need to be made to implement Equation (
2
), and thus the method presented here only requires a causal diagram.

To implement the above constrained optimization, we propose a tuning scheme for neural networks that we call
fair tuning
. Fair tuning consists of first fitting an unconstrained predictor
\widehat{Y}_{\hat{\theta}_{\text{U}}}=f_{\widehat{Y}_{\hat{\theta}_{\text{U}}}}(\mathbf{PA}_{Y})
that minimizes a prediction loss targeting
\text{E}[Y\mid\mathbf{PA}_{Y}]
in Equation (
2
), for example, Mean Squared Error (MSE) for a regression problem. Then, a fair predictor is obtained using the fair tuning loss with respect to
\widehat{Y}_{\hat{\theta}_{\text{U}}}
:

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{\text{FT}}\left(\widehat{Y}_{\theta},\widehat{Y}_{\hat{\theta}_{\text{U}}};\lambda_{\text{SDP}},\lambda_{\text{PPD}}\right)=&\quad\underbrace{\mathcal{L}(\widehat{Y}_{\theta},\widehat{Y}_{\hat{\theta}_{\text{U}}})}_{\text{Pred. loss}}\\
&+\underbrace{\lambda_{\text{SDP}}||\nabla_{\Pi_{N}}f_{\widehat{Y}_{\theta}}||_{1}}_{\text{SPD loss}}\\
&+\underbrace{\lambda_{\text{PPD}}||\nabla_{\Pi_{A}}f_{\widehat{Y}_{\theta}}-\nabla_{\Pi_{A}}f_{\widehat{Y}_{\hat{\theta}_{\text{U}}}}||_{1}}_{\text{PPD loss}},\end{split}

(3)

where
||\cdot||_{1}
is the Manhattan (
L^{1}
) norm and
\lambda_{\text{SDP}}
and
\lambda_{\text{PPD}}
are parameters that give weight to SPD and PPD, respectively, allowing for prioritizing SPD or PPD when Equation (
1
) does not hold. The fair tuning algorithm is presented in its entirety in Algorithm
1
. In Appendix
D
, we show that the time complexity of backpropagation with Equation (
3
) is of the same order as regular backpropagation.

The causal diagram
\mathcal{G}
in Algorithm
1
should indicate fairness constraints through allowed and not-allowed paths. The not-allowed paths decide which direct effects are minimized, removing any possible effect transmitted along these paths. Additionally,
\mathcal{G}
can represent causal assumptions about the allowed paths by representing them using directed arrows, or, if no causal assumption is made, by double-dashed arrows.

\lambda_{\text{SDP}}=10

5.3
Comparison of Derivative and Contrast Fairness Notions

In the previous section, we applied the fair tuning loss (Equation
3
) as is to the binary features. An alternative would be to use contrast fairness notions Causal SP and PP (Definitions
2
and
3
) instead. We implement Definitions
2
and
3
as loss functions, analogous to SPD and PPD losses, to study the effect of tuning for derivative fairness on contrast fairness. For each observation
i
, we create the contrast feature vectors
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{0}
and
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{1}
, where the attribute to contrast is set to 0 in
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{0}
and 1 in
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{1}
, while the other attributes are unchanged. We then define the CSP and CPP losses as

\begin{split}&\mathcal{L}_{\text{CSP}}(\widehat{Z}_{\theta})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left|\hat{z}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{1})-\hat{z}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{0})\right|,\\
&\mathcal{L}_{\text{CPP}}(\widehat{Z}_{\theta},\widehat{Z}_{\hat{\theta}_{\text{U}}})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left|\left(\hat{z}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{1})-\hat{z}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{0})\right)-\left(\hat{z}_{\hat{\theta}_{\text{U}}}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{1})-\hat{z}_{\hat{\theta}_{\text{U}}}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{0})\right)\right|,\end{split}

where
\widehat{Z}_{\theta}
and
\widehat{Z}_{\hat{\theta}_{\text{U}}}
are the logits corresponding to predictors
\widehat{Y}_{\theta}
and
\widehat{Y}_{\hat{\theta}_{\text{U}}}
, respectively. We compute CSP loss for race and sex and CPP loss for charge degree, and compare feature-wise with SPD and PPD losses, respectively.

The results are shown in Figure
6
with the corresponding numbers in Table
2
in Appendix
J
.

Figure 6
:
Comparison between derivative fairness (SPD and PPD) and contrast fairness (CSP and CPP) losses for binary features of the COMPAS dataset. Mean values and 95% confidence bands are computed over 1000 bootstrap samples of the original dataset. Lower is better for all measures. PPD and CPP are computed w.r.t. the Unconstrained predictor.

We see that minimizing SPD loss successfully minimizes CSP loss for SPT 10 and FT 1, even if the assumption of compact support in SPD (Definition
4
) is violated for binary features. The results are less encouraging for FT 10, which exhibits a discrepancy between SPD loss and CSP loss for both race and sex. Similarly, FT 10 shows the biggest discrepancy between PPD and CPP loss for charge degree. However, it is encouraging that the order of the tuned predictors (SPT 10, FT 1, FT 10) remains the same between derivative and contrast fairness. Ultimately, if Causal SP and PP is desired, CSP and CPP losses should be implemented in the tuning.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we formulate the notions of statistical and predictive parity in terms of partial derivatives along paths, SPD and PPD, to better handle continuous variables, building on previous results in path-specific fairness based on contrasts
(
17
)
. We show the relation between our definitions and previous contrast definitions, and derive and study a compatibility criterion for when SPD and PPD are possible to achieve simultaneously. We define a fair predictor in terms of SPD and PPD, and develop a fair tuning method to construct a fair predictor when the compatibility criterion is met. When the compatibility criterion is not fulfilled, fair tuning offers a compromise between SPD and PPD. We compare our method with previous methods that handle continuous attributes and show that our method creates a fair predictor when the criterion is satisfied and offers a flexible way to compromise between statistical and predictive parity when the criterion is not met, better adhering to the concepts of statistical and predictive parity. Finally, we compare derivative fairness notions with contrast fairness notions
(
17
)
when applied to binary protected attributes, and conclude that, in such cases, derivative fairness may result in contrast fairness in practice, but not generally.
