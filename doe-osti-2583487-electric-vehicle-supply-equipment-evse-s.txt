Executive Summary
This report presents an analysis of the requirements for charging station installation and electric
vehicle (EV) operation at the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers (USACE) Chena site, located in a
cold-weather climate in the Fairbanks North Star Borough, Alaska. The report includes findings
from a site visit, as well as a detailed EV charging site plan with cost estimates. The cost for
three 50-ampere pedestal chargers located on the edge of the existing parking lot is estimated at
$53,100, and the cost of three 80-A chargers is estimated at $89,400.

The USACE Chena site reaches extreme cold temperatures of −40°C (−40°F) and below in a
typical winter, often for days on end. Considerations for operating EVs and electrical vehicle
supply equipment (EVSE) (i.e., EV chargers) at this site can be applicable to other cold or
extremely cold locations. Interviews with EV users in cold climates and a literature review
indicated that EVs perform well but have significantly decreased range compared to ideal
operating temperatures (i.e. 21°C or 70°F). Some strategies such as prewarming the vehicle
while it is plugged in and using heated seats and heated steering wheel instead of cabin heat can
improve cold-weather performance.

Storing the EV in a heated garage would mean the battery and cabin are automatically preheated,
the battery would not age as rapidly as when the vehicle is stored outside, and problems with
charging the vehicle are less likely. The lowest-temperate-rated EVSE is rated to −40°C (−40°F)
and sometimes malfunctions in low temperatures. No EVSE is rated to the temperatures the
USACE Chena site experienced for more than a week in winter 2023–2024, −45°C (−50°F),
which are typical for the area. If reliability is a must, entities may want to consider a heated
garage to minimize potential problems with charging equipment. The additional cost of a heated
garage would make it difficult to financially justify transitioning to an EV.

There is a companion technical report titled Electric Vehicle and Charging Infrastructure
Assessment in Cold-Weather Climates: A Case Study of Fairbanks, Alaska that examines the data
on EV and EVSE cold-weather functionality in more detail (Esparza, Truffer Moudra, and
Hodge 2024).

1 Introduction
The National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL) was contracted by the U.S. Army Corps of
Engineers (USACE) to complete an assessment of the requirements and costs of installing
electric vehicle (EV) infrastructure at the USACE Chena site in North Pole, Alaska, as well as
best practices for the operations of EVs in the extreme cold temperatures of Alaska. NREL wrote
two technical reports as requested: this report on the USACE Chena site EV charging needs and
Electric Vehicle and Charging Infrastructure Assessment in Cold-Weather Climates: A Case
Study of Fairbanks, Alaska on the impacts of cold weather on EVs and charging infrastructure
(Esparza, Truffer Moudra, and Hodge 2024).

NREL staff visited the Chena Recreation Area in August 2024. Based on the findings from that
visit and information shared by USACE staff, NREL developed a project plan and cost estimate
to support electrifying the three existing fleet vehicles on the site. This report summarizes cost
estimates for three 50-A and three 80-A alternating current (AC) pedestal chargers. It also
assesses the challenges associated with USACE installing EVSE in North Pole, Alaska.

This report is divided into four parts: identifying the cold weather seen at the site, existing fleet
at the site, EVSE installation requirements, and evaluation of the costs of installing EVSE at the
USACE Chena site.

2 Location and Weather
This section summarizes weather implications for the USACE Chena site. This site is located on
the east side of North Pole, Alaska, which itself is approximately 20 miles east of Fairbanks,
Alaska. Official National Weather Service records for the area report the temperatures at
Fairbanks International Airport.

2.1 Chena Site Overview
In August 1967, there was catastrophic flooding of Fairbanks, Alaska, when the Chena River that
runs through it swelled from extensive rain (U.S. National Oceanic and Atmospheric
Administration, n.d.). As a result of the flood, USACE built and now operates Moose Creek Dam
(U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, n.d.) upstream of the populated lowlands, which prevents
similar flooding from occurring again. The personnel responsible for the dam and recreational
area around it are based out of the USACE Chena site, next to the dam. A map of Fairbanks,
North Pole, and the USACE Chena site is shown in Figure 1.

2.2 Fairbanks Area Cold Snaps in Winter 2023–2024
The interior of Alaska, including Fairbanks and North Pole, experiences cold winters, with
nearly all years reaching −40°C (−40°F) and historic record temperatures in the −50s and −60s
(°F) for the coldest months of the year.

from Fairbanks International Airport (Swingley, n.d.), the location of the official National
Weather Service record. The airport is on the left side of Figure 1 near Broadmoor, about 20
miles west of the USACE Chena site. In Figure 2, the blue band running through the center
shows normal high and low temperatures for each day of the year. The red dots above and below
show the historical record high and low for each day. The black vertical lines show the
temperature range for each day in the year ending in September 2024. All of these are specific to
Fairbanks.

Note that the “normal” high and low temperatures (blue band) for the coldest month of the year,
late December to late January, are at approximately -18°C (0°F) and −29°C (−20°F),
respectively. These are statistical average daily high and low temperatures. The actual
temperatures for each day in the September 2023–September 2024 year, shown as a vertical
black line, deviate from these statistical averages, sometimes significantly.

For the winter 2023–2024, there were two cold snaps when the temperature dipped below −29°C
(−20°F) for more than 4 days: a 2-week cold snap at the end of January into early February, and
almost a week at the end of February into early March. During the first cold snap, the

As federal agencies electrify their fleets, they need to consider the number of EVSE to charge all
the vehicles, based on how those vehicles are used. If the USACE Chena site changed their

3.2 Existing Electrical Infrastructure
At present, the USACE Chena site consists of the following electrical infrastructure and
capacities, based on documents shared by site personnel:

 • Pole with 7.2-kV single-phase power from the local utility.
 • Ground-mounted transformer near the base of the pole, 167 kVA.
 • Pole-mounted transformer, 50 kVA, disconnected, not currently used.
The buildings and infrastructure currently used are fed from service panels in the building, and
these in turn are fed from the ground-mounted transformer near the power pole. The ground-
mounted transformer is near capacity. Any potential EV infrastructure cannot be connected to it.
Additionally, further downstream, the electrical panels have only a few empty spaces left, which
the building operations would prefer to keep open for future building needs.

4.3 EVSE and EV Reporting
Federal agencies have specific reporting requirements related to energy consumption, whether
facility or EVSE. Best practice is to separately meter the EVSE. Federal agencies are also
required to report vehicle data to the Federal Automotive Statistical Tool (FAST) (“Best
Practices for Measuring and Reporting Electricity Use in Federal Fleet Electric Vehicles,” n.d.).
Metering the EVSE also helps with the FEMP Annual Energy Management Report and the
EVSE delivered kWh in the FAST Fueling Center report.

Tracking EVSE consumption can be achieved via either physical meter, telematic tracking, or
via networked EVSE. Because the building and EVSE energy consumption need to be tracked
separately, there are advantages to having the EVSE metered independently, on a separate tap
from the site’s transformer. This way one meter reads the building use, and one the EVSE use. If
there is only one tap from the transformer, the EVSE must be metered separately (either via a
physical meter or an app if the EVSE is networked), and then this usage must be subtracted from
the main meter readings to get building energy.

Tracking individual vehicle data can be achieved through one of the following:

 1. Telematics data, if the vehicle fleet has a telematics device installed

 2. Networked EVSE if individual vehicle charging sessions are tracked (e.g., using a RFID
 and/or through fueling card transactions).

 3. Using the vehicle on-board computer to track vehicle kWh charging data (if applicable)

 4. An estimation Excel calculator developed by NREL and the Federal Energy Management
 Program when electricity consumption data are not available (U.S. Department of
      Energy, n.d.-b).

4.4 Networked EVSE
Networked EVSE has specific advantages and disadvantages compared to non-networked EVSE.
Networked EVSE can simplify data reporting requirements. Dual-port EVSE units can be
configured to share power between multiple EVs. This can potentially decrease, via
programming, the maximum power draw and potentially avoid having to install a new
transformer. Not relevant at present for the USACE Chena site, networked EVSE can be
programmed to charge during off-peak hours or ramp down charging during peak hours to ensure
a full charge but minimum additional costs.

A report for the Department of Defense (Hodge et al. 2022) outlined the disadvantages:

 “Networked EVSE require ongoing sustainment, including paying the charging
 service provider an annual network fee. There are firmware and software
 updates that must occur as well. Because of these complexities, a study found
 that nonresidential networked EVSE in its sample size performed at 68% to 83%
 uptime, while simple EVSE performed above 99% uptime during the study
 period… The study authors noted that this was because nonresidential
 networked EVSE would not charge vehicles without user authentication through

 10

 a mobile application or RFID [radio frequency identification] card, and that
 could not take place if the network was disturbed. It is critical that a sustainment
 plan be included for networked EVSE and preferable that the units function for
 fleet GOVs [government-owned vehicles] without a requirement for
 authentication.”
Additional information about EVSE can be found on the GSA website (“EVSE Purchasing
Resources,” n.d.). Cost ranges of ongoing costs are not included in this estimate, and are
approximately as follows based on the blanket purchase agreement information:

 • Network costs $250–$1,250 per year per port.
 • Maintenance plans $200–$1,000 per year, with port coverage varying.
 • Telematics subscriptions are now included with fleet costs.

5 USACE Chena Site Assessment
Two cost estimates were done for outside mounted pedestal-style chargers: one for 50 ampere
(A) chargers and one for 80-A chargers.

5.1 Assumption of Cost Estimates
The primary costs associated with installation of EVSE include procurement of the charging
equipment, potential procurement or upgrade of electrical equipment and lines, construction
costs, and project costs. The U.S. General Services Administration (GSA) created an EVSE
blanket purchase agreement for federal government buyers that serves as the source for the

6 Requirements for Garage Parking
Cold-weather impacts to EVs and EVSE are addressed in the companion report to this
assessment, Electric Vehicle and Charging Infrastructure Assessment in Cold-Weather Climates:
A Case Study of Fairbanks, Alaska (Esparza, Truffer Moudra, and Hodge 2024). There are
several key findings in that report that impact USACE’s determination of whether to park
vehicles inside or outside.

First, no Level 2 EVSE on the GSA schedule or from the GSA vehicle models’ original
equipment manufacturer is rated to operate below −40°C (−40°F). Most charging equipment is
rated to operate down to −30°C (−22°F). As described in Section 2.1, the temperature in the
Fairbanks area dips below −40°C (−40°F), suggesting that the chargers may not function during
periods of extreme cold. These periods may be a week in duration or longer.

Second, EVs lose a considerable amount of efficiency in cold weather. (Esparza, Truffer
Moudra, and Hodge 2024) found a linear relationship between daily temperature and vehicle
efficiency for three Teslas between −25°C (−13°F) and 22°C (72°F) (R2 = 0.7551). Further, the
report found that the Teslas took some time to start charging in −37°C (−35°F) weather after
being left outside and unplugged for 1.5 days.

Third, EV drivers have reported successfully charging their vehicles when the temperature was
−40°C (−40°F). For example, a driver reported driving their Ford F150 Lightning 95 miles from
Fairbanks to Delta Junction and back in a single day and where the temperature at the start of the
trip was −40°C (−40°F) (“Bandit216” 2022). The driver reported that the vehicle and DC fast
charger that they used functioned as required but lost efficiency – charging was slower, and
energy required per mile driven was higher.

Fourth, storing EVs in a garage provides multiple benefits beyond just efficiency improvements.
It reduces snow accumulation, mitigates battery degradation by protecting against ambient
conditions, and improves ease of use through faster preconditioning.

For prospective EV owners in extremely cold climates, such as Fairbanks, this study supports the
conclusion that an enclosed, heated garage offers advantages, both improving the daily
experience of driving an EV and extending the longevity of the vehicle itself, with the tradeoff of
possibly higher total project costs with the addition of garage maintenance and heating costs.
With continued advancements in battery chemistry and thermal management, the barriers to EV
adoption in extreme climates will likely diminish further, making EVs increasingly viable as
sustainable transportation solutions, even in harsh winter conditions.

7 Summary of Findings
A summary of the findings is as follows:

Can EVs be used in interior Alaska?

 • Yes. EVs are currently being used in interior Alaska.
 • EV driving range is impacted under cold conditions, sometimes resulting in half or less of
 summertime range.
What are best practices for EV use?

 • EV under cold conditions may need to be preheated before operation. Suggest
 preconditioning the vehicle 15–30 minutes from planned departure.
 • Ideally, preheating is done while the vehicle is plugged in, so the energy used for heating
 the battery and preheating the cabin or components comes from the grid and does not
 reduce the vehicle range.
 • Alternately, the vehicles can be housed in a heated garage. This would allow immediate
 departure, as the battery would not need to be preheated.
How is charging under these conditions?

 • Level 2 charger is required to charge EVs in extreme cold temperatures. During extreme
 cold temperatures, most (if not all) of the energy from a Level 1 charger goes towards
 battery warming needs.
 • The Chena USACE site experienced eight periods of 9 or more continuous hours when
 the temperature was below −40°C (−40°F) in winter 2023–2024. These are typically
 overnight when the vehicle is charging.
 • The lowest-temperature-rated Level 2 EVSE units identified on the market are rated to
       −40°C (−40°F).
 • Chargers operating in cold temperatures below or near temperature rating for the units
 have been documented to stop working due to the cold (Feder 2023; Esterdahl, Tim 2022;
 Ciminillo, n.d.).
 • Components, particularly plastic components, become brittle in extremely cold
 temperatures. For example, cable sheathing is prone to stiffness unless rated for
 extremely cold temperatures. This may impact EVSEs.
 • Charging time for some vehicles appears highly dependent on temperature, and for others
 almost independent. It is not clear whether the vehicles whose charge times are
 independent have a better charging system, or whether the batteries will suffer faster
 degradation due to charging under very cold conditions.

How much does it cost to install Level 2 charging ports at the USACE Chena site?

 • Three 50-A Level 2 charging ports with the existing transformer are estimated to cost
 approximately $53,100.
 • Three 80-A Level 2 charging ports and a new transformer are estimated to cost
 approximately $89,400.

 18

There are several points for USACE’s evaluation of a heated garage for the vehicles:

 • Charging of fleet vehicles typically occurs at night.
 • This past year at the USACE Chena site, cold temperatures during the night were below
 the temperature limit of currently available EVSE for more than a week.
 • In a typical winter, the USACE Chena site will experience these extreme cold
 temperatures for multiple nights.
 • Some EVSE units do not charge below their temperature limit.
 • Potentially this could mean the EVs may not charge.
 • If it is crucial that vehicles be ready to go in the morning, USACE should consider a
 heated garage for the charging equipment (EVSE) and EVs. This would improve
 charging, decrease the chance of EVSE failing, improve the performance of the EVs, and
 decrease the degradation of the battery in the EV.
In summary, EVs can be used in extreme cold climates with a proper understanding of the cold-
weather impacts. However, the installation of a heated garage at the Chena site would make it
expensive for USACE to transition to EVs.
