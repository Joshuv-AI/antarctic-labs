Cumulative electroconvulsive therapy sessions and focal epilepsy: A nationwide cohort study in Japan
Authors: Masahiro Hata, Yuki Miyazaki, Hisaki Omori, Shun Takahashi, Manabu Ikeda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveElectroconvulsive therapy (ECT) is widely used for severe psychiatric disorders, yet concerns remain regarding possible epileptogenic effects associated with prolonged or repeated exposure. We examined the association between cumulative ECT sessions and focal epilepsy in a nationwide Japanese cohort.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study using a nationwide claims database including 3733 patients who received ECT between 2008 and 2025. For analyses of incident focal epilepsy, patients with a prior diagnosis of epilepsy before the first ECT session were excluded from the at‐risk population. New‐onset focal epilepsy was defined as a G40.2 diagnosis recorded after ECT initiation. Person‐time accrued from the first ECT session to epilepsy diagnosis or last follow‐up. INTRODUCTION

Electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) is an effective and safe treatment for severe psychiatric disorders, including treatment‐resistant depression and schizophrenia. Although its short‐term safety is supported by a low estimated mortality rate of 2.1 per 100 000 treatments, 1 its long‐term neurological effects, such as the potential risk of developing epilepsy, remain poorly understood. For example, a large population‐based study found no association between ECT and subsequent dementia risk, suggesting that some long‐term neurological outcomes may not be adversely affected by ECT. 2

However, the potential relationship between ECT and epileptogenesis remains controversial. Experimental studies in the 1960s yielded conflicting results: some reported that repeated electrical stimulation increased seizure thresholds and suppressed seizures, 3 , 4 whereas others suggested a proepileptogenic effect via a mechanism known as electrical kindling. 5 This hypothesis has since been widely used as a model for seizure development and antiepileptic drug testing. 6

Clinical studies have reported mixed findings regarding the potential association between ECT and epilepsy. Some studies have documented lower‐than‐expected rates of epilepsy among patients receiving ECT, 7 whereas others found no significant difference relative to the general population. 8 A population‐based cohort study from Denmark observed a modest increase in epilepsy risk among younger ECT recipients, though residual confounding could not be excluded. 9 That study also performed subgroup analyses stratified by session number (≤ 15 vs. > 15 sessions); however, it did not address prolonged treatment courses extending well beyond typical acute treatment ranges. Overall, most prior studies did not specifically examine whether the risk of epilepsy increases with the number of ECT sessions. In contrast, case reports have described the development of focal epilepsy, particularly involving the temporal lobe, in patients undergoing extended courses of ECT. 10 , 11 , 12 These observations raise the possibility of a dose–response relationship in which cumulative exposure to ECT contributes to epileptogenesis, consistent with the concept of electrical kindling demonstrated in animal models. 5 Such findings may provide biological plausibility for a link between prolonged ECT and the development of focal epilepsy.

Given these gaps in the literature, this study aimed to investigate the association between ECT exposure and subsequent diagnosis of focal epilepsy, specifically defined by ICD‐10 code G40.2, a subtype with greater clinical specificity and biological plausibility than broader epilepsy classifications. Using a large, nationwide hospital claims database in Japan, we addressed three key questions: (1) What is the incidence of new‐onset focal epilepsy among patients treated with ECT? (2) Are such diagnoses associated with meaningful clinical actions, such as discontinuation of ECT or prescription of antiseizure medications? and (3) Is there evidence of a dose–response pattern between the number of ECT sessions and the likelihood of diagnosis?

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and data source

This study was a retrospective cohort analysis using a deidentified, nationwide hospital claims database provided by Medical Data Vision Co., Ltd. (MDV; Tokyo, Japan). The MDV database contains inpatient and outpatient insurance claims from acute care hospitals participating in Japan's Diagnosis Procedure Combination (DPC) reimbursement system. We analyzed data from April 2008 through March 2025. Available data included patient demographics, ICD‐10‐coded diagnoses, medical procedures, and prescription records. ECT was identified using the procedural code I000.

Cohort definition and inclusion/exclusion criteria

Patients were eligible for inclusion if they received ECT (procedure code I000) during the study period. The overall cohort comprised all such patients, regardless of epilepsy history. For the primary analysis of new‐onset focal epilepsy, individuals with any prior diagnosis of epilepsy (ICD‐10 codes G40) recorded before their first ECT session were excluded.

To contextualize the coverage of the MDV dataset, we compared the number of ECT sessions in the MDV database with those recorded in the National Database of Health Insurance Claims and Specific Health Checkups of Japan (NDB). The NDB is a nationwide administrative claims database maintained by the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare that captures approximately 98% of all health insurance claims in Japan. 13 However, the NDB does not include unique patient identifiers, limiting its utility for longitudinal analyses. 13

Exposure and outcome definitions

The exposure of interest was the cumulative number of ECT sessions per patient. This variable was analyzed both as a continuous measure (for regression modeling) and as categorical intervals in 10‐session increments (for descriptive stratification).

The primary outcome was a new diagnosis of localization‐related (focal) epilepsy (ICD‐10 code G40.2) recorded after the first ECT session. Time from the first ECT session to the G40.2 diagnosis was calculated to assess temporal patterns.

Covariates

Demographic covariates included age and sex. Psychiatric diagnoses were categorized using ICD‐10 codes as schizophrenia spectrum disorders (F2), mood disorders (F3), or both. These variables were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and included in the multivariable regression model. To evaluate potential multicollinearity, variance inflation factors (VIFs) were calculated for each covariate. All VIFs were less than 2, indicating no meaningful multicollinearity among predictors.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic and clinical characteristics of the cohort. Incidence rates of focal epilepsy were calculated per 100 000 person‐years, with follow‐up defined from the date of the first ECT session to the earliest of either the diagnosis of focal epilepsy or the last available observation date for each patient.

A Kaplan–Meier‐style plot was generated for descriptive purposes, restricted to patients who eventually developed G40.2, to visualize the distribution of latency from the first ECT session to diagnosis. This analysis was intended as a descriptive depiction of case latency rather than an estimate of cohort‐level cumulative incidence.

A multivariable logistic regression model was used to evaluate the association between the number of ECT sessions (as a continuous variable) and the likelihood of receiving a G40.2 diagnosis, adjusting for age, sex, and psychiatric diagnoses (F2, F3). Model fit was assessed using the likelihood ratio test.

Because cumulative ECT exposure is inherently time‐dependent and may introduce immortal time bias when modeled as a fixed variable, we conducted an exploratory time‐varying Cox proportional hazards analysis. In this model, cumulative ECT sessions were treated as a time‐dependent exposure, and patients contributed person‐time to the < 50‐session group until reaching 50 sessions and to the ≥ 50‐session group thereafter. The ≥ 50‐session threshold was selected as a pragmatic indicator of prolonged or maintenance ECT exposure. Descriptive inspection of crude diagnosis rates suggested higher frequencies beyond this level; therefore, this threshold‐based analysis was considered exploratory and performed as a sensitivity analysis to account for time‐dependent exposure.

We also investigated clinical decisions following G40.2 diagnosis, including whether ECT was discontinued and whether antiseizure medications were prescribed. These medications were identified using the Japanese drug classification code “N03A0,” which corresponds to the WHO ATC category N03A (antiepileptics).

All analyses were conducted using Python (version 3.8.11) with packages including pandas, statsmodels, and lifelines. A two‐sided p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Osaka University Hospital (approval number: 25119). In accordance with the Ethical Guidelines for Life Science and Medical Research Involving Human Subjects in Japan, the requirement for informed consent was waived because only deidentified data were used and no personally identifiable information was included.

Dataset representativeness

Between April 2014 and March 2024, the MDV database captured 47 966 ECT procedure claims, corresponding to individual ECT treatment sessions. During the same period, the NDB recorded 74 104 ECT procedure claims nationwide. This time frame was selected for comparison because publicly available NDB data are restricted to this period. Accordingly, the MDV database accounted for 64.7% of all ECT procedures conducted in Japan during this 10‐year interval.

Incidence of new‐onset focal epilepsy

Among the 3733 patients who received ECT, 96 (2.6%) were newly diagnosed with localization‐related (focal) epilepsy (ICD‐10 code G40.2) following treatment. This corresponded to an incidence rate of 3748 per 100 000 person‐years.

Of these 96 patients, 72 (75.0%) had comorbid diagnoses of both schizophrenia spectrum disorders (F2) and mood disorders (F3), 20 (20.8%) had F2‐only diagnoses, and 4 (4.2%) had F3‐only diagnoses. No patients diagnosed with focal epilepsy lacked both F2 and F3 diagnoses.

Treatment decisions following G40.2 diagnosis

Among the 96 patients diagnosed with focal epilepsy (G40.2), ECT was discontinued in 68 cases (70.8%). Of these, 67 patients (98.5%) were prescribed antiseizure medications. Among the remaining 28 patients (29.2%) who continued ECT after the diagnosis, 27 (96.4%) also received antiseizure medications.

Logistic regression analysis

In the unadjusted logistic regression model, the number of ECT sessions was significantly associated with the likelihood of receiving a G40.2 diagnosis (regression coefficient, 0.0101; p = 0.0032), corresponding to an estimated odds ratio (OR) of approximately 1.01 per additional session (Figure 1). The overall model was statistically significant based on the likelihood ratio test (p = 0.0111). This association remained significant in the adjusted model that included age, sex, and psychiatric diagnoses (F2 and F3) as covariates (adjusted regression coefficient, 0.0099; p = 0.0043).

In an exploratory time‐varying Cox model accounting for time‐dependent exposure and adjusted for age and sex, patients who reached 50 or more cumulative ECT sessions exhibited a higher hazard of focal epilepsy compared with periods before reaching this threshold (hazard ratio, 2.26; 95% CI, 1.02–4.98; p = 0.044). However, the number of events beyond this threshold was limited, warranting cautious interpretation.

Time to diagnosis

Among the 96 patients who were eventually diagnosed with focal epilepsy (ICD‐10 code G40.2), we generated a case‐only Kaplan–Meier‐style plot to descriptively illustrate the distribution of latency from the initiation of ECT to diagnosis. This visualization demonstrated a wide range of time intervals: while some cases were diagnosed within a few weeks, others were not diagnosed until more than 4000 days after the initial treatment session (Figure 2). These findings indicate a long‐tailed distribution and highlight the presence of very delayed cases. Among patients who developed focal epilepsy, the median interval between the last ECT session preceding diagnosis and the first G40.2 diagnosis was 110 days (interquartile range [IQR], 30–465 days; range, 0–3589 days), indicating substantial variability in the temporal relationship between recent ECT exposure and diagnosis. Importantly, this figure should be interpreted as a descriptive depiction of case latency rather than an estimate of cohort‐level survival probability.

DISCUSSION

In this large, nationwide cohort study, we examined the association between ECT and the subsequent diagnosis of focal epilepsy. Among 3733 patients without a prior history of epilepsy, 96 (2.6%) were newly diagnosed with focal epilepsy (ICD‐10 code G40.2) after receiving ECT. To our knowledge, this is one of the few studies to examine the association between cumulative ECT exposure and subsequent focal epilepsy diagnosis, with particular emphasis on treatment session number, using a nationwide claims database with longitudinal follow‐up.

The clinical relevance of the G40.2 diagnoses is suggested by subsequent treatment decisions: approximately 71% of patients discontinued ECT, and 98% received antiseizure medications. These patterns are consistent with diagnoses that were regarded as clinically meaningful rather than incidental coding.

No consistent age‐related trend was observed in the incidence of G40.2 diagnoses, which varied across age groups. In contrast, a dose–response‐like pattern was evident with respect to the number of ECT sessions: diagnosis rates generally exceeded 5% among patients who underwent 50 or more sessions, peaking at 9.09% in the 80–89 session group. Multivariable logistic regression confirmed a significant association between session count and the likelihood of receiving a G40.2 diagnosis, even after adjusting for age, sex, and psychiatric diagnoses. Neither schizophrenia spectrum (F2) nor mood (F3) disorders were independently associated with G40.2 diagnosis, likely due to their high prevalence among ECT recipients. These findings suggest a cumulative exposure pattern across increasing session counts. This epidemiological pattern may be conceptually compatible with cumulative neurophysiological effects of repeated electrical stimulation, as described in experimental kindling models. 4 Kindling refers to the progressive increase in seizure susceptibility following repeated subconvulsive electrical stimulation, a phenomenon originally demonstrated in animal models and widely used as a model of epileptogenesis in epilepsy research. 14 Although this model provides biological plausibility for a cumulative effect of repeated electrical stimulation, it remains unclear whether similar mechanisms operate in patients receiving ECT, and there is currently no direct evidence that ECT induces focal epilepsy through structural brain injury or the formation of a persistent epileptogenic focus. However, given the observational and claims‐based nature of the present study, these results should be interpreted as hypothesis‐generating rather than mechanistic evidence. Although the per‐session association was modest, the cumulative effect across extended treatment courses may become clinically relevant. This cumulative exposure pattern was further supported in an exploratory time‐varying Cox analysis designed to mitigate immortal time bias, although the number of events at very high exposure levels was limited. Furthermore, the ≥ 50‐session cutoff was not prespecified and was instead selected to represent prolonged exposure; therefore, this threshold‐based analysis should be interpreted as hypothesis‐generating rather than confirmatory.

The observed incidence of focal epilepsy in our ECT‐treated cohort exceeded population‐based estimates. Prior studies from Denmark and the United States have reported incidence rates of 44 to 100 per 100 000 person‐years in the general population, 15 , 16 and a recent Japanese study reported a rate of 72.1 per 100 000 person‐years. 17 Differences in diagnostic coding practices and the clinical characteristics of patients undergoing ECT may partly account for this discrepancy. Individuals receiving ECT represent a distinct population characterized by severe psychiatric morbidity and intensive medical follow‐up, factors that may contribute to surveillance bias and confounding related to illness severity. Epidemiological studies have suggested that some psychiatric disorders may be associated with an increased risk of epilepsy; for example, major depression has been reported as a risk factor for incident unprovoked seizures. 18 Therefore, psychiatric comorbidity should be considered when interpreting the observed association between ECT exposure and focal epilepsy. In addition, the absence of a matched psychiatric control group limits direct estimation of the excess risk attributable to ECT. Future studies incorporating matched psychiatric controls without ECT exposure will be necessary to clarify the independent contribution of ECT to epilepsy risk. Accordingly, the magnitude of the observed incidence should not be interpreted as a direct estimate of causal risk attributable to ECT. Rather, the primary objective of this study was to evaluate the association between cumulative ECT exposure and focal epilepsy within an exposed cohort. The internal cumulative exposure pattern observed across increasing session counts may be compatible with a cumulative effect, although future studies incorporating matched psychiatric controls will be necessary to clarify causal relationships.

Kaplan–Meier‐style analysis further showed marked heterogeneity in latency to diagnosis: some cases emerged within weeks of ECT initiation, while others appeared more than 4000 days later. This long‐tailed distribution suggests variability in diagnostic timing and indicates that delayed presentations may occur during extended follow‐up. These observations are broadly consistent with case reports describing temporal lobe epilepsy following prolonged ECT courses. 10 , 11 , 12 The interval between the last ECT session preceding diagnosis and the first G40.2 diagnosis varied substantially, suggesting that focal epilepsy diagnoses were not uniformly clustered immediately after the most recent ECT exposure. This finding further supports cautious interpretation of the temporal relationship between ECT exposure and subsequent diagnosis. While not establishing delayed epileptogenesis, this variability supports consideration of ongoing neurological monitoring in patients undergoing extended or maintenance ECT.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, because this was an observational study, causal relationships cannot be established. Although cumulative ECT sessions were examined in logistic regression analyses, session count is inherently time‐dependent; therefore, bias related to time‐dependent exposure cannot be fully excluded. Second, follow‐up duration was determined by available claims data and varied across patients, warranting cautious interpretation of incidence estimates. Third, administrative claims data lack detailed clinical information, including seizure semiology, electroencephalography findings, and neuroimaging characteristics, which could have strengthened diagnostic validation. Undiagnosed or undocumented epilepsy prior to ECT cannot be excluded, and diagnoses based on administrative ICD‐10 codes may be subject to misclassification. Furthermore, several established risk factors for epilepsy, including prior head trauma, central nervous system infections, febrile seizures, and exposure to seizure‐provoking medications, could not be comprehensively assessed. Therefore, residual confounding by unmeasured epilepsy risk factors cannot be excluded. In addition, detailed ECT treatment characteristics, including electrode placement, stimulation parameters, treatment frequency, distinctions between acute, continuation, and maintenance ECT, and whether repeated sessions represented a single prolonged treatment course or multiple acute treatment series, were unavailable in the database. Therefore, the cumulative number of ECT sessions should be interpreted as an overall measure of ECT exposure rather than a precise indicator of treatment intensity or phase. Accordingly, the observed associations should not be interpreted as causal. Nevertheless, the high frequency of ECT discontinuation and antiseizure medication prescriptions following diagnosis suggests that many cases were regarded as clinically significant.

From a clinical perspective, these findings may support consideration of neurological monitoring in patients receiving extended courses of ECT, particularly during prolonged or maintenance treatment. A prior case report documented the emergence of temporal EEG spikes after more than 80 ECT sessions in a patient without clinical seizures. 19 Although anecdotal, this observation suggests that subclinical epileptiform changes may occur during prolonged exposure. Further prospective studies incorporating systematic electrophysiological assessment would be required to clarify the potential role of EEG surveillance in this context.

CONCLUSIONS

In this nationwide cohort study using real‐world data, our findings suggest an association between cumulative ECT exposure and focal epilepsy, although causality cannot be established. A cumulative exposure pattern was observed across increasing session counts, and in some cases diagnoses occurred several years after treatment initiation. These findings may warrant consideration of long‐term neurological monitoring in patients undergoing extended ECT courses. Clinicians should remain attentive to the possibility of delayed epilepsy diagnoses, and further research is needed to clarify underlying mechanisms and inform individualized risk mitigation strategies.
