Preface
During the development of the original Hydropower Supply Chain Gap Analysis report, 1 the long
lead times associated with large power transformers emerged as a key concern for the
hydropower industry, prompting further examination. Procurement constraints for this critical
component stem from broader issues, including limited global manufacturing capacity and
competition for specialized components and upstream raw materials. These factors highlight the
need for a clearer understanding of the supply conditions affecting transformer availability and
their potential implications for hydropower project planning.

The Hydropower Supply Chain Gap Analysis report, published in August 2024, was developed
in response to Executive Order 14017, which directed federal agencies to evaluate the resilience
of critical U.S. supply chains. The report was prepared by the U.S. Department of Energy’s
Office of Energy Efficiency and Renewable Energy to assess domestic manufacturing capacity
for critical hydropower components and identify supply chain gaps.

The primary objective of the report was to provide a targeted evaluation of the domestic
hydropower supply chain, with an emphasis on identifying key constraints and informing
strategies to strengthen U.S. manufacturing capabilities. A specific focus was given to large
hydropower systems (>100 MW), where supply chain issues are most pronounced and have
significant implications for project development timelines and broader energy system objectives.

This supplemental report provides a more detailed analysis of those upstream materials and
manufactured product supply chains. By outlining the structural factors that contribute to
procurement delays, this report aims to offer information that supports more effective forecasting
and early decision-making. The intent is to help hydropower stakeholders anticipate challenges
and consider planning measures that may reduce the impact of large power transformer
procurement constraints.

Executive Summary
Large power transformers (LPTs) are indispensable to U.S. hydropower operations, serving as
generator step-up units that interconnect hydropower facilities to the high-voltage transmission
grid. Their reliability is central to hydropower rehabilitation and grid modernization efforts, yet
the LPT supply chain faces mounting stress from aging infrastructure, limited domestic
production, and long lead times. This report is a supplement to the 2024 Hydropower Supply
Chain Gap Analysis report (Putsche et al. 2024). It focuses specifically on the unique
vulnerabilities of LPTs and their secondary components in the context of hydropower
rehabilitation projects. The analysis herein includes insights gleaned from interviews with
hydropower original equipment manufacturers, vendors, developers, and asset owners regarding
their experiences with the LPT supply chain and its challenges. The Hydropower Supply Chain
Gap Analysis investigated supply chain issues to expand domestic manufacturing for
hydropower technologies emphasizing the need for a strong supply chain to ensure future clean
energy and grid reliability needs.

Several key issues were identified in the Hydropower Supply Chain Gap Analysis report, such as
hydropower component demand, which is irregular and difficult to forecast primarily because of
long asset lifetimes and limiting incentives for domestic investment. In addition, there is a
notable lack of domestic manufacturing for key components such as large turbines,
hydrogenerators, and electrical steel. In fact, some components have no domestic source. Other
issues include federal procurement practices based on up-front cost, strong foreign competition,
and the availability of skilled labor. These issues clearly indicate that without further efforts to
improve demand signals and supply chain conditions, the U.S. hydropower supply chain will be
limited in its ability to meet future energy needs.

The U.S. hydropower fleet is among the oldest in the world, with an average plant age of nearly
80 years. Many units now require modernization, including the replacement or refurbishment of
LPTs. However, procurement timelines for new transformers measured from purchase order to
receipt have lengthened from 12–18 months to 30–36 months (U.S. Government Accountability
Office 2023) for typical LPTs. For the extra-high-voltage transformer units, lead time can extend
up to 60 months (U.S. Department of Energy 2024) due to complex engineering requirements,
which creates significant risks for project scheduling and grid reliability. Costs have also risen
sharply, with LPT prices in 2025 roughly 50% to 70% higher than their 2019 baseline (Wood
Mackenzie 2025).

A primary bottleneck is upstream material supply. Grain-oriented electrical steel, which forms
the magnetic core of transformers, is produced domestically, but U.S. output is constrained in
both grade variety and total volume relative to demand. As a result, manufacturers rely on
imports from Japan and South Korea, particularly for high-grade grain-oriented electrical steel.
Similarly, copper conductors, on-load tap changers, bushings, and insulation materials are
claimed to face high levels of import dependence. Altogether, more than 80% of U.S. demand
for LPTs is currently met through imports (Bureau of Industry and Security 2021), exposing the
sector to trade restrictions, tariff disputes, and global supply chain disruptions.

 vi

Domestic manufacturing capacity has expanded in recent years, with facility upgrades
announced by Delta Star, Hitachi Energy, and Pennsylvania Transformer and new capacity
planned by Siemens Energy. Yet these expansions remain insufficient to meet rising demand
from hydropower, data centers, and renewable integration. Production of the largest extra-high-
voltage units remains concentrated globally, with only a handful of facilities worldwide capable
of manufacturing units above 400 MVA. Hydropower rehabilitation projects in particular face
additional challenges due to custom design requirements, remote locations, and regulatory needs
around domestic content. Regional cost disparities driven by transportation logistics, labor
markets, and import price volatility further exacerbate project risks.

Regional disparities in LPT pricing highlight additional barriers. Logistics can represent 3%–
20% of transformer cost, depending on site accessibility, while labor shortages and specialized
workforce retirements compound risks of project delays (U.S. Department of Energy 2024). Case
studies of recent projects demonstrate how transportation of custom-engineered 100–400-ton
transformers through mountain passes, waterways, and restricted bridges requires complex
multimodal logistics (Office of Electricity Delivery and Energy Reliability 2014).

Secondary components of pumped storage hydropower (PSH) facilities, such as turbines,
hydrogenerators, and governors, add further vulnerabilities. Only a few U.S. facilities can forge
large shafts or cast runners over 10 tons, and there are no domestic suppliers of hydrogenerators
larger than 20 MW. Electronic control systems for excitation and protection are heavily reliant
on Asian manufacturing. These gaps amplify project risks, especially as PSH deployment
accelerates to support grid reliability.

Recent policy measures could mitigate these supply chain challenges. The Inflation Reduction
Act established, and the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA) modified, a domestic content
bonus credit under the investment tax credit and production tax credit, which provides an
additional 10 percentage points of credit value for projects that meet domestic content
requirements. In May 2024, Internal Revenue Service Notice 2024-41 provided a safe harbor
table for hydropower and PSH projects to help them more easily qualify for the bonus credit. To
qualify, projects must ensure that all iron and steel used in applicable components are sourced
from the United States and that manufactured products meet escalating domestic cost thresholds,
beginning at 40% in 2024 and increasing to 55% by 2029.

Generator step-up transformers are specifically identified as qualifying manufactured products
under this framework. Rehabilitation projects may also qualify under the “80/20 retrofit rule,”
which allows facilities with at least 80% new investment to access the bonus, though any reused
components are treated as zero for domestic content purposes. For projects seeking to claim the
investment or production tax credits and which begin construction in 2026 or later, the OBBBA
tightened restrictions on procurement from foreign entities of concern, including most entities in
China, Russia, North Korea, and Iran, adding both new compliance obligations and opportunities
for U.S. suppliers. While these measures reduce uncertainty and strengthen incentives for
reshoring, gaps remain. Hydropower-specific manufactured components such as turbines,
generators, and governors still lack clearly defined cost percentages in the safe harbor
framework, leaving developers with partial ambiguity when seeking to qualify for the bonus.

 vii

In the interim, hydropower operators are taking adaptive measures, such as placing transformer
orders earlier, sharing spare units, and phasing modernization schedules. Yet these stopgaps
cannot fully offset the risks posed by systemic bottlenecks. Building new manufacturing
capacity, reestablishing domestic expertise in large transformer production, and strengthening
supply resilience will require sustained effort and coordination across government, utilities, and
private industry.

Ultimately, securing the LPT supply chain for hydropower rehabilitation is not merely a
technical or economic challenge; it is a matter of national security. Hydropower provides a
reliable and flexible source of generation that underpins grid stability. Ensuring timely access to
transformers and secondary components is essential to sustaining this role. Federal support,
industry partnerships, and proactive investment are critical to ensuring that the U.S. hydropower
sector can continue to modernize and expand in line with the nation’s energy security and grid
reliability goals.

1 Supply Chain for Large Power Transformers
1.1 Overview
Large power transformers (LPTs) are critical infrastructure assets of the U.S. electric grid and
hydropower fleet. They function primarily as generator step-up units and are typically rated at
100 MVA or greater (Office of Electricity Delivery and Energy Reliability 2014; Putsche et al.
2024) with high-side voltages 2 of 100 kV (Office of Electricity Delivery and Energy Reliability
2014). LPTs are often custom designed and engineered to incorporate magnetic cores, copper or
aluminum windings, insulating materials, and steel tanks, among other features. Their unit
weights commonly range between 100 and 400 tons (Office of Electricity Delivery and Energy
Reliability 2014), making them logistically challenging to transport and adding to their installed
cost.

Because multiple generating units may share a common transformer or a transformer bank, a
single failure can curtail significant portions of plant output at once. For instance, at Chief
Joseph Dam, failure of a single transformer bank has the potential to take numerous generating
units offline simultaneously, amplifying both reliability and economic impacts. At Bonneville
First Powerhouse, two units share a transformer bank, but their power transmission capability is
deliberately restricted to reduce outage risks. These use cases highlight the vulnerability of
shared configurations. Compounding this risk, replacement times for LPTs often exceed 18–36
months, making outages difficult to recover from and further emphasizing the importance of
spares, monitoring, and coordinated replacement planning (Hydroelectric Design Center 2012).

The U.S. LPT market is characterized by high demand, aging infrastructure, and limited
domestic production capacity. The assembly landscape involves both centralized factory
production and opportunities for on-site refurbishment in hydropower projects. Tank fabrication,
integration of active parts, filling with insulating fluids, and rigorous testing are all precision-
intensive steps requiring specialized infrastructure and highly skilled labor.

The manufacturing stages and upstream inputs illustrate the complexity of LPT production.
High-value raw material inputs such as grain-oriented electrical steel (GOES) and copper
conductors are predominantly imported. LPTs as finished products and fabricated
subcomponents like bushings and on-load tap changers (OLTCs) are also largely sourced from
overseas suppliers. Approximately 70% of recent LPT installations in the United States were
imported (U.S. International Trade Commission [USITC] 2024a), with long procurement cycles
driven by constrained global supply chains and concentrated manufacturing bases.

LPTs are among the most capital-intensive components in the electrical grid. The cost of a single
unit can be as high as $10 million (USITC 2024b), depending on its power rating, voltage class,
and other project specifications. The United States is heavily reliant on the import of high-grade
GOES and finished LPTs, with primary supply coming from Japan, South Korea, Germany,
Mexico, and Brazil. This dependence creates systemic risks when global supply disruptions,
geopolitical instability, or trade policy shifts arise. Additionally, these supply challenges make

1.2 Technology Trends
LPTs are generally defined as transformers rated above 100 MVA, which typically have high-
side voltages above 100 kV. The fundamental design elements of LPTs—magnetic cores, copper
or aluminum windings, insulating media, and robust steel tanks—have remained relatively
consistent over the past century, but significant advancements in materials, manufacturing
processes, cooling and monitoring systems, and efficiency have emerged over time. Within the
U.S. hydropower market, modernization efforts are a key driver of LPT demand. The U.S.
hydropower fleet has an average age of 79 years, and some plants have been operating for over
100 years. Many facilities are now upgrading or replacing transformers to extend the operational
life of the hydropower plant, address safety requirements, and improve environmental
performance.

In terms of physical characteristics, LPTs are massive, often weighing 100 to 400 tons (200,000–
800,000 lbs) each, depending on the rating, design, and auxiliary equipment (Office of
Electricity Delivery and Energy Reliability 2014). They are typically custom designed for
specific applications. Design drivers include power and voltage ratings along with functional
requirements such as generator step-up, industrial step-down, phase shift, or autotransformers.
Hydropower plants require a generator step-up transformer to match transmission system
voltages. The core-type LPT shown in Figure 1 is a typical configuration for generator step-up
transformers. A hydropower facility may use a three-phase transformer, or three single-phase
transformers. A three-phase transformer requires less material and costs less than an equivalent
bank of three single-phase transformers; however, single-phase transformers have smaller
dimensions and may be more suitable for sites with transportation constraints (U.S. Army Corps
of Engineers 2024).

The main components of transformers are the tank—which contains the core, windings, OLTC,
and oil—bushings, oil conservator, and cooling system. The core and windings are the active
electromagnetic components of the transformer; the OLTC adjusts the voltage ratio under
varying load conditions; bushings provide insulated pathways for secure high-voltage electrical
connections; and the oil-cooling system provides cooling and electrical insulation, maintaining
dielectric strength and reducing overheating. Digital monitoring systems are included in modern
LPTs to enhance durability and operational efficiency.

Copper and electrical steel account for more than 50% of the total equipment cost. Transformer
cores are primarily made from GOES (Chakraborty 2018). High magnetic permeability (Hi-B)
grade electrical steel reduces losses and allows for the use of less material compared with lower
grades of GOES. Continuously transposed conductors (CTCs) made from copper (or, less
frequently, aluminum) are used in windings. Advancements in CTC manufacturing have
improved voltage regulation, heat dissipation, and loss profiles.

Cooling technology has also evolved. Traditional oil natural, air natural cooling has been
supplemented or replaced in many applications by oil natural, air forced and oil forced, air forced
systems, allowing for higher load capacities without overheating (USITC 2024a). In
rehabilitation projects, upgrades to cooling systems are common, improving both performance
and lifespan (Putsche et al. 2024).

Insulating materials such as kraft paper, pressboard, or engineered polymers provide the
necessary thermal and long-term dielectric stability for the service life of the transformer.
Historically, cellulose-based paper was the dominant insulation medium, but newer designs
incorporate aramid fibers and thermally upgraded paper, extending transformer life and
improving thermal performance.

 3

Another notable trend is the shift from using traditional mineral oil to using synthetic or natural
ester fluids as the insulating and cooling medium. These fluids have higher flash points than
mineral oil, increasing fire safety. The biodegradability and reduced environmental risk of
synthetic and natural ester fluids are particularly important for hydropower plants located near
sensitive water bodies (Malde, Coker, and Tuck 2024). In 1979, the U.S. Environmental
Protection Agency Toxic Substances Control Act banned the use of polychlorinated biphenyls,
or PCBs, in cooling oil, making them more environmentally suitable as well.

1.3 Current State of the LPT Supply Chain
The current state of the LPT supply chain for the U.S. hydropower sector is defined by an aging
fleet of transformers operating beyond their average service life, limited domestic manufacturing
and assembly capacity, extended procurement lead times, and a heavy reliance on imports for
both finished units and critical raw material inputs. The total number of LPTs in the United
States was estimated to be between 4,900 and 6,799 in 2022. While this total is increasing with
the addition of more than 700 LPTs each year, approximately 170 LPTs reach their design
lifetime annually, emphasizing the growing need for replacement alongside new installations
(Nguyen et al. 2022).

In 2019, the U.S. Department of Commerce found that 82% of LPTs (617 units) were imported
for domestic usage, and only 18% were domestically produced (Bureau of Industry and Security
2021). As of 2023, U.S. manufacturers were meeting approximately 30% of domestic demand
for LPTs (USITC 2024a). U.S. demand and manufacturing of LPTs are shown in Figure 2.
Manufacturers have a limited number of production slots to make various transformer types,
including LPTs. The facilities listed in Table 1 can produce transformers rated at or above 100
MVA, although they may also produce smaller transformers. Although the share of domestic
production has increased, domestic manufacturing capability has not expanded to match the
increase in demand. Between 2011 and 2023, apparent consumption of LPTs in the United States
increased by 28% while manufacturing capacity increased by 11%. During this period, domestic
manufacturers were able to ramp up production from existing facilities, increasing capacity
utilization from 40% to 78% (USITC 2024a). Further increases in annual production would
likely require additional manufacturing capacity. Since 2023, domestic LPT producers Delta Star
and Hitachi Energy USA have expanded their manufacturing facilities (both in Virginia), and
Pennsylvania Transformer announced an expansion of its facility in North Carolina (Hitachi
Energy 2022; Delta Star Inc. 2025; North Carolina Office of the Governor 2025). Siemens
Energy announced that it is building a new production facility in Charlotte, North Carolina,
which is expected to begin operations in 2027 (Delaloye 2024). In addition, Hitachi Energy will
build a second facility in South Boston, Virginia, to manufacture LPTs beginning in 2028
(Hitachi Energy 2025).

Several specialized raw material inputs represent potential bottlenecks for LPT production:

Grain-oriented electrical steel: GOES is the single most important input for transformer cores,
yet U.S. supply is constrained both in grade variety and manufacturing capacity. Cleveland-
Cliffs is the only GOES manufacturer in the United States operating two production facilities.
The Cleveland-Cliffs facilities produce limited quantities of conventional Hi-B grade GOES,
which is preferred for LPT cores because of lower core losses and higher magnetic efficiency. As
a result, U.S. manufacturers frequently import premium-grade GOES; imported GOES is
typically processed by U.S. core fabrication companies where it is laminated before being
integrated into transformers by original equipment manufacturers.

Copper: CTCs for windings require high-quality oxygen-free copper (Dubey 2017). The United
States consumes close to 1.6 million metric tons of copper annually (U.S. Geological Survey
2025). The majority of U.S. copper mining occurs in Arizona, and scrap recovery provides close
to one-third the U.S. supply. Approximately 75% of mine production is refined domestically.
Superior Essex (headquartered in Georgia) and Rea Magnet Wire (based in Indiana) are the
primary U.S. producers of supply magnet wire and rectangular conductors for transformers in
North America (Guidehouse 2022). However, for CTCs, which are a critical component of LPT
windings, Sam Dong is the only domestic manufacturer. 3 This reliance on a single U.S. supplier
creates a supply chain bottleneck, prompting original equipment manufacturers to turn to
imported CTC.

OLTC: Reinhausen is the only U.S. OLTC producer. ABB/Hitachi and Reinhausen also operate
factories in Europe that supply compatible units. Lead times are significant—often 18 weeks or
more—creating potential schedule risks for transformer assembly. 4

Bushings: High-voltage bushings are another supply chain concern. Hitachi Energy
manufactures bushings domestically, but production is generally limited to the ≤235 kV class.
For higher ratings, utilities must rely on imports, which face longer lead times compared to
OLTCs. 5

Synthetic esters: Several companies produce and supply synthetic and natural esters used for
transformers in the United States (Table 2). Compared to traditional mineral oils, ester dielectric
fluids are more biodegradable and less flammable.

1.4 Manufacturing Stages and Upstream Inputs
The manufacturing of LPTs for hydropower facilities is a multistage process involving precise
engineering design and customization, specialized materials, and skilled labor. Certain stages
from manufacturing to installation, detailed in Table 3, depend on critical raw material inputs
that have their own supply chain vulnerabilities, making the process particularly sensitive to
disruptions. The most critical upstream input is GOES, which forms the laminated core of the
transformer. High-grade GOES production has been limited in in the United States for decades,
making the LPT industry heavily dependent on imported GOES.

1.4.1 Upstream to Midstream
The manufacturing process begins with extensive design work tailored to site-specific
requirements necessitating detailed modeling of electrical, thermal, and mechanical stresses
before fabrication can begin. Once designs are finalized, the first physical step is the preparation
of core and winding materials. Winding core assembly requires skilled labor and is one of the

 8

most time-consuming stages of transformer production compared to other steps such as
conservator assembly. Industry interviewees noted that newer assembly equipment is more
sophisticated, improving output and efficiency. GOES is processed into thin laminations that
form the magnetic core while copper or aluminum conductors are drawn and shaped into forms
such as foil, round wire, or CTC cables. The core is then cut, stacked, and annealed to minimize
energy losses, and conductors are wound into high- and low-voltage coils with layers of
insulation applied between turns. These precision steps require specialized machinery and skilled
technicians to maintain strict tolerances and ensure long service life.

1.4.2 Downstream to Delivery
Following fabrication, the transformer parts such as core, coils, insulation, and cooling structures
are assembled and mounted inside a heavy welded steel tank. Accessories such as radiators,
conservators, bushings, and OLTCs are installed, and the unit is dried and filled with mineral or
ester-based insulating fluid under vacuum. Modern LPTs are also outfitted with monitoring
systems that track temperature, pressure, and partial discharge, enabling real-time diagnostics
and predictive maintenance. Before leaving the factory, each unit undergoes rigorous testing,
including impulse, partial discharge, and temperature rise tests, as well as vibration checks for
hydropower applications. The final stage is transport and installation, which is a complex
undertaking due to the size and weight of these transformers. Specialized railcars, multi-axle
trailers, and detailed route planning are required to move them from factory to site. In some
hydropower rehabilitation projects, on-site or modular subcomponent assembly is being explored
to reduce transport risks and shorten schedules.

1.5 Assembly Landscape
The existing reserves of used or remanufactured units are decreasing, and overseas suppliers are
suffering their own demand challenges. As such, hydropower projects are facing delays and cost
overruns because of insufficient production capacity relative to demand. In the United States,
manufacturing capacity gaps for LPTs used in hydropower projects and other energy systems are
affecting grid modernization efforts. These enormous, highly customized transformers are
greater than 34.5 kV and are usually designed for the specific working circumstances of
hydroelectric plants, which include fluctuating load cycles and mechanical stresses from turbine-
generator systems. Decades ago, most of the large-scale assembly lines for LPTs were offshored,
and their reestablishment in the United States necessitates a skilled workforce and significant
financial investment.

To understand the current LPT assembly landscape, we conducted interviews with suppliers and
people in the industry. They emphasized that demand will keep growing because of
electrification and the growth of data centers. Manufacturers are focusing on making new high-
capacity units (100–200 MVA and above, some classified as 230 kV and others as >345 kV).
Experts pointed out that there will be $2 billion in new investments in U.S. manufacturing, but
imports are still needed for units over 345 kV and 765 kV. One U.S. GOES manufacturer makes
a small amount of high-quality or premium GOES and is working to improve its capabilities.
They shared that Nippon Steel’s purchase of U.S. Steel has stabilized the short-term steel
outlook. But tariffs still make it hard to import goods from targeted countries, which limits
sourcing options. Suppliers do not consider components such as tanks and housings as problems
but say that bottlenecks in specialized parts are the biggest problem.

People who were interviewed said that bushings and OLTCs are now the biggest risks in the
market. COVID made existing problems worse. Before the pandemic, it took from 40 weeks to a
year to get 230-kV bushing. Now, 345-kV bushings take more than 130 weeks, and there are no
domestic suppliers for 765-kV bushings. There are only two U.S. manufacturers that support the
345-kV market and none that cover the 765-kV market. Global companies are still limited in
capacity, and customer wait times increase if specific types of insulators are ordered (e.g., resin-

 10

impregnated synthetic, resin-impregnated paper, and oil-impregnated synthetic) that require
additional lead time. As a result, the only option is to rely on available inventory. U.S.
manufacturers also import bushings and OLTCs from Germany and Switzerland. The current
lead time to manufacture bushings and OLTCs is about 80 to 90 weeks, even though companies
are working to increase their capabilities. Some regional suppliers of CTC have exited the
market, which has forced remaining companies to take additional orders. The United States has a
few producers of pressboard and insulating materials, but there are not many options outside of
China. Stakeholders said that winding is still a long and difficult process, even though final
assembly is fairly quick. They also said that it is difficult to find and train qualified workers. The
interview process revealed that even though long-term demand is clear and domestic investment
needs more time to bear fruit, the U.S. LPT supply chain is still structurally weak because of
long lead times, limited infrastructure, and a lack of skilled workers in the domestic market.

1.6 Estimated Regional Pricing
Regional pricing for LPTs in the United States varies significantly due to transportation logistics,
labor market conditions, and project-specific needs. For new procurement, the cost of LPTs
could range from roughly $15,000 6 to $21,000 7 per MVA 8 (USITC 2024a), depending on unit
rating, voltage class, country of origin, transportation needs and customization requirements. For
a 100-MVA unit, this corresponds to approximately $1.5–$2.1 million per unit. In some cases,
particularly for highly customized units with complex logistics, costs can reach as high as
$40/kWac, translating to about $4 million for a 100-MVA unit. A 2020 industry survey by the
U.S. Department of Commerce found that GOES and CTC, two major components, each
contribute about 25% of the total production cost of an LPT (Bureau of Industry and Security
2021).

Logistics and transportation can account for approximately 3% to 20% (U.S. Department of
Energy 2024) of the total cost of an LPT, depending on factors such as distance, routing, and
escort requirements for oversized loads. Remote hydropower project facilities such as the ones in
hilly terrains or rural parts of the country often face the highest logistical premiums due to
limited rail access and complex road networks. Oversized transformer shipments typically
require route surveys, permits, and, in some cases, infrastructure modifications, all of which add
substantially to the final installed cost. For imported units, maritime shipping rates, port handling
fees, and inland transportation can collectively add hundreds of thousands of dollars, particularly
during periods of global supply chain disruption. However, regions with existing transformer
manufacturing and assembly facilities could benefit from reduced transportation costs, shorter
lead times, and lower logistical risks.

Labor cost differentials by region also contribute to price variation. Assembly and installation
labor rates in the Northeast and on the West Coast are typically 15% to 25% higher than in other
parts of country, driven by prevailing wage rates and cost of living. This locational factor is
magnified for rehabilitation projects requiring specialized field assembly crews, where labor
mobilization and per diem costs can be substantial.

Supply chain constraints and price volatility in upstream inputs such as GOES and CTC wires
also directly affect regional pricing. Any fluctuations in global commodity markets can shift LPT
pricing by as much as 5% to 10% over a short period, and these increases are often amplified in
regions farther away from supply hubs.

In summary, while base LPT costs are largely driven by unit rating and design complexity,
regional factors such as transportation, labor, and supply chain stability can significantly affect
final installed pricing. Strategic steps such as developing closer-to-site manufacturing and
assembly hubs and expanding domestic capacity could help reduce both cost variability and
project risk and enhance LPT supply chain resiliency within the United States.

1.7 Import Reliance
The U.S. LPT market remains heavily reliant on imports of both finished LPTs and upstream raw
material inputs, driven by limited domestic manufacturing and assembly capacity as well as
constrained domestic production of critical upstream inputs such as high-quality GOES. It is
estimated that roughly 80% of LPTs consumed in the United States are imported. Rapidly
increasing domestic demand for LPTs is intensifying competition for the limited supply of
electrical steel and other essential inputs, both from domestic production and imports. LPTs
typically require an estimated 200–400 kg of GOES per MVA of transformer capacity, implying
that a 100-MVA transformer may contain 20–40 metric tons of GOES. The highest grade of
GOES continues to be produced exclusively by Nippon Steel in Japan and remains unavailable
from any U.S. supplier. U.S. GOES producer Cleveland-Cliffs manufactures intermediate-quality
grades, but these remain more expensive than Nippon Steel’s high-quality GOES and offer no
efficiency advantage over imported alternatives, making them uncommon in LPT manufacturing.

As a result, U.S. LPT manufacturers rely heavily on imports primarily from Japan and South
Korea for both high-grade and competitively priced intermediate-grade GOES. This dependency
on foreign supply creates a significant vulnerability in the transformer supply chain, as any trade
disruptions, export restrictions, or shifts in global steel production capacity could directly
constrain already limited U.S. transformer manufacturing output. Table 4 provides an overview

in the United States if the duties were revoked (USITC 2024b). These duties on South Korean
imports could effectively constrain one of the most cost-effective and competitive foreign
sources of LPTs for the U.S. market.

1.8 Supply Challenges
Many hydroelectric plants operate for longer than the 40- to 45-year design lifetime of an LPT,
which leads to demand for replacement transformers for older hydroelectric plants. Long lead
times, high costs, and complex logistics present challenges for hydropower operators who wish
to procure replacement LPTs.

 15

Lead times: The time between placing an order and receiving an LPT has increased from 5–16
months in 2010 to an average of 30 months in the first half of 2025 (Wood Mackenzie 2025;
Office of Electricity Delivery and Energy Reliability 2014). For owners of aging hydropower
assets, this represents a substantial increase in the potential unplanned downtime that could be
caused by the failure of an LPT. For LPTs that are already at or beyond their design lifetime,
longer lead times are especially challenging because the failure rate increases with the age of the
unit (Tenbohlen, Hanif, and Martin n.d.). The process of procuring a replacement for an LPT
nearing the end of its service life may need to begin sooner to account for longer lead times.
Since current lead times for LPTs are stretching up to four years, procurement activities could
begin years in advance, rather than months or weeks.

Domestic transformer manufacturers highlighted some critical transformer components that have
very long lead times and limited domestic supply. Bushings for LPTs are currently the item with
the longest lead time, up to 130 weeks. OLTCs take 80–90 weeks to procure and have just 1 or 2
U.S. manufacturers; otherwise, they are typically sourced from Europe. One domestic
transformer manufacturer said they order extra inventory ahead of time or ask customers to be
open to different makes or specifications for a particular product to address the long lead times
for OLTCs and bushings. Pressboard is another critical item with a single domestic manufacturer
and is often imported as well. Sourcing pressboard is a global challenge, except perhaps from
China.

LPT costs: Transformer costs have increased sharply since 2021, with prices in 2025
approximately 80% higher than the relatively constant value between 2014 and 2021 (Figure 8).
Due to high levels of import dependence for both finished LPTs and components and materials
used in domestic LPT production, increased tariff rates will likely result in higher costs.

Workforce and expertise: Highly specialized labor, skilled winders, and engineers whose
ability can be described as an art form are required to benchmark the standard of windings and
cores used in LPTs. With a great proportion of the hydropower engineering workforce nearing

 16

retirement age, the niche expertise to design generator step-up units for hydroelectric plants,
which are typically made to custom standards, is in a critical condition. A 2020 U.S. Department
of Commerce survey reports that 89% of American companies that manufacture components
struggle to find qualified workers (U.S. Department of Energy 2024). This notion is further
supported by interviews conducted with manufacturers and vendors of hydropower components
who noted shortages of workers with more than 10 years of experience in the industry. Many of
these workers are moving to other industries due to wage differences.

Logistics and transportation challenges: Transport logistics are complex for LPTs, which are
oversized loads weighing more than 100 tons, but the challenges are particularly acute for LPTs
used in hydropower. Many hydroelectric dams are sited in rugged or remote locations where
access roads may feature weight-restricted bridges, steep grades, or tight curves. There is a
limited domestic fleet of specialized vehicles that can transport LPTs, including multi-axle heavy
haul trailers and Schnabel railcars (which suspend the transformer as part of the railcar frame),
an example of which is shown in Figure 9. Facilities located along navigable waterways may be
able to deliver LPTs by barge (Roberts 2021). Each transportation stage requires special permits
for oversized loads from every jurisdiction along the route. Roughly 3% to 20% of a
transformer’s total cost is related to shipping and logistics (U.S. Department of Energy 2024).

Permitting and regulatory delays: Prolonged regulatory procedures indirectly impact LPT
supply chains. Safety and environmental regulation of transformers can pose delays for
transformer replacements. U.S. Environmental Protection Agency regulations on mineral oil use
add further compliance steps for asset owners, and the duration between removing an old
transformer and installing a new one can be extended by these requirements. For example,
switchyard renovations may have to contend with additional environmental permitting or permit
amendments if the footprint or containment of a new transformer differs from the old one.
Permitting delays coupled with other supply chain challenges such as long lead times further
increase demand uncertainty for LPTs. To provide manufacturers with more precise signals

 17

regarding future transformer demand, the U.S. Department of Energy and the Federal Energy
Regulatory Commission have started assessing opportunities to reduce the time it takes for
interconnection approvals and grid expansions (U.S. Government Accountability Office 2023).

Trade restrictions and import policies: International trade issues influence the LPT supply
chain. In 2018, under section 232 of the U.S. Trade Expansion Act, the U.S imposed a 25% tariff
on most imported steel. While the intent of the policy was to spur domestic steel production,
instead transformer manufacturers faced higher material costs (Erickson 2022). An analysis from
the American Public Power Association found that proposed duties on copper and other LPT
inputs may affect transformer prices and could discourage energy projects that need step-up
transformers (Ciampoli 2025). In discussion with stakeholders, original equipment
manufacturers expressed concerns about sourcing from China, Russia, and other foreign entities
of concern (FEOC) because of security and supply availability concerns.

BABA and BAA policies: Two key policies govern domestic content requirements for both
federal acquisition and the use of federal funds in infrastructure projects: the Buy American Act
(BAA) and the Build America, Buy America Act (BABA). These laws aim to increase the
domestic content of goods procured under federal agency projects and/or infrastructure projects
using federal funds. BAA pertains to contracts with federal agencies while BABA applies to
federally funded infrastructure projects requiring iron, steel, manufactured products, and
construction materials (Putsche et al. 2024).

Although these policies aim to support American manufacturing, they present challenges for
hydropower developers, asset owners, and equipment manufacturers because American
manufacturers and suppliers are limited in the current market. Developers, owners, operators,
and suppliers in hydropower face challenges finding equipment that meets standards, especially
for large hydropower projects. While projects may seek waivers if a product is unavailable (in
sufficient quantity or quality), increases the project cost by over 25%, or is inconsistent with the
public interest, attaining these waivers can often take time and resources and may not be possible
in certain circumstances. At the time of writing, the Made in America waiver library contains
2,293 proposed or approved waivers (Made in America n.d.). A submitted waiver may be
challenged or protested by the funding agency. Manufacturers must monitor their waiver’s status
in this portal and await the Office of Management and Budget determination.

Because most major hydropower components, including LPTs, are fabricated to custom
specifications and are site-specific, adhering to compliance and documentation rules sometimes
makes the procurement process harder. While the BAA can provide long-term benefits for the
hydropower sector and enhance the industry’s self-sufficiency in supply chain capacity, it poses
industry concern about near-term supply chain bottlenecks and the reliability of the grid as
domestic production capacity struggles to meet ongoing demand.

3 Conclusion
The U.S. hydropower sector is at a critical juncture, with many LPTs approaching or past their
design lifetimes, yet replacement cycles are constrained by a globalized supply chain with very
limited domestic capacity. Persistent reliance on imports for high-grade GOES, OLTCs, and
bushings makes the sector particularly vulnerable to geopolitical shocks, trade restrictions, and
commodity price volatility. Regional disparities in pricing are exacerbated by logistics costs, and
specialized workforce shortages elevate the risk of delays and overruns in new generation and
rehabilitation projects. The current period underscores how fragile the supply chain has become,
with long procurement lead times and material bottlenecks coinciding with the need to expand
America’s hydropower fleet. While policy tools such as the Domestic Content Bonus under the
investment tax credit and production tax credit create important levers for reshoring LPT
manufacturing, their effectiveness hinges on parallel investments in upstream inputs, skilled
workforce training, and expansion of U.S. assembly infrastructure.

Federal policy developments in 2024 and 2025 provide new opportunities to offset these
vulnerabilities. The Domestic Content Bonus credit extended tax incentive eligibility to
hydropower and PSH projects, and IRS Notice 2024-41 and Notice 2025-08 made it easier to
use. Safe harbor provisions now allow industry to rely on IRS-accepted classifications for wind,
solar, and battery electric storage systems, while the 80/20 retrofit rule enables qualifying
rehabilitation projects to benefit from the incentive. Generator step-up transformers are explicitly
included as manufactured products, and recent 2025 guidance further tightened restrictions on
sourcing from FEOC countries, reshaping procurement practices. Taken together, these measures
not only improve the economics of hydropower and PSH projects through up to a 10% credit
boost but also create strong incentives to expand domestic manufacturing and supply capacity.

Given the strategic significance of U.S. hydropower infrastructure, and especially as hydropower
emerges as a solution to meet the growing demand for firm, flexible power, sustained federal
support and public/private sector cooperation will be essential to secure a steady supply of LPTs.
In the near term, utilities and hydropower plant operators are placing transformer orders much
earlier, sharing spare parts, gradually updating outdated equipment, and advocating for
supportive regulatory frameworks. Through higher utilization of current manufacturing facilities
alongside investing in workforce training programs, existing domestic transformer manufacturers
can help address some of the immediate supply challenges. However, broader actions such as
building new manufacturing capacity, standardizing designs, and creating cutting-edge
transformer technologies will take years to materialize and will require long-term commitment to
ensure that hydropower rehabilitation and expansion projects can proceed without critical delays.
