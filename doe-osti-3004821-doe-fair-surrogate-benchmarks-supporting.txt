1 Executive Summary
Computational Science is being revolutionized by integrating AI and simulation and, in
particular, by deep learning surrogate models that can replace all or part of traditional
large-scale HPC computations. Such surrogates can achieve remarkable performance im-
provements, as much as several orders of magnitude, and save both compute time and
energy. The Surrogate Benchmark Initiative (SBI) project creates a community repos-
itory and FAIR (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, and Reusable) data ecosystem for
HPC application surrogate benchmarks. The SBI team comes from Argonne National
Laboratory (ANL), Indiana University (IU), Rutgers University, the University of Ten-
nessee, Knoxville (UTK), and the University of Virginia(UVA). SBI repositories include
data, code, and all relevant collateral artifacts, that the science and engineering com-
munity needs to use and reuse these data sets and surrogates. SBI repositories generate
active research from both the participants in SBI and the broad community of AI and
domain scientists.
 This project develops surrogates that use several different neural nets to learn and
quickly infer the results of simulations and data systems and capture them as surrogate
benchmarks with a rich set of metadata, covering. Data; Model; Metrics specification;
Machine specification; Science, Speed, Power Results, We research FAIR metadata for
these benchmarks. We develop application surrogate examples as benchmarks across
many fields (ANL, UTK, IU, UVA). We also study non Surrogate benchmarks that have
many common features and similar issues regarding FAIRness. We work with MLCom-
mons (UVA, UTK), which is a major machine learning benchmarking activity where we
get metadata ontologies, software, and benchmarks, benchmarks have datasets, models,
and metadata, and they need a technical framework developed by UTK and Rutgers and
deployed by UVA. We study features of Surrogates, including performance, training set
size, and uncertainty quantification (Rutgers, UVA and IU).

3 Project Objectives
The major objectives of the Surrogate Benchmark Initiative (SBI) project are to create
a community repository and FAIR (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, and Reusable)
data ecosystem for HPC application surrogate benchmarks. SBI repositories include
data, code, and relevant collateral artifacts that the science and engineering community

 4

needs to use and reuse these data sets and surrogates. SBI repositories generate active
research from both the participants in SBI and the broad community of AI and domain
scientists. Fields interesting to the SBI project include Applied Math, Astrophysics,
Biomolecular Sciences, Climate, Computational Biology, Computational Fluids, Com-
puter Science, Cosmology, Earthquake Science, Fusion, High Energy Physics, Molecular
Docking, Nanoengineering, and Plasma Physics. SBI collaborates with a leading machine
learning benchmarking activity called MLCommons which is a parent organization for
MLPerf producing machine learning Performance Benchmarks. MLCommons has over
1400 members from over 80 institutional members (mainly from industry) and strong
existing involvement of the Department of Energy laboratories through the HPC and
science data MLCommons working groups.
 Within the SBI Project, we mirror the process laid out by MLCommons and MLPerf
as much as possible. SBI builds tutorials around each deposited benchmark and col-
laborates with users from a broad range of fields to make new surrogates and new SBI
benchmarks based on an initial set of four produced in-house. SBI working groups and
other community activities are set up to advance all issues around the surrogate con-
cept. SBI will design and build general middleware to support the generation (training
from HPC simulations) and the use of surrogates. This will also make it easier for gen-
eral users to develop new surrogates and help make their major performance increases
pervasive across DOE computational science. SBI benefits application communities and
computer systems research.
 SBI also aims to support several AI research areas. The benchmarks will drive re-
search on efficient generic surrogate architectures and how they fit with different hardware
systems. Another specific activity will be research on the uncertainty quantification of
the surrogate estimates and how to build this into surrogates. Thirdly, there will be
important studies of the amount of training data needed to get reliable surrogates for
a given accuracy choice. Finally, the SBI team has already derived some simple but
effective performance models or surrogates. Still, these need extension as deeper uses of
surrogates become understood and exhibited in SBI repository depositions. The project
funds staff to ensure that non-project users are appropriately supported and that SBI’s
use of FAIR data principles is effective. MLCommons’s user interface technology is being
extended to enable convenient FAIR use of SBI repositories and access through software
code repositories supporting these efforts.

4.6 HPC for AI Systems
As many HPC systems exist in the Department of Energy, as well as the university
communities it is important to investigate the topic of the best way to use HPC resources
and the appropriate hardware and software for AI applications. We term this simply
HPC for AI. This addresses the requirement of surrogates to run on available systems.
and conversely what hardware and software is most suitable. This work was enhanced by
the collaborations with ANL and Rutgers University. ANL focused mostly on achieving
speedups on surrogates developed in their laboratory with a collaboration with Indiana

 8

University (funded from ANL and separate from Indiana University research funded by
this grant and reported later in section 4.8.4, Rutgers worked on computing orchestration
and efficient utilization of the resources in benchmark situations for surrogates and other
AI applications.
 As part of this work the following publications resulted in [10], [11], [12], [13], [14],
[15], [16], [17]. The ideas in these papers can be summarized as three separate thrusts
below.

 • Optimization of data pipelines used in training AI was studied in [10], and
 updated in [15] and [12], for decreasing data movement costs for LLM datasets held
 on storage that is not directly linked to the GPUs. The key strategy was to modify
 the shuffling algorithm, which allows fast vector access to disk (with Parquet format
 [18]) without a significant effect on the training quality. This is combined with
 parallelized I/O for the different samples in a batch. [17] introduces Rotated Tensor
 Parallelism (RTP), a novel technique to address the significant memory overheads
 associated with training expansive neural network models by efficiently sharding
 both model parameters and activations. This reduces memory consumption to near
 the theoretical minimum and so enables support for significantly larger models.

 • Enhancing Data Processing in Python Dataframes with Parallel Pro-
 cessing as explored in [11], advancing the Cylon system, which is centered on the
 use of Apache Arrow [19]. The paper introducing a cost model for evaluating the
 set of parallel processing patterns for distributed dataframe operators, that cover
 important data engineering transformations such as database join and clustering
 Furthermore, the paper evaluates the performance of Cylon on the ORNL Summit
 supercomputer.

 • Heterogeneous and Scientific Data Pipelines are studied in [13], [16], [14] by
 combining Cylon from UVA and Radical Pilot [20] developed at Rutgers to form
 Deep Radical-Cylon(RC). This integrates data engineering, deep learning frame-
 works, and workflow engines across several HPC environments, including cloud and
 supercomputing infrastructures. Deep RC supports heterogeneous systems with
 accelerators, allows the usage of communication libraries like MPI, GLOO and
 NCCL across multi-node setups, and facilitates parallel and distributed deep learn-
 ing pipelines by utilizing Radical Pilot as a task execution framework. It was tested
 with 11 neural forecasting models (PyTorch) and hydrology models (TensorFlow)
 under identical resource conditions. The design of Deep RC guarantees the smooth
 integration of scalable data frameworks, such as Cylon, with deep learning processes,
 exhibiting strong performance on cloud platforms and scientific HPC systems.

structed by a hierarchical, auto-regressive decoder that simulates the shower’s physical
propagation. This hybrid integration is made possible by two core innovations: a novel
method to condition the QPU on incident particle energy by manipulating qubit flux
biases, and a fast, adaptive algorithm to calibrate the annealer’s effective temperature
against thermal noise. Table 1 compares the quality of classical and Quantum surrogates

4.8.2 Virtual Tissue Surrogates
An additional Surrogate the project worked on is virtual tissue resulting in three publi-
cations [33], [34], [35].

 11

 Neural networks (NNs) have been demonstrated to be a viable alternative to tradi-
tional direct numerical evaluation algorithms, with the potential to accelerate computa-
tional time by several orders of magnitude. In [35] we study the use of encoder-decoder
convolutional neural network (CNN) algorithms as surrogates for steady-state diffusion
solvers. The construction of such surrogates requires the selection of an appropriate task,
network architecture, training set structure and size, loss function, and training algorithm
hyperparameters. It is well known that each of these factors can have a significant impact
on the performance of the resultant model. Our approach employs an encoder-decoder
CNN architecture, which we posit is particularly wellsuited for this task due to its ability
to effectively transform data, as opposed to merely compressing it. We systematically
evaluate a range of loss functions, hyperparameters, and training set sizes. Our results
indicate that increasing the size of the training set has a substantial effect on reducing
performance fluctuations and overall error. Additionally, we observe that the performance
of the model exhibits a logarithmic dependence on the training set size. Furthermore, we
investigate the effect on model performance by using different subsets of data with vary-
ing features. Our results highlight the importance of sampling the configurational space
in an optimal manner, as this can have a significant impact on the performance of the
model and the required training time. In the later pages [33], [34], we made importanbt
extensions. First we added in statistical effects in the underlying model and secondly
we developed a surrogate based on the diffusion model which allows us to extend the
approach to a powerful digital twin.

4.8.3 Tsunami Surrogate
The tsunami surrogate is another surrogate that was part of the project and resulted in
the following publication: [36].
 Tsunamis in the last two decades have resulted in the loss of life of over 200,000 people
and have caused billions of dollars in damage. There is therefore great motivation for the
development and improvement of current tsunami warning systems. The work conducted
represents advancements made towards the creation of a neural network-based tsunami
warning system which can produce fast inundation forecasts with high accuracy. This
was done by first improving the waveform resolution and accuracy of Tsunami Squares,
an efficient cellular automata approach to wave simulation. It was then used to create a
database of precomputed tsunamis in the event of a magnitude 9+ rupture of the Cascadia
Subduction Zone, located only 100 km off the coast of Oregon, US. Our approach utilized
a convolutional neural network which took wave height data from buoys as input and
proved successful as maps of maximum inundation could be predicted for the town of
Seaside, OR with a median error of 0.5 m.

4.8.4 Material Science Surrogates
IU worked broadly on developing AI surrogates for molecular dynamics simulations of
soft material systems, and advancing our understanding of the various tradeoffs asso-
ciated with surrogate generation and use[37], [38], [39], [40], [17]. Work was done on

dynamics simulations [39]. In addition, simulations of other polymer-based systems were
performed to expand the ecosystem of simulation-derived datasets for training AI models
[38]. The accuracy-speedup tradeoff as a function of sample size and quality was sys-
tematically studied and new speedup metrics and sample size reduction strategies were
proposed [37]. Work was performed to quantify uncertainties in surrogate predictions
and enable a deeper understanding of how confidence in surrogate predictions trades off
with surrogate accuracy. The IU team also explored related lines of research including
the use of non-traditional computing resources (e.g., cloud computing) to perform sim-
ulations for training surrogates [38], and the development of sustainable surrogates for
scientific simulations where a deeper understanding of energy usage in surrogate creation
was elucidated.
 Results were shared with the community through various mechanisms including the
publications noted below as well as via an invited talk at the 2023 Midwest Thermody-
namics and Statistical Mechanics (MTSM) Conference and via contributed talks at APS

than current ptychography reconstruction packages and reduces sampling requirements
[42]. IU contributed to this effort while speeding up the scaffolding.

4.8.6 AutoPhaseNN
AutoPhaseNN [43] is a physics-aware unsupervised deep convolutional neural network
(CNN) that learns to solve the phase problem without ever being shown real space images
of the sample amplitude or phase. By incorporating the physics of the X-ray scattering
into the network design and training, AutoPhaseNN learns to predict both the amplitude
and phase of the sample given the measured diffraction intensity alone. Additionally,
unlike previous deep learning models, AutoPhaseNN does not need the ground truth
images of sample’s amplitude and phase at any point, either in training or in deployment.
Once trained, the physical model is discarded and only the CNN portion is needed which
has learned the data inversion from reciprocal space to real space and is 100 times faster
than the iterative phase retrieval with comparable image quality. Furthermore, we show
that by using AutoPhaseNN’s prediction as the learned prior to iterative phase retrieval,
we can achieve consistently higher image quality, than neural network prediction alone,
at 10 times faster speed than iterative phase retrieval alone.

Horovod for data-parallel distributed training. The dataset comes from simulations run
by ExaLearn, with universe volumes split into cubes of size 128x128x128 with 4 redshift
bins. The total dataset volume preprocessed for MLPerf HPC v0.5 in TFRecord format

adoption of digital twins. So we recommend continued work in this area. The need for
good metadata and ontologies grows even as the use of Large Language Models LLMs
to query unstructured information. One needs an underlying structure with which to
control and record the results of LLMs.

6 Lessons Learned
One unexpected advance during this project was in the growth in use of online video
meetings which were very successful and today they are further enhanced by LLM-based
automatic meeting summarizers. So one lesson is the power and ease of cross institution

 17

collaboration. We further found new organizations bring up like MLCommons, the AI
Alliance and the Trillion Parameter Consortium. These help to further broaden and
deepen collaboration.
 We also learned about the importance and diversity of surrogates. during the project
the diffusion model and related conditional flow matching became the method of excel-
lence in many applications. More work is needed to understand which problems are best
matched to which AI architectures and how this is affected by issues like problem size
and problem structure.
 The progress of AI for Science is dramatic but very fractured and although open
access is often used, the lack of good metadata and attention to reproducibility means
that many results are not properly recorded or followed up on. This implies fair metadata
and community best practices need to be emphasized.
