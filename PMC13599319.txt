Prevalence and risk factors associated with inadvertent intraoperative hypothermia in patients undergoing trauma surgery: a single-center retrospective study
Authors: Yue Li, Hansheng Liang, Liang Sun, Xinyue Gao, Yi Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTrauma has emerged as a major cause of death worldwide. Inadvertent perioperative hypothermia (IPH) is closely related to the prognosis of trauma patients. However, risk factors related to intraoperative hypothermia have not been defined in this population.ObjectiveThis study aimed to analyze the risk factors and predictive value of these factors related to inadvertent intraoperative hypothermia in adults undergoing trauma surgery under general anesthesia.MethodsPatients aged 18 years or older who underwent trauma surgery under general anesthesia at a tertiary hospital from January 2023 to May 2025 were retrospectively enrolled. The demographic, anesthesia-related and surgery-related data of the patients were collected. Inadvertent intraoperative hypothermia was identified as a core temperature monitored in nasopharynx < 36℃. Univariate and multivariate analyses were used to identify factors associated with inadvertent intraoperative hypothermia. The area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was used to verify the discriminative ability of multivariate logistic regression results.ResultsA total of 885 patients were analyzed, the incidence of inadvertent intraoperative hypothermia was 23.3% (95% CI 20.6%-26.2%). The risk factors associated with inadvertent intraoperative hypothermia included low body mass index (BMI) (OR = 0.932, 95%CI 0.887–0.979, P = 0.005), low base line body temperature (OR = 0.076, 95%CI 0.048–0.121, P < 0.001), longer duration of surgery (OR = 1.221, 95%CI 1.011–1.476, P = 0.039), large volume of fluid infusion (OR = 1.033, 95%CI 1.000-1.067, P = 0.048) and combined positions (OR = 2.093, 95%CI 1.035–4.231, P = 0.040). ROC curve analysis showed that baseline body temperature (AUC: 0.793, 95%CI 0.757–0.829) had highest predictive efficiency for inadvertent intraoperative hypothermia among above-mentioned five factors. Meanwhile, the combination of all risk factors had the highest predictive efficacy (AUC: 0.835, 95%CI 0.804–0.866).ConclusionsThis study represents the first report on the incidence rate of inadvertent intraoperative hypothermia in patients undergoing trauma surgery and identify key risk factors including lower baseline body temperature, longer duration of surgery, lower BMI, more fluid infusion and combined positions. We also contribute the multifactor model demonstrated superior predictive value for inadvertent intraoperative hypothermia.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Background

Major trauma accounts for 10% of all deaths worldwide, ranking as the sixth leading cause of death, and is the primary cause of mortality and disability in people under 35 years old [1, 2]. In China, trauma stands as the principal cause of death for those under the age of 45 [3]. Statistics from 15,567 severe trauma patients indicate that 30–50% of severe trauma patients experience hypothermia, accompanied by various metabolic disorders and worsened prognosis [4]. Meanwhile, patient mortality increases with the severity of hypothermia [5, 6].

In trauma patients, hypothermia can trigger the “lethal triad of death”, which consists of hypothermia, acidosis, and coagulopathy [7]. Acidosis often results from inadequate organ perfusion following hemorrhagic shock, which directly impairs thrombin production and leads to coagulation dysfunction [8]. Hypothermia affects the function of thrombin and fibrinogen and, by impacting thrombin, also causes platelet adhesion dysfunction, thereby disrupting the entire coagulation cascade [9]. Once the lethal triad of trauma occurs, it should be identified and intervened early; otherwise, it can form a vicious cycle, ultimately becoming irreversible and leading to patient death. Body temperature is the most easily measurable factor among the triad.

In the surgery setting, inadvertent perioperative hypothermia (IPH) is defined as a core body temperature below 36℃ that occurs during the perioperative period due to non-medical reasons [10]. It is a common complication of general anesthesia and surgery. Since hypothermia is inherently likely to occur during anesthesia and surgery, this adds additional risk for trauma patients undergoing surgery. Currently, there are numerous clinical measures for maintaining warmth in the operating room, including using warming blankets, applying insulating dressings, pre-warming infused fluids, using forced-air warmers and so on. Therefore, aggressive temperature management is a feasible strategy to prevent the lethal triad in surgical trauma patients. However, inadvertent intraoperative hypothermia remains as a frequent adverse event in the trauma surgery setting in spite of those preventive measures.

Currently, research on hypothermia in trauma patients has primarily focused on prehospital and emergency care, leaving a considerable gap in studies related to the intraoperative anesthesia period. This prompts us to investigate whether the risk factors for inadvertent intraoperative hypothermia possess distinct characteristics. This study aims to determine the factors associated with inadvertent intraoperative hypothermia and predictive value of these factors in trauma patients.

Methods and materials

Ethics and consent

This single-center retrospective study was conducted at Peking University People’s Hospital. This study was strictly conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was approved by the Biomedical Research Ethics Committee of Peking University People’s Hospital (Approval Number: 2024PHB459-001). Since all data were collected from the hospital’s electronic medical record system and no patient follow-ups were conducted, the Ethics Committee granted a waiver for written informed consent. All personal data were kept strictly confidential.

Study population

This study screened patients who underwent surgery in the trauma care center under general anesthesia between January 2023 and May 2025. Patients excluded were those without temperature monitoring, aged under 18 years, classified as ASA (American Society of Anesthesiologists) physical status V, or undergoing non-trauma-related surgeries.

Perioperative management

In accordance with routine clinical practice, patients in our institution received nasopharyngeal temperature monitoring as intraoperative core temperature. The ambient temperature in the operating room is maintained at 22–25 °C. All patients were covered with unheated cotton blankets preoperatively and surgical drapes intraoperatively for thermal insulation. A circulating-water warming mattress set at 38℃ was placed beneath the patients’ bodies during surgery. Active warming measures such as fluid warming or forced-air warming devices were initiated only when core temperature dropped to 36℃. Inadvertent intraoperative hypothermia was defined as a core temperature below 36℃ during surgery, while maintenance of core temperature ≥ 36℃ throughout the procedure was considered normothermia [11]. The data acquisition system records nasopharyngeal temperature readings at 5 min intervals. To ensure correct placement of the nasopharyngeal probe, the operator initially inserts it to a depth of approximately 15 cm. After the temperature reading stabilizes, the probe depth is fine-tuned to the position that yields the highest temperature reading, with a target depth range of 10–20 cm [12]. To exclude “false” hypothermia caused by factors such as equipment malfunction or accidental probe displacement, we verified that all subsequent records within 5 min after the first sub-36℃ reading remained below 36℃ before confirming hypothermia occurrence.

Data acquisition and outcome

All data were obtained from the hospital’s electronic medical record system. Demographic information were collected included age, gender, height, and weight (used to calculate Body Mass Index, BMI) as well as ASA classification. Clinical data were extracted from medical records included: preoperative diagnosis, surgical site, type of surgery, surgical positioning, duration of surgery, duration of anesthesia, baseline body temperature, intraoperative lowest body temperature, discharge body temperature (body temperature upon leaving the operating room), whether active warming measures were implemented intraoperatively, blood loss volume, blood transfusion volume, fluid infusion volume, opioid dosage (morphine equivalent dose), usage of inhaled anesthetics, and administration of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (flurbiprofen axetil). Further tracking focused on the mortality outcome up to discharge. Based on preoperative diagnosis, patients were categorized as having multiple trauma or single trauma, and whether traumatic brain injury was present. Surgical sites were classified as trunk or limb procedures, and whether body cavities were entered. Surgery types were defined as emergency or elective, and further differentiated as open or closed procedures. Surgical positioning was categorized as single position or combined positions. Duration of surgery referred to the time from surgical incision to completion of wound closure. Duration of anesthesia spanned from initiation of anesthesia induction to patient departure from the operating room. Baseline body temperature was defined as the first nasopharyngeal temperature recorded after anesthesia induction.

The primary outcome was the occurrence of inadvertent intraoperative hypothermia, and patients were divided into hypothermia and normothermia groups based on whether hypothermia occurred during surgery. The secondary outcome was mortality up to hospital discharge.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range). Data were compared using Student’s t-test (for normally distributed data) or the Mann-Whitney U test (for non-normally distributed data). Categorical variables are expressed as number of patients (percentage), and analyzed using the Chi-square test, Chi-square test with continuity correction, or Fisher’s exact test.

Variables with P-values < 0.1 in univariate analysis and without mutual correlation were included in the multivariate logistic regression analysis using backward stepwise regression. Results were expressed as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). The predicted probabilities from individual and combined variables identified in the multivariate logistic regression analysis were used to construct receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. The predictive performance of the multivariate model for inadvertent intraoperative hypothermia was evaluated comprehensively using the area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, and specificity.

Statistical analyses were performed via SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp., Chicago, IL, USA). P < 0.05 was considered indicative of statistical significance.

Results

Demographics and clinical baseline data

A total of 1091 patients underwent surgery in the trauma care center under general anesthesia between January 2023 and May 2025 were initially reviewed. After exclusions of 98 patients without temperature monitoring, 24 under 18 years of age, 11 classified as ASA physical status V, and 73 who underwent non-trauma-related surgeries, 885 patients were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). Among these 885 patients, 206 experienced inadvertent intraoperative hypothermia, yielding an incidence rate of 23.3% (95% CI 20.6%-26.2%). And 49 patients had a baseline body temperature below 36℃, corresponding to a baseline hypothermia incidence of 5.5% (95% CI 4.0%-7.1%).

Risk factors associated with inadvertent intraoperative hypothermia

Multivariate logistic regression was performed on factors with P-values ≤ 0.1 in univariate analysis and without linear correlation (Table 2). The clearly controversial interventions of forced-air warming and fluid warming were also excluded. This is related to the timing of its use, which we will analyze in the discussion. Ultimately, five variables were independently associated with inadvertent intraoperative hypothermia (Table 3). Higher baseline body temperature (OR = 0.076, 95%CI 0.048–0.120, P < 0.001) and higher BMI (OR = 0.932, 95%CI 0.887–0.979, P = 0.005) were protective factors against hypothermia, while longer duration of surgery (OR = 1.221, 95%CI 1.011–1.476, P = 0.039), larger fluid infusion volume (OR = 1.033, 95%CI 1.000-1.067, P = 0.048) and combined positions (OR = 2.093, 95%CI 1.035–4.231, P = 0.040) were identified as independent risk factors for inadvertent intraoperative hypothermia.

Predictive value of risk factors

Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis was conducted to evaluate the predictive value of risk factors for inadvertent intraoperative hypothermia in trauma surgery. As shown in Fig. 2, the ROC analysis revealed that BMI (AUC: 0.572, 95%CI 0.529–0.616), fluid infusion volume (AUC: 0.628, 95%CI 0.584–0.672) as well as surgical duration (AUC: 0.625, 95%CI 0.582–0.669), demonstrated poor predictive performance. The combined positions also had a low AUC value (AUC: 0.517, 95%CI 0.471–0.563) and an extremely low sensitivity (8%), but its specificity in predicting inadvertent intraoperative hypothermia is as high as 94.6%. Baseline body temperature showed higher predictive value compared with other individual factors (AUC: 0.793, 95%CI 0.757–0.829). In contrast, the multivariate combination achieved an AUC of 0.835 with the sensitivity of 70.9%, specificity of 81.9%, and substantially better predictive value (95%CI 0.804–0.866) (Table 4).

Discussion

The validated risk factors for hypothermia

The results of the multivariate analysis in this study showed that baseline body temperature exerted the most significant influence on the occurrence of inadvertent intraoperative hypothermia in trauma patients. Each 1℃ increase in baseline body temperature was associated with a 92.4% reduction in hypothermia risk, establishing it as the most predictive independent factor for inadvertent intraoperative hypothermia. Additionally, for every additional hour of surgery duration, the risk of hypothermia increased by 22.1%. Each 1 kg/m² increment in BMI corresponded to a 7.8% decrease in hypothermia risk, while every 100 mL increase in fluid administration raised the incidence of inadvertent intraoperative hypothermia by 3.3%. Patients with multiple injuries frequently require intraoperative repositioning; combined positions was associated with a 2.093-fold higher incidence of inadvertent intraoperative hypothermia compared to procedures maintaining a single position throughout.

Baseline body temperature has been consistently validated as a significant predictor of inadvertent intraoperative hypothermia [13–15]. A lower initial temperature not only provides a reduced starting point for thermoregulation but may also reflect underlying impairment in thermoregulatory function, increasing susceptibility to hypothermia [13]. In this study, baseline core temperature was recorded using a nasal probe following endotracheal intubation. As anesthesia progresses, redistribution of heat from the core to peripheral compartments further decreases core temperature [16]. Thus, preoperative warming—particularly prewarming—is essential for patients with low baseline temperatures. Current perioperative guidelines recommend prewarming for patients with pre-anesthesia core temperatures below 36℃ [17]. Evidence indicates that even brief prewarming periods (e.g., 10 min) can effectively prevent hypothermia in gynecological laparoscopic surgery [18]. In our institution, all patients routinely received a 38℃ warming blanket upon entering the operating room, which may partly explain the lower overall incidence of inadvertent intraoperative hypothermia. However, other active warming strategies, such as fluid warming and forced-air warming, were often initiated reactively after hypothermia had developed. Proactively implementing combined warming measures in patients with low baseline temperatures could further reduce inadvertent intraoperative hypothermia incidence.

BMI has also been consistently identified as a factor influencing inadvertent intraoperative hypothermia risk [19–21]. Patients with higher BMI generally possess greater adipose tissue, which provides insulation against heat redistribution from core to periphery under general anesthesia. Additionally, elevated leptin levels commonly seen in individuals with higher body fat may stimulate sympathetic activity and increase metabolic heat production. Conversely, low-BMI patients, with reduced fat reserves, are more vulnerable to hypothermia [22]. As BMI is readily available from basic patient data, targeted hypothermia prevention should be initiated early in these at-risk individuals.

Previous studies have confirmed that surgical duration significantly influences inadvertent intraoperative hypothermia risk [21, 23]. Prolonged surgery extends anesthetic exposure, increasing cumulative anesthetic doses that cause peripheral vasodilation, elevate sweating thresholds, and persistently suppress central thermoregulation [24]. Concurrently, extended exposure of the surgical field, combined with heat loss through bleeding and urination, exacerbates core temperature decline [25]. Thus, collaborative efforts between surgical and anesthesia teams to minimize operative and anesthetic time are strongly recommended.

Fluid infusion represents another key determinant of intraoperative temperature [16, 26]. Administration of room-temperature fluids extracts substantial body heat as the fluid warms to core temperature. Moreover, large-volume infusion often indicates significant blood loss or shock. Beyond surgical bleeding, coagulopathy substantially contributes to major intraoperative hemorrhage, while shock predisposes patients to acidosis—bringing us back to the lethal triad in trauma. Although intraoperative lactate levels were not analyzed in this study due to inconsistent monitoring, the interplay between hypothermia, acidosis, and coagulopathy forms a well-established vicious cycle [27]. The association between inadvertent intraoperative hypothermia and fluid infusion further validates the direct relationship among hypothermia, coagulopathy, and acidosis, underscoring the clinical relevance of our findings.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to identify combined positions as an independent risk factor for inadvertent intraoperative hypothermia. Previous reports have associated lateral, prone, and lithotomy positions with higher hypothermia risk compared to supine positioning [19, 22, 28]; however, the specific risk of combined positions has not been previously quantified. This finding may be particularly relevant in trauma surgery, where polytrauma patients often require procedures involving multiple anatomical sites. Intraoperative repositioning necessitates removing sterile drapes, physically moving the exposed patient, and repeating skin preparation—disrupting active warming measures and accelerating heat loss. Clinically, patients whose temperatures had stabilized with active warming often experience sharp declines—sometimes up to 1℃—following repositioning. Therefore, more aggressive warming strategies and temporarily elevated room temperatures should be considered for cases anticipated to require position changes.

Although each factor independently influences hypothermia risk, ROC curve analysis indicates that integrating multiple predictors offers the optimal strategy for identifying trauma patients at risk for inadvertent intraoperative hypothermia. In practical terms, the most effective warming protocols should be prioritized for patients with low baseline body temperature, low BMI, anticipated prolonged surgery, expected substantial fluid administration, and potential need for intraoperative repositioning.

The incidence of inadvertent intraoperative hypothermia

In this study, the incidence of inadvertent intraoperative hypothermia (< 36 °C) in trauma patients under general anesthesia was observed to be 23.3%, which is close to that reported in only a few related studies [29] and lower than the 42%–47% reported in most studies [13, 30–32]. This may be attributed to our institution’s historical emphasis on perioperative thermal protection, with the implementation of relatively comprehensive temperature management protocols.

In addition, in clinical practice, many severely injured trauma patients are already at risk of hypothermia before arriving in the operating room, which may render them ineligible for surgery. Similarly, among this population, although some patients are eligible for surgery, they are often excluded from statistical analyses in most studies due to their high ASA classification. An analysis of hypothermia and outcomes in 4,492 severe trauma patients indicated that among those with an Injury Severity Score (ISS) ≥ 20, after excluding preoperative or intraoperative deaths, approximately 60% of patients with recorded body temperature data were hypothermic upon arrival in the emergency department or at operating room admission [33]. This underscores the strong association between trauma and hypothermia, suggesting that the reality of hypothermia in trauma patients is far from as optimistic as the seemingly low incidence rate of 23.3% would imply. It also highlights the potential benefit of timely temperature management within the clinician’s control.

Active warming measures with inadvertent intraoperative hypothermia

There is compelling evidence that active warming measures effectively reduce the incidence of hypothermia [34, 35]. In the present study, the primary active warming methods examined included warming blankets, forced-air warming systems, and blood and fluid warming. Although ambient operating room temperature was also considered, retrospective data were unavailable in our institution.

No significant difference was observed in the use of warming blankets between patients who developed intraoperative hypothermia and those who maintained normothermia. This can be attributed to the standard presence of warming blankets in every operating room and their mandatory use for all patients. Nevertheless, practical constraints—such as device malfunction, specific patient positioning requirements, or the use of specialized surgical tables—often prevented full compliance, resulting in a usage rate below 100%.

While the other two warming interventions showed statistically significant differences in utilization between hypothermic and non-hypothermic patients, both were applied more frequently in the hypothermic group—a finding that appears counterintuitive. This pattern, however, can be readily explained from both methodological and clinical viewpoints. As an observational study, the timing and indication for initiating active warming were not controlled. Moreover, since hypothermia could occur at any time during surgery, warming measures were often initiated only after the patient had already become hypothermic. In addition, forced-air warmers and fluid-warming devices are not standard equipment in all operating rooms at our facility. In practice, these devices are typically deployed only when a patient is already hypothermic or deemed at high risk. Consequently, their use often represents a reaction to hypothermia rather than a preventive strategy. As forced-air warming devices and fluid-warming devices represent reactive countermeasures to hypothermia, they were not incorporated as candidate risk factors in the multivariate regression analysis.

These observations highlight a persistent lag in clinical intervention. With advancing research on perioperative temperature management, the concept of prewarming has gained recognition as an effective preventive strategy [26, 36]. However, the limited focus on intraoperative hypothermia specifically in trauma patients has resulted in insufficient awareness and systematic prevention of this complication.

Clinical applicability: recommendations for temperature management in high-risk patients

Based on the independent risk factors validated in this study and previous experience, a more detailed clinical temperature management strategy can be developed for high-risk patients with low baseline body temperature, low BMI, complex injury patterns, and anticipated complicated surgical procedures.

Monitoring

Continuous temperature monitoring is recommended, especially in unstable patients.

Prewarming

For all high-risk patients, initiate warming using a forced-air warming blanket for at least 15 min before induction (or as soon as the patient enters the holding area) [37], and increase the operating room temperature to 24–25℃ [25].

Intraoperative active warming protocol

All patients should be covered with a warming blanket, while high-risk patients should additionally be covered with insulating drapes. Forced-air warming should be initiated at the start of surgery, before any drop in core temperature occurs, and should not be delayed until the temperature falls to 36 °C. When rapid or large-volume fluid infusion is required, all intravenous fluids and blood products should be prewarmed [38] and administered through a fluid warmer [39]. For high-risk patients, all infused fluids should be warmed regardless of the volume.

Repositioning

Temporarily increase the operating room temperature for 15 min before and after repositioning. Keep forced-air warming running as much as possible throughout the repositioning process. Immediately after repositioning, remeasure the core temperature and resume all warming measures.

If the core temperature still drops below 36.0 °C despite the above measures, consider adding a second forced-air warmer (e.g., one on the lower body and one on the upper body). Increase the fluid warmer temperature if the device allows. Reduce surgical exposure (e.g., temporarily cover exposed areas).

limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective design introduced confounding variables and variability in intraoperative management, precluding analysis of some well-established risk factors (e.g., age, minimally invasive techniques). Future studies should use standardized protocols and rigorous designs.

Second, heterogeneity in trauma patients (injury location/severity, surgery type, approach) limits generalizability. Patients with ASA V were excluded due to small numbers and extreme data, so findings may not apply to this vulnerable group. Future studies should focus on homogeneous trauma/surgical populations.

Third, outcome analysis was limited to in-hospital mortality; long-term follow-up data are lacking.

Fourth, baseline temperature was defined as the first nasopharyngeal temperature after induction, which may underestimate true preoperative core temperature due to early heat redistribution. Mildly hypothermic patients might thus be misclassified, attenuating the observed association. Future studies should measure core temperature upon arrival in the OR/holding area, before induction.

Fifth, lactate levels were not analyzed due to inconsistent monitoring. Acidosis is part of the lethal triad (with hypothermia and coagulopathy), so this limits assessment of their interplay. Future studies should monitor lactate, coagulation, and temperature together to evaluate the lethal triad and guide resuscitation.

Conclusion

Inadvertent intraoperative hypothermia appears to be a readily modifiable factor within the lethal triad of trauma patients, and therefore deserves further attention. Our findings identify low baseline body temperature, low BMI, prolonged surgical duration, larger fluid infusion volume, and intraoperative repositioning as independent risk factors. The combination of multiple factors showed the highest predictive value for inadvertent intraoperative hypothermia in this cohort. These results support the need for proactive warming strategies, including prewarming, in high-risk patients. However, prospective studies are still required before a standardized predictive or interventional protocol can be proposed.
