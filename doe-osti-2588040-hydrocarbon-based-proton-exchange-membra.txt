Abstract

 This work aims to evaluate alternative proton exchange membranes (PEMs) that

are environmentally safe, cost-effective, and structurally sound for use in PEM fuel cells

and water electrolyzers. A sulfonic acid-functionalized polynorbornene-based ionomer

was synthesized via an SN2 substitution reaction and investigated as a potential PEM for

electrochemical applications. The chemical structure of the target polymer was studied

using nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy and attenuated total reflectance

– Fourier transform spectroscopy (ATR-FTIR), confirming the sulfonic acid

functionalization. Gel permeation chromatography (GPC) revealed a sufficient molecular

weight distribution, verifying its ability to form membranes. Membranes were prepared by

drop-casting methods using a petri dish and a film applicator. Solution concentrations and

casting parameters were optimized to achieve membranes with a controlled wet thickness

of 20-50 µm. Additionally, key performance properties of the membranes were explored,

where they exhibited sufficient ion exchange capacity (IEC), proton conductivity, water

uptake, and swelling degree. These results demonstrate the polymer’s potential for use

in electrochemical devices such as proton exchange membrane fuel cells (PEMFCs) and

proton exchange membrane water electrolyzers (PEMWEs).

 I. INTRODUCTION

 Domestic energy security has recently emerged as a national priority, aiming to

increase affordability and reduce reliance on imported natural resources.1 A solution to

this is the use of electrochemical devices such as PEM fuel cells and water electrolyzers.

PEMFCs are galvanic cells that convert chemical energy from H2 and O2 directly into

electrical energy, where water and heat are the byproducts, as seen in Figure 1a.2

PEMWEs are electrolytic cells that use electrical energy for the non-spontaneous

(electricity-requiring) splitting of water into H2 and O2, as seen in Figure 1b.2 Hydrogen, a

high-density energy carrier, serves as fuel and stores energy for these electrochemical

cells.

 PEMs, part of the MEA in electrochemical devices, allow the conduction of

protons from anode to cathode. These PEMs are formed from ion-conducting polymers

called ionomers. In effort to maximize their performance, high proton conductivity is

needed to ensure adequate power output. The standard operating temperature of both

cells is 80 °C, and the relative humidity (RH) is maintained in the range of 30-100% to

accommodate the operating conditions of PEMFCs and PEMWEs.3 Thermal, chemical,

and mechanical stability, as well as conductivity, are tested within these operating

parameters. Minimal diffusion of gases across the membrane is another key property of

PEMs. It is important to ensure that hydrogen gas does not pass through the membrane

(fuel crossover), as the combination of H2 and O2 can lead to an explosion and reduce

the performance of the device.4 Furthermore, the membrane’s swelling behavior is

important, as too much swelling increases the resistance of proton conduction and

decreases dimensional stability. Thus, a lower swelling degree is optimal.

 Water acts as the medium that transports protons through the membrane via the

vehicular mechanism, as seen in Figure 2a.5 Therefore, sufficient water uptake is

important as it maintains the membrane hydration necessary for proton conduction.

Additionally, a second proton-transporting mechanism called proton-hopping (or surface

mechanism) can be used, where protons move between sulfate groups, as seen in Figure

2b.5 Typically, this mechanism is a much slower process.

 Traditionally, a perfluorosulfonic acid (PFSA) polymer known as NafionTM is

considered the commercial standard of PEMs. However, Nafion is classified as a

per/polyfluorinated alkyl substance (PFAS), a group of chemicals known for their

environmental contamination. Due to the extremely high stability of the C–F bonds, these

‘forever chemicals’ have a slow degradation rate under natural environmental conditions

and can exist for centuries.6 Additionally, the monomers, additives, and byproducts

associated with the synthesis of Nafion are smaller perfluorinated compounds that are

capable of penetrating natural organisms.7 Therefore, the demand for an environmentally

safe and high-performing PEM has increased.

 This study focuses on the investigation and characterization of a sulfonic acid-

functionalized polynorbornene-based PEM to be used as an alternative to Nafion, through

the optimization of its key working properties – IEC, conductivity, water uptake, and

swelling degree. A polynorbornene-based polymer was chosen, as their backbone

contains rigid bicyclic hydrocarbon rings (no C–F bonds). Post-polymerization

 6

functionalization of the polymer with sulfonic acid sites will facilitate proton conduction

within the membrane. This functionalization occurs through an SN2 substitution reaction

of the precursor terminal alkyl bromide functionality with a thiol compound bearing the

sodium sulfonate functionality (Figure 3).

 It should be mentioned that the ratio of the 1st monomer (x) to the 2nd (1-x)

monomer can be controlled by adjusting the reaction parameters. This ratio determines

the % sulfonic acid in the polymer product. Through this polymer synthesis, membrane

fabrication, and evaluation of key PEM properties, this work aims to evaluate the

developed proton conducting membrane as a potential alternative in PEM fuel cells and

water electrolyzers.

 II. EXPERIMENTAL

 a. Post-Polymerization of target sulfonated polynorbornene polymer (SN2)

 A stir bar was added to a 100 mL 2-necked round bottom flask (RBF) and dried

with a heat gun. While the flask was cooling, an RBF heating plate with a controlled

temperature probe was then connected to a standard hot plate. Once the RBF cooled to

room temperature, the alkyl bromo functionalized polymer in a solid powder form, was

added via a funnel. 50 mL of stock THF was then added to the flask and set to stir

Subsequently, DBU (base), was weighed and added to the solution. A fin condenser

connected to an N2 Schlenk line was then assembled. In a separate vial, the thiol and 10

mL of stock DMSO were mixed. Once dissolved, the solution was added dropwise into

the RBF and left to stir overnight at 80 °C.

 b. Polymer precipitation

 The polymer solution was first cooled to room temperature. Subsequently, all

volatiles were removed using vacuum distillation. Cold 1 M HCl (aqueous) was added to

the reaction solution and stirred vigorously until all the brown dispersion had transformed

into a white polymer powdery material. The white polymer was vacuum filtered and

washed with copious amounts of DI water until neutral pH in the resulting filtrate solution.

The polymer was collected and dried under vacuum at 80 °C until a constant weight. The

yield was 2.0 g of polymer (78%).

 c. Membrane casting in petri dish vs film applicator

 For casting the synthesized polymer in a petri dish, a 3 wt.% solution was made

using DMSO as the solvent. The solution was heated at 100 °C and set to stir for one

 8

hour. After, the solution was sonicated for 30 minutes at 80 °C. A good solubility and

viscosity was visualized, so the solution was filtered with a syringe and glass wool on a

petri dish. The petri dish was placed in the oven at 60°C overnight to evaporate the

solvent. Once dry, DI water was added to the petri dish. A humidity chamber was made

using a hot plate and glass cover to promote the detaching of membrane edges from the

petri dish. The full membrane was then stored in a vial with DI water to await

characterization. This protocol was used for further petri dish casting.

 For drop-casting the synthesized polymer with the film applicator, multiple trials

with varying weight percent solutions were tested. First, a 3 wt.% solution was made with

DMSO as the solvent. The resulting solution had very low viscosity, and initial castings

produced <10 µm thick membranes that could not be taken off the glass plate.

Subsequent trials were performed, and 6.5 wt.% was found to be the ideal weight percent

of this polymer solution. Using this solution, different features of the film applicator were

tested to optimize the thickness of the resulting membrane. To achieve membranes

between 30-50 µm thick when wet, the 4 x 4 cm sweeping blade gap should be set to

1250-1300 mm, casting speed set to 2-3 mm/s, and heating plate set to 90 °C. For casting

with the 10 x 10 cm blade, the gap should be set to 1500-1550 mm. After the solvent has

evaporated from the membrane, DI water was used to swell the membrane and help it lift

from the glass plate. The full membrane was then stored in a vial with DI water to await

characterization. This protocol was used for further film applicator casting.

 III. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

 a. Spectroscopic characterization of ionomer

 The targeted sulfonic acid-functionalized polynorbornene polymer was

successfully synthesized via a substitution reaction. This was confirmed through the

spectroscopic characterization of the polymer in its ionomer form. NMR spectroscopy, an

analytical technique used to determine the structure of a compound, was used to show

the disappearance of the carbon-halogen bond in the alkyl bromo functionalized polymer

and the addition of the sulfonic acid in the target polymer. The proton NMR for the parent

polymer showed a distinct CH2 –Br peak around 3.3-3.5 ppm, and the target polymer

showed a characteristic SO2–OH peak around 9.6 ppm. This shows the substitution of

bromine with the sulfonic acid functional group. Additionally, the comparison of both

polymers allows for the determination of % SO3H (52%).

 ATR-FTIR, an analytical technique used to identify chemical bonds within a

compound by producing an infrared absorption spectrum, was also used to show the

substitution of the sulfonic acid functional group. The IR spectra for the target polymer

shows a distinct O–H stretch around 3200 cm-1 as well as characteristic O=S=O stretches,

representing the conversion to the sulfonic acid functional group. These stretches are

absent in the alkyl bromo functionalized polymer.

Red line: ATR-FTIR spectral data for sulfonic acid-functionalized polymer.

 Additionally, determination of the molecular weight distribution (chain size of the

polymer) provides insight into whether a membrane can successfully form, as a molecular

weight of >50 kg/mol is required for polynorbornenes to maintain adequate dimensional

stability.8 The alkyl bromo functionalized polymer was used for weight distribution analysis

and measured via GPC (Figure 6).

 11

Figure 6- GPC spectral data for the alkyl bromo functionalized polymer in its ionomer

form, where THF was used as the eluent.

 The peak visualized in Figure 6 was integrated via GPC software to determine the

molecular weight distribution. The weight-average molecular weight (Mw) was determined

to be 205 kg/mol, the number-average molecular weight (Mn) was determined to be 72

kg/mol, and the resulting polydispersity index (PDI = Mw/Mn) was determined to be 2.85.

These values confirm that the synthesized polymer has sufficient molecular weight

characteristics and can form membranes.

 b. Membrane work-up and characterization

 Optimizing the thickness of a PEM is essential, as it indirectly affects the proton

transport efficiency by increasing or decreasing the resistance of proton conduction.9

During fabrication, the thickness of a membrane can be controlled for different casting

methods. When casting in a petri dish, thickness is controlled by adjusting the amount of

polymer used. When using a film applicator, thickness is controlled by adjusting the blade

gap. Once the membranes had been successfully cast, its thickness was tested using a

 12

thickness meter. The parameters for optimized thickness are around 30-60 µm.10 From

the petri dish, the target polymer membrane thickness when wet was around 35-50 µm,

while membranes cast with the film applicator ranged from 20-50 µm. All measurements

were taken at room temperature. Additionally, as shown in Table 1, key properties of

PEMs - IEC, conductivity, water uptake, and swelling degree - were measured and

analyzed.

 The first membrane property analyzed was the IEC. The theoretical IEC was

calculated from the NMR spectra (Figure 4), and the experimental IEC was measured

using an automatic potentiometric titrator. Table 2 lists a theoretical IEC of 2.2 mmol/g

and an experimental IEC of 2.13 mmol/g. The relative agreement of these values to each

other indicates sufficient quantification of ion exchangeable groups at 52% sulfonic acid

conversion. Proton conductivity (σ) was also evaluated, where the membrane exhibited

an experimental measurement of 70 mS/cm at 80 °C. Further properties, such as water

uptake and swelling degree, were measured at both room temperature and 80 °C. At

room temperature, the water uptake was 17% and the swelling degree was measured at

across a range of temperatures.

 IV. FUTURE WORK

 Future work will focus on further enhancing membrane fabrication using the film

applicator casting method to improve thickness control and mechanical stability of the

membrane. Another objective is to use the film applicator to sufficiently cast a large-scale

membrane (10 cm x 10 cm) for gas permeability testing. Furthermore, the sulfonic acid-

functionalized membranes will be used in electrochemical devices to assess their

performance as proton conducting membranes under electrochemical device operating

conditions. This will help ascertain the practical capabilities of these membranes in real-

world applications.

 V. CONCLUSION

 The purpose of this study was to investigate and characterize a sulfonic acid-

functionalized polynorbornene-based PEM to be used as an alternative to Nafion, through

optimizing its key working properties: IEC, proton conductivity, water uptake, and swelling

degree. A sulfonic acid-functionalized polynorbornene polymer was successfully

synthesized, verified through NMR and ATR-FTIR spectroscopy. GPC results showed

excellent molecular weight distribution for membrane formation. Membranes were

fabricated using two casting methods – a petri dish and film applicator. Using a petri dish,

optimal results were obtained with a 3 wt.% solution, while a 6.5 wt.% solution was ideal

 14

for the film applicator. Favorable film applicator settings were comprised of a 2-3 mm/s

casting speed, 1250-1300 mm blade gap, and 90 °C heating plate. Thickness, a

controlled variable, ranged between 20-50 µm for both the petri dish and film applicator

casting methods. The membranes showed sufficient ion exchange capacity (2.13 mmol/g)

and proton conductivity (70 mS/cm) at 52% sulfonic acid content. The membranes also

demonstrated efficient water uptake and swelling behavior at room temperature and 80

°C. These results reveal the polymer’s potential use as PEMs for future use in

electrochemical devices.
