Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

NREL will assist East Carolina University in its development of the foundational knowledge and
proof of concept that are needed for future process scale up and commercialization of a sustainable
wave-to-water (direct pressurization) desalination unit powered by a wave energy converter.
NREL will provide guidance and support to East Carolina University on its: use of the NREL
developed WEC-Sim based wave to water system, exploring the WEC-Sim/ASPEN Plus data
exchange communication, and using experimental data to hopefully provide validation of
analytical models.

Background:

Seawater desalination is a small but growing part of the global water industry. Most
desalination uses reverse osmosis (RO) or multiple-effect distillation (MED) technologies,
both of which generate a considerable volume of brine waste—more than one gallon of waste
per gallon of clean water produced—which is challenging to treat. Moreover, since most
seawater desalination facilities are usually installed and operated near the ocean (for economic
reasons), the locally elevated salinity from concentrated brine waste discharged during plant
operation creates significant environmental impacts and threatens the marine ecosystem. To
overcome the waste problem, zero-waste discharge desalination processes have been recently
proposed that uses supercritical water (SW) technology. This SW process still requires further
advancement in multiple aspects (e.g., mechanistic understanding of salt precipitation, process
optimization, corrosion control, and scale issues); moreover, the higher energy requirement
per unit volume of water produced compared to conventional RO and MED systems is an
impediment to technology commercialization. The energy consumption of SW desalination is
roughly about 1.15 MJth/kgfeed, while that for conventional RO is between 0.88–1.00
MJth/kgfeed. The focus of the proposed effort is for NREL to assist Participant in Participant’s
development of a sustainable and innovative water desalination system that does not produce
brine waste and can be powered without significantly increasing the cost of clean water
produced. NREL and Participant hypothesize that integrating RO and SW desalination
systems and powering them with renewable energy (solar and wave) using a novel A.I.-based
controller to create the needed consistency in the power source will significantly reduce the
drawbacks of both technologies.

Purpose / Objective:

The main goal is for NREL to assist Participant in the design and development of a sustainable
wave-to-water (direct pressurization) desalination unit powered by a wave energy converter
(WEC) that is (1) operated through a machine learning (ML)-based smart control system and
(2) coupled with innovative zero waste discharge and energy recovery units. The project’s
goal is for the Participant to develop a novel wave-to-water zero discharge desalination system
that uses deep machine learning for multi-process performance optimization, supercritical
CO2 energy recovery, and electricity generation system.

The work hopes to advance knowledge across multiple disciplines to provide the foundation
for the development of a novel wave-to-water zero discharge desalination system that uses
machine learning for multi-process performance optimization, supercritical CO2 energy
recovery, and electricity generation system. The merit of the project lies in the proposed
multistage process integration, ML innovation, and energy recovery intensification. Future
work (beyond the scope of this RFTS) might include prototype building to advance WEC
technology and zero discharge desalination.

Summary of Research Results:

Task Descriptions:

Task 1:

1.1 Participant will design and develop a hydrodynamic model of an oscillating surge wave
energy converter for direct pressurization (Hydrodynamic Model) and carry out baseline
hydrodynamic simulations using AQWA/WAMIT.

1.2 NREL will help Participant team identify early issues related to how accurate the
Hydrodynamic Model represents Participant’s concept.

Per the task description, the NREL team helped the ECU team build a hydrodynamic model based
on the initial concept designed by the ECU team. Initially, the ECU team members also utilized
WAMIT to produce hydrodynamic coefficients. By comparing hydrodynamic coefficients’
resulting from WAMIT solver, we confirmed its agreements, subsequently, transferring this
knowledge to the ECU team so that their team members can build the hydrodynamic model by
themselves.

Figure 1 shows an example of a mesh comparison between two team members when they first
built a mesh model for WAMIT solution. Both the team members also did studies of mesh
convergence (see Appendix A1) to ensure model accuracy. Figure 2 shows an example of
hydrodynamic coefficient comparison between two teams. Overall, the result looks excellent
agreement, except for the output of zero-frequency, which the one of the ECU team has a
unexpected spike of added mass coefficient. By exchanging the input as well as discussion via bi-
weekly meeting, the ECU team resolved the issue due to small rounding differences in the mesh
(*gdf) locations.

Task 2:

2.1 NREL will provide guidance to Participant on how to use NREL’s WEC-Sim numerical
model.

2.2 Participant will model Participant’s concept of a direct pressurized W2W desalination
conversion system (W2W Model) and simulate such system using NREL’s WEC-Sim numerical
model.

2.3 NREL will provide its real-world expertise during Participant’s modeling to guide
Participant so that the Participant may maximize model fidelity between simulation and
prototyping. Through regular meetings with the Participant, NREL will assist Participant with
verification and correct selection of key model variables and characteristics that incorporate
prototype building expertise that will hopefully help Participant streamline technology
development and translate into a higher technical readiness level.

After completing the first set of hydrodynamic modeling, we re-built a simple WEC model which
was based on rotational stiffness and damping coefficients instead of an actual PTO model
(including RO system). As described, this aforementioned step helped the ECU team identify key
design variables including OSWEC geometric parameters (width, thickness, momentum of inertia
(MOI), center of gravity (CG)) and PTO parameters. A time-step-sized convergence study was
also conducted to determine proper timestep size.

While building WEC-Sim model, the NREL team identified a WEC-Sim bug (WEC-Sim version
4.x or below), which a mean offset value of free pitch decay analysis of OSWEC body occurred.
More specifically, this occurred when the WEC-Sim model includes PTO stiffness. Figure 4 shows
an example of WEC-Sim simulation considering pitch free decay analysis of the OSWEC model.
As one can be seen, the time series pitch response has non-zero mean offset values, and it increases
with respect to PTO stiffness. To overcome this issue, the team implemented PTO stiffness using
two alternative options including (1) manually defining PTO stiffness via body hydro stiffness
coefficient or (2) change hydrostatic stiffness in pitch mode of the h5 file. As one can be seen in
Figure 4, the expected mean value was obtained.

Figure 3: Time series of pitch free decay response under different way of PTO stiffness definitions
 (left) full responses (right) zoom-in time window.

As the part of pitch free-decay analysis for the OSWEC device to find a natural frequency of the
system, we also found that the natural frequency of the OSWEC model is varied with different
initial displaced pitch angles. Figure 4 displayed a time series of pitch free decay under different
initial pitch angles.

 Figure 4: Time series of pitch free decay response under different initial displaced pitch angles

To further confirm this interesting observation using WEC-Sim, the NREL conducted high-fidelity
CFD simulations using STAR-CCM+ solvers. An example of CFD simulation output for different
initial pitch angles is graphically displayed in Figure 5. Interestingly, we also observed the same
behavior in which different initial angles result in different natural frequencies for the OSWEC.
As WEC-Sim is based on potential flow theory which assumes small rotation angle, this finding
needs to further validate to confirm simulation result. With the CFD simulation, which can
consider all physical effects including high-nonlinear effect with respect to large initial angle, the
damping effect caused by viscous flow results in faster damping motion. Some result of this work
was presented in the conference paper [1].

Task 3:

3.1 Participant will perform W2W Model simulation and analysis for selected wave conditions
using the Participant’s CFD code Ansys Fluent and provide results to NREL.

3.2 NREL will provide feedback based on the results.

3.3 Participant will make required improvements to the model as per NREL’s team feedback.

3.4 NREL will work with Participant to carry out a verification and validation study, where
Participant’s model will be compared against experimental data. NREL and Participant will
perform an assessment of required model improvements. Based on the verification and
validation study, and assessment NREL will suggest improvements/provide expertise on practical
challenges on the design and integration of the complete system.

The NREL team also supported for helping the ECU teamer member create ANSYS FLUENT
model so that they can run CFD simulations as a part of model verification/validation as well as
extreme event quantification. The preliminary result of this work was presented in the conference
paper [3]. An example of FLUENT results displayed in Figure 6.

Task 4:

4.1 Participant will model and simulate the Participant’s supercritical water system (ASPEN
Model) on ASPEN Plus for brine recycling from R.O. system.

4.2 NREL will help Participant evaluate strategies to determine how to integrate the ASPEN
