From Mechanistic to Compositional Interpretability
Ward Gauderis, Thomas Dooms, Steven T. Homer, Kola Ayonrinde, Geraint A. Wiggins

Abstract
Mechanistic interpretability aims to explain neural model behaviour by reverse-engineering learned computational structure into human-understandable components. Without a formal framework, however, mechanistic explanations cannot be objectively verified, compared, or composed. We introduce compositional interpretability , a category-theoretic framework grounded in the principles of compositionality and minimum description length. Compositional interpretations are pairs of syntactic and semantic mappings that must commute to enforce consistency between a model’s decomposition and its observed behaviour. We deconstruct explanation quality into measures of faithfulness and complexity to cast interpretability as a constrained optimisation problem, and introduce compressive refinement to systematically restructure models into simpler parts without altering their function. Finally, we prove a parsimony criterion under which syntactic compression theoretically guarantees more concise, human-aligned explanations. Our framework situates prominent mechanistic methods as subclasses of refinement, and clarifies why their compressibility heuristics tend to align with human interpretability. Our work provides a measurable, optimisable foundation for automating the discovery and evaluation of mechanistic explanations.

Introduction

Neural models derive their capabilities from emergent complexity rather than explicit design. While they learn sophisticated and meaningful functions, their behaviour is difficult to understand because their internal representations are opaque to humans
(
Ayonrinde & Jaburi 2025
)
. Yet interpreting them could yield new scientific insights
(
Brixi et al. 2025
;
Nainani et al. 2025
;
Pearce et al. 2026
)
and give us greater control over their behaviour
(
Templeton et al. 2024
)
. Doing so requires a measurable definition of interpretation quality that can be tested and optimised
(
Barbiero et al. 2025
;
Madsen et al. 2024
)
. Without it, the resulting explanations cannot systematically be compared or composed.

Interpretability approaches can be seen as spanning a spectrum from intrinsically interpretable models to post-hoc explanations. Post-hoc methods such as saliency and attribution
(
Binder et al. 2016
)
explain individual predictions through surface-level heuristics, but provide little insight into the model’s internal mechanisms
(
Ayonrinde & Jaburi 2025
;
Adebayo et al. 2020
;
Kim et al. 2018
)
. Conversely, intrinsically interpretable models aim to make the structure of the computation transparent by design but require changes to architecture or training
(
Madsen et al. 2024
)
. Mechanistic interpretability sits between these endpoints by seeking to relate observed behaviour to internal features
(
Alain & Bengio 2018
)
or to mechanisms discovered through compressibility heuristics such as sparsity
(
Ayonrinde et al. 2024b
;
Bricken et al. 2023
;
Gao et al. 2024
)
. While the goal is sound, current mechanistic methods approximate faithfulness rather than enforce it structurally.

The standard reverse-engineering view constructs interpretations in three stages: decomposition, description, and validation
(
Sharkey et al. 2025
)
. These correspond to breaking a model into simpler functional parts, assigning them explanatory hypotheses, and testing their correctness, respectively. Decomposition fixes the structural foundation, or syntax, on which the latter two stages operate. If that basis is non-compositional, then description and validation can be locally correct while remaining globally uninformative. With a flawed syntax, even perfectly described parts will not add up to a complete explanation of global behaviour.

To avoid the above shortcomings, we argue that interpretations should be
compositional
, made of individually describable parts whose composition equals the whole;
faithful
, approximating the model’s internal computation rather than merely its input-output behaviour; and
concise
, having low description length for a human interpreter. Compositionality enables bidirectional tracing between local parts and global behaviour, while faithfulness prevents explanations from becoming just-so stories. Together, these criteria cast interpretability as constrained optimisation: among all faithful compositional explanations, prefer the most concise.

This paper formalises interpretability through the notion of a
compositional interpretation
(
Section

2
), which requires grounding a model in both its structure (syntax) and behaviour (semantics). We characterise the quality of such an interpretation by its faithfulness and complexity (
Section

3
), and upper-bound complexity by a tractable measure that can be directly optimised. The optimisation takes the form of
compressive refinement
(
Section

4
), restructuring the model into simpler parts while preserving its semantics.
We show how compressive refinement aligns interpretability under the principles of explanatory optimism and compositional sparsity.
Finally, we situate compositional interpretability relative to other prominent formalisms (
Section

5
). A summary of the formal notation and colour-coding is provided in
Appendix

D
.

2
Defining Compositional Interpretations

The objective of interpreting neural models is rarely specified, leading to ambiguity about what constitutes a valid explanation.
We build on an
explanatory view
of interpretability, which holds that explaining model behaviour requires examining internal mechanisms, not just input-output pairs. This perspective distinguishes between two forms of faithfulness
(
Ayonrinde & Jaburi 2025
)
.

Behavioural faithfulness
means that the explanation and the model produce the same outputs. Post-hoc interpretability methods typically aim for this, describing what a model computes by generating explanations that correlate with its data-local surface behaviour, thereby answering questions like “Why did the model make this particular decision?”
In contrast,
explanatory faithfulness
means the internal steps of the step-by-step explanation match the model’s own mechanisms, not just its input-output behaviour. Mechanistic interpretations pursue this by examining how a model operates and elucidating the mechanisms by which it reaches conclusions. The mechanistic approach addresses broader questions such as “How did the model solve this general class of problems?”, aiming to explain the model’s generalisation capabilities rather than just individual decisions
(
Sharkey et al. 2025
)
.

Definition 2.1

(Explanatory faithfulness)
.

An explanation
E
is
explanatorily faithful
to a model
M
over a data distribution
\mathcal{D}
, to the extent that intermediate activations
s_{i}
at each layer

that are given by an algorithmic explanation
E
closely match the intermediate activations
x_{i}
of the model
M
for input data in
\mathcal{D}

(
Ayonrinde & Jaburi 2025
)
.

Two aspects deserve emphasis. First, explanation quality depends on the interpreter’s goals and abilities. Second, faithfulness is relative to a data distribution and may be approximate. This motivates a non-binary notion of faithfulness, which we develop in
Section

4
. For now, we discuss the structural implications of explanatory faithfulness.

Compositionality

Neural networks expose a limitation of naive reductionism: despite complete knowledge of their low-level components, their emergent behaviour remains opaque, posing a fundamental challenge for interpretability
(
Ayonrinde & Jaburi 2025
)
. Achieving explanatory faithfulness—matching an explanation’s structure to a model’s computational mechanisms—requires decomposing the model into meaningful components. While a model’s layers offer a natural starting point for such a decomposition (
Definition

2.1
), there is no guarantee that these architectural boundaries align with intelligible atoms of computation.
Indeed, because multi-layer computations can be flattened or vice versa
(
Lin et al. 2017
;
Danhofer et al. 2025
)
, architectural depth is an implementation choice. Coupled with the discovery of cross-layer circuits
(
Olsson et al. 2022
;
Lindsey et al. 2024
)
, this suggests the fundamental units of computation are functional, not architectural.
These observations motivate the need for a more general, functionally grounded principle for explanatory faithfulness:
compositionality
, commonly attributed to Frege
(
Coecke 2021
)
.

Definition 2.2

(Compositionality)
.

The meaning of the whole can be derived from the meanings of its parts and how these parts are structured together.

Compositionality describes the relationship between a system’s global properties and those of its components
(
Puca et al. 2023
;
Montague 1970
)
. For interpretability, this enables a divide-and-conquer approach: a model’s behaviour should emerge from the local understanding of its parts (composition), while behaviour must also be traceable back to the contributions of those parts (decomposition)
(
Puca et al. 2023
)
.
However, compositionality is not theory-neutral; it requires a formal theory of both syntax (the parts and their structure) and semantics (their meaning)
(
Hupkes et al. 2020
)
. The central challenge, therefore, is to identify a formal syntax and semantics for a model that is both faithful to its mechanisms and useful for human understanding.

Category theory provides the mathematical language to formalise this distinction between syntax and semantics and to reason about compositional structures
(
Fong & Spivak 2018
;
Dudzik et al. 2022
)
. Compositional methods in AI are largely inspired by fields like quantum mechanics, which shift the focus of study from isolated components to their structured interactions
(
Coecke & Kissinger 2018
)
.
These methods combine explicit structure with distributed representations, helping overcome the curse of dimensionality in model generalisation and interpretation
(
Tull et al. 2024
;
Coecke 2021
;
Hitzler & Sarker 2021
)
.
The application of category theory in AI has already produced unifying frameworks for organising insights across the field
(
Shiebler et al. 2021
;
Dudzik et al. 2022
)
, whose utility has been demonstrated in model design
(
Gavranović et al. 2024
;
Khatri et al. 2024
;
Rodatz et al. 2025
)
and conceptual, causal and generative modelling
(
Tull et al. 2023b
;
Gauderis & Wiggins 2023
;
Lorenz & Tull 2023
;
Tull et al. 2023a
)
.

While any model can be framed compositionally, this is only useful when oriented toward a specific goal

(Compositional Interpretation)
.

Given a compositional model

\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}I_{S}}}:\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}S}}\to\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}H}}

by assigning explanations to the structural components of

•

A semantics-based,

\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}I_{C}}}:\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}C}}\to\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}H}}

by producing explanations based on the behaviour implemented by

For consistent groundings, this diagram must commute

2

:

The commutativity condition,
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}I_{S}}}=\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}I_{C}}}\circ\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}[[\,\cdot\,]]}}

The compositional interpretation
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}I_{S}}}
ties the explanation to the model’s structure, while the post-hoc interpretation
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}I_{C}}}
ties it to the model’s input-output behaviour.
Without this condition, interpretations risk becoming disconnected from the mechanisms they claim to describe.
An interpretation defined solely in terms of semantics, without factoring through a valid syntax, remains causally disconnected from the model’s mechanisms: it describes
what
the model does, not
how
it is done. Consequently, components with distinct implementations might be given identical explanations despite structural differences.
Conversely, an interpretation that is defined solely in terms of syntax labels internal components without verification against their semantics: these labels may not reflect their actual function.
Components with identical semantics might be given different, misleading explanations based on superficial structural distinctions.
By requiring syntax and semantics to agree, a compositional interpretation connects mechanism to behaviour.

In practice, interpretations are partial, meaning they may not account for every component of a model. A
compositionally-interpretable model
admits a complete compositional interpretation
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}I_{S}}}
, where every syntactic component receives a human-understandable explanation.

Tull et al. 2024
show that this definition generalises the existing notion of intrinsically interpretable models, such as decision trees or linear models, which are considered transparent precisely because their explicit structure permits direct and complete interpretation . The central objective of compositional interpretability is therefore

the search for compositional interpretations that are as complete as possible
.

3
Evaluating Compositional Interpretations

Compositionality is only a
structural constraint
: it defines the space of valid explanations but does not distinguish the good from the bad. When offered multiple valid choices, which explanation should be preferred?
We take inspiration from Kowalski’s (
Kowalski 1979
) dictum

\text{Algorithm}=\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\textbf{Logic}}}+\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}\textbf{Control}}}.

In logic programming, the logic component determines the meaning of the algorithm, whereas the control component only affects its implementation. By separating the two, Kowalski argued that computer programs can be improved by freely optimising control for computational efficiency, while the algorithm’s logic must be held constant.
Compositional interpretability mirrors this separation as
3
3

3

This also parallels Marr’s
(
Marr 1982
)
computational and algorithmic levels of analysis, respectively.

\text{Model}=\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\textbf{Semantics}}}+\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}\textbf{Syntax}}}.

We treat the semantics as the fixed logic—the input-output behaviour we aim to understand—and the syntax as the variable control structure that decomposes it. Our objective differs from Kowalski’s only in the target metric: rather than optimise syntax for efficiency, we optimise for intelligibility.

Our constrained optimisation view of interpretability is formalised through the
Minimum Description Length
(MDL) principle
(
Grünwald 2007
)
. In model selection, MDL identifies the best model
{M}
for a dataset
\mathcal{D}
as the one that minimises the total description length
L(\,\cdot\,)
in bits:

L(\mathcal{D})=L(M)+L(\mathcal{D}\mid M).

The total description cost is the length of the model
L(M)
plus the length of the data encoded, given that model
L(\mathcal{D}\mid M)
.
This two-part code operationalises Occam’s razor: all else being equal, prefer the more concise explanation
(
Grünwald 2007
)
.
We apply this principle at a meta-level: interpretability is framed as a constrained communication problem where the goal is to communicate the model’s semantics
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}[[D]]}}
as efficiently as possible in a human-comprehensible language
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}H}}

(
Ayonrinde et al. 2024a
;
Braun et al. 2025
)
. We treat the model’s fixed semantics
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}[[D]]}}
as the data and the compositional interpretation
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}I_{S}}}(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}D}})
as the hypothesis. The description length of the semantics then decomposes as:

L(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}[[D]]}})=\underbrace{L(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}I_{S}}}(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}D}}))}_{\text{Complexity}}+\underbrace{L(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}[[D]]}}\mid\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}I_{S}}}(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}D}}))}_{\text{Faithfulness}}

The first term measures the
complexity
of the explanation. The second quantifies
faithfulness
in the information lost by the explanation. It is the cost of correcting the explanation to match the model’s behaviour.
Formulating interpretability through MDL presents distinct challenges compared to standard machine learning: the hypothesis class potentially spans all human-interpretable statements
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}H}}
, making the complexity of an explanation subjective and dependent on the interpreter’s goals, priors, and computational capabilities. However, unlike inferring a model from noisy empirical data, we have access to the system’s closed-form description. Knowing the ground truth without aleatoric uncertainty allows us to separate the MDL terms. Rather than minimising the sum as a soft trade-off, we treat it as a rate-distortion problem and minimise the explanation’s complexity subject to a strict lower bound on faithfulness.

We thus refine the central criterion of compositional interpretability as
the search for complete compositional interpretations that yield the least complex explanations while remaining faithful up to a user-specified threshold
.
In the remainder of this section, we formalise these measures for compositional interpretations so their optimisation can be automated. Evaluating them naively in the human-interpretable category
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}H}}
would require subjective human involvement. To bypass this, we instead ground them directly in the model’s syntax
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}S}}
and semantics
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}C}}
.

Faithfulness

The
faithfulness measure

L(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}[[D]]}}\mid\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}I_{S}}}(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}D}}))
quantifies the information needed to correct an explanation. Rate-distortion theory operationalises this by bounding a semantic distortion metric
d
(e.g.,
L_{2}
norm or KL-divergence) between the model’s true semantics and those reconstructed from the explanation. However, restricting this bound to the global input-output behaviour
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}[[D]]}}
ensures only behavioural faithfulness, lacking the mechanistic insight required by
Definition

2.1
. While compositional interpretations are structurally well-defined (
Definition

2.4
), they are not inherently informative: describing a component merely as “a mathematical function” is valid but trivial. To ensure explanations capture specific structural mechanisms, we impose the stricter measure of
compositional faithfulness
: the interpretation must be detailed enough to reconstruct the semantics of
each
component that it explains.

\forall\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}f}}\in\text{mor}(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}S}})

d(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}[[\,f\,]]}},\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}I^{*}_{C}}}(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}I_{S}}}(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}f}})))\leq\epsilon

This condition subsumes explanatory and behavioural faithfulness: reconstructing individual component mechanisms necessarily recovers global behaviour upon composition. Consequently, the interpreter can understand the global system by combining local component explanations according to the diagrammatic structure.
The metric
d
defines the semantic reconstruction error relevant to the interpreter, making faithfulness relative rather than absolute. By relaxing
d
to a premetric over specific subdistributions, we ensure faithfulness where it matters while permitting abstraction elsewhere. For example, evaluating
d
only over naturally occurring or task-relevant data captures observational alignment, whereas evaluating it over interventional data ensures the explanations are causally faithful
(
Joshi et al. 2026
)
.

Complexity

The
complexity measure

L(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}I_{S}}}(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}D}}))
of an explanation is inherently more elusive than faithfulness since it depends on the interpreter’s subjective frame of reference. The lack of a universal metric has posed a significant obstacle to automated evaluation of interpretations. However, the dual nature of compositional interpretations offers a tractable surrogate. Just as we operationalised faithfulness in the semantics
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}C}}
instead of
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}H}}
, we bound complexity by the structural derivation of the explanation rather than by the explanation itself. Relying on the factorisation
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}I_{S}}}=\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}I_{C}}}\circ\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}[[\,\cdot\,]]}}
and functoriality of
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}[[\,\cdot\,]]}}
, we define the
compositional description length
: the cost of describing the model’s compositional representation plus the complexity of interpreting its components’ semantics.

Definition 3.2

(Compositional description length)
.

Let
M=(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}D}},\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}S}},\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}C}},\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}[[\,\cdot\,]]}})
be a compositional model and
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}I_{S}}}:\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}S}}\to\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}H}}
a compositional interpretation for
M
. The
compositional description length

L(M,\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}I_{S}}})
of its explanation
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}I_{S}}}(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}D}})
is defined as the sum of its representation and interpretation complexities.

L(M,\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}I_{S}}}):=L^{\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\text{rep}}}}(M)+L^{\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}\text{int}}}}(M,\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}I_{C}}})

Where
representation complexity

L^{\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\text{rep}}}}(M)
is the syntax-based cost of describing the semantics of the individual components in
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}S_{D}}}
and their wiring in
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}D}}
separately.

L^{\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\text{rep}}}}(M):=L(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}D}})+\sum_{\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}f}}\in\text{mor}(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}S_{D}}})}L(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}[[\,f\,]]}})

And
interpretation complexity

L^{\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}\text{int}}}}(M,\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}I_{C}}})
is the cost of describing the post-hoc interpretation method for the individual semantic elements in
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}C_{D}}}
.

L^{\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}\text{int}}}}(M,\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}I_{C}}}):=L(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}I_{C}}}\rvert_{\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}{C}_{D}}}})

The decomposition splits the description length of an explanation into two distinct parts: an objective structural cost and a subjective alignment cost.
The
representation complexity

L^{\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\text{rep}}}}(M)
depends solely on the model. It captures the trade-off between the granularity of the decomposition
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}D}}
and the semantic complexity of the atomic units
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}[[f]]}}
. This makes it a tractable target for automated optimisation.
Conversely, the
interpretation complexity

L^{\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}\text{int}}}}(M,\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}I_{C}}})
isolates the subjective difficulty of aligning the semantics of the atomic units in
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}D}}
with human concepts. This cost is inherently variable, scaling with the conceptual gap
(
Ayonrinde 2025
)
between the components’ semantics relative to the interpreter’s mental model
(
Sutter et al. 2025
)
.
Optimising
L(M,\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}I_{S}}})
therefore balances syntactic parsimony with semantic simplicity: we seek a decomposition that is concisely described yet composed of parts that are naturally aligned with the interpreter.
As
L(M,\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}I_{S}}})
represents the information cost of the compositional derivation, it upper-bounds the theoretical minimal description length
L(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}I_{S}}}(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}D}}))
of the explanation itself.

Proposition 3.3

(Description length bound)
.

Compositional description length
L(M,\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}I_{S}}})
is an upper bound on the minimal description length
L(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}I_{S}}}(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}D}}))
of a model’s explanation.

L(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}I_{S}}}(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}D}}))\leq L(M,\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}I_{S}}})

The proof is deferred to
Appendix

B
.

4
Improving Compositional Interpretations

Since
L(M,\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}I_{S}}})
corresponds to the length of a realisable encoding, it serves as a tractable surrogate to optimise
L(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}I_{S}}}(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}D}}))
. Optimising this bound formalises the natural workflow in mechanistic interpretability: identifying a suitable decomposition, interpreting its components, and verifying their descriptions causally
(
Sharkey et al. 2025
)
. Since structurally modifying a model is more tractable than solving the subjective alignment problem, recent research has predominantly been successful in reducing representation complexity
L^{\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\text{rep}}}}(M)

(
Gao et al. 2025
;
Dunefsky et al. 2024
;
Bushnaq et al. 2025
)
, while interpretation complexity
L^{\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}\text{int}}}}(M,\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}I_{C}}})
remains less explored
(
Sutter et al. 2025
;
Voita & Titov 2020
;
Oldfield et al. 2025
)
. In this work, we align with this strategy, focusing on the first step: finding decompositions that minimise representational cost.
We formalise this process as
syntactic refinement

(
Tull et al. 2024
)
: compressing the model’s representation by optimising the syntax while keeping the semantics fixed.

Definition 4.1

(Syntactic refinement)
.

A
syntactic refinement
of a compositional model
M=(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}D}},\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}S}},\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}C}},\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}[[\,\cdot\,]]}})
into a semantically equivalent model
M^{\prime}=(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}D^{\prime}}},\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}S^{\prime}}},\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}C}},\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}[[\,\cdot\,]]^{\prime}}})
is a functor
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}R}}:\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}S}}\to\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}S^{\prime}}}
such that
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}D^{\prime}}}=\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}R}}(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}D}})

\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}R^{\prime}}}:\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}S^{\prime}}}\to\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}S}}
yields
syntactic abstraction
. By functor composition, one can selectively refine and abstract representations while preserving a trace to the original model.

compressive
.

Compressive refinement
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}R}}
operationalises the redistribution of information from semantic atoms to syntactic structure, subject to
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}[[\,\cdot\,]]}}=\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}[[\,\cdot\,]]^{\prime}}}\circ\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}R}}

into a diagram of simpler sub-components (increased
L(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}D}})
but lower
\sum_{\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}f}}\in\text{mor}(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}S_{D}}})}L(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}[[f]]}})
), the model’s internal regularities become explicit (

). An efficient refinement seeks the optimal granularity where the reduction in component complexity outweighs the cost of describing the additional wiring.

This gives a formal rationale for the search for “atoms of computation”
(
Olah et al. 2020
)
: primitive components that are algebraically simple and structurally explicit. However, mathematical simplicity does not guarantee human understanding. As captured by the interpretation complexity term in
Definition

3.2
, syntactic compression alone is insufficient. A refinement might simplify syntax at the cost of introducing components that are harder to interpret. For instance, a syntactic refinement could decrease representation complexity
L^{\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\text{rep}}}}(M)
by replacing linearly alignable components with nonlinear ones
(
Elhage et al. 2022
)
, thereby increasing interpretation complexity
{L}^{\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}\text{int}}}}(M,\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}I_{C}}})
. Consequently, a compressive refinement improves the explanation only if the reduction in representation complexity outweighs any increase in interpretation complexity. We formalise this necessary condition via the
parsimony criterion
.

Theorem 4.2

(Parsimony criterion for refinement)
.

Let
M^{\prime}
be a compressive refinement of
M
. Let
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}I_{S}}}
and
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}I_{S}^{\prime}}}
be the compositional interpretations of
M
and
M^{\prime}
respectively, with the same semantic grounding
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}I_{C}}}:\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}C}}\to\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}H}}
.
The refined model
M^{\prime}
yields a more parsimonious explanation in terms of compositional description length

L(M^{\prime},\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}I_{S}^{\prime}}})\leq L(M,\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}I_{S}}})

if and only if the gained representation simplicity outweighs the interpretation complexity:

\underbrace{L^{\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\text{rep}}}}(M)-L^{\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\text{rep}}}}(M^{\prime})}_{\text{Decrease in representation complexity}}\geq\underbrace{L^{\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}\text{int}}}}(M^{\prime},\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}I_{C}}})-L^{\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}\text{int}}}}(M,\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}I_{C}}})}_{\text{Increase in interpretation complexity}}

The parsimony criterion underscores that a compressive refinement is not an end in itself but a means to balance two tensions in finding concise compositional explanations:

1.

Representation vs. interpretation complexity:
There is an external risk that optimising the objective representation complexity
L^{\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\text{rep}}}}(M)
comes at the cost of the subjective interpretation complexity
{L}^{\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}\text{int}}}}(M,\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}I_{C}}})
. A concise description is not necessarily easy for a human to interpret.

2.

Wiring vs. component complexity:
Internal to representation complexity, there is a tension between the wiring complexity
L(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}D}})
and the semantic complexity of the individual components
\sum_{\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}f}}\in\text{mor}(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}S_{D}}})}L(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}[[f]]}})
. Decomposing components reduces their individual cost but increases the diagram size
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}D}}
. An exponentially large diagram undermines behavioural faithfulness, as the interpreter cannot tractably compose all interactions.

While these trade-offs suggest that naively optimising
L^{\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\text{rep}}}}(M)
alone could potentially lead to uninterpretable model decompositions, practical evidence indicates that compression often yields interpretable results
(
Gao et al. 2025
;
Dunefsky et al. 2024
;
Braun et al. 2025
;
Hinton & Salakhutdinov 2006
)
. We argue that under compressive refinements, the two pressures described above are typically aligned rather than adversarial, a convergence we attribute to two fundamental principles, as follows.

Representation vs. interpretation complexity

To resolve the first tension between representation and interpretation complexity, we rely on the intuition that the regularities a model exploits to solve a task often correspond to concepts humans use to understand that task. This intuition is captured by the
Weak Principle of Explanatory Optimism
, a conjecture implicit in the whole field of interpretability
(
Ayonrinde & Jaburi 2025
)
.

Conjecture 4.3

(Weak principle of explanatory optimism)
.

Most important behaviour of an intelligence with implicit explanatory knowledge within some explanatory complexity class is human-understandable
(
Ayonrinde & Jaburi 2025
)
.

Since structure is fundamentally linked to compressibility, compressive refinement acts as a filter for these regularities. Lossy compression that retains only the most important model behaviour (measured by
d
in
Definition

4.1
) strips away redundancy to isolate the core mechanisms. The conjecture implies that if the model performs well, these isolated mechanisms are human-understandable.
To ensure that the model’s core computational structure actually aligns with human concepts, we formalise this explanatory optimism as a Bayesian prior. In the MDL framework, representation complexity is explicitly defined by a coding distribution:
L^{\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\text{rep}}}}(M):=-\log_{2}P^{\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\text{rep}}}}(M)

(
Grünwald 2007
)
. By choosing a
P^{\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\text{rep}}}}(M)
that encodes our prior
belief
in explanatory optimism, assigning higher probability to representations we expect to be interpretable (such as those exhibiting sparsity, low dimensionality, or hierarchy), we actively align representation complexity with interpretation complexity.

Corollary 4.4

(Comonotonic coding)
.

Any compressive refinement satisfies the parsimony criterion (
Theorem

4.2
) if its coding distribution
P^{\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\text{rep}}}}(M)
is
comonotonic
with the induced interpretation distribution:

P^{\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\text{rep}}}}(M)\leq P^{\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\text{rep}}}}(M^{\prime})\implies 2^{-L^{\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}\text{int}}}}(M,\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}I_{C}}})}\leq 2^{-L^{\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}\text{int}}}}(M^{\prime},\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}I_{C}}})}

Consequently, the closer our chosen coding distribution captures actual interpretation complexity, the more reliably a compressive refinement improves the full compositional description length
7
7

7

luckiness function
within the Normalised Maximum Likelihood framework

)
. This replaces the subjective Bayesian

. By explicitly adopting a comonotonic coding distribution, compressive refinement is guaranteed to yield more concise explanations.

Wiring vs. component complexity

To address the second tension between wiring and component complexity, ensuring that the string diagram
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}D}}
remains manageable through syntax refinement, we invoke a result from approximation theory regarding the nature of the functions neural networks can approximate.
Compositional sparsity
ensures that the search for “atoms of computation” is feasible. The graph size
L(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}D}})
is expected to grow only polynomially because the semantics approximated by the model admit a sparse decomposition.

Theorem 4.5

(Compositional sparsity)
.

Deep (neural) networks of polynomial size efficiently approximate compositionally sparse functions. A function is
compositionally sparse
if it can be decomposed into polynomially many constituent functions, each depending on a small, constant number of variables. This class includes all efficiently Turing-computable functions
(
Danhofer et al. 2025
)
.

Together, the operationalised principles of explanatory optimism and compositional sparsity justify minimising representation complexity as a tractable surrogate for the full description length. Compressive refinement distils the model into a sparse syntax with concise semantics that are aligned with human concepts. Therefore, compressive refinement yields better compositionally interpretable models.

5
Related Work

Mechanistic interpretability

Existing methods use various syntactic refinements
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}R}}:\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}S}}\to\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}S^{\prime}}}
(
Definition

4.1
), differing in how tightly
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}S^{\prime}}}
must track the given
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}S}}
. Early work often used the identity refinement
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}R}}=\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}\textnormal{id}_{S}}}
, treating architectural units such as neurons as the atoms of analysis
(
Olah et al. 2017
)
. Contemporary research is systematically moving to stronger decomposition classes, ranging from piecewise substitution
(
Bricken et al. 2023
)
, to relying on architectural invariants
(
Lindsey et al. 2024
)
, or disregarding the original syntax altogether
(
Gao et al. 2025
)
. Compositional interpretability makes this balance explicit and provides a parsimony criterion for decomposition selection. A more detailed comparison can be found in
Appendix

C
.

Measurable interpretability

To bridge the traditional intrinsic and post-hoc interpretability paradigms,
Madsen et al. 2024
propose
inherently faithfulness-measurable models
. Rather than imposing inflexible constraints that directly generate explanations, such models allow efficient evaluation of explanation faithfulness by design. When a measurable target metric exists, one can search directly for metric-optimal explanations rather than rely on heuristic proxies like sparsity or low-rankness
(
Zhou & Shah 2023
)
.
Compositional interpretability extends this principle to mechanistic explanations, but recognises that any metric of faithfulness must be balanced against a measure of complexity. Only when both criteria are efficiently measurable can the discovery of interpretable decompositions be cast as a standard optimisation procedure.

Symmetry-formalised interpretability

Barbiero et al. 2026
propose defining actionable interpretability through invariance under four symmetries. These strict constraints naturally guide the design of intrinsically interpretable models
(
Barbiero et al. 2025
)
, but are difficult to apply to mechanistic or post-hoc interpretations of black-box models, where such symmetries are not guaranteed to hold.
Compositional interpretability instead builds on two fundamental principles: compositionality and MDL for explanation grounding and selection, respectively. These two principles recover analogous desiderata:
inference equivariance
is implied by the functoriality of compositional interpretations,
information invariance
by minimisation of representation complexity via compressive refinement,
concept-closure invariance
by minimisation of interpretation complexity, and
structural invariance
by separation of syntax from semantics. Replacing symmetries with an MDL objective yields a spectrum of compositionally interpretable models that also includes non-interpretable models. In this spectrum, actionable interpretability is reframed from an architectural constraint into a continuous optimisation objective.

Causal abstraction

Geiger et al. 2025
frame mechanistic interpretability as causal alignment between low-level model decompositions and high-level explanations. While rigorous for top-down hypothesis testing, this approach assumes the high-level explanation and the decomposition search space are known a priori
(
Geiger et al. 2024
)
. Moreover,
Sutter et al. 2025
note that without assuming linear representations, this alignment becomes unidentifiable: any arbitrary high-level explanation can be mapped onto any low-level decomposition.
Compositional interpretability focuses instead on the antecedent step of structural decomposition. It instead searches bottom-up for simple decompositions from which candidate algorithms can be extracted. Its formulation is directly compatible with causal abstraction and inference
(
Lorenz & Tull 2023
;
Lorenz & Tull 2026
)
: our definition of syntactic refinement aligns with the stronger notion of
strict component-level abstraction
, and our formulation of compositional faithfulness can incorporate causal validity through the semantic distortion measure
d
. As such, compressive refinement supplies the candidate decompositions and algorithms required for distributed alignment search.

6
Conclusion

Summary

This paper establishes a formal foundation for mechanistic interpretability based on the first principles of compositionality and minimum description length. We argue that interpretability methods should explicitly aim to make models
compositionally interpretable
, and that reducing an explanation’s description length provides a quantifiable objective to improve its quality. We show how structural measures of
faithfulness
and
complexity
can serve as tractable surrogates for minimising compositional description length. Finally, we demonstrate that
compressive refinement
generalises current mechanistic approaches to neural network decomposition and supply a parsimony criterion under which minimising representation complexity is guaranteed to produce better compositional explanations.

Future work

We discuss three directions to translate these theoretical foundations into practical tools for interpretation.

•

We focus on decomposition; the description and validation stages of the reverse-engineering workflow
(
Sharkey et al. 2025
)
require separate treatment, and integrating compressive refinement with causal inference
(
Joshi et al. 2026
)
is a natural next step.

•

Automated compressive refinement requires faithfulness and complexity to be efficiently measurable and optimisable. Tensor networks could match these requirements, and early results suggest they can tractably scale these objectives
(
Dooms et al. 2024
;
Hamreras et al. 2025
)
.

•

While allowing subjective priors, standard complexity assumes unbounded computational capacity, poorly modelling humans. Epiplexity
(
Finzi et al. 2026
)
proposes a bounded-observer alternative to better capture extractable structure.

Impact Statement

This paper presents work whose goal is to advance the field of Machine
Learning. There are many potential societal consequences of our work, none of
which we feel must be specifically highlighted here.

String diagrams

)
are a category-theoretic tool for visually describing compositional structures and formally reasoning about them. They place the composition of processes, representing the flow of information, at the centre of analysis. By linking topology to algebra, intuitive visual manipulations directly correspond to formal algebraic proofs of behavioural equivalence, with the mathematical details hidden from view. Diagrams can be manipulated via equational rules,
summarised by the principle: “Only connectivity matters”
(
Coecke & Kissinger 2018
)
.

Definition A.1

(String Diagram)
.

A
string diagram

\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}D}}
is a graphical representation of a composite process (formally a morphism
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}D}}:\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}I}}\to\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}O}}
in a monoidal category
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}S}}
) characterised by:

•

Wires
representing types (objects
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}A}},\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}B}},\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}C}},\ldots\in\text{Obj}(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}S}})
);

•

Boxes
representing processes between types (morphisms
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}M}}:\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}A}}\to\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}B}}\in\text{Hom}_{\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}S}}}(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}A}},\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}B}})
).

Diagrams are composed via:

•

Sequential composition:
Connecting output wires of one box to matching input wires of another vertically (composition of morphisms
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}M}}:\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}A}}\to\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}B}}
and
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}N}}:\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}B}}\to\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}C}}
gives
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}N}}\circ\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}M}}:\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}A}}\to\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}C}}
);

•

Parallel composition:
Placing boxes side-by-side horizontally (monoidal product of morphisms
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}M}}:\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}A}}\to\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}C}}
and
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}N}}:\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}B}}\to\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}D}}
gives
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}M}}\otimes\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}N}}:\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}A}}\otimes\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}B}}\to\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}C}}\otimes\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}D}}
)

String diagrams further allow defining
states
as boxes with no inputs,
effects
as boxes with no outputs, and
scalars
as boxes with neither inputs nor outputs.

Appendix B
Proof of the description length bound

Proof.

Proof of
Proposition

3.3

By
Definition

2.4

L(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}I_{S}}}(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}D}}))=L(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}I_{C}}}(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}[[\,D\,]]}}))

The description length of a mapping’s output is bounded by the sum of the description length of the mapping and its input (information non-increase). Therefore

L(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}I_{C}}}(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}[[\,D\,]]}}))\leq L(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}[[\,D\,]]}})+L(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}I_{C}}}\rvert_{\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}{C}_{D}}}})

Because, by
Definition

2.3
,
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}[[\,\cdot\,]}}]
is a structure-preserving (monoidal) functor, the description length of the semantic object
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}[[\,D\,]]}}
is bounded by the sum of the description lengths of its constituent parts and their arrangement (information subadditivity)

L(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}[[\,D\,]]}})\leq L(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}D}})+\sum_{\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}f}}\in\text{mor}(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}S_{D}}})}L(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}[[\,f\,]]}})

Combining these two inequalities yields

L(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}I_{S}}}(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}D}}))\leq L(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}D}})+\sum_{\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}f}}\in\text{mor}(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}S_{D}}})}L(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}[[\,f\,]]}})+L(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}I_{C}}}\rvert_{\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}C_{D}}}})=L^{\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\text{rep}}}}(M)+L^{\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}\text{int}}}}(M,\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}I_{C}}})

The right-hand side matches the definition of
L(M,\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}I_{S}}})
in
Definition

3.2
, proving that
L(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}I_{S}}}(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.9258,0.6016,0.25}D}}))\leq L(M,\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.5977,0.7617,0.2969}I_{S}}})
.

Appendix C
Comparison of interpretability methods

Mechanistic interpretability methods trade off the scope of refinement against traceability to the original model: a broader refinement space can expose more natural atoms of computation, but at the cost of losing that trace.

lists common methods along this spectrum, with a representative example for each category.
