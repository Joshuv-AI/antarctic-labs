Skeletal transformation to chiral nanocarbon molecules
Authors: Junichiro Hirano, Tomoyuki Ikai, Shinpei Kusaka, Ryotaro Matsuda, Hiroshi Shinokubo, Norihito Fukui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Three-dimensional nanocarbon molecules are crucial building blocks for advanced carbon materials. However, the dependence of current synthetic methods on stepwise bond-forming approaches limits the available chemical space in this field. Here, we demonstrate that a skeletal-transformation approach solves two challenges in nanocarbon synthesis. Firstly, the inner-bond cleavage of π-conjugated hydrocarbons provides access to a ten-membered ring that exclusively contains sp2-hybridized carbons. The subsequent ring-expansion affords three gigantic decagon-containing chiral nanocarbon molecules with figure-eight or bathtub conformations consisting of up to 170 sp2-hybridized carbons. Secondly, the subsequent reformation of an internal double bond in the structure is applicable to two of three obtained nanocarbon molecules, which enables the regio- and enantio-selective synthesis of a helically twisted nanographene containing up to 26 six-membered rings. The crystal-packing structure of this chiral nanographene is characterized by a homochiral porous framework consisting of π-stacked double-helical assemblies. These results demonstrate that the skeletal-transformation approach, which has so far targeted bioactive molecules, can be applied to nanocarbon synthesis.

Introduction

After the discovery of fullerene in 1985, the field of nanocarbon chemistry has rapidly grown and currently represents the mainstream of materials science. Bottom-up approaches based on sophisticated organic syntheses have proved their effectiveness in accessing various carbon materials such as fullerenes, carbon nanotubes, and graphene nanoribbons (Fig. 1a)1–6. Furthermore, more complex three-dimensional nanocarbon structures, which represent substructures of more elaborate carbon materials, including graphene moiré superlattices, helical graphene, toroidal nanocarbon, and Mackay crystals, have been developed over the last decade7–20. However, the accessible chemical space in contemporary nanocarbon synthesis has been unconsciously limited because the retrosynthesis of target nanocarbon materials is based on stepwise bond formation between precursor fragments.Fig. 1Background information and the objectives of this study.a Conventional nanocarbon synthesis via a stepwise bond-formation approach. b Synthesis of a ten-membered macrocycle via cyclo-oligomerization. c Asymmetric synthesis of gigantic chiral nanocarbon structures. d Skeletal-transformation approach to obtain chiral nanocarbon molecules.

One challenging synthetic target for the stepwise bond-formation approach is nonplanar gigantic nanocarbon structures that contain medium-sized sp2-hybridized carbon rings with more than eight carbon atoms. The construction of such distorted rings usually suffers from low selectivity in the cyclization step (Fig. 1b)21–23. This low efficiency hinders further transformation of the resulting medium-sized rings into unconventionally distorted nanocarbon molecules. Notably, decagon-containing π-systems remain rare, although the all-sp2-hybridized-carbon 10-membered ring represents a key fragment of imaginary carbon materials such as pollux24. Another challenge for the stepwise bond-formation approach is the asymmetric synthesis of chiral nanocarbon molecules. The last three decades have witnessed an extensive development of asymmetric syntheses of chiral polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons25–29. However, the enantioselective synthesis of gigantic chiral nanocarbon structures has been limited to two recent pioneering reports employing a rhodium-catalyzed [2 + 2 + 2] cycloaddition or the hydrogenation of carbonyl groups followed by an intramolecular Friedel–Crafts alkylation (Fig. 1c)30–32.

Here, we demonstrate that the skeletal transformation via the cleavage and reformation of internal double bonds of π-conjugated hydrocarbons effectively solves the aforementioned challenges in nanocarbon synthesis (Fig. 1d). The development of skeletal transformations has recently induced a paradigm shift in the retrosynthesis of natural and pharmaceutical products33,34. However, a similar strategy for the synthesis of nanocarbon molecules, apart from the molecular surgery of fullerene, remains underexplored35,36.

Results

Synthesis and structure of decagon-containing nanocarbons

The current study is based on our recent finding that the oxidative inner-bond cleavage of readily available dibenzo[g,p]chrysenes (DBCs) enables the concise, large-scale, and enantioselective synthesis of figure-eight π-conjugated molecules known as cyclobisbiphenylenecarbonyls (CBBCs)37,38. Alternatively, this transformation can be regarded as a rational protocol to construct an all-sp2-carbon 10-membered ring. Herein, with this idea in mind, we synthesized three π-extended CBBC derivatives, i.e., PhenCBBC 1, DBCCBBC 2, and HBCCBBC 3, in which the original benzene rings of the CBBC core were transformed to phenanthrene (Phen), DBC, and hexa-peri-hexabenzocoronene (HBC) moieties, respectively (Fig. 1d).

The synthesis of 1–3 is summarized in Fig. 2. The Suzuki–Miyaura cross-coupling of 3,6,11,14-tetrabromo CBBC 5 with 2-formylphenylboronic acid afforded the coupling product 6 in 70% yield. The Wittig reaction of 6 with (methoxymethyl)triphenylphosphonium chloride and a subsequent benzannulation provided PhenCBBC 1 in 34% yield over two steps (76% yield per Phen unit). While the standard benzannulation reaction employs a bismuth catalyst39, following optimization, we selected a gold catalyst for our reaction (see Supplementary Table S1). Intermediate 6 was transformed to DBCCBBC 2 based on a protocol by Jin and co-workers40. Specifically, the condensation of 6 with 2,7-di-tert-butylfluorene (7), followed by an oxidative ring expansion with CuCl, tert-butyl peroxybenzoate, and trifluoroacetic acid (TFA), furnished DBCCBBC 2 in 20% yield over two steps (67% yield per DBC unit). The synthesis of HBCCBBC 3 was accomplished using the general HBC synthesis41. A Sonogashira coupling of brominated CBBC 5 with 4-tert-butylphenylacetylene afforded alkynylated 9 in 69% yield. The four-fold microwave-assisted [4 + 2] cycloaddition of 9 with 2,3,4,5-tetrakis(4-(tert-butyl)phenyl)cyclopenta-2,4-dien-1-one (10) provided adduct 11 in 82% yield. Finally, a Scholl reaction of 11 with DDQ and trifluoromethanesulfonic acid (TfOH) in CH2Cl2 furnished HBCCBBC 3 in 11% yield via the formation of 24 carbon–carbon bonds (58% yield per HBC unit; 91% yield per bond).Fig. 2Synthesis of π-extended CBBCs.Transformations from brominated CBBC 5 to PhenCBBC 1, DBCCBBC 2, and HBCCBBC 3. We have also subjected enantiomerically pure brominated CBBC (P,P)-5 (>99% ee) to the above transformations, which yielded π-extended CBBCs (P,P)-1 and (P,P)-2 without any loss of enantiomeric excess (see Supplementary Figs. S3, S4). Considering that we reported the asymmetric synthesis of 5 in 80% ee37, these transformations represent the formal asymmetric synthesis of chiral nanocarbon molecules. Unfortunately, the synthesis of 3 involved heating at 280 °C, which exceeded the racemization barrier of CBBC and prevented the stereoretentive synthesis from enantiopure precursor 537.

The X-ray crystal structures of 1–3 are shown in Fig. 3a. These π-extended CBBC molecules exhibit gigantic π-surfaces consisting of 58, 106, and 170 sp2-hybridized carbon atoms, respectively. PhenCBBC 1 and HBCCBBC 3 adopt a figure-eight conformation, similar to that of non-substituted CBBC 12. On the other hand, DBCCBBC 2 adopts a bathtub conformation, which represents a metastable conformation of CBBC 1242. The unit cell of HBCCBBC 3 contains two crystallographically inequivalent molecules as a racemic pair. Importantly, the four HBC units of HBCCBBC 3 overlap to afford two π-stacked pairs with π–π distances of 3.48–3.74 Å (see Supplementary Figs. S62, S63; Table S2). Owing to the figure-eight backbone of the CBBC core, the pairs of HBC units are piled to form twisted bilayer arrangements with twisting angles of 7–34° (see Supplementary Figs. S60, S61), which is reminiscent of graphene moiré superlattices43. The noncovalent interaction (NCI) plots for HBCCBBC 3 indicate the presence of van der Waals interactions between the stacked HBC units, while non-substituted CBBC 12 exhibits negligible intramolecular interactions (Fig. 3b). The major intermolecular interactions in the crystal packings of DBCCBBC 2 and HBCCBBC 3 involve CH–π interactions with tert-butyl groups and π–π interactions with co-crystalized C6F6 (see Supplementary Figs. The conformation of 1–3 in solution was evaluated using variable-temperature 1H NMR measurements (see Supplementary Figs. S36–S38). At room temperature, 1–3 exhibit symmetric NMR patterns, in which the four polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon units (Phen for 1; DBC for 2; HBC for 3) are equivalent. The spectra of PhenCBBC 1 and HBCCBBC 3 remain symmetric even at –40 °C, suggesting that they adopt a preferred figure-eight conformation. In contrast, the spectrum of DBCCBBC 2 becomes broad at –40 °C and less symmetric at –70 °C, whereby the signals due to the tert-butyl groups appear as four singlets. These results indicate that DBCCBBC 2 undergoes fast interconversion between the stable bathtub conformation and a metastable figure-eight conformation. The activation free energy at the coalescence temperature (–30 °C) was estimated to be 11 kcal mol–1. The predominance of the bathtub conformation of DBCCBBC 2 was corroborated via density functional theory (DFT) calculations at the B3LYP-D3(BJ)/6-311 G(d,p)//B3LYP/6-31 G(d) level, indicating that the bathtub conformation is by 11.1 kcal mol–1 more stable than the figure-eight conformation. It should be noted that the structural optimization at the B3LYP‑D3(BJ)/6‑31G(d) level afforded an implausible geometry featuring unrealistically close π‑stacking between the DBC segments, which is significantly deviated from the X-ray structure, probably due to the overestimation of dispersion interactions. The above simulated energy difference is subject to quantitative uncertainty due to the inherent difficulty in accurately estimating dispersion forces of gigantic nanocarbon molecules. The destabilization of the figure-eight conformation was attributed to the steric hindrance around the extended π-surfaces.

The enantiomers of PhenCBBC 1, DBCCBBC 2, and HBCCBBC 3 were separated using chiral high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). Because the stereoretentive synthesis of 3 from enantiopure precursor 5 was impossible (see above), the configuration of 3 was determined by simulation of the circular dichroism (CD) spectrum using time-dependent DFT (TD-DFT) calculations (see Supplementary Fig. S76). Then, the racemization barriers of the π-extended derivatives 1–3 were evaluated. For this purpose, the enantiomers of 1, 2, and 3 were heated to 443 K in 1,2,4-trichlorobenzene, and the decrease of the CD signals was monitored (Fig. 4a). Non-substituted CBBC 12 undergoes racemization at 443 K with a half-life time of 1.5 h. The CD intensity of 1 and 2 slowly decreases with half-life times of 3.5 and 36 h, respectively. Furthermore, the CD intensity of HBCCBBC 3 remained constant. Even upon further increasing the temperature to 553 K, no detectable decrease in CD intensity was observed (Fig. S87). These results demonstrate the excellent configurational stability of 3. It is worth noting that the resolution of chiral nanocarbon structures containing seven- and eight-membered rings is often impossible due to fast racemization, even at room temperature7,44. We further analyzed the racemization behaviors of PhenCBBC 1, DBCCBBC 2, and non-substituted 1237 by determining their activation enthalpies (ΔH‡) and entropies (ΔS‡) (Figs. S85 and S86 and Table S5). No statistically significant difference has been observed in ΔH‡ (PhenCBBC 1: 27.4 ± 1.2 kcal mol–1, DBCCBBC 2: 29.4 ± 1.7 kcal mol–1, CBBC 12: 29.9 ± 0.9 kcal mol–1), whereas the ΔS‡ values of 1 (−18.6 ± 2.7 cal mol–1 K–1) and 2 (−18.1 ± 1.7 cal mol–1 K–1) are substantially more negative than that of 12 (−11.2 ± 2.0 cal mol–1 K–1). Our previous DFT simulations suggested that racemization of CBBC 12 proceeds via inward rotation of the two carbonyl groups in opposite directions, leading to an untwisted transition‑state conformation resembling that of DBC. In such a transition state, the fused phenanthrene and DBC units in PhenCBBC 1 and DBCCBBC 2, respectively, cause no significant steric repulsion, which is consistent with the negligible change in activation enthalpy. In contrast, the more negative activation entropies of 1 and 2 rather than 12 are attributed to the larger π‑surfaces, which result in reduced conformational freedom and increased solvation in the transition state. These results highlight the effective role of π-extension, resulting in the entropy-driven enhancement of configurational persistency.

Electronic properties of the decagon-containing nanocarbon molecules

The UV/vis absorption and fluorescence spectra of PhenCBBC 1, DBCCBBC 2, HBCCBBC 3, and CBBC 12 in CH2Cl2 are shown in Fig. 4b. The absorption tails of 1, 2, 3, and 12 reach approximately 410, 480, 550, and 360 nm, respectively, and are red-shifted by the π-extended structures of the molecules. CBBC 12 is non-emissive under ambient conditions due to the intersystem crossing37. On the other hand, the π-extended derivatives 1, 2, and 3 emit fluorescence at 472, 514, and 553 nm, respectively, with quantum yields of 1.5%, 9.0%, and 3.6% as well as lifetimes of 1.5, 3.6, and 4.1 ns, respectively (see Supplementary Fig. S75). The radiative/nonradiative decay constants calculated from these experimental values are 1.0 × 107 s–1/6.6 × 108 s–1 for 1, 2.5 × 107 s–1/2.5 × 108 s–1 for 2, and 8.8 × 106 s–1/2.4 × 108 s–1 for 3. These results indicate that π-extension suppresses nonradiative decay, which is attributable to the restricted molecular motion in the excited state arising from the effective intramolecular dispersion forces between the large π-surfaces.

The CD and circularly polarized luminescence (CPL) spectra of the enantiomers of PhenCBBC 1 are shown in Fig. 4c and appear as mirror images of each other. The CD spectra consist of three intense peaks at 335, 305, and 272 nm as well as a broad peak around 400 nm. The CPL spectra fall in a range between 420 and 630 nm. Importantly, the highest dissymmetry factors gCD and gCPL are 1.4 × 10–2 (419 nm) and 1.8 × 10–2 (484 nm), respectively, which are ~10 times higher than those of conventional chiral organic molecules45. The high g values of 1 were attributed to the parallel arrangement of the transition electric and magnetic dipole moments in the D2-symmetric structure46. On the other hand, the other π-extended CBBC derivatives 2 and 3 emit CPL with gCPL values of 3.4 × 10–3 and 5.6 × 10–3, respectively (see Supplementary Figs. S72 and S73).

The cyclic voltammograms of PhenCBBC 1, DBCCBBC 2, HBCCBBC 3, and CBBC 12 were measured in CH2Cl2 using the ferrocene/ferrocenium couple (Fc/Fc+) as an external reference (Figs. 4d and S83). The redox potentials were obtained by differential pulse voltammetry (Fig. S84). Non-substituted CBBC 12 exhibits no redox waves between –0.2 and 1.8 eV. On the other hand, PhenCBBC 1, DBCCBBC 2, and HBCCBBC 3 are redox-active. PhenCBBC 1 exhibits an irreversible oxidation wave around 1.3 V. DBCCBBC 2 and HBCCBBC 3 exhibit three and four reversible oxidation waves (0.81, 0.95, and 1.05 V for 2; 0.50, 0.70, 0.86, and 0.96 V for 3), respectively, which highlights their multi-charge redox capability induced by their π-extended structures. The first oxidation potential of 3 is considerably lower than that of monomeric HBC (0.92 V), suggesting the presence of effective intramolecular interactions, including direct conjugation and through-space interaction between the HBC units47.

Inner-bond reformation to form double-helical nanographene

Next, we examined the reformation of the internal double bond of DBCCBBC 2 via McMurry coupling (Fig. 5a)48,49. The treatment of racemic DBCCBBC 2 with TiCl4 and metallic zinc in THF at 65 °C afforded π-extended double helicene 4 in 81% yield. This result stands in sharp contrast to the conventional peripheral-expansion strategy based on stepwise bond formation. Specifically, the attempted direct synthesis of 4 from precursor 13 afforded a trace amount of 4. Although the accurate yield of 4 could not be determined due to the presence of various by-products (see Supplementary Fig. S9), the estimated yield is 3% at best. We also examined the inner-bond reformation of PhenCBBC 1 and HBCCBBC 3. The reaction of 1 yielded poorly soluble solids. To address this issue, we synthesized the tert-butyl substituted derivative 1’ (see Supplementary Fig. S77). The inner-bond reformation of 1’ proceeded efficiently, affording the corresponding product 14 in 89%. In contrast, the attempted inner-bond reformation of HBCCBBC 3 was unsuccessful, with most of the starting material recovered. This outcome is likely attributable to steric hindrance, as the expected product adopts extended π-surfaces at the sterically congested bay regions of the DBC core.

We also subjected enantiomerically pure DBCCBBC (P,P)-2 ( > 99% ee) to the bond-reformation strategy (Fig. 5a). The McMurry coupling was conducted at 30 °C in order to suppress the racemization of (P,P)-4 (half-life times: 1.7 h at 80 °C and 66 h at 30 °C; See Supplementary Fig. S78) and furnished (P,P)-4 in 63% yield with 94% ee. The slight decrease in the enantiomeric excess from 99% ee was attributed to racemization during the reaction. However, a further decrease in the reaction temperature decelerated the generation of (P,P)-4. As noted above, brominated CBBC 5 was synthesized by asymmetric glycol cleavage37, and the transformation of its enantiomer (P,P)-5 (>99% ee) to DBCCBBC (P,P)-2 proceeded without any loss of enantiomeric excess (see Supplementary Fig. S4). Overall, we have formally achieved the asymmetric synthesis of a sterically crowded helical nanographene molecule via a skeletal-transformation approach.

Recrystallization of racemic 4 afforded a single crystal suitable for X-ray diffraction analysis. In the crystal, 4 adopts a quasi-D2-symmetric double-helicene structure with a helical turn number (Nh) of 1.13 (Fig. 5b). The centroid distance between the two terminal benzene rings (d) is 4.08 Å. Each helical coil of 4 consists of 10 benzene rings, which is longer than those of previously reported multiple helicenes50. The crystallization resulted in the spontaneous resolution of the enantiomers. Consequently, the unit cell consists of only one enantiomer of 4 with the P3221 space group. Interestingly, the crystal packing structure shows unique one-dimensional chiral pores with an inscribed-cylinder diameter of 9 Å and the largest width of 17 Å. One pore is surrounded by double-helical assemblies consisting of π-stacked 4, in which six molecular units form a helical pitch with a pitch length of 35 Å. The crystallinity was persistent even after the removal of the incorporated solvent molecules (see Supplementary Figs. S79–S82). The polycrystalline samples of 4 adsorbed CO2 with a sufficient uptake of ca. 110 mL (STP) g–1 at 1 bar and 195 K, confirming the presence of permanent porosity (Fig. 5c). Porous assemblies based on π-stacking, referred to as π-stacked organic frameworks (πOFs), have recently emerged as a rapidly growing research area51. However, homochiral πOFs featuring chiral nanospace remain underexplored. Herein, we demonstrate that a helical nanographene serves as a promising building block for the construction of such homochiral πOFs.

Compound 4 exhibits yellowish-green fluorescence at 480 nm with a moderate quantum yield of 22% (see Supplementary Fig. S71). The separated enantiomers show mirror image CD and CPL spectra with dissymmetry factors gCD and gCPL of 1.6 × 10–3 (284 nm) and 9.8 × 10–4 (467 nm), respectively (see Supplementary Fig. S74).

Discussion

We synthesized three decagon-containing nanocarbon molecules, i.e., PhenCBBC 1 DBCCBBC 2 and HBCCBBC 3. The successful synthesis of 1–3 is based on the efficient formation of an all-sp2-carbon 10-membered ring via the oxidative inner-bond cleavage of DBC. The π-extended CBBC derivatives 1–3 exhibit various interesting properties such as unusual nonplanar structures (i.e., figure-eight and bathtub conformations), excellent configurational stability, multi-charge redox capability (up to four charges), and efficient CPL (gCPL up to 1.8 × 10–2). Particularly, the investigation on the configurational stability of 1 and 2 revealed the entropy-driven enhancement of configurational persistency due to the large π-surfaces. Furthermore, the reformation of the internal double bond of DBCCBBC 2 achieved the asymmetric synthesis of chiral nanographene 4, which is extremely difficult to synthesize via a conventional peripheral-expansion strategy, even as a racemate. The crystal packing structure of 4 has a distinctive porous framework consisting of double helix π-stacked assemblies. These results showcase the effectiveness of the skeletal-transformation approach for the synthesis of nanocarbon molecules, and it should be helpful in the design and synthesis of advanced future materials.

Methods

Materials

All used reagents, solvents, and materials were purchased from Tokyo Chemical Industry (TCI), FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, Sigma-Aldrich, Kanto Chemical, Nakalai Tesque, BLDpharm, Angene Chemical, or Alfa Aesar. Unless otherwise noted, materials obtained from commercial suppliers were used without further purification. Dry toluene, dichloromethane, THF, and 1,4-dioxane were supplied from the Glass Countor solvent purification system. The activation of Zn was carried out by sequential washing with HCl aq., EtOH, and diethyl ether. If not mentioned otherwise, all reactions were performed under standard conditions (room temperature, ambient pressure, in air).

Instrumentation

Analytical thin-layer chromatography was performed using fluorescent-labeled silica-coated plates (TLC silica gel 60 F254, Merck). Detection was conducted using UV-light (λex = 254 nm). Flash column chromatography was performed using silica gel (Wakogel 60N (particle size: 63–212 μm), C-300 (particle size: 45–75 μm), or C-400HG (particle size: 20–40 μm)). Preparative thin-layer chromatography (pTLC) was performed using silica gel [Wakogel B-5F (particle size: 45 μm 80% up)]. Preparative resolution was conducted using a JAI LabACE LC-7080 plus preparative HPLC with a chiral column (DAICEL CHIRALPAK IA, ∅ 20 mm × 250 mm, DAICEL CHIRALPAK IE, ∅ 20 mm × 250 mm). Analytical HPLC separation was performed on a Shimadzu CBM-20A system with a UV detector (SPD-20A), and Daicel Chiralpak IA-3, IC-3, and IE-3 placed in a column oven (CTO-20AC). Recycle GPC was conducted using a JAI LabACE LC-7080 plus preparative HPLC with a GPC column (JAIGEL-2HR Plus).

1H NMR (500 MHz) and 13C NMR (126 MHz) spectra were recorded on a Bruker AVANCE III HD spectrometer. 13C NMR (151 MHz) spectra were recorded on a JEOL JNM-ECA600II spectrometer. Chemical shifts were reported as the delta scale in ppm relative to CDCl3 (δ = 7.26 ppm), (CDCl2)2 (δ = 5.91 ppm), and CD2Cl2 (δ = 5.32 ppm) for 1H NMR and CDCl3 (δ = 77.16 ppm) and (CDCl2)2 (δ = 74.00 ppm) for 13C NMR. The following abbreviations were used to describe the multiplicities: s = singlet, bs = broad singlet, d = doublet, t = triplet, q = quartet, quin = quintet, sext = sextet, sep = septet, m = multiplet.

High-resolution atmospheric-pressure chemical ionization time-of-flight (APCI-TOF) and electrospray ionization time-of-flight (ESI-TOF) mass spectra were taken on a Bruker micrOTOF instrument using a positive ionization mode.

X-ray data were obtained using a Rigaku XtaLAB Synergy-R/NLN diffractometer with a MicroMax007HFMR X-ray generator and a HyPix-6000HE detector. The structures were solved using a direct method (SHELXT) and refined by a full-matrix least-squares method on F2 for all reflections using the programs of SHELXL-2014. All non-hydrogen atoms were refined with anisotropic displacement parameters. The hydrogen atoms were placed in idealized positions and refined as riding models with the relative isotropic displacement parameters. Crystallographic data have been deposited with the Cambridge Crystallographic Data Centre as a supplementary publication. Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) patterns at room temperature were measured on a Rigaku MiniFlex600 operating at 40 kV/15 mA, producing Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) at room temperature, ranging from 3° to 55°, except for any additional information.

UV/vis/NIR absorption spectra were recorded on a Shimadzu UV-2550 or JASCO V 670 spectrometer. Emission spectra were recorded on a JASCO FP-8550 spectrometer. Absolute photoluminescence quantum yields (PLQYs) were determined using an integrating sphere unit attached to the same spectrometer. Fluorescence lifetimes were recorded on a HAMAMATSU Quantaurus-Tau spectrometer. CD spectra were recorded on a JASCO J-1500 CD spectrometer. The radiative (kr) and nonradiative (knr) rate constants were calculated from the measured PLQYs (Φ) and emission lifetimes (τ) according to kr = Φ/τ and knr = (1−Φ)/τ. The CPL spectra at 298 K in solution were recorded on a JASCO CPL-300 with a 1.0-mm quartz cell. A scanning rate of 100 nm min–1, an excitation slit width of 3000 µm, a monitoring slit width of 3000 µm, a response time of 4 s, and four times accumulations were employed.

Redox potentials were measured using cyclic voltammetry on an ALS electrochemical analyzer model 612C. Cyclic voltammograms were obtained under the following conditions: solvent: CH2Cl2, electrolyte: 0.1 M Bu4NPF6, working electrode: glassy carbon, counter electrode: Pt, reference electrode: Ag/AgNO3, scan rate: 0.05 V/s.

The microwave-assisted reactions were conducted using Biotage® Initiator+ (0–30 bar, 0–400 W).

The TG chart was recorded on a NETZSCH STA 2500 Regulus.

All calculations were carried out using the Gaussian 16 software package52. The structural optimization and frequency calculations were performed with the density functional theory (DFT) method with the restricted B3LYP53,54 level, employing the 6-31G(d) basis sets. For HBCCBBC 3, empirical dispersion corrections were included using Grimme’s D3 scheme with Becke–Johnson damping55,56. The TD-DFT calculations of HBCCBBC 3 were conducted at the CAM-B3LYP/6-31G(d) level57. The noncovalent interaction (NCI) analysis was carried out using the crystal structure as the input geometry with the NCIPLOT program58. NBO calculations were performed at the B3LYP/6-31G(d) level. Cube files for the NCI analysis were generated with Multiwfn59 and visualized using VMD60.

The adsorption isotherm of CO2 was measured using a Microtrac-BEL Belsorp-Max gas adsorption analyzer with an ULVAC-cryo cryostatic temperature controller. The sample was dried under vacuum at 298 K prior to the measurement.

Synthesis of 6

A 100 mL round-bottom flask containing 3,6,11,14-tetrabromo CBBC 537 (0.34 g, 0.50 mmol), 2-formylphenylboronic acid (0.45 g, 3.0 mmol), Pd(dppf)Cl2∙∙CH2Cl2 (0.16 g, 0.20 mmol), and Cs2CO3 (0.98 g, 3.0 mmol) was purged with N2. To the mixture, degassed toluene (8.0 mL), ethanol (2.0 mL), and water (2.0 mL) were added. After the mixture was stirred at 100 °C for 28 h, 2-formylphenylboronic acid (0.15 g, 1.0 mmol) was added. The mixture was stirred at 100 °C for 24 h. The reaction mixture was cooled to room temperature. After filtration through Celite®, the reaction mixture was extracted with CH2Cl2. The organic phase was washed with brine and dried over anhydrous Na2SO4. After removal of the solvent in vacuo, the mixture was purified by silica gel column chromatography (eluent: CH2Cl2/AcOEt = 20/1).
