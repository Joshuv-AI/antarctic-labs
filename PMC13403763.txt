Bioimpedance Analysis to Predict Clinical Outcomes of Liver Cirrhosis in a Prospective Cohort Study
Authors: Mohammed Kanan, Michelle Luster, Saira Khaderi, Fasiha Kanwal, Aaron P Thrift, Abeer Alsarraj, Emad Sorial, Emad Salem, Hao Duong, Hashem B El‐Serag
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsBioimpedance analysis (BIA) is a non‐invasive method for estimating body composition, which plays a role in the development of cirrhosis. We investigated if BIA measures were associated with cirrhosis complications.MethodsWe analyzed data from 566 participants in a prospective cohort study of cirrhosis patients from tertiary care clinics who underwent BIA at enrollment and regular follow up (20.3% female; 47.0% non‐Hispanic White, 23.1% Hispanic, 27.7% Black; 47.7% metabolic and alcohol‐associated liver disease [MetALD], 30.4% metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease [MASLD], 1.9% alcohol‐associated liver disease [ALD], 9.0% active hepatitis C virus [HCV]). We performed multivariable logistic regression to assess the association of BIA measures with etiology and prevalent decompensation and Cox proportional hazards regression to assess incident decompensation and HCC.ResultsAfter adjustment for sex and race, body fat mass was associated with 14‐fold increased odds of MASLD (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 14.10, 95% confidence interval [CI] 5.25–37.85) and decreased odds of ALD (aOR 0.73, 95% CI 0.60–0.88). Lower odds of prevalent decompensation were seen in the highest tertile of trunk lean mass (aOR 0.39, 95% CI 0.21–0.73) and left arm lean mass (aOR 0.40, 95% CI 0.21–0.75) compared with their lowest tertiles. The middle tertile of visceral fat surface area showed an inverse association with incident decompensation (adjusted hazard ratio 0.19, 95% CI 0.04–0.88). There were no significant associations between BIA measures and incident HCC.ConclusionsBIA could be used as a prognostic tool for patients with cirrhosis, supporting its incorporation into clinical workflows.Keywords: alcohol, epidemiology, hepatitis C, nonalcoholic fatty liver disease, risk stratification

Introduction

Cirrhosis is a chronic disease of the liver estimated to affect over 700 000 individuals globally in 2021 [1]. While hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection has historically been the primary cause of cirrhosis, its prevalence has declined due to widespread use of antiviral medications that achieved high cure rates. Alcohol‐associated liver disease (ALD) and metabolic dysfunction‐associated liver disease (MASLD) are becoming the leading etiology for cirrhosis [2, 3, 4].

Cirrhosis is associated with devastating complications, such as hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), hepatic decompensation, and liver failure [5]. Despite guidelines recommending surveillance with imaging and serum biomarkers, HCC surveillance is underutilized. Only 20%–30% of eligible patients with cirrhosis receive guideline‐concordant semiannual surveillance [6, 7, 8, 9]. In addition, the risk of complications is not uniformly distributed among patients with cirrhosis; many do not develop these complications. Therefore, identifying high‐risk patients could improve care.

Body composition has emerged as a possible predictor of developing cirrhosis complications. The presence of conditions associated with cirrhosis, such as malnutrition, sarcopenia, and volume distribution abnormalities, can help predict outcomes and prognosis [10, 11]. Bioimpedance analysis (BIA) is a non‐invasive, cost‐efficient method of measuring body composition. BIA deploys weak electrical currents that can be used to measure body fat, muscle, and water [12]. We previously demonstrated that several BIA‐derived measurements indicative of body fat and muscle were associated with MASLD or ALD etiology [13]. However, the existence of associations between BIA measures and important clinical outcomes, hepatic decompensation and HCC, remain unclear.

Incorporating objective BIA measures into risk stratification tools could enable earlier detection and management of high‐risk patients for cirrhosis complications. This study used data from a prospective cohort study to evaluate the associations between baseline BIA measures and developing hepatic decompensation and HCC among patients with cirrhosis, and comparing them with those of conventional body composition measures, BMI and WHR.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This study utilized data from the Texas Hepatocellular Consortium (THCCC), a longitudinal prospective cohort study of patients with cirrhosis recruited from tertiary care clinics throughout Texas.

Study Population and Recruitment

THCCC participants had a diagnosis of liver cirrhosis, based on histological confirmation, imaging findings, elastography findings, and/or serum biomarkers, including Fibrosis‐4, FibroTest/FibroSure, and the aspartate aminotransferase‐to‐platelet ratio index. The exclusion criteria were: having a history of HCC or other primary non‐hepatic malignancies or evidence of uncontrolled hepatic decompensation at the time of study enrollment. Recruitment for the THCCC started in December 2016 and was ongoing at the time of writing and was approved by the Institutional Review Boards at BCM and MEDVAMC. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

The THCCC participants included in the present study were recruited between December 2016 and March 2025 at two of the THCCC sites: BCM and MEDVAMC in Houston, Texas. Those with pacemakers or other implantable medical devices, with amputations of any limbs, or who could or would not remove their socks were not eligible for BIA and were excluded from our analysis. Further, we excluded those who were diagnosed with HCC within 30 days of THCCC enrollment to avoid reverse causality.

Study Variables

At baseline, THCCC participants who were eligible underwent BIA. BIA was performed using either an InBody 570 at BCM (May 2018 to March 2025, n = 162) and an InBody 520 at MEDVAMC (December 2016 to March 2021, n = 194). Then, MEDVAMC transitioned to an InBody 570, which was used through March 2025 (n = 210). Output measures, including those of lean body mass, body fat percentage, and water distribution, from both BIA machines were consistent (Table S1).

Baseline characteristics were obtained via structured surveys and clinical interviews. Race/ethnicity was categorized into four groups: Hispanic, non‐Hispanic Black, non‐Hispanic White, and Other. We used medical history and medication use to define comorbid conditions. Diabetes mellitus and hypertension were identified by searching the participant's electronic medical records and from patient self‐report. The etiology of cirrhosis was defined as MASLD, ALD, HCV infection, and other causes (e.g., hepatitis B infection, autoimmune liver diseases, alpha‐1 anti‐trypsin deficiency, primary biliary cholangitis, primary sclerosing cholangitis, hereditary hemochromatosis, Wilson's disease).

Definitions of Cirrhosis Etiologies

MASLD was defined as having least one of the following criteria: (1) BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 (≥ 23 kg/m2 for Asian populations) or waist circumference ≥ 94 cm for men or > 80 cm for women, (2) type 2 diabetes mellitus based on either diagnosis or use of diabetic medications, (3) a diagnosis of hypertension; or (4) dyslipidemia based on elevated triglycerides or cholesterol or use of lipid‐lowering therapy. ALD was defined based on physician diagnosis or self‐reported alcohol use, including past heavy drinking or any current alcohol use. Heavy alcohol use was defined as > 7 drinks/week for women and > 14 drinks/week for men, corresponding to approximately > 14 g/day and > 28 g/day, respectively, assuming one standard drink contains 14 g of alcohol. MetALD was defined as MASLD with concomitant ALD (meeting criteria for both MASLD and ALD). HCV status was classified as: (1) active based on a positive HCV RNA test and no sustained virologic response at baseline; (2) cured based on completed HCV treatment or sustained virologic response at baseline; or (3) negative based on the absence of both HCV RNA and HCV antibodies.

Follow Up and Assessment of Liver‐Related Outcomes

THCCC participants were followed at 6‐month intervals to look for evidence of HCC or decompensation and document liver transplantation or death. Diagnosis of HCC was made based on histopathological confirmation or radiographic criteria consistent with Liver Imaging Reporting and Data System (LI‐RADS) category 5 (arterial enhancement with venous/delayed phase washout) on MRI or CT [14]. LI‐RADS 4 lesions were evaluated by multidisciplinary tumor boards, and HCC was confirmed based on expert consensus interpretation.

For this study, we conducted reviews of included patients' electronic medical records including problem lists, medical notes, and medications in July 2025. A history of hepatic decompensation was defined as the presence of one or more of the following events: ascites, variceal bleeding, hepatic encephalopathy, or hepatorenal syndrome. Clinically apparent decompensation was categorized as prevalent if the events occurred prior to or within 6 months of BIA and as incident if the events occurred more than 6 months following BIA.

Statistical Analysis

We performed logistic regression modeling to evaluate the associations of BIA measures with cirrhosis etiologies (MASLD, ALD, HCV) and prevalent decompensation. For time‐to‐event analysis, we used Cox proportional hazards regression to assess the associations of BIA measures with incident decompensation and incident HCC. For analyses of associations between BIA measures and etiologies, we compared those with the underlying etiologic risk factor (MASLD, ALD, HCV) to the rest of the study population. MASLD, ALD, and HCV were not considered to be mutually exclusive. For HCV, we compared the active HCV group with a combined group of cured HCV and HCV‐negative. To reduce potential confounding variables, we constructed multivariate models to adjust for sex and race, generating adjusted ORs (aORs) and adjusted HRs (aHRs). We categorized BIA measures into tertiles (lowest, middle, highest) to assess their associations with clinical outcomes. For patients with multiple BIA assessments, only the baseline BIA result was used for analysis. In a prespecified secondary analysis, we excluded non‐bleeding varices and repeated all models for prevalent and incident decompensation. Several subgroup analyses were conducted, including for men and those with metabolic dysfunction and alcohol‐associated liver disease (MetALD). Because BIA measures may be affected by fluid overload, we performed a sensitivity analysis excluding participants with clearly documented current ascites at the time of baseline BIA. As a sensitivity analysis, we also modeled MASLD as a mutually exclusive category, excluding participants with active HCV or heavy alcohol use. As a sensitivity analysis, we also performed multivariable models with additional adjustment for any of the following clinical factors (MELD, serum albumin, diabetes, BMI) that were found significant (p < 0.1) predictors of HCC in unadjusted Cox PH models. The analyses were intended to be exploratory, and therefore formal multiplicity adjustment was not applied. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS 9.4, and a p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient Characteristics

A total of 566 patients with cirrhosis were included in this analysis (Table 1). The mean age ± standard deviation (SD) of this cohort was 62.48 ± 9.36 years; 115 (20.3%) were female, 266 (47.0%) were non‐Hispanic White, 157 (27.7%) were non‐Hispanic Black, and 131 (23.1%) were Hispanic. The mean ± SD BMI and WHR were 29.58 ± 5.79 kg/m2 and 0.99 ± 0.07 cm, respectively. Among participants, 304 (53.7%) had diabetes, and 287 (50.7%) had hypertension. In total, 162 patients had evidence of decompensation (28.6%), including 125 (77.2%) with prevalent decompensation and 37 (22.8%) with incident decompensation events during a median follow‐up period of 2 years (interquartile range [IQR] 1.098–3.680). A total of 56 patients developed incident HCC during the median follow‐up time of 2.8 years (IQR 1.175–4.137). When categories were defined as mutually exclusive and assigned by a predefined clinical hierarchy (active HCV first, then MASLD only, ALD only, MetALD, and Other), the distribution showed MetALD as the most common etiology (47.7%), followed by MASLD only (30.4%), active HCV (9.0%), and ALD only (1.9%).

For ALD‐related cirrhosis, positive associations were observed for BIA measures associated with lean body mass (total and for both arms, trunk, and both legs), dry lean mass, skeletal muscle mass, and total body water. By contrast, there were negative associations seen for BIA measures associated with body fat mass (total and for both arms and legs), visceral fat surface area, and BMI. After adjustment for sex and race, only total and left leg body fat mass (total: aOR 0.73, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.60, 0.88; left leg: aOR 0.78, 95% CI 0.61, 0.99), visceral fat surface area (aOR 0.77, 95% CI 0.60, 0.99), and BMI (aOR 0.73, 95% CI 0.61, 0.88) remained significantly associated with a lower likelihood of ALD (Table 2).

There was a significant positive association between HCV‐related cirrhosis and lean mass of the right arm. HCV‐related cirrhosis was also negatively associated with body fat of both arms and legs and visceral fat surface area. The negative associations with arm body fat (right arm: aOR 0.51, 95% CI 0.26, 0.99; left arm: aOR 0.49, 95% CI 0.25, 0.97) and visceral fat surface area (aOR 0.61, 95% CI 0.40, 0.93) persisted after adjusting for sex and race.

In a sensitivity analysis excluding participants with documented current ascites at baseline (n = 46), the overall pattern of associations was broadly similar to that observed in the primary analyses (Table S4).

Continuous Variable Analyses

Prevalent decompensation had a significant inverse correlation with BIA markers of muscularity, including lean body mass (total and for both arms, trunk, and both legs), dry lean mass, and skeletal muscle mass. After adjustment for race and sex, this significant association persisted for lean body mass in both arms and trunk. (Table 3). For incident decompensation, we observed significant negative correlations with lean body mass, particularly in the trunk and both arms; however, these associations were not statistically significant after adjustment. For incident HCC, there were no significant associations seen for BIA measures, BMI, or WHR.

Tertile Analyses

We observed significantly reduced odds of prevalent decompensation in individuals in the highest and middle tertiles of lean body mass (total and for both arms, trunk, both legs), total body water, dry lean mass, and skeletal muscle mass. WHR was inversely associated with prevalent decompensation (middle tertile: OR 0.56, 95% CI 0.33, 0.93; highest tertile: OR 0.55, 95% CI 0.33, 0.92; Table 4). After adjusting for sex and race, the remaining statistically significant associations were with the highest tertiles of trunk and arm lean mass (trunk: OR 0.39, 95% CI 0.21, 0.73; right arm: OR 0.44, 95% CI 0.23, 0.81; left arm: OR 0.40, 95% CI 0.21, 0.75; Table 5).

Patients in the highest tertile of lean body mass had significantly reduced odds of developing incident decompensation, particularly in the trunk and both arms. We also observed a significant inverse association between the middle tertile of right leg lean mass and incident decompensation. After adjustment for sex and race, the reduced risk of incident decompensation remained for individuals in the middle tertile of visceral fat surface area (aHR 0.19, 95% CI 0.04, 0.88), which was not statistically significant in the unadjusted model.

Subgroup Analyses

The subgroup analysis of male participants (N = 451) showed significant inverse associations between prevalent decompensation and dry lean mass, total body water, and lean body mass (trunk and both arms). There were no statistically significant associations between HCC and body composition measures (Table S2).

The subgroup analysis of MetALD showed similar trends to those of the full dataset. In individuals with cirrhosis and MetALD (n = 270), prevalent decompensation had significant inverse associations with lean mass (left arm and trunk) and skeletal muscle mass, but we saw no significant associations with incident decompensation or HCC (Table S3).

In a sensitivity analysis in which MASLD was modeled as a mutually exclusive category excluding active HCV and heavy alcohol use, the overall pattern of associations was similar to that seen in the primary analysis, with strong positive associations persisting for multiple BIA measures, particularly adiposity‐related measures (Table S5).

Additional adjustment for clinical factors (MELD, serum albumin, diabetes, BMI) was considered for the HCC analysis, but only serum albumin was retained in the final multivariable model because it was significant (p < 0.1) in the unadjusted analyses. This addition did not change the results (Table S6).

Discussion

This study analyzing data from 566 patients enrolled in a large, prospective, longitudinal cohort study that includes multiple etiologies [15] investigated the possible utility of multifrequency BIA for risk stratification by looking for associations between BIA measures and cirrhosis etiology, prevalent and incident decompensation, and HCC. We found that BIA measures of muscularity, particularly lean body mass, were inversely associated with both prevalent and incident decompensation. Further, neither BIA nor conventional body composition measures were significantly associated with HCC incidence.

Previous studies evaluating BIA measures as prognostic factors in cirrhosis examined smaller populations; focused on single metrics, used monofrequency devices; or included limited etiologies [16, 17, 18, 19, 20]. Our present study shows that both prevalent and incident decompensation are inversely associated with BIA markers of muscularity. Patients with cirrhosis who had values in the middle or highest tertiles of lean body or skeletal muscle mass had significantly reduced odds of having prevalent decompensation compared with those in the lowest tertile. Lean body mass in the trunk and in both arms was also significantly inversely associated with incident decompensation. This finding was more prominent in the tertile analyses, which could have helped uncover non‐linear risk relationships that were not apparent using continuous variables. For example, Ebadi et al. described a non‐linear association between BMI and post‐liver transplant survival, with best survival observed at BMI 28–37 kg/m2 [21]. Most significant associations observed in the unadjusted analyses did not persist after adjustment for sex and race/ethnicity. While men have higher muscle mass than women, they have also been shown to have a higher risk of decompensation [22, 23], and several studies have documented differences in body fat composition and risks of chronic liver diseases among different racial/ethnic groups [24, 25].

Our finding that BIA measures of muscularity are associated with hepatic decompensation aligns with the literature indicating sarcopenia is a major risk factor for morbidity and mortality in cirrhosis [26]. We also found that none of the body composition measures (conventional or BIA) were associated with the development of HCC in our cohort. HCC risk appears more closely associated with underlying etiology (e.g., viral, metabolic dysfunction) and the severity of hepatic decompensation [27]. However, given the limited number of HCC events observed in follow up, our power to detect small differences was limited [13, 28]. Based on our data, BIA might be more suitable as a marker of short‐term frailty risk rather than long‐term cancer risk.

Our data show strong positive associations between BIA measures of body fat mass and MASLD‐related cirrhosis. Further, for ALD‐ and HCV‐related cirrhosis, we showed positive associations with BIA measures of lean mass and an inverse association with BIA measures of body fat. These observations are consistent with previous studies demonstrating links between obesity and MASLD and between alcohol consumption and muscle wasting [29].

The limitations of this study relate to the inclusion and exclusion criteria during prospective enrollment in the THCCC. BIA was not performed on many THCCC participants due to contraindications (e.g., pacemaker, amputation) or logistics during the COVID pandemic. Excluding patients with uncontrolled hepatic decompensation could have caused severe cases of cirrhosis to be underrepresented in the dataset. While THCCC participants are scheduled for follow up every 6–12 months, the median follow‐up time in our dataset was 2.8 years, which may not be long enough to capture the full disease progression spectrum. The primary models were adjusted for sex and race/ethnicity, and therefore residual confounding from other factors is possible. However, consideration of additional clinical factors (MELD, serum albumin, diabetes, BMI) was performed and additional adjustment for serum albumin in the final model did not change the results. Given the number of associations examined, type I error was possible; the findings should therefore be interpreted cautiously as exploratory and hypothesis‐generating [13]. Future studies should evaluate whether longitudinal changes in BIA measures improve prediction of specific decompensation outcomes [30, 31]. Future studies with larger cohorts and longer follow‐up periods are essential to investigate the subtype‐specific relationships between BIA measures and individual decompensation events.

In conclusion, this study shows that BIA has potential to be a prognostic tool for assessing cirrhosis. It also highlights the importance of using objective measures of muscle mass as parameters for risk stratification of patients with cirrhosis.
