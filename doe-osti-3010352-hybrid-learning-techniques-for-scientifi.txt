  1. Abstract summarizing the general topics addressed during the award.

 The research initiatives supported by the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) Grant DE-SC0022265
 are fundamentally aimed at pioneering advanced machine learning (ML) techniques for scientific
 data compression within high-performance computing (HPC) environments. This comprehensive
 body of work addresses the critical challenge posed by the exponential growth of data generated
 by scientific simulations in domains such as fusion energy, climate modeling, and computational
 fluid dynamics (CFD). A core objective is to develop compression algorithms that achieve
 substantial data reduction—often by orders of magnitude—while rigorously ensuring the fidelity
 of both the primary data (PD) and scientifically crucial derived quantities of interest (QoI).

 The methodologies deployed under this grant integrate sophisticated deep learning architectures,
 prominently featuring autoencoders, advanced generative models like conditional diffusion, and
 hybrid learning techniques. Key innovations include the development of Guaranteed
 Autoencoders (GAE) and the Guaranteed Conditional Diffusion with Tensor Correction (GCDTC)
 framework, which provide explicit, instance-level error bounds on reconstructed data.
 Furthermore, specialized strategies such as nonlinear constraint satisfaction are employed to
 preserve the integrity of QoI, a vital requirement for the trustworthiness of downstream scientific
 analyses. This research also focuses on the design and implementation of scalable, GPU-
 accelerated software pipelines that seamlessly integrate into existing HPC workflows, ensuring
 both computational efficiency and practical applicability. The CAESAR framework, for example,
 unifies foundation and generative models to create an adaptive and efficient compression
 solution for spatio-temporal scientific data. Collectively, these efforts represent a significant
 advancement in mitigating the scientific data deluge, enabling more effective data management,
 accelerated scientific discovery, and optimized utilization of HPC resources.

  2. Brief technical description of most relevant projects/publications.

 The research supported by DOE Grant DE-SC0022265 addresses the escalating data crisis in
 modern computational science, driven by the massive data volumes produced by high-fidelity
 simulations across disciplines like fusion energy, climate science, and computational fluid
 dynamics (CFD). These scientific applications generate tens of petabytes of data daily,
 necessitating highly efficient and reliable data compression algorithms for long-term storage and
 subsequent analysis. Unlike conventional image or video compression, scientific data
 compression demands strict guarantees on reconstruction errors for primary data (PD) and,
 crucially, for derived Quantities of Interest (QoI) to ensure the validity of scientific discoveries.
 The research under this grant pioneers machine learning (ML)-based methodologies that meet
 these stringent requirements, delivering high compression ratios without compromising scientific
 accuracy.

 A cornerstone of this research involves the innovative application and enhancement of
 autoencoder (AE) architectures for scientific data compression. Autoencoders are neural
 networks designed to learn efficient, low-dimensional representations (encodings) of input data

and then reconstruct the original input from these compressed representations. This process
inherently enables dimensionality reduction, a critical step for managing large datasets.

The researchers have developed Guaranteed Autoencoders (GAE), a significant advancement that
provides explicit error guarantees on an instance-by-instance basis, a feature often lacking in
traditional neural network-based compression. The GAE approach leverages a key observation:
neural networks employing piecewise linear activation units (PLUs) can be represented as
instance-specific linear operators. This linearity allows for the direct and precise relationship
between compressed and reconstructed data to be identified, enabling augmentation of the
reconstruction until a predefined error bound is met. This method is generalizable,
accommodating both fully connected and convolutional layers, making it widely applicable across
various scientific data structures.

Furthermore, hybrid compression techniques combine ML models like autoencoders with
standard compression methods and tensor decompositions. For instance, one approach
integrates tensor decompositions, autoencoders, product quantizers, and error-bounded lossy
compressors to reduce PD with bounded errors. These hybrid methodologies are implemented
as end-to-end, scalable, GPU-based pipelines, demonstrating their efficacy in compressing
massive nuclear fusion simulation data from codes like XGC by a factor of more than 150, while
using only a small percentage of computational resources. The pipeline typically involves
decomposing data into subdomains for parallel processing, compressing each subdomain
independently, and then applying post-processing techniques to refine error bounds.

Ensuring guaranteed error bounds is paramount in scientific data compression, as even small
errors can have deleterious impacts on downstream processing and scientific conclusions. The
research under DE-SC0022265 develops sophisticated mechanisms to achieve this, moving
beyond mere statistical averages to strict, instance-level guarantees.

Principal Component Analysis (PCA)-based Residual Correction: A primary mechanism for
bounding reconstruction errors involves applying PCA to the residual data, which is the difference
between the original and the autoencoder-reconstructed data. The PCA extracts a basis matrix,
representing the directions of maximum variance in the residual. For data instances where the
initial reconstruction error exceeds a specified threshold, a minimal set of PCA coefficients
corresponding to the largest error contributions are selected and stored. These coefficients are
then used to correct the reconstructed data, iteratively enhancing accuracy until the error falls
within the user-defined bounds. Quantization and entropy coding are applied to these coefficients
to optimize storage.

Tensor Correction Networks: To further refine the reconstructed outputs and improve
compression quality, tensor correction (TC) networks are incorporated. These networks learn a
reverse mapping from the reconstructed tensors to the original tensors, effectively adjusting the
outputs of the autoencoder or diffusion model to bring them closer to the ground truth. Often
designed as overcomplete feedforward networks, TC networks aim to enhance accuracy without
introducing additional latent representations for storage.

Preservation of Quantities of Interest (QoI) via Constraint Satisfaction: A critical distinguishing
feature of this research is its explicit focus on preserving QoI. Scientific applications often rely on
QoI derived from primary data (e.g., moments, physical phenomena detection), which may be
highly sensitive to compression errors. The approach uses a constraint satisfaction post-
processing step to ensure QoI integrity. This involves transforming complex, often nonlinear, QoI
constraints into instance-specific linear constraints. For neural networks with PLUs, the activation
paths define local linear operators, enabling the satisfaction of linear constraints imposed on
autoencoder outputs. This ensures that the reconstructed data, while compressed, remains
faithful to the underlying physical or scientific laws and relationships. This is crucial, for example,
in fusion applications where moment preservation is vital. For climate applications, QoI like
tropical cyclones and atmospheric rivers are preserved by applying low-error compression only
to their relevant regions, leading to differential error bounds.

The research extends beyond traditional autoencoders to embrace advanced generative models,
recognizing their potential for high-fidelity compression.
1. Guaranteed Conditional Diffusion with Tensor Correction (GCDTC): This novel compression
 paradigm leverages conditional diffusion models, a class of generative models that denoise
 data through iterative steps. GCDTC is specifically designed for lossy scientific data
 compression, employing a mixture of 3D block-based conditioning and 2D denoising U-Nets
 to capture spatiotemporal correlations in structured datasets. After training, a deterministic
 denoising decoder reconstructs the data, which is then further refined by tensor correction
 and error guarantee steps to control maximum error distortion within acceptable scientific
 limits. Experimental results on climate (E3SM) and chemical combustion (S3D) datasets
 demonstrate GCDTC's superiority over standard convolutional autoencoders and competitive
 quality against existing algorithms like SZ3.
2. CAESAR: A Unified Framework for Foundation and Generative Models: The CAESAR
 (Conditional AutoEncoder with Super-resolution for Augmented Reduction) framework
 represents a significant step towards unifying foundation and generative models for spatio-
 temporal scientific data compression. The baseline model, CAESAR-V, is built on a variational
 autoencoder with scale hyperpriors and super-resolution modules to achieve high
 compression. An enhanced version, CAESAR-D, incorporates conditional diffusion to
 interpolate the latent spaces of missing frames between compressed keyframes, enabling
 high-fidelity reconstruction without explicit storage. CAESAR features a GPU-accelerated
 post-processing module that enforces error bounds, achieving real-time compression with
 rigorous accuracy guarantees. This framework demonstrates significantly better NRMSE rates
 and achieves two to three orders of magnitude compression for scientifically acceptable error
 bounds compared to state-of-the-art rule-based compressors.
3. Attention Mechanisms: The integration of attention mechanisms into data reduction
 pipelines enhances adaptive, high-fidelity compression. Attention allows the models to
 dynamically focus on the most salient features and regions within complex scientific data,
 optimizing where compression efforts are concentrated. This adaptive strategy improves
 overall compression ratios while ensuring that critical information is preserved, particularly
 valuable for spatiotemporal scientific data with varying importance across regions or time.

 The practical utility of this research is underscored by its focus on developing scalable, GPU-
 accelerated software pipelines that are deployable in real-world HPC environments. These
 fast algorithms are crucial for handling the immense data throughput demands of scientific
 simulations. The compression pipelines are designed for efficient parallel I/O, often utilizing
 frameworks like ADIOS2, which is already common in many scientific codes. The compression
 processes are offloaded to staging nodes, ensuring that they do not impact simulation
 performance. The computational resources required for compression are typically only a few
 percent of those needed for data generation, making these approaches highly practical for
 on-the-fly compression scenarios.

 Applications in Diverse Scientific Domains:
 • Fusion Energy Simulations: The techniques have been extensively validated on data
 generated by large-scale fusion codes like XGC, which can produce tens of petabytes in a
 single day. The physics-informed compression, including the preservation of moments,
 has proven effective in achieving high compression while maintaining scientific
 acceptability.
 • Climate Modeling: For climate applications, the methods have been applied to data from
 models like E3SM, enabling adaptive compression with feature preservation, particularly
 for quantities like tropical cyclones and atmospheric rivers.
 • Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD): Data generated by Sandia's compressible
 reacting direct numerical simulation (DNS) code, S3D, which involves complex chemical
 mechanisms and hundreds of species, has been successfully compressed. The
 Guaranteed Block Autoencoder with Tensor Correction (GBATC) achieved two to three
 orders of magnitude reduction in CFD data, outperforming traditional compressors like
 SZ3 and delivering smaller errors on QoI such as net production rates for species.

In essence, the research supported by DE-SC0022265 provides a comprehensive, rigorous, and
practical toolkit of ML-driven compression solutions that are transforming how massive scientific
datasets are managed in HPC, thereby enabling continued progress in scientific discovery across
critical domains.
