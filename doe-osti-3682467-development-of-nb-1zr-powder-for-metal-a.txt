 ABSTRACT

Advanced reactor concepts require structural materials that operate above the practical ceiling of the Fe-
and Ni-based alloys currently accepted in the American Society of Mechanical Engineers’ Boiler and
Pressure Vessel Code Section III Division 5. Refractory metals meet that temperature demand but are
difficult to fabricate by conventional thermomechanical routes into small-lot, geometrically complex
components. This report documents the development of Nb-1Zr for laser powder bed fusion (LPBF)
under the US Department of Energy, Office of Nuclear Energy’s Advanced Materials and Manufacturing
Technologies Program. Commercial gas-atomized Nb-1Zr powder was procured, and spherical Nb-1Zr
powder was additionally produced in-house by ultrasonic atomization of rod stock, demonstrating a
laboratory-scale route to refractory feedstock. A central composite design of laser power, scan speed, and
hatch spacing was executed on a Nikon SLM Solutions SLM 125HL LPBF system, yielding a relative
density plateau near 99% above approximately 90 J/mm3 with no cracking observed in the cross section.
Electron backscatter diffraction showed a weakly textured, bimodal grain structure with low retained local
misorientation, and chemical analysis confirmed that oxygen content was unchanged from powder to
printed part. To extend Nb-1Zr toward higher-strength variants, a modular powder functionalization
strategy was developed in which resonant acoustic mixing applied dispersant particles to the qualified
base powder. Resonant acoustic mixing produced substantially more uniform ZrO₂ coverage than that
produced during 5 days of roller milling, while preserving particle sphericity.

 1. INTRODUCTION

The US Department of Energy, Office of Nuclear Energy, Advanced Materials and Manufacturing
Technologies (AMMT) Program’s core initiative is to develop a framework for shortening the interval
between material concept and qualified nuclear components (Li et al., 2025). AMMT’s organizing
premise is that additive manufacturing (AM) is not a drop-in substitute for conventional fabrication but
rather is a process in which composition, thermal history, microstructure, and performance are
simultaneously linked (Li et al., 2025). The qualification framework developed under AMMT, therefore,
couples in situ process monitoring data and high-throughput characterization with physics-informed
models, limiting reliance on stand-alone repetitive testing and destructive examination (Li et al., 2025).
That infrastructure has matured around austenitic stainless steels and Ni-based superalloys, and extensive
property databases provide a stable benchmark against which AM-specific behavior can be isolated
(Dryepondt et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025; Massey et al., 2024; van Rooyen et al., 2023).

Advanced reactor concepts now under development push core outlet temperatures beyond what that
benchmark set of alloys can support. High-temperature gas-cooled and molten salt reactors target 600°C–
1,000°C; heat-pipe-based systems target 650°C–1,200°C; and space reactor concepts target 600°C–
1,500°C (Bennett et al., 1996; Muroga, 2017). The six Fe- and Ni-based alloys admitted to American
Society of Mechanical Engineers’ (ASME’s) Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code (BPVC) Section III
Division 5 carry a practical unirradiated ceiling near 800°C–900°C, and irradiation depresses the usable
window considerably further, with alloys like stainless steel 316 falling to roughly 550°C above 0.1 dpa.
Alloy 617, the newest alloy accepted to the ASME BPVC, extends furthest up the temperature range
(950°C), but its approximately 12 wt % Co activates to 60Co under neutron flux, constraining use in or
near the core. For temperatures above 800°C, refractory metals (e.g., Nb, Ta, Mo, W, and their alloys) are
the structural class of alloys that remains viable, and the case for them is well established. The question
has never been whether they meet the temperature demand but rather whether they can be manufactured
into components on a schedule, at an absorbable cost, and without oxygen or water vapor degradation
concerns.

Interest in refractory alloys for nuclear applications originated over six decades ago during the original
space race. The Space Nuclear Auxiliary Power (SNAP) programs of the 1960s (Voss, 1984) began with
the Hastelloy-N-clad, U-ZrH-fueled SNAP-2 through SNAP-10A systems, operating between 500°C and
700°C. The program then progressed to the substantially more aggressive SNAP-50 concept, which
targeted 1,000 kW from a UN-fueled, molten-Li-cooled core, with Nb-1Zr cladding and an expected
operating temperature of approximately 1,100°C (CANEL, 1964). Later programs reinforced the Nb
selection; for example, the SP-100 fuel pin used PWC-11 (Nb-1Zr-0.1C) cladding with a Re liner for
cladding to UN fuel (Buden, 1993), and a Nb-1Zr Brayton Cycle heat receiver that consumed roughly
400 kg of the alloy (Cooper Jr & Hoffman, 1984). SNAP-50 was canceled before deployment, but the
alloy outlived the program and continues to be commercially available and used in other industries, such
as defense. What has yet to be properly established is an AM route to bring the refractory alloy into
nuclear service.

A renewed national push to deploy these systems is underway. Executive Order 14369, Ensuring
American Space Superiority (December 2025), directs deployment of nuclear reactors in orbit or on the
lunar surface by 2030; National Science and Technology Memorandum 3 (April 2026) reiterates a similar
objective, supported by a program portfolio spanning kWe through MWt demonstrators (El-Genk, 2009).
Conventional refractory metal fabrication relies on tooling and multistep thermomechanical processing,
which is poorly matched to small-lot, geometrically complex parts such as heat pipes, fuel pin space
grids, and thin-walled shielding cases. Plastic flow in body-centered cubic (BCC) metals at low
homologous temperature is governed by the Peierls barrier, in which W and Mo sit at the high end of that

 2

distribution with ductile-to-brittle transition temperatures (DBTT) well above room temperature (Hyer et
al., 2025). AM processing requires cooling from the melting point (approximately 3,400°C for W) down
through the brittle regime, causing residual stress accumulation, and cracking often follows.

Nb has a Peierls stress several times lower than that of W or Mo (Hyer et al., 2025) and a DBTT below
ambient, which was among the reasons for the success of Nb-1Zr, an alloy that could already be formed
into complex geometries 60 years ago. The high-temperature creep resistance of Nb-1Zr is lower than that
of W or Mo alloys such as W-25Re or TZM. However, commercial variations of the alloy including
PWC-11 (C variant) promote the formation of secondary strengthening phases (CANEL, 1964). Creep
resistance of Nb alloys like PWC-11 or FS-85 is comparable to that of TZM, albeit with lower overall
strength (CANEL, 1964). However, W-Re and TZM remain difficult to process into desired shapes,
whether by additive or conventional manufacturing. The controlling degradation mechanism for Nb is,
therefore, not lattice friction but rather interstitial embrittlement (i.e., O, N, C) (Cooper Jr & Hoffman,
1984), which shifts the processing burden from thermal management to powder handling and atmosphere
control.

As shown in Figure 1, the AMMT program is pursuing multiple AM processes. Laser powder bed fusion
(LPBF) is the flagship process (Li et al., 2025) because of its ease of in situ monitoring of the layer-by-
layer process, the large dataset in the literature across alloy systems, and the general ability to fabricate
complex structures with high geometric resolution (<200 µm). LPBF systems typically operate at room
temperature, and cracking is observed in materials that accumulate excessive thermal residual stress (i.e.,
W and Mo alloys) (Kumar et al., 2026). Fabricating complex structures from W and Mo alloys without
cracking has been well demonstrated on electron-beam powder bed fusion systems because the powder
bed can be preheated easily and continuously held at temperature by rastering the electron beam
(Fernandez-Zelaia et al., 2021; Fernandez-Zelaia et al., 2022; Kumar et al., 2026; Ledford et al., 2023).
Some commercial LPBF systems allow the user to preheat the powder bed to reduce the cooling range,
but this is not yet a common feature for LPBF (Kumar et al., 2026).

This report details the investigation of Nb-1Zr by LPBF, combining the high processability of a nuclear-
origin alloy with the flagship process of the AMMT program. As essentially a base alloy, Nb-1Zr requires
a strengthening phase for high-temperature strength stability. Commercial sourcing of refractory powders
was limited, but some powder was acquired off the shelf. This research also demonstrated that spherical
Nb-1Zr powders could be produced with an in-house atomization system. The commercial material was
processed with a standard LPBF system with a Yb-fiber Gaussian laser, and the results showed high
processability and no cracking issues. Investigation into making Nb-1Zr a modular material involved
exploration of dispersants (e.g., ZrO₂) that can be added to the Nb-1Zr stock powder, expecting a reaction
during laser melting; coating methods were developed on inexpensive Ni-base powder before precious
refractory feedstock was committed to the project. As will be discussed in more detail, the modular
material processing method can be applied to tailor a base alloy for specific property enhancements rather
than requiring development of a new alloy.

 2. POWDER PROCESSING OF NB-1ZR

2.1 Powder Processing

Spherical metal powders for LPBF are most commonly produced by gas atomization, in which a molten
stream is disintegrated by high-velocity inert gas jets, as shown in Figure 2(a). The process is well
established and scales readily. However, it yields a broad particle size distribution, requiring downstream

 4

classification to recover the fraction suitable for powder bed fusion. Figure 2(b) shows that ultrasonic
atomization delivers molten material onto a vibrating sonotrode, where capillary waves at the surface
shed droplets of a size set by the excitation frequency, producing a narrower distribution at lower
throughput. Ultrasonic atomization is currently suitable for lab-scale exploration of alloys and gives
enough powder for LPBF trials before scaling gas atomization.

  Figure 2. (a) Gas and (b) ultrasonic atomization processes used to fabricate spherical metal powders from
 stock solid.

The Nb-1Zr powders were commercially acquired from Elmet Technologies (Minnesota, USA) with a
given particle size distribution of 15–45 µm. The Nb-1Zr rod stock was acquired from Goodfellow
Advanced Materials (Pennsylvania, USA) and atomized with an Amazemet (Warsaw, Poland) rePowder
ultrasonic atomization arc-melting module with a TZM sonotrode operating at 20 kHz; powders were
sieved with a 200-mesh screen (particle sizes below 75 µm).

Figure 3 compares the two feedstocks. Figure 3(a) shows that the commercial Elmet powder is centered
near 22 µm, consistent with the 15–45 µm classification supplied by the vendor, whereas the in-house
Amazemet powder is centered near 50 µm with a measurable population extending to 85 µm. The coarser
center of the ultrasonically atomized powder reflects the droplet size set by the sonotrode excitation
frequency, and the powder was analyzed in the as-atomized condition.

Secondary electron micrographs (Figure 3[b]) for both sets of powders show that the powders are
predominantly spherical. The Elmet powder carries a higher satellite fraction, with fine particles adhered
to the surfaces of larger ones, consistent with droplet collision in the gas atomization plume. At higher
magnification, the Elmet particles display a well-defined dendritic solidification structure across the free
surface, whereas the Amazemet particles are comparatively smooth with only faint surface relief.
Occasional nonspherical fragments are present in the in-house powder, reflecting the stochastic unmelted
runoff of fiber-like pieces that slipped through the sieve screen, produced from the feeding rod during the
ultrasonic atomization.

2.2 LPBF Processing

Only the Elmet powder was processed by LPBF in this work. The in-house atomized material was
reserved for characterization and will be further refined before the research team produces a suitable
amount of powder for LPBF studies. All builds were performed on a Nikon SLM Solutions (Lubeck,
Germany) SLM 125HL LPBF system, as shown in Figure 4(a). The SLM 125 is a single-laser system
with a 125 × 125 × 125 mm build envelope, a 400 W Yb-fiber laser delivering a nominally Gaussian
beam at approximately 70 µm focal diameter, and a recirculating Ar atmosphere maintained below 0.1
vol% O₂. The reduced build volume for the system was inserted into the powder bed elevated shaft,
adjusting the powder bed size to approximately 50 × 50 mm. A pure Mo build plate was used as the
starting substrate.

Simple cubic geometries with a 5 × 10 mm cross section, 12 mm tall, were fabricated for each parameter
combination; the 20 cubes were printed 10 each over two different builds (an example of a build is shown
in Figure 4[b]). Five of the 20 cubes were not completed to full height, a problem that was attributed to
recoater interference as the part edges grew. The printing problem could have been caused by random
residual strain issues but likely was not related to VED. A representative geometrically complex part (i.e.,
an Oak Ridge National Laboratory oak leaf) printed from the same powder is shown in Figure 4(c); this
printed piece demonstrated that the alloy tolerates processing fine geometric features to near net shape.

The cubes were removed from the build plate using a diamond wire saw and were ultrasonically cleaned
in ethanol. Volumetric density of the cubes was measured using the Archimedes immersion method, per
ASTM International Standard B962, by floating the cubes in 200 proof ethanol. Three measurements
were taken for each cube, with drying between each measurement; the densities for each printed cube are
given in

Table 1. A relative density was calculated in comparison with the theoretical density of Nb-1Zr, 8.57
g/cm3 (from design data provided by ATI, Texas, USA), and is given for the cubes as a function of VED
in Figure 4(d). The relative density appears to increase with increasing energy density until a plateau of
approximately 99% density is achieved at about 90 J/mm3.

Select cubes were cross-sectioned and metallographically prepared for direct observation of internal
defects and follow-on characterization. Metallographic preparation steps included cold mounting in
epoxy, followed by grinding and polishing with SiC and diamond colloids to a final finish performed with
0.05 µm colloidal silica. Optical images were captured on a Keyence (Osaka, Japan) VHX-6000
microscope. Figure 4(e) presents images of a few cross sections at different VEDs. Below the threshold of

2.3 Microstructural Characterization

Sample 10 fabricated at 122 J/mm3 was analyzed with a Tescan (Brno, Czech Republic) Mira3 scanning
electron microscope (SEM) operating at 20 kV. Backscatter electron micrographs were taken across the
sample’s cross section, with a mosaic stitching shown in Figure 5(a) and a higher magnification inset
revealing the general grain structure. Most importantly, Figure 5(a) confirms that no cracking was
observed anywhere across the sample. The inset reveals residuals of the melt pools seeming left behind
because of grain formation within the melt pool boundaries. Further characterization was performed using
electron backscatter electron diffraction (EBSD) equipped on the Tescan SEM. EBSD was performed
with an EDAX Velocity Plus camera operating with an accelerated voltage of 10 kV at a step size of
1 µm.

Figure 5(b) gives the texture analysis, whereas Figure 5(c) gives the local strain mapping for sample 10
from the EBSD characterization. All data processing was performed in EDAX’s OIM v8.1 software
(EDAX, New Jersey, USA). Figure 5(b) shows the inverse pole figure mapping of grains. The structure is
not the columnar morphology typically reported for LPBF, in which grains extend across multiple layers
along the build direction. Instead, the grains appear as larger U-shaped structures that stretch across the
residual melt pool width, with multiple smaller grains distributed within them. The arithmetic and area

somewhat bimodal grain structure distribution; the standard deviation exceeding the arithmetic mean

The absence of cracking does not by itself establish that thermal residual strain was avoided, so local
misorientation was mapped to assess retained strain directly. Figure 5(c) gives the kernel average
misorientation at a 0°–5° scale and a more concentrated 2°–5° scale. Some residual strain is still present
along grain boundaries, but the severity of the strain is not high. Values above a 2° misorientation would
suggest higher strain concentration, but strain concentration is minimal across the grain structure.

Sample 3 (96 J/mm3) and sample 5 (77 J/mm3) were randomly selected from the remaining cubes.
Samples 3 and 5, along with a sample of the as-received Elmet powder, were sent to IMR Test Labs (New
York, USA) for chemical analysis. Induction-coupled plasma mass spectroscopy (ICPMS) was used to
measure the transition elements Nb and Zr, inert gas fusion (IGF) was used for measuring the O content,
and combustion analysis measured the C concentration. Table 2 shows the chemical testing results for the

Ni-based powders. The level of O appeared to stay consistent with the measurements performed by IMR
on the powder and the two printed samples, with all below 1,220 ppm.

Octane Elect Super Si-Drift EDS detector. Figure 6(a) shows an EDS spectral map of the given area
collected at the micron scale. Nb and Zr appear to be in solution with one another, and no segregation of
O or C is apparent. Because of the possibility that SEM-EDS cannot resolve the size of secondary
particles in the printed material, a thin-foil lamella was prepared from sample 10 using a Thermo-Fisher
(Massachusetts, USA) Helios 5 plasma focused ion beam (FIB).

The thin foil was characterized by a Thermo-Fisher Spectra Ultra transmission electron microscope
(TEM) operating at 300 kV. The sample was imaged in scanning TEM (STEM) mode with high-angle
annular dark field (HAADF) to observe any atomic number contrast that might indicate secondary phase
formation, as shown in Figure 6(b). Subsequent EDS spectral mapping was performed in the same area
with an Ultra-X EDS detector, also shown in Figure 6(b). A similar lack of any chemical segregation is
observed in the TEM-EDS spectral map compared with that of the SEM-EDS spectral map. Nb has
appreciable oxygen solubility—approximately 4 at% at 1,500°C—and the measured 1,180 ppm
corresponds to roughly 0.7 at%, which is below that limit. The most consistent interpretation is that
oxygen is retained in solid solution in the as-built condition. Zr additions to Nb are nonetheless known to
getter O and form ZrO₂. Therefore, fine ZrO₂ precipitation below the resolution of the present
measurements cannot be excluded, and higher-resolution characterization, such as atom probe
tomography, would be required to observe any fine scale oxides or secondary phases that might be
present on the nano level.

 3. RAM COATING STRATEGY

3.1 Modular Materials Approach

Nb-1Zr is a single-phase solid solution and, as established in Section 1, lacks a strengthening phase for
high-temperature stability. Developing new Nb-based compositions by conventional melting and
atomization is slow and consumes scarce feedstock for each iteration. Figure 7 shows an illustration
following an alternative, which is to treat a single qualified base powder as a platform and modify it at the
particle surface. Dispersants, inoculants, and surface chemistry modifications can be introduced onto the
base powder, with the intended reaction or dispersion occurring during laser melting rather than during a
separate melting operation. This modular approach allows several candidate additions to be evaluated
from one lot of base powder, and any resulting composition remains printable on standard LPBF
equipment. Limitations in dispersant density likely need a balance of concentration addition but should be
optimizable.

This approach is well matched to the qualification framework described in Section 1. Qualifying a new
alloy conventionally requires the property database to be rebuilt for each composition; this task is the
principal cost driver in bringing a material into nuclear service and the reason that the AMMT effort has
concentrated on a small number of well-characterized systems. Modifying a single qualified base powder
at the particle surface leaves the bulk composition and the LPBF parameter set largely intact, so the
process monitoring signatures, thermal history, and defect populations established for the base alloy
remain the reference against which each variant is assessed. Deviations introduced by an addition are then
measurable against that baseline rather than against an entirely new dataset. The Nb-1Zr parameter set
and microstructural baseline reported in Section 2 serve this role for the present work: they define the
reference condition from which functionalized variants depart, and they establish that the base alloy is
printable to high density on unmodified commercial equipment. Extending a qualified base alloy in this
way is a more tractable route to improved high-temperature components than that of qualifying each
candidate composition independently. Furthermore, this approach keeps the resulting materials within the
in situ monitoring and high-throughput characterization infrastructure that AMMT has built.

3.2 Demonstration of Coating Approach with Surrogate Feedstock

Coating development was performed on the alloy Haynes 230 rather than on Nb-1Zr. The available Nb-
1Zr inventory was limited and costly, as noted in Section 2.1, and process development inevitably
consumes powder across failed and exploratory conditions. Alloy 230 was available at low cost and is,
like Nb-1Zr, a solid-solution alloy, making it a reasonable structural surrogate for the coating step. Alloy
230 also exhibits solidification cracking during LPBF (Dryepondt et al., 2024), so it offers a test case in
which a successful inoculation strategy would produce a measurable benefit.

The initial selected dispersant was ZrO2, acquired from USNano (Florida, USA) with a given particle size
of 800 nm. Powders were mixed by resonant acoustic mixing (RAM) using a Resodyn LabRAM II
(Montana, USA), shown in Figure 8(a). Figure 8(b) demonstrates the RAM process, in which a low-

 12

frequency acoustic field generates microscale mixing zones throughout the vessel, breaking up oxide
agglomerates and distributing the particles onto the powder surfaces without the mechanical contact of
grinding media or extended residence times of conventional milling. A 500 g quantity of alloy 230
powders was mixed with 5 g (for 1 wt %) of ZrO₂ powders for 60 min using six cycles of 10 min each,
with a 20 min cooling time between cycles. Researchers experimented with agitation levels of 10–30 g-
level (multiples of 9.81 m/s²) to determine proper agitation versus the increasing temperature of the
canister over the 10 min run time. Additionally, for comparison, a second batch was blended by roller
milling on a U.S. Stoneware jar mill (Ohio, USA), shown in Figure 8(c), for 5 days at 30 rpm.

Figure 8. Powder coating by resonant acoustic mixing. (a) Resodyn LabRAM II acoustic mixer. (b) Schematic of
 acoustic mixing, in which the applied acoustic field breaks up agglomerates and distributes particles uniformly
across the powder surfaces. (c) U.S. Stoneware roller mill used for comparison. (d) Secondary electron images of as-
  received Ni 230, Ni 230 + ZrO2 after RAM, and Ni 230 + ZrO₂ after 5 days of roller milling, shown at common
 magnification.

The two mixing routes produced visibly different coating morphologies, as shown in Figure 8(d). The as-
received Ni 230 particle is smooth and spherical, with the cellular solidification structure of the gas-
atomized surface clearly resolved and no adhered fine particles apart from occasional satellites. After
RAM, the particle surface is uniformly decorated with fine ZrO₂, distributed across the full visible surface
with no bare regions and with the underlying cellular structure obscured by the coating layer. Individual
oxide features are near the resolution limit of the image, indicating that the acoustic field broke up the as-
supplied oxide agglomerates and dispersed them close to their primary particle size. By contrast, the 5-
day roller-milled powder retains large ZrO₂ agglomerates several micrometers across, adhered at discrete
points on the particle surfaces, with substantial areas of bare Ni 230 visible between them. The roller-
milled particles also show flattened facets and localized deformation at contact points, consistent with the
repeated mechanical impacts of jar milling, whereas the RAM particles retain their original spherical
morphology.

The comparison establishes that coating uniformity is set by the mixing method rather than by the oxide
itself: the same ZrO₂ powder produced near-continuous surface coverage in minutes under RAM and
incomplete, agglomerated coverage after 5 days of roller milling. Retention of particle sphericity is

 13

equally relevant for powder bed fusion because flowability and packing density govern layer uniformity
during recoating. On both counts, RAM is the preferred route for producing functionalized feedstock, and
it was used for all subsequent coating work.

 4. CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE WORK

Nb-1Zr was established as a processable refractory feedstock for LPBF, and a route to functionalizing that
feedstock was demonstrated on a surrogate alloy. Following are the principal findings:

• Commercial gas-atomized Nb-1Zr powder centered near 22 µm is directly usable as LPBF feedstock.
 Ultrasonic atomization of rod stock produced spherical Nb-1Zr powder in-house at a coarser median
 size, establishing a laboratory-scale route to alloy variants that are not commercially available, subject
 to classification before use.
• A 20-condition central composite design on the SLM 125HL identified a broad processing window.
 Relative density rises steeply below approximately 90 J/mm3 and plateaus near 99% across the
 remainder of the design, with lack-of-fusion porosity dominating at low VED. No cracking was
 observed in any cross section examined.
• The as-built microstructure consists of grains spanning the melt pool width with a population of finer
 grains distributed within them, giving arithmetic and area averaged grain sizes of 11 ± 16 µm and
 64 ± 34 µm, respectively. Texture is weak, and elevated kernel average misorientation is confined to
 narrow networks along grain boundaries, indicating limited retained residual strain.
• Oxygen concentration was unchanged within measurement scatter from powder (1,180 ppm) to
 printed material (1,160–1,220 ppm), demonstrating that a standard commercial LPBF system with a
 recirculating argon atmosphere retains feedstock chemistry in an interstitially sensitive refractory
 alloy without modification.
• No secondary phases were resolved by SEM-EDS or by HAADF-STEM with EDS mapping. Oxygen
 is most consistently interpreted as retained in solid solution in the as-built condition.
• Resonant acoustic mixing produced near-continuous ZrO2 coverage on Haynes 230 powder in 60 min
 of mixing while preserving particle sphericity, whereas 5 days of roller milling left coarse
 agglomerates and bare surface between them. Coating uniformity is therefore set by the mixing
 method rather than by the oxide, and RAM was adopted for subsequent functionalization work.

Future work will proceed along three lines. Initial LPBF trials of RAM-coated alloy 230 have been
performed and are undergoing characterization; results, including crack density and the fate of the oxide
addition in the melt pool, will be reported in a subsequent milestone. The in-house atomized Nb-1Zr will
be classified and processed by LPBF to confirm that in-house feedstock reaches the density and chemistry
established here for commercial powder. Finally, mechanical property evaluation of LPBF Nb-1Zr, in
particular elevated-temperature creep against the PWC-11 reference, is required before the alloy can be
assessed against reactor service conditions, and functionalized variants will be evaluated against the
baseline established in this report.
