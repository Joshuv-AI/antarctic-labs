Sphere Packings in Higher Dimension (after Boaz Klartag)
Guillaume Aubrun

Abstract
. Let δ n L \delta_{n}^{L} be the maximal density of a lattice sphere packing in the n n -dimensional Euclidean space. We explain how Boaz Klartag proved the inequality δ n L ⩾ c ​ n 2 ​ 2 − n \delta_{n}^{L}\geqslant cn^{2}2^{-n} where c > 0 c>0 is a universal constant. In higher dimension, even for non-lattice sphere packings, this new lower bound is a substantial improvement. Klartag’s proof uses the probabilistic method in two different ways. The first, very standard, relies on the statistical properties of a uniformly chosen random lattice. The second, completely new, studies the stochastic evolution of an ellipsoid constrained to contain non nonzero lattice points in the interior.



This text is an English translation of the notes prepared (in French) for the Bourbaki seminar given by the author in June 2026.

1.
Introduction

In the Euclidean space
(\mathbf{R}^{n},\|\cdot\|)
, we denote by
B(x,r)
the open ball of center
x
and radius
r>0
. A
sphere packing
is a family
(B(x_{i},r))
of balls of the same radius
r
that are pairwise disjoint, which amounts to saying that their centers satisfy
\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|\geqslant 2r
for
i\neq j
. The
density
of such a packing is defined, when it exists, as the limit

\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{\vol\left(B(0,R)\cap\bigcup_{i}B(x_{i},r)\right)}{\vol(B(0,R))}

Let
\delta_{n}
denote the maximum density of a sphere packing in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
. Since the problem is invariant under scaling, we may assume the spheres have radius
1
. Here is the exhaustive list of values known to date.

•

\delta_{1}=1
. Trivial.

•

\delta_{2}=\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{12}}
. This density is achieved for balls centered at the points of the hexagonal lattice.

•

\delta_{3}=\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{18}}
. This is Kepler’s conjecture proved by
[
13
]
. This density is achieved for balls centered at the points of the face-centered cubic lattice.

•

\delta_{8}=\frac{\pi^{4}}{384}

[
23
]
. This density is achieved for balls centered at the points of the
E_{8}
lattice.

•

\delta_{24}=\frac{\pi^{12}}{12!}

[
8
]
. This density is achieved for balls centered at the points of the Leech lattice.

Let
\delta_{n}^{L}
denote the maximum density of a lattice sphere packing, i.e., a sphere packing whose sphere centers form a lattice in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
. Computing
\delta_{n}^{L}
is a fundamental question in the geometry of numbers. The value is known for
n\leqslant 9
and
n=24
. A reference on these questions is
[
10
]
; the case
n=9
was recently resolved in
[
12
]
.

It is obvious that
\delta_{n}^{L}\leqslant\delta_{n}
. In the rare cases where the value of
\delta_{n}
is known, it equals
\delta_{n}^{L}
. However, it is believed that the values of
\delta_{n}^{L}
and
\delta_{n}
differ for all sufficiently large
n
.

This paper is concerned with the estimation of
\delta_{n}
and
\delta_{n}^{L}
as the dimension
n
tends to infinity. Let us first recall the history of these questions.

It is very easy to prove the inequality
\delta_{n}\geqslant 2^{-n}
by considering a maximal family of pairwise disjoint balls of radius
r
and noting that balls with the same centers and radius
2r
cover
\mathbf{R}^{n}
. The inequality
\delta_{n}^{L}\geqslant 2^{-n}
is more delicate; it is a consequence of the Minkowski–Hlawka theorem, stated by Minkowski as early as 1893 and proved in
[
14
]
. We will give a proof in Section
2
using
random
lattices. For large
n
, no explicit example is known of a lattice sphere packing in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
with density greater than
2^{-n}
.

Let us now mention the known upper bounds. The very short paper
[
5
]
proves the inequality

\delta_{n}\leqslant\frac{n+2}{2}\Bigl(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\Bigr)^{n}.

This result was substantially improved by
[
15
]

(1)

\delta_{n}\leqslant(\alpha+o(1))^{n}

where
\alpha\approx 0.66<\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
is an explicit constant. Better estimates of the
o(1)
term were subsequently given by
[
9
]
and
[
19
]
.

This paper is primarily concerned with lower bounds.
The “trivial” bound
2^{-n}
was first improved by
[
18
]
to

(2)

\delta_{n}^{L}\geqslant cn\cdot 2^{-n}.

Here and throughout this paper, the letters
c
and
C
denote unspecified positive reals that do not depend on the dimension.
Other approaches yielding better estimates for the constant
c
were subsequently proposed by
[
11
]
, then
[
3
]
and
[
21
]
.

The next advance is due to
[
22
]
, who showed,
by considering random lattices with algebraic symmetries, that the inequality

\delta_{n}^{L}\geqslant cn(\log\log n)\cdot 2^{-n}

holds for infinitely many
n
. A different approach, combinatorial in spirit and not using lattices
[
6
]
yielded for every dimension the lower bound

\delta_{n}\geqslant\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}-o(1)\Bigr)n\log n\cdot 2^{-n}.

A better result, which we present here in detail, is the following.

Theorem 1

(
[
16
]
)
.

There exists a constant
c>0
such that for every integer
n\geqslant 1
,

\delta_{n}\geqslant\delta_{n}^{L}\geqslant cn^{2}\cdot 2^{-n}.

For an AI-generated argument showing the lower bound
\delta_{n}^{L}\geqslant cn^{2}(\log\log n)2^{-n}
for infinitely many
n
, see the very recent preprint
[
1
]
.

This progress may seem modest given that an exponential gap remains between Klartag’s lower bound and the best upper bound (
1
). Note however that
[
22
]
conjectures that, up to logarithmic corrections, the quantity
2^{n}\delta_{n}^{L}
is bounded above by a polynomial in
n
. We now know that such a polynomial must have degree
\geqslant 2
.

Section
2
presents Rogers’ argument giving the lower bound (
2
). The idea is to construct, given a random lattice, an ellipsoid of large volume compatible with the lattice, i.e., whose interior contains no nonzero lattice point. This ellipsoid is determined by the
n
successive minima of the lattice.

Klartag refines these ideas by using the probabilistic method
twice
: within a random lattice, he considers a random compatible ellipsoid. The definition of this random ellipsoid relies on a stochastic process whose analysis involves all lattice points of sufficiently small norm, and not only the successive minima.

This Brownian exploration process can be defined in any closed convex set, which we do in Section
3
. Klartag’s proof is detailed in Section
4
.

2.
Rogers’ Argument

A
lattice
is a set of the form
\Lambda=A(\mathbf{Z}^{n})
for
A\in\mathsf{GL}_{n}(\mathbf{R})
. The number
\covol(\Lambda)\coloneqq|\det(A)|
does not depend on the choice of the matrix
A
and is called the
covolume
of the lattice
\Lambda
. The set of lattices in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
with covolume
1
is identified with
\mathsf{SL}_{n}(\mathbf{R})/\mathsf{SL}_{n}(\mathbf{Z})
. The Haar measure on
\mathsf{SL}_{n}(\mathbf{R})
induces on this set a finite measure (see
[
4
, Section 2.2]
) which can be normalized to have total mass
1
.

Thus, for every real number
v>0
, the set of lattices in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
with covolume
v
admits a unique probability measure invariant under the action of
\mathsf{SL}_{n}(\mathbf{R})
. Throughout this paper, the expression
random lattice of covolume
v
refers to this probability measure.

We will make central use of the following integration formula, due to
[
20
]
. If
\Lambda\subset\mathbf{R}^{n}
is a random lattice of covolume
v
, then for every function
f\colon\mathbf{R}^{n}\to\mathbf{R}
integrable with respect to the Lebesgue measure

(3)

\mathbf{E}\Bigl[\sum_{x\in\Lambda^{*}}f(x)\Bigr]=\frac{1}{v}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}f

where
\Lambda^{*}\coloneqq\Lambda\setminus\{0\}
.

Let
B_{n}\coloneqq B(0,1)
be the open unit ball of
\mathbf{R}^{n}
and

\omega_{n}\coloneqq\pi^{n/2}/\Gamma\left(\frac{n}{2}+1\right)
its volume. The
minimum norm
of a lattice
\Lambda
is the quantity
r=\min\{\|x\|\,:\,x\in\Lambda^{*}\}
. The balls
\{B(x,r/2)\,:\,x\in\Lambda\}
form a sphere packing of density
r^{n}\omega_{n}/2^{n}\covol(\Lambda)
.

The probabilistic method provides an immediate proof of the Minkowski–Hlawka inequality
\delta_{n}^{L}\geqslant 2^{-n}
. If
\Lambda
is a random lattice of covolume
\omega_{n}
, Siegel’s formula (
3
) applied to the function
f={\bf 1}_{B_{n}}
gives

\mathbf{E}\left[|B_{n}\cap\Lambda^{*}|\right]=1.

Since the number
|B_{n}\cap\Lambda^{*}|
is even, it equals
0
with probability
\geqslant\frac{1}{2}
. Thus, there exists a lattice

\Lambda
in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
with covolume
\omega_{n}
and minimum norm
\geqslant 1
, which implies that the family
\{B(x,\frac{1}{2})\,:\,x\in\Lambda\}
is a sphere packing of density
\geqslant 2^{-n}
.

We now detail Rogers’ proof of the inequality
\delta_{n}^{L}\geqslant cn2^{-n}
. We call an
ellipsoid
the image of the open unit ball under an invertible linear map. The starting point is the observation that if
\Lambda\subset\mathbf{R}^{n}
is a lattice and
\mathcal{E}
is an ellipsoid containing no point of
\Lambda^{*}
, then

(4)

\delta_{n}^{L}\geqslant\frac{\vol(\mathcal{E})}{\covol(\Lambda)}2^{-n}.

Indeed, if
\mathcal{E}=T(B_{n})
for
T\in\mathsf{GL}_{n}(\mathbf{R})
, then the family
\left\{B(x,1)\,:\,x\in 2T^{-1}\Lambda\right\}
is a sphere packing of density
\omega_{n}/\covol(2T^{-1}\Lambda)=2^{-n}\vol(\mathcal{E})/\covol(\Lambda)
.

Let
\Lambda
be a random lattice of covolume
v\coloneqq\frac{\omega_{n}}{n}
.
Consider the function

f(x)\coloneqq\Bigl(\log\frac{1}{\|x\|}\Bigr)_{+}=\Bigl(\log\frac{1}{\|x\|}\Bigr){\bf 1}_{B_{n}}(x).

By Siegel’s formula (
3
), we have

\mathbf{E}\Bigl[\sum_{x\in\Lambda^{*}}f(x)\Bigr]=\frac{1}{v}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}f=\frac{n}{\omega_{n}}\int_{B_{n}}\log\frac{1}{\|x\|}\,\mathrm{d}x.

This integral is computed in polar coordinates as

\frac{1}{\omega_{n}}\int_{B_{n}}\log\frac{1}{\|x\|}\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{\int_{0}^{1}\log(1/r)r^{n-1}\,\mathrm{d}r}{\int_{0}^{1}r^{n-1}\,\mathrm{d}r}=\frac{1/n^{2}}{1/n}=1/n.

It follows that

\mathbf{E}\Bigl[\sum_{x\in\Lambda^{*}}f(x)\Bigr]=1

and in particular, there exists a lattice
\Lambda
of covolume
v
satisfying
\sum_{x\in\Lambda^{*}}f(x)\leqslant 1
. Fix such a lattice.

For
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
, set

\lambda_{i}\coloneqq\inf\{\lambda\,:\,\dim\left(\mathrm{Vect}(\Lambda\cap\lambda B_{n})\right)\geqslant i\}.

The numbers
\lambda_{1}\leqslant\cdots\leqslant\lambda_{n}
are called the
successive minima
of the lattice
\Lambda
. There exist elements
y_{1},\dots,y_{n}
of
\Lambda
forming a basis of
\mathbf{R}^{n}
and satisfying
\|y_{i}\|=\lambda_{i}
. We have

\displaystyle-\log(\lambda_{1}\cdots\lambda_{n})

\displaystyle=\log\frac{1}{\|y_{1}\|}+\dots+\log\frac{1}{\|y_{n}\|}

\displaystyle\leqslant f(y_{1})+\dots+f(y_{n})

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{y\in\Lambda^{*}}f(y)=1.

We deduce the inequality
\lambda_{1}\cdots\lambda_{n}\geqslant 1/e
. Let
(e_{1},\dots,e_{n})
be an orthonormal basis satisfying, for
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
,

\mathrm{Vect}(e_{1},\dots,e_{i})=\mathrm{Vect}(y_{1},\dots,y_{i}).

Consider the ellipsoid

\mathcal{E}=\Bigl\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}e_{i}\,:\,\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\alpha_{i}^{2}}{\lambda_{i}^{2}}<1\Bigr\}

and let
x=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}e_{i}
be an element of
\Lambda^{*}
. Let
j
be the maximal index such that
\alpha_{j}\neq 0
. We have
\|x\|\geqslant\lambda_{j}
, and moreover

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\alpha_{i}^{2}}{\lambda_{i}^{2}}=\sum_{i=1}^{j}\frac{\alpha_{i}^{2}}{\lambda_{i}^{2}}\geqslant\frac{\|x\|^{2}}{\lambda_{j}^{2}}\geqslant 1.

Thus the ellipsoid
\mathcal{E}
contains no point of
\Lambda^{*}
. In view of (
4
), this implies the inequality

\delta_{n}^{L}\geqslant\frac{\vol(\mathcal{E})}{v}2^{-n}=n2^{-n}\lambda_{1}\cdots\lambda_{n}\geqslant e^{-1}n2^{-n}.

This completes the proof of the lower bound (
2
).

3.
Brownian Exploration of a Closed Convex Set

Throughout this section, we fix a Euclidean space
E
of dimension
n
and a closed convex subset
\mathscr{C}\subset E
containing no affine line. We also fix a
standard Brownian motion

(B_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
in
E
, i.e., a stochastic process with values in
E
such that, for every orthonormal basis
(e_{1},\dots,e_{n})
of
E
, the
n
scalar stochastic processes
(\langle B_{t},e_{i}\rangle)_{t\geqslant 0}
, for
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
, are independent Brownian motions. Let
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
be the canonical (complete) filtration of
(B_{t})
. That is, for
t\geqslant 0
,
\mathcal{F}_{t}
is the smallest
\sigma
-algebra containing all negligible sets and making measurable the random variables
(B_{s})_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}
.

We will define a stochastic process with values in
\mathscr{C}
which we call the
Brownian exploration
of
\mathscr{C}
. Informally, the Brownian exploration of
\mathscr{C}
is constructed by conditioning the Brownian motion
(B_{t})
to belong to a face of
\mathscr{C}
reached at an earlier time. This process is a martingale that almost surely reaches an extreme point of
\mathscr{C}
in finite time.

For every
x\in\mathscr{C}
, the set

(5)

V(x,\mathscr{C})\coloneqq\{y\in E:\exists\varepsilon>0\ :\ |t|<\varepsilon\Longrightarrow x+ty\in\mathscr{C}\}

is a vector subspace of
E
. The equality
V(x,\mathscr{C})=E
holds if and only if
x
is an interior point of
\mathscr{C}
, and the equality
V(x,\mathscr{C})=\{0\}
holds if and only if
x
is an extreme point of
\mathscr{C}
. Within the vector subspace
V(x,\mathscr{C})
, the point
0
is interior to the convex set
(\mathscr{C}-x)\cap V(x,\mathscr{C})
.

Proposition 1
.

Let
x_{0}
be an interior point of
\mathscr{C}
. There exists a continuous martingale
(X_{t})
adapted to the filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{t})
and satisfying the stochastic differential equation

(6)

X_{0}=x_{0},\ \ \ \mathrm{d}X_{t}=p_{t}(\mathrm{d}B_{t})

where
p_{t}
denotes the orthogonal projection onto the subspace
V(X_{t},\mathscr{C})
, with the following properties: almost surely,

(a)

for every
t\geqslant 0
, we have
X_{t}\in\mathscr{C}
,

(b)

there exist an extreme point
z
of
\mathscr{C}
and a real
T>0

(
both random
)
such that
X_{t}=z
for all
t\geqslant T
,

(c)

for all
0\leqslant s\leqslant t
, we have
V(X_{t},\mathscr{C})\subset V(X_{s},\mathscr{C})
.

This process
(X_{t})
is called the
Brownian exploration process of
\mathscr{C}
starting from
x_{0}
. Its construction uses the following lemma recursively.

Lemma 2
.

Let
V
be a Euclidean space and
K\subset V
a closed convex set, distinct from
V
and containing
0
in its interior. Let
(\beta_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
be a standard Brownian motion in
V
. The random variable

\tau\coloneqq\inf\{t\geqslant 0\,:\,\beta_{t}\not\in K\}

satisfies
0<\tau<\infty
almost surely. Moreover, almost surely,
\beta_{\tau}\in\partial K
.

Proof.

Since
K\neq V
, by the Hahn–Banach theorem, there exists an element
z\in V
of norm
1
such that the linear form
\langle z,\cdot\rangle
is bounded below on
K
. The scalar stochastic process
(\langle z,\beta_{t}\rangle)_{t\geqslant 0}
is a Brownian motion and therefore satisfies almost surely
\inf\{\langle z,\beta_{t}\rangle\,:\,t\geqslant 0\}=-\infty
(see
[
17
, Corollary 2.3]
), which implies the existence of a real
t
such that
\beta_{t}\not\in K
. We have thus shown that
\tau
is almost surely finite. The remaining properties follow from the fact that the function
t\mapsto\beta_{t}
is almost surely continuous.
∎

Proof of Proposition
1
.

Set
X_{0}=x_{0}
. To construct
(X_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
, define a sequence of stopping times

0=\tau_{0}<\tau_{1}<\dots<\tau_{n}

such that if
t\in[\tau_{i},\tau_{i+1}[
then
V(X_{t},\mathscr{C})=V_{i}
, where
V_{i}\coloneqq V(X_{\tau_{i}},\mathscr{C})
.
Let
\pi_{i}
denote the orthogonal projection onto
V_{i}
. We define
(X_{t})
so that, for
t\in[\tau_{i},\tau_{i+1}[
,

X_{t}=X_{\tau_{i}}+\pi_{i}(B_{t}-B_{\tau_{i}}).

The construction proceeds by induction on
i\in\{0,\dots,n\}
. Assuming
\tau_{0},\dots,\tau_{i}
and
(X_{t})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant\tau_{i}}
have been constructed, define
\tau_{i+1}
as follows.

•

If
V_{i}\neq\{0\}
, set

\tau_{i+1}=\tau_{i}+\inf\{t>0\,:\,V(X_{\tau_{i}}+\pi_{i}(B_{\tau_{i}+t}-B_{\tau_{i}}),\mathscr{C})\neq V_{i})\}.

Apply Lemma
2
to the Euclidean space
V_{i}
, the convex set
K=(\mathscr{C}-X_{\tau_{i}})\cap V_{i}
, and the process
(\beta_{t})
defined by
\beta_{t}\coloneqq\pi_{i}(B_{\tau_{i}+t}-B_{\tau_{i}})
, which is a standard Brownian motion in
V_{i}
. Note also that since
\dim(V_{i})\geqslant 1
and
\mathscr{C}
contains no line, the set
K
is not equal to
V_{i}
. We can therefore conclude that almost surely,
\tau_{i+1}<\infty
and
\dim(V_{i+1})<\dim(V_{i})
.

•

If
V_{i}=\{0\}
, the induction terminates. Set
X_{t}=X_{\tau_{i}}
for all
t\geqslant\tau_{i}
. Also set
(arbitrarily)
\tau_{k}=\tau_{j}+k
for every integer
j<k\leqslant n
.

By construction,
(X_{t})
is a continuous martingale satisfying condition (a), condition (b) with
T=\tau_{n}
and condition (c) of Proposition
1
.
Moreover, for any
t>0
and
0\leqslant i<n

\int_{\tau_{i}\wedge t}^{\tau_{i+1}\wedge t}\,\mathrm{d}X_{s}=\int_{\tau_{i}\wedge t}^{\tau_{i+1}\wedge t}p_{s}(\mathrm{d}B_{s}).

Summing over
i
gives

\int_{0}^{\tau_{i+1}\wedge t}\,\mathrm{d}X_{s}=\int_{0}^{\tau_{i+1}\wedge t}p_{s}(\mathrm{d}B_{s}).

Since on the event
s>\tau_{i+1}
we have
\mathrm{d}X_{s}=0
and
p_{s}=0
, we obtain

X_{t}-X_{0}=\int_{0}^{t}p_{s}(\mathrm{d}B_{s}),

which is the integrated form of (
6
).
∎

Remark 1
.

For
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
, the law of the
i
-th stopping time
\tau_{i}
defined in the preceding proof is a non-atomic measure on
\mathbf{R}_{+}
. Indeed, let
\varepsilon>0
be small enough that the ball
B(x_{0},\varepsilon)
is contained in
\mathscr{C}
, and let
\tau^{\prime}\coloneqq\inf\{t>0\,:\,\|B_{t}-x_{0}\|\geqslant\varepsilon\}
be the corresponding exit time. By the strong Markov property, the law of
\tau_{i}
is the convolution of the law of
\tau^{\prime}
with the law of the
i
-th stopping time of the exploration process of
\mathscr{C}
starting from a point chosen uniformly on the sphere
\partial B(x_{0},\varepsilon)
. Since
\tau^{\prime}
has a non-atomic law
[
7
]
, so does
\tau_{i}
.

We will use this remark to prove Propositions
4
and
5
. However, it is not needed to prove Theorem
1
, provided one replaces these propositions by a weakened variant where the conclusion is in integrated form, such as the one used in inequality (
8
).

In the remainder of this section, fix
x_{0}\in\mathscr{C}
and let
(X_{t})
denote the Brownian exploration process of
\mathscr{C}
starting from
x_{0}
. As in Proposition
1
,
p_{t}
denotes the orthogonal projection onto
V(X_{t},\mathscr{C})
. We will prove two coupling inequalities illustrating the idea that Brownian exploration “diffuses more slowly” than standard Brownian motion.

Proposition 2
.

For any norm
\|\cdot\|
on
E
, for all
t>0
and
\lambda>0
,

\mathbf{P}(\|X_{t}-x_{0}\|\geqslant\lambda)\leqslant 2\,\mathbf{P}(\|B_{t}\|\geqslant\lambda).

Proof.

Consider the process
(B^{\prime}_{t})
defined by the relation
X_{t}=x_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\left(B_{t}+B^{\prime}_{t}\right)
. Since for every
t\geqslant 0

X_{t}=x_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}p_{s}(\mathrm{d}B_{s})

and

B_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{Id}(\mathrm{d}B_{s}),

by linearity of the stochastic integral we have

B^{\prime}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}(\mathrm{Id}-2p_{s})(\mathrm{d}B_{s}).

The quadratic variation matrix of the process
(B^{\prime}_{t})
equals

[B^{\prime}]_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}{{}^{t}}(\mathrm{Id}-2p_{s})(\mathrm{Id}-2p_{s})\mathrm{d}s=t\mathrm{Id}

since
p_{s}
is an orthogonal projection. By a theorem of Lévy
[
17
, Theorem 5.4]
, this implies that
(B^{\prime}_{t})
is a standard Brownian motion in
E
. We therefore have, writing
\|X_{t}-x_{0}\|\leqslant\frac{1}{2}(\|B_{t}\|+\|B^{\prime}_{t}\|)
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(\|X_{t}-x_{0}\|\geqslant\lambda)

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbf{P}\left(\|B_{t}\|+\|B^{\prime}_{t}\|\geqslant 2\lambda\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbf{P}(\|B_{t}\|\geqslant\lambda)+\mathbf{P}(\|B^{\prime}_{t}\|\geqslant\lambda)

and the result follows.
∎

Proposition 3
.

Let
z\in E
and
a
be a real number.
Let

\tau_{1}\coloneqq\inf\{t\geqslant 0\,:\,\langle z,X_{t}\rangle=a\}

and

\tau_{2}\coloneqq\inf\{t\geqslant 0\,:\,\langle z,x_{0}+B_{t}\rangle=a\}.

Then, for every real
T>0
,

\mathbf{P}(\tau_{1}\geqslant T)\geqslant\mathbf{P}(\tau_{2}\geqslant T).

Proof.

Assume without loss of generality that
\|z\|=1
,
x_{0}=0
and
a>0
. The scalar stochastic process
(M_{t})
defined by
M_{t}\coloneqq\langle z,X_{t}\rangle
is a continuous martingale. Its quadratic variation satisfies

[M]_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\|p_{s}(z)\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\leqslant\int_{0}^{t}\|z\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s=t.

The Dambis–Dubins–Schwartz theorem implies (possibly on an enlarged probability space, see
[
17
, Theorem 5.5]
) the existence of a scalar Brownian motion
(\beta_{t})
such that

M_{t}=\beta_{[M]_{t}}

for every real
t\geqslant 0
. Since
[M]_{t}\leqslant t
, we have

\forall t<T,\ \beta_{t}<a\ \Longrightarrow\ \forall t<T,\ M_{t}<a.

The right-hand event is
\{\tau_{1}\geqslant T\}
, and the left-hand event has the same probability as
\{\tau_{2}\geqslant T\}
since
(\langle z,B_{t}\rangle)
is a scalar Brownian motion.
∎

Finally, we state a consequence of Itô’s formula.

Proposition 4
.

Let
\Omega
be an open subset of
E
containing
\mathscr{C}
, and let
F\colon\Omega\to\mathbf{R}
be a
C^{2}
function.
Suppose that for every
t>0
,

(i)\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{E}[\|\nabla F(X_{s})\|^{2}]\,\mathrm{d}s<\infty,\ \ \ (ii)\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{E}[\|\nabla^{2}F(X_{s})\|]\,\mathrm{d}s<\infty.

Then the function
g:t\mapsto\mathbf{E}[F(X_{t})]
is
C^{1}
and satisfies for every
t\geqslant 0

g^{\prime}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{E}\left[\tr(\nabla^{2}F(X_{t})p_{t})\right].

Proof.

Itô’s formula reads

F(X_{t})=F(x_{0})+\int_{0}^{t}\langle\nabla F(X_{s}),p_{s}(\mathrm{d}B_{s})\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\left[\tr(\nabla^{2}F(X_{s})p_{s})\right]\,\mathrm{d}s.

The process
(M_{t})
defined by
M_{t}\coloneqq\int_{0}^{t}\langle\nabla F(X_{s}),p_{s}(\mathrm{d}B_{s})\rangle
is a local martingale. Hypothesis (i), combined with the fact that
p_{s}
is a contraction, implies that
(M_{t})
is a martingale and therefore
\mathbf{E}[M_{t}]=0
for every
t\geqslant 0
. Hypothesis (ii) ensures that
g(t)
is well defined for
t\geqslant 0
and equals

g(t)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{E}\left[\tr(\nabla^{2}F(X_{s})p_{s})\right].

It follows from the proof of Proposition
1
and Remark
1
that the discontinuities of the function
t\mapsto p_{t}
are finite in number and their law is non-atomic. Since the function
t\mapsto\nabla^{2}F(X_{t})
is almost surely continuous, this implies that the function
t\mapsto\mathbf{E}\left[\tr(\nabla^{2}F(X_{t})p_{t})\right]
is continuous. Thus
g
is
C^{1}
and satisfies the announced formula.
∎

4.
Klartag’s Proof

4.1.
Preliminaries

The space
\mathrm{Sym}_{n}
of real symmetric
n\times n
matrices is a vector space of dimension
n(n+1)/2
. We equip it with the Euclidean structure given by the inner product
\langle A,B\rangle=\tr(AB)
. For
A
and
B
in
\mathrm{Sym}_{n}
, we write
A\preccurlyeq B
when the matrix
B-A
is positive semidefinite. To every symmetric positive definite matrix
A
we associate the open ellipsoid

\mathcal{E}_{A}\coloneqq\{x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}\,:\,\langle Ax,x\rangle<1\}=A^{-1/2}(B_{n})

which satisfies
\vol(\mathcal{E}_{A})=\omega_{n}/\sqrt{\det(A)}
. Its boundary
\partial\mathcal{E}_{A}
is the set of vectors
x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
satisfying
\langle Ax,x\rangle=1
.

Given a lattice
\Lambda\subset\mathbf{R}^{n}
, consider the closed convex set

\mathscr{C}_{\Lambda}\coloneqq\{A\in\mathrm{Sym}_{n}\,:\,\langle Ax,x\rangle\geqslant 1\textnormal{ for all }x\in\Lambda^{*}\}.

A symmetric positive definite matrix
A
belongs to
\mathscr{C}_{\Lambda}
if and only if the ellipsoid
\mathcal{E}_{A}
contains no point of
\Lambda^{*}
. The following lemma describes the set
V(A,\mathscr{C}_{\Lambda})
defined by (
5
).

Lemma 3
.

Let
\Lambda
be a lattice in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
and
A\in\mathscr{C}_{\Lambda}
. Then
A
is positive definite and satisfies the inequality

(7)

\sqrt{\det(A)}\geqslant\frac{\omega_{n}2^{-n}}{\delta_{n}^{L}\covol(\Lambda)}.

Moreover,

V(A,\mathscr{C}_{\Lambda})=\{M\in\mathrm{Sym}_{n}\,:\,\langle Mx,x\rangle=0\textnormal{ for all }x\in\Lambda^{*}\cap\partial\mathcal{E}_{A}\}.

Proof.

The positive semi-definiteness of
A
follows from the fact that the set
\left\{x/\|x\|\,:\,x\in\Lambda^{*}\right\}
is dense in the unit sphere of
\mathbf{R}^{n}
. If
A
is positive definite, inequality (
4
) reads

\delta_{n}^{L}\geqslant\frac{\vol(\mathcal{E}_{A})}{\covol(\Lambda)}2^{-n}=\frac{\omega_{n}/\sqrt{\det(A)}}{\covol(\Lambda)}2^{-n}

from which we deduce (
7
). If
A
is only assumed positive semidefinite, the conclusion still holds by applying this argument to
A+\varepsilon\mathrm{Id}
for arbitrarily small
\varepsilon>0
.

Let
M\in V(A,\mathscr{C}_{\Lambda})
. For every
x\in\Lambda^{*}\cap\partial\mathcal{E}_{A}
, we have
\langle Ax,x\rangle=1
. The definition of
V(A,\mathscr{C}_{\Lambda})
implies
\langle(A+tM)x,x\rangle\geqslant 1
for
|t|
small enough, hence
\langle Mx,x\rangle=0
.

Conversely, let
M\in\mathrm{Sym}_{n}
satisfy
\langle Mx,x\rangle=0
for all
x\in\Lambda^{*}\cap\partial\mathcal{E}_{A}
. Since a lattice is discrete and
A
is positive definite, there exists
\alpha>1
such that
\langle Ax,x\rangle\geqslant\alpha
for all
x\in\Lambda^{*}\setminus\partial\mathcal{E}_{A}
. If
t
is real with
|t|
sufficiently small, we have
\alpha^{-1}A\preccurlyeq A+tM
, and thus for every
x\in\Lambda^{*}

\displaystyle\langle(A+tM)x,x\rangle=1

\displaystyle\ \textnormal{ if }\ x\in\partial\mathcal{E}_{A}

\displaystyle\langle(A+tM)x,x\rangle\geqslant\alpha^{-1}\langle Ax,x\rangle\geqslant 1

\displaystyle\ \textnormal{ if }\ x\not\in\partial\mathcal{E}_{A}

which implies
A+tM\in\mathscr{C}_{\Lambda}
. We have thus shown
M\in V(A,\mathscr{C}_{\Lambda})
.
∎

The matrices
A\in\mathscr{C}_{\Lambda}
of minimal determinant, for which equality holds in (
7
), correspond to a packing of ellipsoids
\{\mathcal{E}_{A}+x\,:\,x\in 2\Lambda\}
of optimal density
\delta_{n}^{L}
. Since the function
\log\circ\det
is strictly concave on
\mathscr{C}_{\Lambda}
, these minimum points are extreme points of
\mathscr{C}_{\Lambda}
.

4.2.
Structure of the Proof

Fix a standard Brownian motion
(W_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
in the Euclidean space
\mathrm{Sym}_{n}
.
We will consider a Brownian exploration process constructed from
(W_{t})
as in Section
3
.

Let
\Lambda\subset\mathbf{R}^{n}
be a lattice with minimum norm
>1
, which means that the matrix
\mathrm{Id}
lies in the interior of
\mathscr{C}_{\Lambda}
. Let
(A_{t})
be the Brownian exploration process of
\mathscr{C}_{\Lambda}
starting from
\mathrm{Id}
. To simplify notation, we will pretend that the conclusions of Proposition
1
hold everywhere on the underlying probability space; the argument becomes rigorous by adding the qualifier “almost surely” to each statement.

By Proposition
1
, for every
t
, the matrix
A_{t}
lies in
\mathscr{C}_{\Lambda}
. For
t
large enough, it is an extreme point of
\mathscr{C}_{\Lambda}
. The Brownian exploration process “chooses” an extreme sphere packing. We will see that it is possible to bound below the density of this packing, which amounts to bounding above
\det(A_{t})
.

Since
(A_{t})
is a martingale and the function
\log\circ\det
is concave, the function
g\colon t\mapsto\mathbf{E}[\log\det A_{t}]
is decreasing. We will estimate its rate of decrease as a function of the distribution of short vectors in
\Lambda
. This requires some notation. Consider the Gaussian tail function
\Phi
defined for a real
a
as

\Phi(a)\coloneqq\int_{a}^{\infty}e^{-x^{2}/2}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\sqrt{2\pi}}.

In other words,
\Phi(a)
is the probability that a standard Gaussian random variable takes a value
\geqslant a
. Next, for a real
t>0
, define a radial function
f_{t}\colon\mathbf{R}^{n}\to\mathbf{R}
by

f_{t}(x)\coloneqq 2\,\Phi\Bigl(\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{\|x\|^{2}}\Bigr)\Bigr).

Finally, given a lattice
\Lambda
, set

K_{t}(\Lambda)\coloneqq\sum_{\mathclap{x\in\Lambda^{*}\cap D_{t}}}f_{t}(x)

where
D_{t}\coloneqq\{x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}\,:\,(1-3\sqrt{tn})\|x\|^{2}\leqslant 1\}
.

Set
t_{\max}\coloneqq\frac{20\log n}{n^{2}}
. Theorem
1
follows from the next two propositions.

Proposition 5
.

Let
\Lambda\subset\mathbf{R}^{n}
be a lattice with minimum norm
>1
. Let
(A_{t})
be the Brownian exploration process of
\mathscr{C}_{\Lambda}
starting from
\mathrm{Id}
. The function
g\colon t\mapsto\mathbf{E}[\log\det A_{t}]
is
C^{1}
and satisfies, for
t\in[0,t_{\max}]
, the inequality

g^{\prime}(t)\leqslant-\frac{n^{2}}{4}+K_{t}(\Lambda)+3\sqrt{t}n^{5/2}+\varepsilon_{n}

where
(\varepsilon_{n})
is a sequence tending to
0
as
n\to\infty
.

Proposition 6
.

Let
t\in[0,t_{\max}]
. If
\Lambda\subset\mathbf{R}^{n}
is a random lattice of covolume
\omega_{n}
, then

\mathbf{E}[K_{t}(\Lambda)]\leqslant(2+\varepsilon_{n})\exp\Bigl(\frac{n^{2}t}{8}\Bigr)

where
(\varepsilon_{n})
is a sequence tending to
0
as
n\to\infty
.

Let us deduce Theorem
1
by combining these two propositions. Let
\Lambda\subset\mathbf{R}^{n}
be a random lattice of covolume
\omega_{n}
. Set
T\coloneqq\frac{16\log n}{n^{2}}
. This satisfies
e^{n^{2}T/8}=n^{2}
.

By Proposition
6
(the expectation referring to the random lattice
\Lambda
),

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\Bigl[\int_{0}^{T}K_{t}(\Lambda)\,\mathrm{d}t\Bigr]

\displaystyle\leqslant(2+\varepsilon_{n})\int_{0}^{T}\exp\Bigl(\frac{n^{2}t}{8}\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leqslant(2+\varepsilon_{n})\frac{8}{n^{2}}\exp\Bigl(\frac{n^{2}T}{8}\Bigr)=8(2+\varepsilon_{n}).

Take
n
large enough so that
8(2+\varepsilon_{n})<20
. Markov’s inequality gives

\mathbf{P}\Bigl(\int_{0}^{T}K_{t}(\Lambda)\,\mathrm{d}t\geqslant 40\Bigr)<\frac{1}{2}.

Recall also that a random lattice of covolume
\omega_{n}
has minimum norm
>1
with probability
\geqslant\frac{1}{2}
. We deduce that there exists a lattice
\Lambda
of covolume
\omega_{n}
and minimum norm
>1
for which

\int_{0}^{T}K_{t}(\Lambda)\,\mathrm{d}t\leqslant 40.

Applying Proposition
5
to such a lattice gives (the expectation now referring to the stochastic process
(A_{t})
)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[\log\det A_{T}]=g(T)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}g^{\prime}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t

(8)

\displaystyle\leqslant-\frac{n^{2}T}{4}+\int_{0}^{T}K_{t}(\Lambda)\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}3\sqrt{t}n^{5/2}\,\mathrm{d}t+\varepsilon_{n}T

\displaystyle\leqslant-4\log(n)+40+2T^{3/2}n^{5/2}+\varepsilon_{n}T

\displaystyle\leqslant-4\log(n)+C

since
T^{3/2}n^{5/2}=o(1)
. There therefore exists a matrix
A\in\mathscr{C}_{\Lambda}
such that

\det(A)\leqslant\exp(-4\log(n)+C)=\frac{e^{C}}{n^{4}}

and hence, by (
4
),

\delta_{n}^{L}\geqslant\frac{2^{-n}}{\sqrt{\det(A)}}\geqslant cn^{2}\cdot 2^{-n}.

4.3.
Proof of Proposition
5

We will use estimates for the operator norm (denoted
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{op}}
) of random matrices. Recall that
(W_{t})
is a standard Brownian motion in the Euclidean space
\mathrm{Sym}_{n}
. The law of
W_{1}
is known as the Gaussian Orthogonal Ensemble (GOE). The following inequality is a consequence of the isoperimetric inequality in Gaussian space (see equation (6.38) in
[
2
]
): for every
\varepsilon>0
,

\mathbf{P}(\|W_{1}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\geqslant 2\sqrt{n}+\varepsilon)\leqslant\exp(-\varepsilon^{2}/2).

For every
t>0
, the random variable
W_{t}/\sqrt{t}
has the same distribution as
W_{1}
. Proposition
2
then implies, for every
\varepsilon>0
, the inequality

(9)

\mathbf{P}\bigl(\|A_{t}-\mathrm{Id}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\geqslant\sqrt{t}(2\sqrt{n}+\varepsilon)\bigr)\leqslant 2\exp(-\varepsilon^{2}/2).

We will draw two consequences from this inequality. First, for every non-negative integer
k
and every
t>0
,

(10)

\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{E}\left[\|A_{s}\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{k}\right]\,\mathrm{d}s<\infty\ \textnormal{ and }\ \int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{E}\left[\|A_{s}^{-1}\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{k}\right]\,\mathrm{d}s<\infty.

The first of these inequalities follows from (
9
) since sub-Gaussian random variables have all their moments finite; the second follows from the first by writing

\|A_{t}^{-1}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leqslant\frac{\|A_{t}\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{n-1}}{\det(A_{t})}

and using the fact, stated in Lemma
3
, that the determinant is bounded below by a value
>0
on
\mathscr{C}_{\Lambda}
.

Second, this inequality will allow us to restrict our study to the case where
A_{t}
is very close to the identity matrix. For every
t>0
, introduce the “favorable” event

G_{t}=\bigl\{\|A_{t}-\mathrm{Id}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leqslant 3\sqrt{tn}\bigr\}.

It follows from (
9
) that this event has probability very close to
1
, since the complementary event satisfies

\mathbf{P}(\overline{G_{t}})\leqslant 2e^{-n/2}.

We now begin the proof of Proposition
5
.
Apply Itô’s formula in the form of Proposition
4
to the function
F=\log\circ\det
. For
A\in\mathscr{C}_{\Lambda}
and
B\in\mathrm{Sym}_{n}
, the Taylor expansion

\displaystyle\log\det(A+\varepsilon B)

\displaystyle=\log\det A+\log\det(\mathrm{Id}+\varepsilon A^{-1}B)

\displaystyle=\log\det A+\varepsilon\tr(A^{-1}B)-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\tr((A^{-1}B)^{2})+O(\varepsilon^{3})

gives

\displaystyle\langle(\nabla F)(A),B\rangle

\displaystyle=\tr(A^{-1}B)

\displaystyle\langle(\nabla^{2}F)(A)B,B\rangle

\displaystyle=-\tr(A^{-1}BA^{-1}B).

Writing
\|A\|\coloneqq(\tr A^{2})^{1/2}
, we have
\|\nabla F(A)\|\leqslant\|A^{-1}\|\leqslant\sqrt{n}\|A^{-1}\|_{\mathrm{op}}
and
\|\nabla^{2}F(A)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leqslant\|A^{-1}\|^{2}\leqslant n\|A^{-1}\|^{2}_{\mathrm{op}}
. It follows from (
10
) that the hypotheses of Proposition
4
are satisfied. Consequently, the function
g
is
C^{1}
and satisfies for
t\geqslant 0

g^{\prime}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{E}\left[\tr(\nabla^{2}F(X_{t})p_{t})\right]

where
p_{t}
is the orthogonal projection onto the subspace
V(A_{t},\mathscr{C}_{\Lambda})
, whose dimension we denote
N_{t}
.

The inequality
\tr(A^{-1}BA^{-1}B)\geqslant\|A\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{-2}\tr(B^{2})
implies
\nabla^{2}F(A)\preccurlyeq-\|A\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{-2}\mathrm{Id}
. Thus, for
t\geqslant 0
,

g^{\prime}(t)\leqslant-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{E}\left[\|A_{t}\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{-2}N_{t}\right]\leqslant-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{E}\left[{\bf 1}_{G_{t}}\|A_{t}\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{-2}N_{t}\right].

If the event
G_{t}
holds, then

\|A_{t}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leqslant 1+3\sqrt{tn}
and therefore

\|A_{t}\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{-2}\geqslant(1+3\sqrt{tn})^{-2}\geqslant 1-6\sqrt{tn}.

Using the bound
N_{t}\leqslant\dim\mathrm{Sym}_{n}\leqslant n^{2}
, we obtain

g^{\prime}(t)\leqslant-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{E}\left[{\bf 1}_{G_{t}}N_{t}\right]+3\sqrt{t}n^{5/2}.

Let
\mathcal{E}_{t}\coloneqq\mathcal{E}_{A_{t}}
be the ellipsoid associated with the matrix
A_{t}
. By Lemma
3
,

V(A_{t},\mathscr{C}_{\Lambda})=\{x\otimes x\,:\,x\in\Lambda^{*}\cap\partial\mathcal{E}_{t}\}^{\perp}

and therefore (the factor
\frac{1}{2}
coming from
(-x)\otimes(-x)=x\otimes x
),

N_{t}\geqslant\dim\mathrm{Sym}_{n}-\frac{1}{2}|\Lambda^{*}\cap\partial\mathcal{E}_{t}|.

We therefore have

\displaystyle g^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle\leqslant-\frac{1}{2}\dim\mathrm{Sym}_{n}\times\mathbf{P}(G_{t})+\frac{1}{4}\mathbf{E}\left[{\bf 1}_{G_{t}}|\Lambda^{*}\cap\partial\mathcal{E}_{t}|\right]+3\sqrt{t}n^{5/2}

\displaystyle\leqslant-\frac{n^{2}}{4}+\frac{1}{4}\mathbf{E}\left[{\bf 1}_{G_{t}}|\Lambda^{*}\cap\partial\mathcal{E}_{t}|\right]+3\sqrt{t}n^{5/2}+n^{2}e^{-n/2}.

If the event
G_{t}
holds, then
A_{t}\succcurlyeq(1-3\sqrt{tn})\mathrm{Id}
and consequently every
x\in\Lambda^{*}
satisfies
\langle A_{t}x,x\rangle\geqslant(1-3\sqrt{tn})\|x\|^{2}
. Thus the condition
x\in\partial\mathcal{E}_{t}
, equivalent to
\langle A_{t}x,x\rangle=1
, can only be satisfied if
x\in D_{t}
. We therefore have

{\bf 1}_{G_{t}}|\Lambda^{*}\cap\partial\mathcal{E}_{t}|=\sum_{\mathclap{x\in\Lambda^{*}\cap D_{t}}}{\bf 1}_{G_{t}}{\bf 1}_{\{x\in\partial\mathcal{E}_{t}\}}

and by linearity of expectation,

(11)

\mathbf{E}\left[{\bf 1}_{G_{t}}|\Lambda^{*}\cap\partial\mathcal{E}_{t}|\right]\leqslant\sum_{\mathclap{x\in\Lambda^{*}\cap D_{t}}}\mathbf{P}(x\in\partial\mathcal{E}_{t}).

We next use the following observation: for every
t>0
and
x\in\Lambda^{*}
,

x\in\partial\mathcal{E}_{t}\iff\exists s\in[0,t]\,:\,\langle A_{s}x,x\rangle\leqslant 1.

This equivalence follows from two facts. On one hand,
if
s\leqslant t
then
V(A_{t},\mathscr{C}_{\Lambda})\subset V(A_{s},\mathscr{C}_{\Lambda})
by Proposition
1
(iii), so the equality
\langle A_{s}x,x\rangle=1
implies
\langle A_{t}x,x\rangle=1
. On the other hand, since
A_{s}\in\mathscr{C}_{\Lambda}
, saying
\langle A_{s}x,x\rangle\leqslant 1
is equivalent to
\langle A_{s}x,x\rangle=1
.

This allows us to write, using the coupling inequality proved in Proposition
3
and setting
y\coloneqq\frac{x}{\|x\|}
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(x\in\partial\mathcal{E}_{t})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}\Bigl(\inf_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}\langle A_{s}x,x\rangle\leqslant 1\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbf{P}\Bigl(\inf_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}\langle(\mathrm{Id}+W_{s})x,x\rangle\leqslant 1\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}\Bigl(\inf_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}\langle W_{s}y,y\rangle\leqslant\frac{1}{\|x\|^{2}}-1\Bigr).

The process
\beta_{s}\coloneqq\langle W_{s}y,y\rangle
is a scalar Brownian motion. A consequence of the reflection principle
[
17
, Theorem 2.4]
is the following formula, for every
a>0
:

\mathbf{P}\Bigl(\inf_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}\beta_{s}\leqslant-a\Bigr)=\mathbf{P}\Bigl(\sup_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}\beta_{s}\geqslant a\Bigr)=2\mathbf{P}(|\beta_{t}|\geqslant a)=2\Phi\Bigl(\frac{a}{\sqrt{t}}\Bigr).

Applied with
a=1-\|x\|^{-2}
, this formula yields the inequality

\mathbf{P}(x\in\partial\mathcal{E}_{t})\leqslant 2\Phi\Bigl(\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{\|x\|^{2}}\Bigr)\Bigr)\eqqcolon f_{t}(x).

We can therefore deduce from (
11
) that

\mathbf{E}\left[{\bf 1}_{G_{t}}|\Lambda^{*}\cap\partial\mathcal{E}_{t}|\right]\leqslant\sum_{\mathclap{x\in\Lambda^{*}\cap D_{t}}}f_{t}(x)\eqqcolon K_{t}(\Lambda)

which completes the proof of Proposition
5
.

4.4.
Proof of Proposition
6

We may assume
n
is large enough that
3\sqrt{nt_{\max}}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}
. Let
\Lambda\subset\mathbf{R}^{n}
be a random lattice of covolume
\omega_{n}
.
Apply Siegel’s formula (
3
) to the function
f_{t}{\bf 1}_{D_{t}}
to write

\mathbf{E}\left[K_{t}(\Lambda)\right]=\frac{1}{\omega_{n}}\int_{D_{t}}f_{t}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{2}{\omega_{n}}\int_{D_{t}}\Phi\Bigl(\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{\|x\|^{2}}\Bigr)\Bigr)

After integration in polar coordinates, we obtain

\mathbf{E}\left[K_{t}(\Lambda)\right]=2n\int_{0}^{(1-3\sqrt{nt})^{-1/2}}r^{n-1}\Phi\Bigl(\frac{1-r^{-2}}{\sqrt{t}}\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}r.

Making the substitution
y=\frac{1-r^{-2}}{\sqrt{t}}
, we get

\mathbf{E}\left[K_{t}(\Lambda)\right]=n\sqrt{t}\int_{-\infty}^{3\sqrt{n}}\Phi(y)(1-y\sqrt{t})^{-\frac{n+2}{2}}\,\mathrm{d}y.

An integration by parts then yields

\mathbf{E}\left[K_{t}(\Lambda)\right]=2\Phi(3\sqrt{n})(1-3\sqrt{nt})^{-n/2}+2\int_{-\infty}^{3\sqrt{n}}e^{-y^{2}/2}(1-y\sqrt{t})^{-n/2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\sqrt{2\pi}}.

Using the inequality
\frac{1}{1-x}\leqslant\exp(2x)
valid for
x\in[0,\frac{1}{2}]
and the inequality
\Phi(x)\leqslant\exp(-x^{2}/2)
valid for all
x\geqslant 0
, we get

\Phi(3\sqrt{n})(1-3\sqrt{nt})^{-n/2}\leqslant\exp\bigl(-4.5n+3n^{3/2}\sqrt{t}\bigr).

Since
t\leqslant t_{\max}=\frac{20\log n}{n^{2}}
, this quantity tends to
0
as
n\to\infty
.

We next use the inequality
\frac{1}{1-x}\leqslant\exp(x+x^{2})
valid for all
x\leqslant\frac{1}{2}
to obtain

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{3\sqrt{n}}e^{-y^{2}/2}(1-y\sqrt{t})^{-n/2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\sqrt{2\pi}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{-\infty}^{3\sqrt{n}}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{y^{2}}{2}+\frac{ny\sqrt{t}}{2}+\frac{ny^{2}t}{2}\Bigr)\,\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\sqrt{2\pi}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{y^{2}}{2(1-nt)^{-1}}+\frac{ny\sqrt{t}}{2}\Bigr)\,\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\sqrt{2\pi}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-nt}}\exp\Bigl(\frac{n^{2}t}{8(1-nt)}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp\bigl(\frac{n^{3}t^{2}}{8(1-nt)}\bigr)}{\sqrt{1-nt}}\times\exp\Bigl(\frac{n^{2}t}{8}\Bigr).

This computation used the Laplace transform of Gaussian densities: for
\sigma=(1-nt)^{-1/2}
and
\alpha=n\sqrt{t}/2
,

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{y^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\Bigr)\exp(\alpha y)\,\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\sqrt{2\pi}}=\sigma\exp\Bigl(\frac{\alpha^{2}\sigma^{2}}{2}\Bigr).

Under the hypothesis
t\leqslant t_{\max}=\frac{20\log n}{n^{2}}
, the factor in front of
\exp(n^{2}t/8)
tends to
1
as
n\to\infty
, which completes the proof of Proposition
6
.
