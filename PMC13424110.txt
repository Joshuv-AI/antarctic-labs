Effects of midsole support plate material on lower-limb kinetics and muscle activation during basketball-specific movements
Authors: Zhouen Peng, Tong Han, Yuhang Li, Yuan Gao, Shengwei Jia, Peng Du, Siqin Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigated whether midsole support plate material alters lower-limb biomechanics during common basketball-specific tasks. Twenty recreational male collegiate basketball players performed countermovement jumps, 45° cutting, and lateral shuffling in a standardized shoe under three conditions: carbon fiber plate, nylon plate, and no plate. Ground reaction forces, lower-limb joint moments (metatarsophalangeal, ankle, and knee), and surface electromyography (sEMG) from key lower-limb muscles were collected and processed using a consistent inverse-dynamics and sEMG processing pipeline. Across tasks, external loading profiles and joint moment magnitudes were broadly comparable between footwear conditions, indicating limited influence of plate material on gross kinetics under the tested movements. Muscle activation outcomes showed only task- and muscle-specific differences, with changes most evident in the quadriceps during cutting, while co-activation ratios at the ankle and knee remained similar across conditions. Overall, modifying midfoot support plate material within a single shoe configuration produced minimal changes in external kinetics and joint moments, with only modest, movement-dependent neuromuscular differences, providing practical guidance for basketball footwear design that targets midfoot support without materially increasing external loads.

Introduction

Basketball is a high-intensity, multidirectional sport that places substantial mechanical and neuromuscular demands on the lower limbs1. Players are frequently required to perform explosive movements such as vertical jumps, sudden accelerations and decelerations, cutting, and lateral shuffling, often under reactive or unpredictable in-game conditions2, 3. These tasks are not only energetically demanding but also expose the lower extremities to repeated high loads and rapid joint torque generation, contributing to a high incidence of non-contact injuries4, 5, 6, 7, 8. Common lower limb injuries include metatarsal stress fractures, plantar fasciitis, and lateral ankle sprains, many of which are associated with improper load attenuation and limited neuromuscular control during high-impact or high-demand movements such as landing or change-of-direction tasks9, 10.

From a biomechanical perspective, many of these injuries can be linked to excessive ground reaction forces (GRFs), unbalanced plantar pressure distributions, and suboptimal muscle activation timing or coordination1, 11, 12. These risk factors are especially pronounced during tasks involving rapid deceleration and multidirectional movement. Consequently, researchers and footwear designers have placed increasing emphasis on shoe-related interventions that aim to reduce external loading while optimizing internal control strategies13. Among the most discussed footwear parameters is midsole bending stiffness, typically modulated via embedded support plates located in the forefoot region. Bending stiffness, defined as the resistance to sagittal plane deformation about the metatarsophalangeal (MTP) joint, directly influences foot–ground interactions, affecting energy storage and return, force transfer, and muscle demand14, 15.

To achieve controlled alterations in midsole stiffness without compromising shoe weight or flexibility, many performance basketball shoes now incorporate embedded plates made from carbon fiber, thermoplastic polyurethane, or nylon composites16. These support plates enhance forefoot stiffness, improve torsional rigidity, and may limit excessive joint excursions during high-load tasks17, 18, 19. However, the biomechanical effects of these modifications are not unidirectional. While increased stiffness may enhance energy return and mechanical efficiency, it may also influence neuromuscular strategies. For example, a stiffer forefoot may limit foot compliance, potentially increasing muscle activation in proximal joints to compensate for reduced deformation. Conversely, more compliant structures may allow natural motion but increase demands on joint stabilizers20.

Although support plate technology has become increasingly common in both commercial and elite basketball footwear, the scientific understanding of its biomechanical implications remains incomplete. Most prior studies have focused on linear running or controlled laboratory walking tasks, often using commercially available footwear that introduces confounding variables such as variations in outsole traction, upper material, or heel-to-toe drop21, 22. This limits the ability to isolate the effects of support plate material. Moreover, the majority of investigations have emphasized kinematic outcomes, whereas kinetic variables and surface electromyographic (sEMG) responses—which reflect both external mechanical loading and internal neuromuscular control—have received comparatively little attention21, 22.

A particularly underexplored question concerns how different support plate materials, rather than the mere presence or absence of plates, affect lower limb biomechanics. Carbon fiber plates are lightweight and offer high stiffness and energy return, but may reduce foot mobility and increase localized loading. In contrast, nylon-based plates provide moderate stiffness and may better accommodate natural foot motion, potentially modulating muscle activation patterns in a more favorable way. However, how these material-specific properties influence lower limb joint loading and muscle coordination during sport-specific tasks such as jumping or cutting is still unclear.

Vertical jumping and side cutting represent two of the most mechanically demanding and frequently executed movements in basketball, each characterized by distinct loading profiles23. Vertical jump performance relies on rapid concentric force generation, while cutting tasks require controlled deceleration, frontal-plane stability, and coordinated redirection of motion. Lateral shuffling is also commonly used in defensive play, imposing unique demands on ankle and knee stability. These task-specific requirements make them ideal for assessing how midsole stiffness influences both kinetic responses and neuromuscular adaptations.

Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the biomechanical effects of three midsole support plate configurations—carbon fiber, nylon, and no plate—on lower limb performance during countermovement jump (CMJ), 45° cutting, and lateral shuffling in basketball athletes. Using a standardized shoe model to control for confounding design variables, we collected synchronized ground reaction force and surface electromyography data to characterize both external loading and neuromuscular activation patterns based on root mean square (RMS) amplitudes.

Based on material-specific differences in stiffness, we hypothesized that (1) carbon fiber plates, due to their high rigidity, would elicit greater quadriceps and gastrocnemius RMS activation compared with the no-plate and nylon plate conditions; (2) nylon plates would induce moderate RMS responses, closer to the no-plate condition; and (3) external mechanical loading variables (GRFs and joint moments) would remain largely unaffected across footwear conditions. The results of this study are expected to provide insights into how forefoot stiffness affects biomechanical function in basketball-specific tasks and to inform future footwear design, athlete-specific customization, and injury prevention strategies.

Materials and methods

Participants

Sample size was estimated using G*Power (v3.1.9.2), based on a one-factor repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) with three conditions (effect size f = 0.25, α = 0.05, power = 0.80). This analysis indicated a minimum required sample size of 18. To maintain statistical power and account for potential dropout, 20 male collegiate basketball players were recruited.

All participants were recreational male basketball players with competitive experience at the collegiate level, including participation in the Hebei Collegiate Basketball Championships, but none had professional or semi-professional playing experience. Their demographic characteristics were as follows (mean ± standard deviation [SD]): age, 23.06 ± 1.69 years; height, 182.68 ± 3.78 cm; weight, 75.12 ± 6.65 kg; body mass index (BMI), 22.53 ± 2.20 kg/m2. All participants wore European size 43 shoes and were right-leg dominant.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) no history of lower extremity injury, fracture, or surgery; (2) no diagnosed vestibular, visual, or neurological disorders; and (3) no participation in vigorous physical activity within 24 h before testing. Exclusion criteria included clinically diagnosed foot deformities (e.g., flatfoot), musculoskeletal injury within the past six months, or systemic illness.

All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Qinhuangdao First Hospital (Approval No. 2024Y008) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Footwear and midsole support plates

A commercially available basketball shoe (ABAR123-5, Li-Ning, China) served as the standardized model across all test conditions. The shoe featured a mid-top profile, neutral cushioning, and a flat outsole designed for indoor court use. All participants wore European size 43 (internal length: 270 mm) to ensure consistent fit and minimize size-related variability. The upper construction and outsole geometry were identical in all conditions (as shown in Fig. 1).

To investigate the biomechanical effects of midfoot support plate stiffness, three internal footwear configurations were created. In the control condition, the shoe retained its original midsole construction without any structural insert (no support plate; Fig. 2a). In the two experimental conditions, a 3 mm-thick support plate was embedded in the midfoot region between the insole and the outsole. The plates were fabricated from carbon fiber (carbon fiber plate condition; Fig. 2b) or nylon (nylon plate condition; Fig. 2c). All plates were custom-contoured to match the anatomical curvature of the shoe base and were fully enclosed within the sole unit, preserving the shoe’s external appearance.

These structural modifications were intended to selectively alter forefoot bending stiffness and midfoot torsional rigidity, while maintaining consistent shoe mass and cushioning properties. The three configurations were used to evaluate their effects on lower limb joint kinetics and neuromuscular activation during basketball-specific movements.

Experimental setup and data collection

Measurement systems

Testing was conducted in a biomechanics laboratory equipped with floor-mounted force platforms and a 16-channel wireless surface electromyography (sEMG) system. GRF data were collected using four force platforms (9260AA6, Kistler, Switzerland; 2000 Hz), integrated into the floor with adjacent extension plates to ensure smooth movement transitions.

sEMG signals were recorded from nine dominant-leg muscles: gluteus maximus, rectus femoris, vastus lateralis, vastus medialis, semitendinosus, biceps femoris, tibialis anterior, medial gastrocnemius, and lateral gastrocnemius, using a wireless system (SP-W02, Delsys, USA; 2000 Hz). Skin preparation and electrode placement followed SENIAM guidelines.

GRF and sEMG signals were recorded simultaneously. Signal quality and system synchronization were checked according to standard laboratory procedures prior to data collection.

Three-dimensional kinematic data were captured using an eight-camera Qualisys motion capture system (Qualisys, Gothenburg, Sweden). A total of 28 retro-reflective markers were applied to the pelvis and the dominant lower limb (right for all participants) using a cluster-based lower-limb protocol combined with a modified, Oxford Foot Model (OFM)-derived single-foot marker set (i.e., not the full bilateral OFM)24, 25. In the present study, we did not compute or report multi-segment foot kinematics as defined in the full OFM; instead, the foot markers were used to track a single foot segment and to define the distal geometry required for inverse-dynamics joint-moment estimation. Accordingly, the marker configuration differs from the standard OFM (13 markers per foot), reflecting a single-foot, task-oriented placement and measures to mitigate marker occlusion during dynamic basketball movements (i.e., anatomical knee/ankle markers removed after the static trial). Specifically, 28 markers were used during the static calibration trial (pelvis: 4; thigh cluster: 4; shank cluster: 4; knee/ankle anatomical markers: 4; foot: 12), and 24 markers remained for dynamic trials after removing the knee and ankle anatomical markers. Pelvis markers were placed on the left/right anterior and posterior superior iliac spines (LASI, RASI, LPSI, RPSI). The right thigh and shank were tracked using four-marker clusters (R_TH1–R_TH4; R_SK1–R_SK4). Medial and lateral femoral epicondyle markers (R_FME, R_FLE) and medial and lateral malleolus markers (R_TAM, R_FAL) were used for the static trial only. The right foot was tracked with 12 markers: calcaneus center (R_FCC) and posterior calcaneus (R_FCC2), hallux (R_PM6), first/second/fifth metatarsal heads (R_FM1, R_FM2, R_FM5), proximal dorsal first/second/fifth metatarsals (R_P1MT, R_P2MT, R_FMT), fibular tubercle (R_FPT), navicular tuberosity (R_FNT), and the medial aspect of the posterior talar tubercle (R_FST). After the static calibration trial, medial knee and ankle markers were removed to reduce occlusion during dynamic trials. Marker names and anatomical landmark definitions are provided in the text above to support replication of the capture protocol. The OFM literature was cited primarily for its anatomical rationale for foot marker placement; the present work adopts these anatomical placement principles rather than implementing the full multi-segment OFM model.

Test movements

Three basketball-specific movements were assessed: CMJ, 45° cutting, and lateral shuffling. Each movement was performed three times under each footwear condition in randomized order, resulting in 27 total trials per participant.

For the CMJ, participants stood upright on the force platform with feet shoulder-width apart. Upon a verbal cue, they performed a rapid countermovement followed by a maximal vertical jump. Arm swing was unrestricted. A trial was considered valid if both feet remained on the force platform during take-off and landing, no preparatory steps were taken, and the effort was deemed maximal.

In the 45° cutting task, participants approached from a 3-meter distance. On the third step, the dominant foot contacted the force platform and initiated a sharp redirection at a 45° forward-right angle. Trials were valid if the foot strike was centered on the platform without hesitation or braking steps. Approach velocity was monitored using timing gates (Brower Timing, USA) to ensure consistency within ± 5% across attempts.

For the lateral shuffling task, participants adopted a defensive stance and executed three consecutive rightward shuffle steps. The third step landed on the force platform, immediately followed by a leftward return. Valid trials required continuous motion and consistent step timing, with trial-to-trial variation below 0.1 s. To further standardize intensity, a metronome (2 Hz) was used to control rhythm and step frequency across trials.

To minimize variability across all tasks, participants were instructed to perform at maximal effort while maintaining consistent approach distances and step lengths. Trials deviating from these standards were repeated under supervision of the research team.

Testing protocol

Prior to data collection, all participants attended a familiarization session conducted two weeks in advance. During this session, study objectives, procedures, and safety considerations were thoroughly explained. Participants provided written informed consent and were introduced to the testing environment. Although task instructions were provided, repeated movement practice was intentionally avoided to minimize motor adaptation effects.

On the testing day, participants first underwent anthropometric assessment and changed into standardized athletic attire. A general warm-up was performed for five minutes prior to sensor placement. sEMG electrodes were applied to nine dominant-limb muscles: rectus femoris, biceps femoris, semitendinosus, vastus lateralis, vastus medialis, medial and lateral gastrocnemius, tibialis anterior, and gluteus maximus. Electrode sites were shaved, cleaned with 75% alcohol, and air-dried prior to application. Electrodes were aligned with the muscle fiber orientation and positioned over the muscle bellies to optimize signal fidelity. Placement followed SENIAM guidelines26, 27: rectus femoris—midway between the anterior superior iliac spine and the superior patella; vastus lateralis—two-thirds on the line from the anterior superior iliac spine to the lateral patella; vastus medialis—80% on the line from the anterior superior iliac spine to the medial patella; biceps femoris—midway between the ischial tuberosity and the lateral epicondyle of the tibia; semitendinosus—midway between the ischial tuberosity and the medial condyle of the tibia; tibialis anterior—one-third on the line between the fibular head and the medial malleolus; gastrocnemius medialis—the most prominent bulge of the medial head; gastrocnemius lateralis—one-third on the line from the head of the fibula to the heel; gluteus maximus—50% on the line between the sacral vertebrae and the greater trochanter.

A static trial was recorded to calibrate baseline posture and establish electrode reference signals. Participants stood barefoot on the force plate in a standardized posture (feet shoulder-width apart, arms extended forward) for 20 s. This static trial was collected barefoot for model calibration; all dynamic trials were performed in the assigned footwear condition. Following calibration, dynamic trials were performed under the three footwear conditions in randomized order. Each participant completed three successful trials of countermovement jumping, 45° cutting, and lateral shuffling, with sufficient rest provided between efforts to minimize fatigue.

To ensure consistency in biomechanical analysis, task-specific analysis windows were defined using GRF signals. For the CMJ, the propulsion phase was identified from peak knee flexion to toe-off, marked by the point when vertical ground reaction force (vGRF) dropped to zero. For the 45° cutting task, the support phase was defined from initial contact (vGRF ≥ 10 N) to toe-off (vGRF ≤ 10 N) of the dominant foot. The same GRF thresholds were used to define the stance phase of the third step in the lateral shuffle. Only trials demonstrating clear GRF profiles, accurate foot placement, and consistent execution were retained for analysis.

Data processing and analysis

Marker trajectories were low-pass filtered using a zero-lag, fourth-order Butterworth filter (cutoff frequency 6 Hz), with the cutoff frequency selected a priori to attenuate high-frequency marker noise while preserving the dominant movement content relevant for inverse-dynamics moment estimation, consistent with commonly adopted marker-trajectory preprocessing recommendations for inverse dynamics28, 29. GRF signals were time-synchronized with marker trajectories within the motion analysis workflow for inverse dynamics. GRF and marker trajectory data were imported into Visual3D (C-Motion, USA) for inverse dynamics30. Kinematic data were used only to compute joint moments; segment kinematics were not interpreted as primary outcomes. Accordingly, foot markers were used to define the distal geometry and track a single foot segment for joint-moment computation rather than to derive or interpret multi-segment OFM outputs.

Kinetic analyses focused on the ankle, knee, and MTP joints. For each task, movement-specific analysis windows were defined using GRF thresholds. Within these intervals, peak joint moments were extracted for all three anatomical planes at the ankle and knee, and in the sagittal plane at the MTP joint. Joint moments were calculated using a conventional inverse dynamics approach in Visual3D and were normalized to body mass and height; GRF signals were normalized to body weight to facilitate inter-subject comparisons.

sEMG signals were processed in MATLAB R2019a (MathWorks, USA). Raw signals were band-pass filtered using a fourth-order Butterworth filter (20–500 Hz), followed by low-pass filtering at 50 Hz. Signals were then full-wave rectified to generate linear envelopes representing muscle activation profiles. All procedures followed SENIAM guidelines to ensure methodological consistency and signal fidelity.

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 26.0, IBM, USA). All data are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD). A one-way repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted to evaluate differences among the three footwear conditions. When a significant main effect was observed, post hoc comparisons were performed using the least significant difference (LSD) method. The threshold for statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Ground reaction forces

No significant differences were found in ground reaction forces among the three footwear conditions during CMJ, 45° cutting, or lateral shuffling (p > 0.05 for all directions and tasks; Table 2). Therefore, midsole support plate material/stiffness did not significantly affect external GRF magnitudes in any direction across the tested tasks. Given the absence of statistically significant differences, no further interpretation of between-condition GRF patterns was made.

Joint moments

No significant differences were observed in lower limb joint moments during CMJ, 45° cutting, and lateral shuffling (see Tables 3, 4 and 5, presented separately to allow task-specific clarity). Peak joint moments at the MTP, ankle, and knee were statistically comparable across the carbon fiber, nylon, and no-plate conditions (P > 0.05). Accordingly, between-condition differences in joint moments were not interpreted further in the Results.

Discussion

This study examined the specific effects of varying midsole support plate materials—carbon fiber, nylon, and no plate—on lower limb biomechanics during CMJ, 45° cutting, and lateral shuffling. These three movement patterns represent high-frequency, high-demand tasks in basketball performance. As the primary interface between the athlete and the playing surface, basketball footwear plays a critical role in modulating external loading and neuromuscular responses.

By using a standardized shoe model and controlling for all other footwear characteristics, the experiment focused on how changes in midfoot support plate stiffness influenced ground reaction forces, joint moments, and muscle activation at the metatarsophalangeal, ankle, and knee joints. Overall, the present findings indicate that altering support-plate material/stiffness produced minimal changes in GRF and joint moments, and limited changes in muscle activation, under the tested conditions.

Across all three tasks (CMJ, 45° cutting, and lateral shuffling), the vGRF showed minimal variation across footwear conditions. This suggests that the tested support-plate configurations did not meaningfully alter vertical load transmission during brief, high-intensity actions, consistent with previous work showing minimal vGRF sensitivity to sole construction during football-specific movements31. The brief duration of stance phases in these tasks may limit the extent to which midsole stiffness can influence vertical force development32. Accordingly, if support-plate stiffness influences vertical loading, such effects may be more detectable in tasks with longer contact times or different loading characteristics than those examined here.

The data provide little evidence that the incorporation of carbon fiber or nylon support plates meaningfully alters external load transmission during these movements. This observation echoes earlier work by Jia et al.33 and Teng et al.34, who reported similar vGRF levels with and without midsole inserts during jumping. Bruce et al.35 further noted that variations in surface stiffness and shoe construction do not significantly affect GRF peaks in vertical jumps.

Importantly, since elevated vGRF has been implicated in ankle joint stress and the etiology of ankle osteoarthritis36, the lack of increase observed here suggests that neither support plate material elevates injury risk in this regard. However, GRF alone does not fully represent internal tissue loading; therefore, these results should not be interpreted as definitive evidence that joint/tissue stresses are unchanged across footwear conditions.

Similar findings were noted during 45° cutting and lateral shuffling, where vGRF responses remained consistent regardless of footwear configuration. This supports prior results from Lin et al.37 and Liu et al.38, who found that midsole design features—including thickness and lateral support—had negligible impact on peak ground forces during directional changes. Together, these data suggest that structural changes in the midfoot region may not be sufficient to modulate impact magnitude in cutting or shuffling actions.

Joint moment profiles across the MTP, ankle, and knee joints also showed no significant alterations with changes in support plate material. During CMJ, torque outputs remained stable between conditions, corroborating the findings of Bruce et al.35. Unlike the work of Willwacher et al.39, who reported anterior shifts in MTP loading during running with stiffer soles, our data suggest that such effects do not translate directly to vertical jumping tasks—possibly due to differences in foot–ground interaction mechanics.

In 45° cutting, ankle moments remained unaffected, consistent with observations by Arefin et al.40 and Graf et al.41. Although knee frontal-plane loading is often discussed in relation to anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injury risk42, 43, 44, the present study did not detect statistically significant between-condition differences in knee moments; therefore, we did not interpret the data as evidence of injury-risk modulation by plate material/stiffness.

During lateral shuffling, moment values at all measured joints remained largely unchanged across conditions. This aligns with prior work indicating that footwear-induced changes in midsole rigidity do not significantly impact joint torques during lateral displacement45, 46, 47, 48. Given the absence of significant kinetic effects, we avoided attributing non-significant variability to specific strike patterns or compensatory strategies.

Overall, the findings point to a limited influence of midsole support plate stiffness on gross kinetic variables. Across the three tasks, GRF and joint-moment outcomes were largely conserved, indicating that altering plate material/stiffness in isolation did not meaningfully modify external loading or joint torque demands in this protocol.

RMS values reflect the effective muscle discharge during dynamic contraction and are considered indicative of neuromuscular effort. Consistent with recommended statistical reporting practice, only statistically significant between-condition EMG differences were interpreted, and non-significant comparisons were not described as “trends”49. In the CMJ task, no statistically significant differences were observed across footwear conditions for any muscle, indicating that plate material/stiffness did not meaningfully alter muscle activation during vertical jumping under the tested conditions.

During 45° cutting, the carbon fiber condition elicited significantly greater RMS in the vastus lateralis compared to other footwear. This isolated finding suggests a modest, task-specific increase in quadriceps demand under lateral loading with the carbon fiber plate; however, because other muscles and tasks did not show significant differences, the functional relevance of this effect remains uncertain. Prior studies have reported that footwear stiffness can influence quadriceps activation during explosive or change-of-direction tasks50; the present results suggest that such effects, when present, may be limited and task-dependent.

In lateral shuffling, no statistically significant between-condition differences were detected in RMS outcomes, and thus muscle activation patterns were considered comparable across footwear conditions.

Co-activation ratios between agonist and antagonist muscle groups provide insight into joint stabilization strategies under dynamic load. Across all three tasks, no significant differences were observed in knee or ankle joint co-activation ratios among the three footwear conditions. These results are in line with earlier findings by Lesinski et al.51, Fu et al.52, and Udin et al.53, suggesting that moderate alterations in midsole stiffness do not substantially affect neuromuscular coordination patterns when tasks are anticipated and well-controlled. It should be noted that different co-activation equations were applied to CMJ and cutting tasks, based on their biomechanical demands. While this choice enhanced interpretability for each movement, it may limit direct comparability between tasks.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the laboratory protocol and the selected tasks may not capture the full range of in-game movement variability and unanticipated perturbations. Second, kinematic data were collected to enable inverse dynamics computations but were not analyzed as primary outcomes, limiting mechanistic interpretation of potential joint-angle strategy changes. Third, only the tested plate materials/stiffness range within a single shoe configuration was evaluated; different stiffness magnitudes, geometries, or combined design features may yield different biomechanical responses. Finally, although established preprocessing was applied, inverse dynamics estimates can be sensitive to signal-processing choices; replication with alternative preprocessing or sensitivity checks would strengthen confidence in the robustness of the predominantly null findings54.

Taken together, the findings suggest that midfoot support plate stiffness, when manipulated in isolation, does not substantially alter external loading patterns and shows limited influence on muscle activation in most muscles/tasks under the tested conditions. Overall, the primary conclusion is that support-plate material/stiffness had limited biomechanical impact during these basketball-specific movements in this experimental setup, and claims of performance enhancement or injury-risk reduction should be made cautiously.

Conclusion

This study examined the effects of midsole support plate material (carbon fiber, nylon, or no plate) on lower-limb biomechanics during countermovement jumps, 45° cutting, and lateral shuffling in recreational basketball players. Ground reaction forces and joint moments were comparable across footwear conditions across tasks. Muscle activation outcomes were also largely unchanged, with the exception of a task-specific difference observed during 45° cutting. Co-activation ratios remained stable, indicating that moderate midfoot plate modifications within the tested shoe configuration did not meaningfully alter joint coordination during these controlled tasks. Overall, the findings suggest predominantly null effects of plate material/stiffness on external loading and joint kinetics in the tested movements.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, only acute biomechanical responses were assessed; potential neuromuscular adaptations to repeated exposure to different support plate configurations remain unknown. Second, participants were limited to recreational male basketball players, which may restrict generalizability to elite or female athletes. Third, the study examined three laboratory-based tasks under controlled conditions and therefore may not capture unanticipated, game-like perturbations and movement variability. Finally, although established preprocessing was applied, inverse dynamics estimates can be sensitive to signal-processing choices (e.g., filtering and event definitions); future work incorporating preprocessing sensitivity checks would strengthen confidence in the robustness of the predominantly null findings.

Future research should investigate long-term adaptation to footwear stiffness, include more diverse athlete populations, and employ more game-representative tasks to better translate laboratory findings into practical applications.
