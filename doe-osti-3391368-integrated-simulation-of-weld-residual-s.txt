 ABSTRACT

 Nuclear power plant components operate in environments that promote multiple degradation mecha-
nisms, several of which involve crack initiation and growth. An ongoing effort in the U.S. Department of
Energy’s Nuclear Energy Advanced Modeling and Simulation (NEAMS) program is developing a general
capability within the Multiphysics Object Oriented Simulation Environment (MOOSE) framework for
simulating three-dimensional crack growth under a range of driving conditions, including fatigue, stress
corrosion cracking (SCC), brittle fracture, and stress-relaxation cracking. This report demonstrates an
end-to-end workflow that uses this capability to model weld-residual-stress-driven SCC in the J-groove
weld of a pressurized-water reactor control rod drive mechanism penetration in the vessel head. The
workflow consists of a thermomechanical welding simulation with temperature-dependent plasticity,
followed by cooldown to ambient conditions, and a restart of the simulation using the MOOSE extended
finite element method (XFEM) module to propagate a three-dimensional crack through the residual stress
field. New welding capabilities were developed to properly initialize newly activated elements in the weld
region, and robustness improvements were made to the mesh-based algorithm for defining cutting planes
in the 3D XFEM algorithm, allowing it to handle complex crack fronts and stress fields. Together these
advances allowed the simulated SCC crack to grow from an initial elliptical flaw in the weld, across the
weld, through the tube wall, and almost to the triple point (where the weld, tube, and reactor pressure
vessel head intersect) over roughly 36 years of simulated service. These results demonstrate a workflow
that can be extended to fully three-dimensional welding simulations and more complex crack interaction
problems.

 Integrated Simulation of Weld Residual Stress Evolution
 and Crack Propagation Using XFEM

 1. INTRODUCTION

 Nuclear power plant components are exposed to harsh thermal, mechanical, and chemical environments
that drive a range of degradation mechanisms. Fracture plays a prominent role in many of these mechanisms,
including fatigue crack growth, primary water stress corrosion cracking (PWSCC), brittle fracture, and
stress-relaxation cracking. Weld regions are particularly susceptible to fracture because the welding process
causes large residual stresses. Predicting how cracks grow in these regions is essential for assessing the
structural integrity of reactor components and for justifying continued operation. An ongoing effort in the U.S.
Department of Energy’s (DOE’s) Nuclear Energy Advanced Modeling and Simulation (NEAMS) program is
developing capabilities for modeling structural component degradation, including a general capability for
simulating three-dimensional (3D) crack growth under all these driving conditions. This capability has being
built on the open-source Multiphysics Object Oriented Simulation Environment (MOOSE) framework [1]
and the Grizzly [2] and BlackBear application codes.
 The extended finite element method (XFEM) is an attractive method for this class of problems because
it represents strong discontinuities, such as cracks, independently of the underlying finite-element mesh. It
accurately and efficiently captures the effects of a crack on the surrounding displacement and stress fields in
two-dimensional (2D) or 3D representations of solid materials, can be combined with fracture integrals to
provide stress intensity factors (SIFs), and permits incremental crack-front advancement in a manner that is
independent of the mesh [3]. The MOOSE XFEM module uses the phantom-node form of XFEM [4], in
which elements traversed by a crack are replaced by two duplicate elements that together represent the original
domain. MOOSE also supports other fracture methodologies, including continuum damage mechanics [5],
phase-field methods [6], and peridynamics [7], but XFEM is the focus of the present work because of its
inherent abilities to model a propagating discrete crack whose growth is driven by fracture integrals.
 XFEM in MOOSE was originally developed for nuclear-fuel fracture, with demonstrations of critical
crack growth in 2D cross-section models of UO2 light-water reactor (LWR) fuel [8, 9] and a rudimentary
capability for modeling contact between fractured fuel and cladding [10]. Later efforts built on that work
to model 3D stationary cracks [11] for SIF computations in LWR reactor pressure vessels (RPVs), and
added near-tip enrichment functions to reduce the noise produced by the aliasing of crack fronts on 3D
finite-element meshes [12]. XFEM has also been used in MOOSE for modeling moving interfaces such as
the boundary between a growing oxide layer and the base metal [13]. The 2D propagating-crack capability is
comparatively mature, particularly following recent work on crack propagation in graphite that advanced the
use of lower-dimensional cutting meshes [14]. An initial demonstration of 3D fatigue crack growth using
a lower-dimensional cutting mesh was presented in [15], and the 3D crack-growth capability was further
developed in a Fiscal Year 2025 NEAMS milestone [16]; however, the 3D propagating-crack capability has
historically been failure-prone in the presence of free surfaces, corners, and non-uniform crack-front growth
and has seen far less use than the 2D equivalents [14, 17].
 Modeling subcritical fracture growth in weld regions also requires the ability to produce realistic residual
stress fields, which play a dominant role in driving subcritical crack propagation. Earlier studies of PWSCC
based on NEAMS-developed codes, such as the Nuclear Energy University Program project of [18], had
to rely on published images of residual stress distributions to compute SIFs because an integrated welding
simulation capability had not been developed. Recent work at Argonne National Laboratory has developed and
integrated such a capability into MOOSE that includes the underlying element-activation and reinitialization

 1

infrastructure required for robust thermomechanical welding analysis [19, 20].
 For the work discussed in this report, we followed a similar protocol that was used in previous stress
corrosion cracking (SCC) analyses of J-groove welds [21, 22] by combining these two analysis steps into a
single end-to-end workflow and demonstrating it on a representative reactor component. We modeled SCC of
a prototypical J-groove weld in a pressurized-water reactor control rod drive mechanism (CRDM) penetration,
driven by the weld residual stresses. The workflow proceeds in three stages: (1) a thermomechanical welding
simulation with temperature-dependent plasticity, in which weld passes are deposited sequentially using
finite-element activation; (2) a cooldown stage in which the structure relaxes to ambient temperature and
the final residual stress field develops; and (3) a phase in which the residual stress field is reconstructed
onto a different 3D mesh and XFEM is used to propagate a crack through that field. The remainder of this
report documents the capability improvements needed at each stage of the workflow, presents the J-groove
demonstration, and identifies the additional development needed to extend the workflow to fully 3D welding
simulations and more complex crack interaction problems.
 While SCC crack growth in LWR components is an application of interest for the long-term operation
of the existing reactor fleet, the capabilities demonstrated here are applicable to a broad range of nuclear
reactor components for both LWRs and advanced reactors. For example, the integrated welding and crack-
growth capabilities are applicable to stress-relaxation cracking in high-temperature reactor components. The
3D XFEM capabilities for crack growth are also broadly applicable to a variety of fracture problems that
may not necessarily be affected by weld residual stresses, such as creep crack growth in high-temperature
alloys, stress-induced crack growth in graphite components, and rupture of primary coolant system piping
components in LWRs.

 2. CAPABILITY DEVELOPMENT

 This section presents the capability improvements developed for the weld and SCC fracture workflow
demonstrated later in the report. The welding portion of that workflow runs as three sequential MOOSE
simulations that exchange state through the checkpoint/restart mechanism provided by MOOSE. A preheat
stage brings the base materials from ambient temperature up to the preheat temperature. A weld stage then
deposits each pass by activating its subdomain and applying a moving heat source. A cooldown stage relaxes
the assembly back to ambient temperature and produces the final residual stress and plastic eigenstrain fields
used in the subsequent SCC simulations. A separate XFEM simulation then maps the axisymmetric eigenstrain
field onto a quarter-symmetry 3D mesh, recovers the residual stress through a single quasi-static equilibrium
solve, and propagates an SCC crack through that field.
 The developments that enable each step are described in detail here. Element-subdomain modifier
updates and patch-recovery-based reinitialization keep the weld stage well-posed (Section 2.1). A birth strain
formulation is introduced to account for the deformation of newly activated weld elements during elastic
reconstruction of the post-weld stress field (Section 2.2). A residual-stress recovery procedure maps the
mechanical solution of a 2D welding simulation onto the 3D mesh (Section 2.3). Robustness fixes to the 3D
XFEM cutting-mesh algorithm support stable crack growth on free surfaces and into corners (Section 2.4).

 2.1. Updates to Subdomain Modifier
 The variable (re)initialization procedure in the ElementSubdomainModifier in MOOSE has been improved
by leveraging newly developed LIBM ESH application programming interfaces, namely project_vector and
project_solution, which enable projection onto specified element ranges and selected variables [23, 24].

 2

In the legacy implementation, the function was projected onto a temporary vector containing all degrees
of freedom for the full solution domain and all variables, after which the relevant entries were extracted
and assigned to the target elements and variables during reinitialization. While functional, this approach
introduced an unnecessary global projection step. The new implementation operates directly on the required
subsets of the model, providing a cleaner and more efficient workflow. In addition, a “do-nothing” option has
been introduced for reinitialization variables, allowing users to explicitly skip reinitialization when desired.
 For the reinitialization strategy for newly activated elements based on patch recovery, as well as the
user-friendly centralized block restriction implementation, the reader is referred to [20] for further details.

 2.2. Birth Strain
 The introduction of element activation creates an implicit shift in the constitutive reference configuration.
When an element is activated (i.e., “born”), its displacement field is initialized from neighboring values, while
its strain history is reset to zero. Consequently, the constitutive model interprets the activated configuration as
a zero-strain reference, even though the element is generally born in a deformed configuration. This produces a
mismatch between the geometric strain computed from the final displacement field and the strain accumulated
by the incremental constitutive model. Mathematically, the welding simulation evolves according to
                                       𝜀mech (𝑥, 𝑡) = 𝜀(𝑢(𝑥, 𝑡)) − 𝜀birth (𝑥),                                  (1)
where 𝜀birth is the strain at activation. Neglecting this birth strain during reconstruction of the solution
onto a separate mesh (such as the one used for SCC simulation in this work) implicitly assumes that zero
displacement corresponds to zero strain for all elements, which contradicts the activation history and leads to
discrepancies in reconstructed stresses.
 To restore consistency, the birth strain must be explicitly incorporated into the reconstruction formulation.
The birth strain is recorded during element activation, which allows the final stress field to be interpreted
as arising from a total small-strain formulation with an additional eigenstrain. Introducing an equivalent
eigenstrain,
                                               𝜀equiv = −ℂ−1 ∶ 𝜎,                                               (2)
allows the stress field to be reproduced within a total formulation referenced to the birth-corrected configuration.
The reconstructed stress then satisfies
 ( )
 𝜎 = ℂ ∶ 𝜀(𝑢) − 𝜀birth − 𝜀equiv . (3)
This formulation is consistent with the incremental welding simulation while enabling reconstruction through
a single equilibrium solve.
 In practice, the procedure consists of recording the birth strain at the moment of element activation,
computing the equivalent eigenstrain from the final stress field, and solving a total small-strain equilibrium
problem using both fields. The resulting displacement field reproduces the stress state of the original welding
simulation. Because the birth strain encodes activation history, the reconstruction remains path-dependent
even though the material model itself is linear elastic. This interpretation clarifies that element activation
introduces an effective eigenstrain-like field that must be accounted for explicitly in any stress reconstruction
procedure.
 This formulation assumes small-strain kinematics and linear elasticity in the reconstruction step. Under
these assumptions, the combination of birth strain and equivalent eigenstrain provides a consistent and
robust framework for reconstructing stresses from simulations involving element activation while preserving
equilibrium and reproducing the final stress state of the original welding process.

 2.3. Residual Stress Recovery
 It is often desirable to use different meshes for the welding and fracture simulations. This is driven by the
differing requirements in terms of mesh refinement and dimensionality between those models to capture the
relevant phenomena. Some of the geometries used in this work were simplified to enable representing them
in 2D for the welding simulations, but full 3D simulations are needed to resolve the evolving crack geometry
for the SCC simulation. The 3D XFEM simulation thus requires the residual stress state computed by the
2D welding and cooldown simulation to be applied as an initial condition on the 3D Cartesian mesh. This is
done in two steps. First, the residual eigenstrain field from the 2D simulation is projected from a cylindrical
symmetry or planar representation onto the 3D mesh. Second, a single quasi-static equilibrium step is solved
on the 3D mesh with this eigenstrain field to recover the corresponding stress and displacement fields.
 The 2D output is read into the 3D simulation using a MOOSE SolutionUserObject, which loads the
solution file from the cooldown stage so that the solution can be sampled and interpolated on the current
finite-element mesh. The solution file uses the ExodusII format [25], a widely used file format for representing
unstructured finite element meshes and associated mesh-based field data. For the case of a 2D axisymmetric
welding model, the variables of interest are the four components of the symmetric plastic eigenstrain tensor

 2.4. Updates to XFEM
 In this work, we use a crack-growth algorithm in which the crack face is represented by a lower-dimensional
cutter mesh, as documented in previous NEAMS milestone reports [15–17]. Crack propagation is modeled
by extending this cutter mesh through the addition of new nodes and elements along the crack front in the
prescribed growth direction. In 2D simulations, the crack face is represented by a 1D cutter mesh composed
of line elements and crack growth is done by adding a single node and line element at the crack front [17]. In
3D simulations, the crack face is represented by a 2D cutter mesh composed of triangular elements, making

 4

crack growth considerably more complex. In this case, the crack front is a line, and propagation requires the
extension of this line in the growth direction along with the insertion of triangle elements representing the
extension of the crack face. The capability to simulate three-dimensional crack propagation in MOOSE using
XFEM was originally introduced in [15].
 In our previous report on incremental XFEM developments to support 3D SCC simulations [16], we doc-
umented limitations of the 3D mesh-based cutting algorithm implemented in CrackMeshCut3DUserObject.
In particular, the algorithm had difficulty evolving the crack-front where it intersected a free surface. The
crack-front of the 2D cutter-mesh contains two types of nodes where it intersects a surface: active nodes,
which lie inside the 3D body, and inactive nodes, which lie outside the body. One problematic case occurred
at the crack-front edge intersecting a free surface that connects an active and inactive node. The growth
magnitude and direction for active nodes is specified by a function or computed from a fracture integral,
whereas the growth of inactive nodes is copied from the connected active node. This copying can move
an inactive node below the surface, where it is then projected back outside the body along the crack-front
segment. The projection snaps the candidate point to the nearest sideset face of the finite-element mesh and
then offsets it outward along the face normal by a distance of 0.1 size_control. Here, size_control is
the input parameter that controls the cutting-mesh edge length used during crack-front refinement and the
projection distance scales with the local cutter mesh resolution. When a crack grows into a geometric corner,
this projection can cause the crack-front to become tangled. This issue has been resolved by reusing the
inactive node from the prior step. A similar issue occurs when an active node grows outside the surface. In
this case, the active node is reclassified as the new inactive node for that surface-cutting growth segment, and
the previous inactive node is frozen and no longer evolves. These updated 3D crack-growth rules have been
implemented in CrackMeshCut3DUserObject, which drives 3D XFEM crack growth, and have been shown
to be robust. Implementation details are provided in [26].
 Several additional robustness fixes were also made to the underlying cut-mesh data structures as part
of this work. Cuts at the same physical edge location now reuse the existing embedded node rather than
terminating with a duplicate-intersection error; when adjacent cutting triangles report the same structural face

 Testing of the XFEM 3D mesh-cutting algorithms was expanded using a test case that employs the circular

the cutting-mesh growth with flat and corner surfaces. The cutting mesh shown in red is rotated about the

 3.2. Control Rod Drive Mechanism J-Groove

3.2.1. Description

 To evaluate the stress fields and crack-growth behavior associated with PWSCC in a representative reactor
component, welding simulations were first performed to obtain the residual stress field in a CRDM J-groove
weld. The resulting stress distribution was then mapped onto a 3D XFEM model for subsequent crack-growth
simulations. The geometry, weld-pass layout, and stage-by-stage analysis follow the protocol used in the
CRDM J-groove residual stress and crack-growth assessments of Hill et al. [21] and Brust et al. [22].
 An axisymmetric modeling framework was employed for the welding simulation to reduce computational
cost while retaining the system’s dominant mechanical and geometrical characteristics. Figure 7 shows a
simplified axisymmetric schematic of the J-groove weld geometry. The figure is presented as a dimensioned
schematic and is not drawn to scale. The model consists of the tube (Alloy 600), weld (Alloy 182), butter layer
(Alloy 182), cladding (SS309), and RPV head (SA-508). Based on the schematic geometry, a quadrilateral
mesh was generated using Cubit, as shown in Figure 8. The mesh resolves the 14 weld passes as separate
subdomains, distinguished by different colors. It is important to note that the axisymmetric representation
used here is appropriate for a CRDM penetration at the center of the RPV’s upper head, but not for one in a
sidehill location, where the tilt of the penetrating tube relative to the vessel surface could be significant. The
same procedures applied here in 2D could be applied in 3D for a detailed analysis of such a scenario.
 The welding analysis is performed in three sequential stages, each defined through a separate MOOSE
input file that restarts from the checkpoint of the previous stage. The preheat stage starts with the five base
subdomains (tube, head, butter, cladding, and the parent weld block) active and brings them from the ambient
temperature 𝑇𝐴 = 293.15 K up to the preheat temperature 𝑇0 = 588.15 K. The weld stage activates the 14
weld-pass subdomains sequentially with the MOOSE element-activation infrastructure, depositing each pass
over 3 s for a total weld time of 42 s. As each weld-pass subdomain is activated, it is mapped onto the parent
weld block so that the deposited material shares a single set of constitutive properties. The cooldown stage
then runs out to 1 × 105 s of simulation time to recover the residual stress and plastic eigenstrain fields at
ambient temperature.
 The SCC analysis is performed by mapping the final welding plastic eigenstrains from the 2D axisymmetric
cooldown simulation onto a 3D quarter symmetry mesh. The quarter symmetry mesh is refined near the
location where the crack will be placed. The crack is then grown with a model for SCC used in our previous
work [16].

3.2.2. Welding Simulation

Simulation Procedure
 The overall simulation procedure is divided into the following steps:

 • Preheating: The base material is heated to a prescribed temperature (𝑇 = 588.15 K) to represent the
 preheating process prior to welding.
 • Welding: The weld is deposited in a pass-by-pass manner. Each weld pass is activated sequentially
 following the predefined weld deposition order, as illustrated in Figure 8. The heat input is applied
 during each pass to simulate the welding process and its associated thermal effects.
 • Cooldown: After completion of all weld passes, the entire structure is allowed to cool down to the

Boundary Conditions
 The boundary conditions for the solid mechanics subproblem are illustrated in Figure 9. A zero vertical
displacement condition, 𝑢𝑦 = 0, is imposed at the upper-left corner to prevent rigid-body motion. In addition,
an equal-value boundary constraint is applied along the right boundary in the radial direction, such that all
nodes on this boundary share the same radial displacement. These boundary conditions are consistently
applied throughout the three simulation stages: preheating, welding, and cooldown.
 For the heat transfer subproblem, the boundary conditions vary across the three stages. During the
preheating stage, no thermal boundary conditions are applied, as the temperature is prescribed and increased
over time rather than being solved for. During the welding stage, the Dirichlet boundary condition 𝑇 = 𝑇𝐴 =
293.15 K is imposed on the right boundary of the domain. All other boundaries exposed to the surrounding
air are subject to a convection heat flux boundary condition with a heat transfer coefficient of 10−5 . Due to the
progressive activation of elements, these convective boundaries are dynamically updated: as new elements are
activated, boundary segments are added or removed depending on whether they are exposed to the ambient
environment. In the cooldown stage, the Dirichlet boundary condition 𝑇 = 𝑇𝐴 remains applied on the right
boundary. The remaining boundaries, which are now fixed in time, are subjected to the same convection heat
flux condition with a heat transfer coefficient of 10−5 .

3.2.4. Stress Corrosion Cracking

 Crack-front growth is determined by the StressCorrosionCrackingExponential reporter, which
computes a per-node growth increment for the CrackMeshCut3DUserObject. The crack-growth direction
at each front node is determined by the maximum hoop stress criterion [3, 29]: the crack extends in the
direction along which the local hoop stress relative to the crack tip is maximized, with the propagation angle
𝜃𝑐 measured from the local crack plane obtained from the mixed-mode stress intensity factors 𝐾𝐼 and 𝐾𝐼𝐼 on
each fracture-integral. At each step, the reporter evaluates the SCC growth rate at every active front node and
identifies the fastest-growing node. The time step size is then set to the time required for that node to advance
by a distance defined by the user-specified max_growth_increment parameter. All other crack-front nodes
are advanced by their own local growth rate multiplied by this same time step. The SCC growth rate used
here was developed for 316L stainless steel in water at 288◦ C [18, 30] and is given by:

 The XFEM cutting mesh used to represent the crack surface, overlaid on the J-groove geometry, is shown
at several simulation steps in Figure 13. The initial elliptical crack, with dimensions of 4 mm × 8 mm, is
shown in Figure 13a. This is the same initial crack size used in [21]. After six simulation steps, the model
has experienced 24.3 years of SCC growth and the cutting mesh has advanced accordingly (Figure 13b). At
this point the initial crack has grown across the weld and has started to grow into the control rod tube. The
growth algorithm moves every crack-front point at each simulation step. The number of growth steps at any
point in the simulation is equal to the number of circumferential growth rings on the crack surface. After 35
years, the crack shown in Figure 13c has grown through the tube, producing a breach between the control rod
and the inner pressure vessel. The surface-tracking algorithm for the crack front advances the front around
the corner between the weld and the tube, allowing the crack to grow down the tube wall. The circumferential
displacement field, 𝑢𝜃 , at the time of breach is shown in Figure 14. The displacements on opposite sides of
the tube are oriented in opposing directions, indicating that the crack is opening. The crack reaches the triple
point between the weld, tube, and butter after 36.5 years, as shown in Figure 13d. Overall, the crack front
progresses as expected and the XFEM cutting algorithm is robust for these 3D tetrahedral meshes.
 Around step 19, the cutting mesh reverses direction as it advances in the lower part of the control rod
tube, and the cutting mesh becomes tangled. The reversal occurs because 𝐾𝐼 becomes negative on those
crack-front nodes, a case that is not yet handled by the current implementation of Equation (6) but will be
addressed in future work (see Section 5). After the cutting mesh becomes tangled, the XFEM simulation
continues for nine additional steps, out to 37.8 years, before hitting a fatal error in the cutting algorithm. The

 4. CONCLUSION

 This report demonstrated an end-to-end workflow for simulating 3D crack growth driven by weld residual
stresses applied to the J-groove weld of a CRDM penetration. The workflow couples a sequential thermome-
chanical welding simulation, a cooldown stage, and a restart into a 3D XFEM fracture simulation. Producing
a working version of this workflow required new capabilities at each of its stages.
 On the welding side, several improvements were made to the ElementSubdomainModifier and surrounding
infrastructure in MOOSE. The variable reinitialization procedure now uses new LIBM ESH project_vector
and project_solution application programming interfaces that operate directly on the activated element
range and on user-specified variables, eliminating an unnecessary global projection. A “do-nothing” option
was added for variables that should not be reinitialized. A patch-recovery-based reinitialization strategy was
added that maintains mesh quality during welding, avoiding the element distortion, negative Jacobians, and
convergence failures seen without it. A birth-strain treatment was introduced that reconciles the constitutive
reference configuration of a newly activated element with its inherited deformed configuration, so the final
stress field can be reconstructed with a single equilibrium solve. Solution transfer between the preheating,
welding, and cooldown stages was performed using the MOOSE checkpoint/restart mechanism. Together,
these capabilities produced a J-groove residual stress field with hoop and axial stresses at the triple point
consistent with the reference 3D analysis of [28] and an axial stress profile along the tube in good qualitative
agreement with that reference.
 On the fracture side, a number of robustness fixes were made to the 3D XFEM cutting-mesh algorithm.
Crack-front nodes that grow outside a free surface are now projected back onto the surface along the crack-front
segment. Special handling of the projected nodes was added to support crack growth into corners of the
geometry. The cutting mesh is also no longer required to advance on every time step, which eliminates
the degenerate zero-area crack-face elements that previously appeared and enables non-uniform crack-front
growth. These rules were validated against a structured test matrix of circular cutting meshes offset along the
x-axis and rotated about the x- and y-axes through both hexahedral and tetrahedral volume meshes. Each
failing configuration was captured as a regression test.
 To apply the full workflow to the CRDM J-groove geometry, an initial 4 mm × 8 mm semi-elliptical flaw
was propagated using a 316L SCC growth-rate model driven by the reconstructed 3D hoop stress field. The
crack grew across the weld and into the control rod tube wall in approximately 24 years. It breached the tube
at 35 years and reached the triple point at 36.5 years. Crack-front advance was largest where the reconstructed
hoop stress was highest, into the tube and toward the triple point. The surface-tracking logic correctly carried
the front around the corner between the weld and the tube. Together, these results show that the welding and
XFEM capabilities developed under this effort can be combined into a single workflow capable of simulating
subcritical crack growth in welded reactor components over service-relevant time scales.

 5. FUTURE WORK

 The demonstration in this report exposed several capability gaps that should be addressed before the
workflow can be applied routinely to PWSCC assessments of reactor components.
Robustness of crack growth under sign reversal of 𝐾𝐼 . After approximately 24 simulated years, the lower
portion of the crack front in the CRDM demonstration developed locally negative crack-growth increments.
This eventually produced a tangled cutting mesh and terminated the simulation at 37.8 years. The 316L SCC
model used here, like most subcritical growth laws, is defined only for tensile mode-𝐼 conditions. The present
implementation does not yet handle the case where 𝐾𝐼 becomes negative on part of the front while remaining

 27

positive elsewhere. A consistent treatment is needed to treat scenarios that involve arresting growth on some
crack-front nodes while continuing to advance the active portion of the front.
Additional crack-front output. The StressCorrosionCrackingExponential reporter used in this work
outputs only the per-step growth increment at each crack-front node. It would be useful to expose several
other quantities computed internally during crack growth, including the local crack-front normal direction, the
mode-𝐼 and mode-𝐼𝐼 stress intensity factors 𝐾𝐼 and 𝐾𝐼𝐼 , the 𝐽 -integral, and the SCC growth-rate regime
that each node is in. Adding these outputs would support post-processing, comparison against experimental
measurements, and the more robust crack-growth treatments described above. They would also be valuable
for debugging: when crack-front advance becomes unphysical, as in the negative-𝐾𝐼 tangling observed in
this work, having the underlying per-node fracture quantities written to file is the most direct way to identify
the sources of failure.
Three-dimensional welding simulations. The J-groove residual stress field used here was reconstructed from
a 2D axisymmetric welding simulation mapped onto the 3D mesh. A fully 3D welding simulation, with the
moving heat source following the actual circumferential weld path, would remove the cylindrical-symmetry
assumption. The resulting residual stress field would vary in the hoop direction, which is expected to drive
more realistic 3D crack-front shapes.
Three-dimensional crack nucleation. The current workflow assumes an initial elliptical surface flaw of
prescribed size and orientation. A general crack-growth capability also requires a criterion for nucleating
new flaws, such as a penny-shaped internal crack or a surface flaw, once a critical condition is reached. The
criterion must produce a flaw of sufficient size to grow under the local driving force while avoiding the
introduction of too many spurious flaws. This work should build on the existing methods that currently
support 2D crack nucleation in MOOSE.
Multiple crack fronts and their interaction. The present cutting-mesh algorithm assumes a single connected
crack surface. Many realistic crack-growth scenarios involve multiple cracks that initiate independently, grow,
and eventually coalesce. Extending the cutting-mesh data structures and growth rules to support multiple
fronts, including the detection and resolution of coalescence events, is a natural next step.
Automatic mesh refinement at the crack tip. The simulations in this report used a static mesh that was
pre-refined in the region the crack was expected to traverse. As the crack advances into less-refined regions,
the fracture-integral integration domain does not adjust with the local mesh, and its accuracy can therefore
degrade. Coupling the XFEM mesh-based cutting to MOOSE adaptive mesh refinement, so that elements
ahead of the crack front are refined automatically as it advances, would reduce the up-front meshing burden
and keep the fracture integrals well-resolved throughout the simulation.
 Together, these improvements would extend the workflow demonstrated here from a single-crack,
axisymmetric-residual-stress demonstration into a general capability for simulating 3D subcritical crack
growth in welded reactor components.
