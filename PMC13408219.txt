Syrinx anatomy and vocalizations in New World vultures (Aves: Cathartidae): Implications for avian vocal evolution
Authors: Bradley A Ibarra, Carmen A Urban, Lucas J Legendre, Julia A Clarke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNew World vultures (Cathartidae) are the only birds proposed to lack a syrinx, the avian vocal organ. The prior literature is inconsistent regarding the presence or lack of key syringeal elements in Cathartidae, such as syringeal muscles, membranes, and/or modified tracheal or bronchial rings. Additionally, New World vultures have largely been described as incapable of producing vocalizations, perhaps due to their described lack of a functional syrinx; despite this, there is a diverse repertoire of sounds made by species within Cathartidae available in online sound libraries. Here, we investigate the cathartid syrinx, airway, and larynx through dissections, diffusible iodine‐based contrast enhanced computed tomography (diceCT), and histology. We further assess available recordings and explore the potential identity of these as larynx‐ or syrinx‐based vocalizations. New World vultures indeed lack labia, bronchial half‐rings, and marked differentiation of the tracheobronchial juncture, challenging simple definitions of a syrinx. However, they retain extrinsic syringeal muscles, have modified terminal bronchial rings, extended bronchial membranes, and muscles from the thoracic wall extending to these bronchial membranes unknown in any other birds, all of which may be involved in sound production. This evidence, in conjunction with the variety of vocalization types produced, indicates that cathartid vultures may in fact have a novel form of the syrinx. These findings have implications for broadening our understanding of the necessary components of a syrinx and provide insights into the loss and addition of syringeal elements in Aves. Assessing syrinx diversity is essential to understanding form‐function relationships in Aves and the evolution of vocal behavior.Keywords: costopulmonary, diceCT, histology, larynx, syrinx evolution, thoracic muscles, vocalizations

INTRODUCTION

Understanding variation in the syrinx, a vocal organ exclusive to birds, is key to study of the evolution of avian vocal production (Ames, 1971; Beddard, 1898; Goller, 2016; Goller, 2022; King, 1989; Kingsley et al., 2018). The vocal folds of the syrinx are located ventral to the heart, or in the necks of some birds (e.g., Legendre et al., 2024), at, or near, the tracheobronchial juncture supported by modified tracheal and/or bronchial rings (Ames, 1971; Beddard, 1898; Clarke et al., 2016; Goller, 2016; Goller, 2022; King, 1989; Kingsley et al., 2018). They generate sound through vibrations induced by the airflow (e.g., Elemans et al., 2015; Gaunt & Gaunt, 1985a, 1985b; Riede & Goller, 2010). Most bird species also present two paired extrinsic syringeal muscles, the m. tracheolateralis (TL) and the m. sternotrachealis (ST), which have been shown to modify the length and diameter of the tracheal or tracheobronchial lumen during vocalization (Elemans et al., 2006; Gaunt & Gaunt, 1985a; Gaunt & Wells, 1973; Riede et al., 2006, 2019; Riede & Goller, 2010; Suthers & Hector, 1985). A syrinx comprised of two pairs of vocal folds located close to a modified tracheobronchial juncture with bronchial half‐rings and two external muscles has been estimated to be ancestral to Aves (Clarke et al., 2016; Goller, 2022; Kingsley et al., 2018; Longtine et al., 2024); further, all known syrinxes are found within the interclavicular air sac (e.g., Klein et al., 2025).

The syrinx has been described primarily in the context of avian classification since the late 17th century (Beddard, 1898; Cuvier, 1795; Forbes, 1880; Fürbringer, 1888; Griffiths, 1994; Ligon, 1967; Müller, 1878; Perrault, 1680) but has only been studied in a functional context as a vocal organ for about 50 years (e.g., Düring & Elemans, 2016; Elemans et al., 2006, 2015; Erdoğan et al., 2015; Gaunt & Gaunt, 1985a; Gaunt & Wells, 1973; Larsen & Goller, 2002; Nottebohm, 1976; Riede & Goller, 2010; ten Cate, 2021). Recently, studies have started to highlight the vast diversity and variation within basic syringeal features and proposed functional hypotheses to link these morphological adaptations to vocalization strategies (Chiappone et al., 2024; Clarke et al., 2016; Elemans et al., 2006, 2015; Gaunt & Gaunt, 1985b; Kingsley et al., 2018; Larsen & Goller, 2002; Legendre et al., 2024; Monte et al., 2020; ten Cate, 2021). However, most studies have focused on songbirds and parrots (Gaunt & Gaunt, 1985b; Kingsley et al., 2018; Larsen & Goller, 2002; Nottebohm, 1976; ten Cate, 2021). Syrinx morphology in many other clades remains undescribed or last considered more than 150 years ago.

The New World vultures, or Cathartidae, are frequently remarked in the literature as the only avian clade to lack a syrinx (e.g., Amador et al., 2025; Beddard, 1898; Buckley et al., 2022; Finkelstein et al., 2020; Forbes, 1881; Griffiths, 1994; Holste et al., 2020; Jones, 2020; King, 1989; Kingsley et al., 2018; Ligon, 1967; Miskimen, 1957; Negus, 1929; Rea, 1983). Cathartidae, a family nested within Accipitriformes (e.g., Kimball et al., 2019; Prum et al., 2015; Stiller et al., 2024) or placed in its own order, Cathartiformes (Jarvis et al., 2014; Mindell et al., 2018), comprises seven extant species (Johnson et al., 2016; Mindell et al., 2018). There has been no functional or evolutionary investigation of their hypothesized loss of the syrinx and associated shift in vocal strategy.

While there has been agreement in the literature on the absence of a syrinx in Cathartidae, there is a high discrepancy and, in some cases, conflict, among previous morphological descriptions of cathartid anatomy. Early studies of the syrinx of Cathartidae are closely tied with some of the earliest descriptions of the avian syrinx. After the naming of the syrinx by Huxley (1871), Forbes (1881) expanded its morphological definition by describing the syrinx of paleognaths, then also believed to lack a syrinx. In this process, Forbes (1881) described the presence of modified bronchial half‐rings and tympaniform membranes as diagnostic characters for the presence of a syrinx, including that of palaeognaths; under this definition, he proposed Cathartidae as the only group of birds truly without a syrinx. Beddard (1898) similarly describes incomplete bronchial rings that give rise to a tympaniform membrane as a core syringeal feature.

In 1890, Beddard described the vocal tract of Cathartidae using observations based on Vultur gryphus, noting their distinction in “having no intrinsic muscles, and in showing no definite modification in the direction of the formation of a syrinx” (Beddard, 1890: 146). However, Beddard (1890) also described muscles in Vultur that were not seen in other birds and proposed these could comprise parts of a unique type of syrinx in Cathartidae. These two sets of three muscles each inserted onto the membranous portion of the bronchi and arose from the thoracic wall (Beddard, 1890; Figure S1). In 1898, Beddard expanded his consideration of cathartid trachea and bronchi, evaluating V. gryphus, Sarcoramphus papa, Gymnogyps californianus, and Cathartes aura (Ridgway, 1880). These taxa were described as sharing the thoracic muscles he first described in 1890. Muscle attachment is described as covering the lung‐ward bronchial membrane (Beddard, 1898). Nonetheless, he considered these species to lack a syrinx (Beddard, 1898).

Since Beddard (1898), comparatively little work has been done to further investigate the syrinx of Cathartidae, leading to a prevailing view in the literature that New World vultures lack a syrinx—or at least most of its key diagnostic elements. Subsequent research on the syrinx of Cathartidae focused on its relevance in classification rather than its role in vocal production (Griffiths, 1994; Ligon, 1967; Miskimen, 1957). Miskimen (1957) described the airway of Ca. aura, reporting the syrinx in this species to be absent based on a proposed complete lack of intrinsic and extrinsic syringeal muscles in the tracheobronchial region. Ligon (1967) reached a similar conclusion in a study that compared several other avian clades (i.e., Ardeidae, Accipitridae, and Ciconiidae) to cathartids. Griffiths (1994) evaluated the airway in three cathartid genera (Cathartes, Vultur, and Coragyps) and identified several typically syringeal features such as membranes, extrinsic muscles, and differentiated rings, but nonetheless reported a syrinx was absent in Cathartidae, aligning with the prior literature. Casteleyn et al. (2018) did not produce primary observations of the cathartid syrinx but cites previous studies to claim that they lack syringeal muscles.

Not only was the syrinx in Cathartidae reported as absent, but most authors have also considered them to be voiceless (Allard, 1934; Beddard, 1890, 1898; Finkelstein et al., 2020; Forbes, 1881; Griffiths, 1994; Holste et al., 2020; Ligon, 1967; Miskimen, 1957; Negus, 1929; Pemberton, 1925; Rea, 1983). Negus (1929) and Miskimen (1957), among others, specifically proposed a functional correlation between the absence of syrinx and the supposed voiceless condition reported in Ca. aura. They suggested a voiceless condition to be a result of structural limitations as opposed to behavioral differences (Miskimen, 1957; Negus, 1929). Ligon (1967) also reported Cathartidae to be voiceless but had described Cathartidae as capable of producing hisses and grunts. More recent authors (Buckley et al., 2022; Finkelstein et al., 2020; Holste et al., 2020; Jones, 2020; Kirk & Mossman, 1998; McGahan, 2011) mention hisses, barks, croaks, sneezes, and grunts, as well as percussive staccato sounds reported only in V. gryphus (McGahan, 2011). Indeed, modern sound libraries, such as Xeno‐canto or the Macaulay Library at the Cornell Lab of Ornithology collectively contain over 400 New World vulture sounds (Figure 1), implying these birds may produce a diverse collection of vocalizations that have yet to be described. No study has hitherto systematically assessed anatomical structures potentially involved in vocal production in New World vultures, nor proposed functional hypotheses regarding the mechanisms involved in producing their recorded calls.

Here, we detail the anatomy of the syrinx, airway, and larynx in male and female individuals of two species of New World vultures, the Turkey Vulture (Ca. aura) and the Black Vulture (Co. atratus). We aim to test hypotheses of structural limitations on vocalization and absence of key syringeal features reported in the previous literature. We image and document the morphology of the airway and laryngeal region utilizing dissection and diffusible iodine‐based contrast‐enhanced x‐ray computed tomography (diceCT) imaging to better understand the relationships between hard tissues, membranes, and musculature. Histological methods are used to examine tissues attached to the bronchi. We compare the anatomy of these species with that described for other avian clades (e.g., Boccius, 1858; Bock, 1978; Clarke et al., 2016; Düring et al., 2013; Griffiths, 1994; King, 1989, 1993; King & White, 1975; Legendre et al., 2024; Monte et al., 2020; Riede & Goller, 2010, 2014). Additionally, we describe sounds produced in Cathartidae utilizing 495 audio recordings available in public databases. Finally, we assess the limitations of available data on vocalizations in the clade and consider potential sources for vocal sounds. This study may offer a framework to further document syrinxes to investigate vocal strategies in other understudied bird clades to provide insight into the evolution of vocal production.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Anatomical dataset

Our dataset consists of 11 larynxes and 16 syrinxes from Ca. aura and Co. atratus specimens of varying age and sex (Table 1). Texas Memorial Museum (TMM) specimens were obtained by the Vertebrate Paleontology Lab (VPL) of the Jackson School Museum of Earth History and prepared in VPL facilities. One Yale Peabody Museum (YPM) specimen was loaned as an excised trachea and syrinx. The Gallus specimen (ZMB 2000.967) in this dataset was imaged at the Museum für Naturkunde Berlin (MFN). TMM specimens were processed in accordance with the recommended preparation and syrinx collection procedures outlined in Bilger et al. (2020) with some modifications (see Methods below). Measurements of airway structures were taken in accordance with Bilger et al. (2020) to compare across species, age, and sex.

Dissections and diceCT

Specimen dissection, larynx collection, and syrinx collection were done at The University of Texas at Austin. All specimens were frozen before dissection and syrinx collection except for TMM M‐18061, which was dissected on the same day of collection. Photographs were taken throughout dissections using a Canon EOS 5D DSLR camera to document key stages of the dissections as well as key features for our study. Specimen dissection and syrinx collection were performed using the methods outlined in Bilger et al. (2020). We further modified this method to collect the entirety of the bronchial muscles unique to Cathartidae by excising the ribs where they attach. The entire rib cage and thoracic cavity were collected for three specimens (TMM M‐18061, TMM M‐15971, TMM M‐15923) for diceCT imaging. Specimens to be scanned were prepared, fixed, and stained for diceCT as outlined by Li et al. (2015, 2016) and the specifications for each scan are noted (Table 2). Specimens were scanned at The University of Texas High‐Resolution X‐ray Computed Tomography Facility (UTCT) with a custom NSI Helical Scanner. Structures were visualized, measured, and digitally segmented using Dragonfly version 2022.2 Build 1399 software (Comet Technologies Canada Inc., Montréal, Canada) and 3D Slicer version 5.8.1 (Fedorov et al., 2012). The relative CT values describing the differential attenuation of X‐rays by tissues, of cartilage, bone, and connective tissue, visualized using diceCT are known (e.g., Li et al., 2015). These ranges correspond approximately to grey scale ranges in raw image data. We assessed the degree of mineralization qualitatively by visually comparing the relative grey scale values and morphology of the tracheobronchial rings.

Histology

We used cryostat histology to assess whether the novel structures attached to the bronchial membranes were skeletal muscles. Tissues from specimens were prepared at UTCT and subsequently cryoprotected with a 20% sucrose solution and embedded in Optimal Cutting Temperature (OCT) Compound from Tissue‐Tek® (Sakura Finetek USA, Inc., Torrance, CA USA) and Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc. (Waltham, MA, USA). Thin sections were then prepared using a CryoStar™ NX50 cryostat (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) at The Center for Biomedical Research Support (CBRS) Microscopy and Imaging Facility at The University of Texas at Austin. Sections of TMM M‐18755 were stained using Hematoxylin–Eosin (H&E) staining (Feldman & Wolfe, 2014). When staining with H&E, we employed methodology developed by the University of California, San Diego (https://mousepheno.ucsd.edu/hematoxylin.shtml). We modified this protocol to accommodate our samples: we stained with Eosin Y for 1 min instead of the recommended 3 min to not overstain the tissues and employed two EtOH baths instead of 3 so as not to over‐dehydrate our samples. Instead of using Citrosol™ (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) as the clearing agent, we used a 50/50 Toluene EtOH solution followed by a 100% toluene bath to clear the slide. Finally, we mounted the coverslips using Permount™ instead of Cytoseal™ 60 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.). Imaging of the slides was done with a Nikon Eclipse Ni compound light microscope equipped with a Nikon DS‐Ri2 color camera (Nikon Corp., Tokyo, Japan) from CBRS facilities.

Acoustic data

Audio files for Cathartidae sound recordings were obtained from Xeno‐canto (https://xeno‐canto.org) and the Macaulay Library at the Cornell Lab of Ornithology (https://www.macaulaylibrary.org). Four hundred and ninety‐five total audio recordings were categorized into vocalizations and mechanical sounds. Vocalizations were further categorized as barks, hisses, percussive sounds, and alarm calls (Data S1 and S2). Mechanical sounds are defined as wingbeats or feeding sounds. Recordings were categorized by compiling the collector's notes for each respective recording, listening to each recording, and viewing the spectrograms for each recording. Sex and age data were unavailable for most recordings. Select audio recordings of particular quality were analyzed in detail (Table S1). Initial visualization and analysis of the acoustic data deployed Audacity v. 3.7.1 (Audacity Team, 2024). Audacity software (Audacity Team, 2024) was then used to crop and export audio files as .wav files. Spectrograms and power spectra were created in R v. 4.5.2 (R Core Team, 2025) using an R script developed by Carlos A. Rodriquez‐Saltos, Hans Bilger, and Michael Chiappone and used in Chiappone et al. (2024); the protocol utilizes the R packages seewave v. 2.2.4 (Sueur et al., 2008), tidyverse v. 2.0.0 (Wickham et al., 2019), cowplot v. 1.2.0 (Wilke, 2025), tuneR v. 1.4.7 (Ligges et al., 2023), and viridis v. 0.6.5 (Garnier et al., 2024).

RESULTS

Laryngeal cartilages

Laryngeal cartilages found in Cathartidae comprise the cricoid, arytenoid, and procricoid cartilages (Figure 2). In both Ca. aura and Co. atratus, the caudal and rostral‐most parts of the cricoid cartilages are more mineralized than the lateral sides of the body of the cricoid (Figure 2). These more mineralized parts of the cricoid include its cartilaginous tip (King, 1989) and wings. The cartilaginous tip of the cricoid has a broad spatulate shape, and the caudal end of the cricoid is unfused (Figure 2). The two arytenoids are unfused and sit between the wings of the cricoid, extending rostrocaudally. Their dorsal processes arise around the midpoint of the arytenoid cartilages. The males in both Ca. aura and Co. atratus have slightly longer dorsal arytenoid processes than the females of their respective species (Figure 2; Table S2). Potential ontogenetic variation of the arytenoids cannot be ruled out as age specifications were not estimated upon intake of our salvage specimens. The procricoid is situated at the caudal end of the cricoid along the midline and is shaped like a tetrahedron with its apex dorsally positioned (Figure 2). There are two short lamellae that appear to be glottal folds on the arytenoids (Figure S2).

The morphology of these cartilages does not appear to lie outside the variation observed among other non‐passerine birds (e.g., Figure S3), in which the arytenoids' length varies widely. The reduced area of mineralization of the lateral region of the cricoid in Cathartidae is similar to that observed in the described adult G. gallus (Figure S3a,e), Ardea cinerea, and Accipitridae (Atalgin et al., 2021; Boccius, 1858; Hogg, 1982; King, 1989; King & White, 1975). The glottal folds on the arytenoid cartilages are also similar in shape and location in both Ca. aura and in G. gallus (ZMB 2000.967; Figure S2). A procricoid cartilage was not reported in A. cinerea (Atalgin et al., 2021); however, it should be noted that there remains very limited data on the avian larynx.

Tracheal and bronchial rings

In both Ca. aura and Co. atratus tracheal ring morphology is distinct after the trachea passes lung‐ward through the interclavicular air sac, cranial to the tracheobronchial juncture. The girth, craniocaudal extent, and degree of mineralization of the tracheal rings just cranial to the tracheobronchial juncture are decreased in the ventral aspect; here, the rings are thin, irregular, and obsolete in places (Figure 3a,d). In contrast, the lateral aspects of these most caudal tracheal rings are well‐mineralized and there is little space between each ring (Figure 3c,f). Ventrally and toward the midline, however, there are larger gaps between these thinner successive tracheal rings. In Ca. aura, these tracheal rings also become dorsally incomplete (Figure 3b). This dorsal incompleteness is also found in tracheal ring 0, which is situated at the tracheobronchial juncture. Only tracheal ring 0, however, is dorsally incomplete in Co. atratus while the cranial rings are complete but thin (Figure 3d). This ring also bears a midline element similar to a pessulus or carina (Kingsley et al., 2018), supporting the bifurcation to the bronchi. This midline element varies in mineralization and in completeness (Figure 3b,d,e). Variability was observed among specimens of both species in the connections between tracheal rings and this ubiquitous, thin midline element. This element lacks a defined ridge that extends cranially or a separately ossified pessuloid element (Chiappone et al., 2024). Lung‐ward of tracheal ring 0 are bronchial rings, which are complete, but exhibit thinning and decreased mineralization medially while having increased mineralization laterally (Figure 3c,f); this organization creates larger gaps between the medial parts of the rings (Figure 3a,b,d,e). The last four bronchial rings in Co. atratus are incomplete towards the midline while in Ca. aura, the last three bronchial rings are incomplete (Figure 3). The bronchial rings terminate well before the bronchi reach the lungs in both species, only covering approximately half of the distance between the tracheobronchial juncture and the lung. Chicks and immature specimens were found to exhibit the same pattern of thin tracheobronchial rings as adult specimens.

In both species, there is asymmetry in the bronchi; the right bronchi are slightly longer and have more rings than the left bronchi (Figure 3, Table S3). The final few rings in both right and left bronchi are incomplete medially (Figure 3). Notably, the final bronchial rings are more heavily mineralized and have rounder cross sections than the more cranial bronchial rings, showing modification of ring morphology in this region (Figure 3f).

Tracheal musculature

The m. tracheolateralis (TL) is present in both Ca. aura and Co. atratus (Figure 4a). It arises just below the glottis and passes caudally down the lateral sides of the trachea until just caudal to the interclavicular air sac; here, the TL pair merge with each other, coming into contact with each other on the ventral side of the trachea along the midline and then contact the m. sternotrachealis (ST) (Figure 4c). The contact between the two muscle pairs is about 5‐ to 10‐mm cranial to the insertion of the ST onto the trachea (Figure 4c). This contact is ambiguous as there is a short region where all of these muscles appear to overlap on the ventral portion of the trachea (Figure 4c). The ST pair passes caudally from the ventral side of the trachea, about 16 mm above the tracheobronchial juncture, to attach to the cranial midline of the sternum between the coracoids (Figure 4b,c). This unusual origin for the ST was noted by Beddard (1898). The ST attaches to the sternum at about a 45‐degree angle to the trachea (Figure 4c). The two ST muscle bodies, although not fused, are in very close contact with each other as they arise from the sternal rostrum, appearing as one unit in both CT and in dissection (Figure 4b).

Membranous structures

In both Ca. aura and Co. atratus, we find no labia or vocal folds at the tracheobronchial juncture (Figure 5d); the tracheobronchial lumen can be said to be empty; no labia are present. Additionally, the trachea also contains no evidence of a labia or modified membranes. In both Ca. aura and Co. atratus, small irregular gaps between both tracheal rings and bronchial rings are filled with connective tissue forming the tracheal wall (Figure S4). Similar tissue fills gaps where the tracheal rings in Ca. aura are incomplete dorsally. No bronchial rings are medially incomplete in the cranial portion of the bronchi, which is where a medial tympaniform membrane would be typically located and circumscribed by these rings. Finally, we find no evidence of an interbronchial ligament; however, we find connective tissue that passes from the right bronchus to the esophagus (Figure S4).

The bronchi, lung‐ward of the short sections close to the tracheobronchial juncture, are not supported by bronchial rings. Membranous connective tissue, forming the bronchial walls, originates from the modified distal‐most bronchial rings and extends to the lungs (Figure 5b). An epithelial layer and an underlying lamina propria are not able to be assessed by CT imaging; thus, these structures were not identified. The right bronchus has a higher bronchial ring count and shorter membranous section than the left bronchus. The membranous sections of the bronchi become more circular and tube‐like as they pass towards the lungs.

Thoracic‐bronchial muscles

In both Ca. aura and Co. atratus, six muscles, three on both the right and left, originate from between the subcostal and internal intercostal muscles on the medial aspect of the sternal component of the juncture between the sternal and vertebral ribs (Figures 5c and S5). Histology of these muscles revealed striations indicative of overlapping proteins in a sarcomere and multiple peripheral nuclei (Figure 6), characterizing them as skeletal muscles (Gaspar et al., 2019). These muscles insert onto the ventral side of the membranous portion of the bronchi (Figure 5c). As the muscles pass from origin to insertion, they are anchored to the dorsal thoracic wall by a thin fascia. These bronchial muscles have a branching pattern at both their attachment and insertion sites, increasing the surface area of attachment and insertion. The first bronchial muscle is the shortest; successive bronchial muscles get progressively longer as they attach further away from the tracheobronchial juncture (Figure 5a). Below these bronchial muscles are the costopulmonales muscles, which have the same origin on the juncture between the sternal and vertebral ribs (Figure 5c). The costopulmonales muscles bear a resemblance in appearance to the bronchial muscles, but do not insert onto the bronchi (Figure 5c).

Acoustic data

Cathartid vultures, as represented in the 495 sound files assessed, can be said to produce a variety of vocalizations, with some having tonal and harmonic elements. These vocalizations include barks, staccatos, boos, grunts, and two types of hisses (Figure 7). In addition to vocal sounds, cathartid vultures are also represented in online sound libraries by abundant mechanical sounds such as wingbeats (Figure 7). Many recordings contain both mechanical and vocal sounds, which are distinguished via power spectra, spectrogram, and recording notes.

For Co. atratus, the most represented species in these databases, there are 217 vocalization recordings and 158 mechanical sound recordings (Figure 1). Co. atratus has the widest vocal repertoire, as represented in these databases, among cathartid vultures, producing barks, grunts, and two types of hisses (Table 3). For V. gryphus there were only seven vocalization recordings and three mechanical sound recordings (Figure 1); the vocalizations include hisses and repeated percussive staccato sounds. This percussive staccato vocalization, based on the limited sample available for Cathartidae, appears to be exclusive to V. gryphus. For G. californianus, there are four vocalization recordings and six mechanical sounds recordings (Figure 1); the vocalizations include hisses, grunts, and boos. Boos were exclusively noted in G. californianus, potentially due to limited sampling of the clade. The remaining species only have hisses represented in vocalization recordings. For Ca. aura, there are 43 vocalization recordings and 46 mechanical sound recordings, for Ca. burrovianus and Ca. melambrotus there are two vocalization recordings and one mechanical sound recording per species, and for S. papa there is one vocalization recording and four mechanical sound recordings (Figure 1).

The first categorized vocalization type, “hisses,” is characterized by low frequency peaks at 300–500 Hz (Figure S6), and a broad and turbulent range of frequency (Figure 7). Hisses lack tonal and harmonic elements. This type of vocalization is represented in recordings of Co. atratus, Ca. aura, Ca. burrovianus, Ca. melambrotus, G. californianus, and S. papa. Hisses are found in both adults and chicks. According to recording notes, this call type is often associated with defensive behavior. Hisses can be of long duration, with some recordings showing hisses lasting for over 20 s (Figure 7).

“Grunts” are found in both Co. atratus and G. californianus and are characterized by a steady and flat low dominant frequency, around 240 Hz in G. californianus (Figure S6), with static turbulent groupings of frequencies above (Figure 7). Within these turbulent frequencies are groups of frequencies that appear to be potential subharmonics of the dominant frequency until 16 kHz. These “turbulent frequencies,” however, may be artifacts of the spectral energy noise floors imposed either by the recording equipment or the amplitude window used to make the spectrograms; thus, interpretations must be made with caution.

Co. atratus exhibits many call types that were not found in other species. “High frequency hisses” are characterized by three distinct stacked groups of frequencies that have a duration of about 0.4 s. The first grouping is around 500 Hz (Figure 7). The second frequency group is centered around 2.5 kHz, and the third group is centered around 5 kHz (Figure 7). “Barks” are characterized by a quick wheeze sound with a dominant frequency between 100 Hz and 350 Hz, a duration of 0.2 s, and harmonics that range from approximately 350 to 1400 Hz (Figures 7 and S6). Next, there is a broadband pulse, with a duration of 0.25 s, ranging from approximately 1700 Hz to greater than 19,000 Hz (Figures 7 and S7). Sweep barks share most characteristics with barks but have a notable upward and downward sweep in frequency (Figures 7 and S6). “Sweep barks” also show a low‐energy frequency band below the dominant frequency; thus, it is possible that the fundamental frequency is too low to be radiated.

“Boo” vocalizations only appear in recordings of G. californianus. Boos are vocalizations that have a similarity to boos in Struthio camelus (Chiappone et al., 2024). Cathartid boos have an upward and downward sweep in frequencies with accompanying harmonics, all below 1 kHz, a dominant frequency around 215 Hz, and a duration of about 0.6 s (Figures 7 and S6).

“Percussive staccato” vocalizations were found only in V. gryphus. They are characterized by rapid (0.2 s interval) low dominant frequency (~140 Hz) sweeping notes; their dominant frequency has accompanying sidebands (Figures 7 and S6). Like the bark call type, the percussive staccato is followed by a pulse of broadband frequencies (Figure 7). This sound is produced while on the ground with the wings outstretched and the mouth open (Tama‐toa‐muka, 2013). Occasionally, this percussive staccato will be paired with slower beating of the wings (Tama‐toa‐muka, 2013).

The most common mechanical sounds captured in online sound libraries are wingbeats. Wingbeats appear as more variably spaced pulses of sound with dominant frequencies around 550 Hz and less dominant lower frequencies around 187 Hz (Figure 7). The amplitudes of these wingbeats are not regular. All species in Cathartidae have recordings of wingbeats (Figure 7).

DISCUSSION

Central to the morphological definition of a syrinx has been the presence of modified mineralized tracheal and/or bronchial rings that provide structural support to vocal membranes or folds (e.g., Beddard, 1898; Clarke et al., 2016; King, 1989; Kingsley et al., 2018). Our findings support differentiation of the caudal bronchial rings in Cathartidae. Distinctly thin ventrally incomplete rings are present in the parts of the trachea enclosed by an interclavicular air sac, and a thin ossified midline element supports tracheobronchial juncture at the divergence of the bronchi (Figure 3a–f). While incomplete cranial bronchial rings are clearly optimized as the ancestral condition for Aves (e.g., Clarke et al., 2016), cathartid vultures depart from the avian ancestral state in having complete cranial bronchial rings. Notably, other vocal avian clades that show complete cranial bronchial rings also present a highly derived bronchial syrinx—for example, the oilbird (Suthers & Hector, 1985) or penguins (Kriesell et al., 2020). Incomplete lungwardmost bronchial rings are further differentiated from other rings in the bronchial series; they are thick and uniquely round in cross section (Figure 3c,f). Co. atratus has four incomplete lung‐ward‐most bronchial rings while Ca. aura has three (Figure 3a,d).

Vocal membranes or folds, attached to these modified mineralized tracheal and/or bronchial rings and acting as sound sources, constitute an essential element of the ancestral avian syrinx (Beddard, 1898; Clarke et al., 2016; Düring & Elemans, 2016; Goller, 2016, 2022; Goller & Larsen, 1997, 2002; King, 1989; Kingsley et al., 2018; Larsen & Goller, 1999). In Cathartidae there are no labia or distinct membranes present in the trachea or close to the tracheobronchial juncture in the species examined that would appear to comprise vocal folds; membranous areas are irregular and not associated with more robust, supportive rings (Figure 4c). We confirm Beddard's (1890, 1898) description of extraordinarily thin, irregular rings.

Only the bronchial membranes are large enough to be drawn significantly into the airway. These membranes are uniquely associated with the incomplete caudal bronchial rings that are more robust and rounder in cross section (Figures 3c,f and 5a,b). Round rings are observed in paleognaths to be uniquely associated with vocal folds (Chiappone et al., 2024) in their otherwise only weakly differentiated tracheobronchial area. Expansive bronchial membranes (Figure S1) were first noted by Beddard (1890, 1898), but they were considered of unclear function. They were hypothesized by Miskimen (1957) to bear sufficient structural support to prevent sound‐producing vibrations. Griffiths (1994) also reported these membranes in Coragyps, Cathartes, and Vultur.

Most syrinxes have muscular attachments to modify vibratory membranes, vocal folds, or the vocal tract (Düring & Elemans, 2016; Kingsley et al., 2018). The two extrinsic muscles common to nearly all avian clades, and estimated to be ancestrally present, are the TL and the ST (Clarke et al., 2016; King, 1989; Kingsley et al., 2018). The TL typically originates on the trachea near the glottis and passes down the lateral margins of the trachea caudally, while the ST typically originates on the dorsal surfaces of the craniolateral processes of the sternum and inserts onto the trachea near the end of the TL (Düring et al., 2013; Goller & Suthers, 1996; Suthers & Hector, 1985). The TL and ST have been shown to act antagonistically to each other, controlling the syringeal aperture (Elemans et al., 2006; Suthers & Hector, 1985). The TL can act to shorten the trachea (Elemans et al., 2006; Gaunt & Gaunt, 1985a; Gaunt & Wells, 1973; Suthers & Hector, 1982, 1985). Conversely, the ST stretches the trachea, reducing tension in the bronchi (Gaunt & Gaunt, 1985a; Gaunt & Wells, 1973; Suthers & Hector, 1982, 1985) and potentially also providing stability to the syrinx (Düring et al., 2013; Goller & Suthers, 1996).

The TL in Cathartidae is similar to that of other described taxa (Düring & Elemans, 2016; Goller & Suthers, 1996; Suthers & Hector, 1982, 1985) and could shorten the trachea and elongate the bronchi and bronchial membranes (Suthers & Hector, 1985). The unique ST in Cathartidae, with an origin on the sternal midline rather than on the lateral processes of the sternum, has an angle of 45° at insertion onto the trachea. It could continue to act caudoventrally to stretch the trachea (Gaunt & Wells, 1973; Suthers & Hector, 1982, 1985) and lessen tension in the enlarged bronchial membranes. It is worth noting that the ST is similar in cross‐sectional area to that of other birds but much shorter. While origins on the paired lateral processes of other birds could usefully anchor or stabilize the tracheobronchial juncture in these other taxa, the narrow area of origin on the midline in Cathartidae would appear less useful for this function.

Two pairs of three thoracic‐bronchial muscles, or costo‐bronchial muscles, first remarked by Beddard (1890, 1898, Figure S1), insert onto the ventral surface of the bronchial membranes (Figure 5). Their orientation indicates they would act ventrolaterally. The action of these muscles could bring the cranioventral part of the bronchial membranes that lie close to the modified terminal bronchial rings under tension. The dorsal part of the bronchi in the area of the bronchial half‐rings may be differentially drawn into the airway. Electromyographic study of these muscles with the TL and ST are needed to assess potential activation during respiration and/or vocal production. Such a study may also provide insight into the observed pulse of broadband frequencies after the harmonics in the bark and percussive staccato call types (Figure 7). These pulses may be related to the relaxation of the syringeal muscles and the subsequent release of tension in the bronchial membranes responsible for the harmonic/tonal note prior, but experimental data is needed. Additionally, these muscles could act to stabilize the airway as the ST has been shown to do in other taxa (Elemans et al., 2006; Goller & Suthers, 1996).

The bronchial muscles in Cathartidae could be derived from the same precursors in ontogeny to the avian costopulmonales muscles, with which they share their area of origin (Fedde et al., 1964; Schachner & Moore, 2025), but not insertion. When present, costopulmonales muscles also arise from the thoracic wall in a similar position medially to the sternal component of the juncture between the sternal and vertebral ribs. However, they insert into the aponeurosis of the horizontal septum, which wraps the ventral surface of the lung, and hypophyses of the thoracic vertebrae (Schachner & Moore, 2025). The costopulmonales have not been found to insert on the bronchi. We cannot rule out that the bronchial muscles and the novel arrangement of the ST in Cathartidae may have a secondary or exclusive respiratory function. However, it is unclear why Cathartidae would uniquely gain novel muscles to function in respiration relative to all other birds, or how potentially adducting part of the bronchial membranes into the airway during expiration would function usefully. Other respiratory functions, such as controlling the distribution of ventilation in the lungs, are accomplished by pulmonary smooth muscles (Cieri, 2019), whereas the bronchial muscles described here are skeletal muscles.

The relationship between cathartid vocal morphology and the clearly harmonic sounds produced by the clade should be investigated with a mechanical model such as the starling box (e.g., Elemans et al., 2009; Knowlton & Starling, 1912). A typical starling box suspends a flexible tube, representing the syringeal membranes, between two rigid tubes, acknowledging the position of the syringeal membranes between the tracheobronchial juncture and the caudal aspects of the bronchi, within an external pressure chamber (Elemans et al., 2009). Cathartid vultures do not have syringeal membranes in this position but instead have bronchial membranes that pass from the caudal aspect of the bronchi to the lungs. Nonetheless, the cathartid bronchial membranes may be modeled as flexible tubes suspended between two rigid tubes because the cranial bronchial rings are complete and the avian lung is considered rigid and fixed in position (e.g., Maina, 2025). The tension exerted on the bronchial membranes by the cathartid bronchial muscles and other syringeal muscles acting elsewhere (e.g., TL and ST) may be modeled as external pressure on the membranes via an external pressure chamber (Elemans et al., 2009). This model accounts for the difference in pressure between the interclavicular air sac and the inside of the syrinx (Elemans et al., 2009). A mechanical model will be valuable in assessing whether the syringeal anatomy in Cathartidae is conducive to producing similar fundamental frequencies and harmonics to those observed in recorded cathartid vocalizations (Figure 7) or if the bronchial muscles have some other functional association, as the bronchial muscles may serve to stiffen the tube and prevent oscillation of the membranes during respiration.

The larynx in cathartids may be deployed in simple hisses or grunts, as hypothesized for other birds (Chiappone et al., 2024; Thys & Eens, 2025); its morphology and associated soft tissues appear consistent with those of other birds. Because the laryngeal cartilages of Cathartidae do not differ significantly in organization, soft tissue structure, or differential mineralization from those in other non‐passerine clades (Boccius, 1858; Hogg, 1982; King & White, 1975; Negus, 1929; Figure S3a–d), we conclude that they likely do not have a distinct and novel function. The avian larynx is composed of the cricoid, arytenoid, and procricoid cartilages and is located dorsal to the basihyal‐urohyal elements of the hyobranchial arch (Düring & Elemans, 2016; Homberger, 2017; Negus, 1929).

Negus (1929) considered an avian larynx with elongate arytenoids to be like those of other reptiles and unable to function in complex sound production. Enlarged arytenoids were proposed to be related to a lack of significant design trade‐offs between a primary larynx function in excluding food from the airway and vocal production (Negus, 1929); the avian larynx is primarily a valve that prevents food from entering the airway (Düring & Elemans, 2016; Kitchen, 1885; Negus, 1929). By contrast, Yoshida et al. (2023) proposed elongate arytenoids and a broad cricoid (both relative to mandible width) as unique to birds and proposed these features to relate to a role in active vocal sound modulation and not vocal production. The upper trachea, oropharyngeal cavity shaped by the hyobranchial elements, and beak all contribute to processing syrinx‐generated sounds (Goller, 2016). In some studies, the glottal opening has been shown to influence the suprasyringeal pressure during phonation (Fletcher et al., 2004; Laje et al., 2001). Modification of the glottal opening in birds including by active constriction of the airway leading to sound modulation is possible, but electromyographical evidence supporting activation of these muscles during phonation of a syrinx is lacking (Goller, 2016). Major hyobranchial movements and not diminutive laryngeal muscles have been demonstrated to dynamically alter the shape of the vocal tract during singing in songbirds (Riede & Suthers, 2009; Suthers et al., 2016). In addition to these studies, zebra finches have been shown to produce aerodynamic whistles inside the larynx, not the syrinx, during inspiration (Anttonen et al., 2025). Although these whistles are soft and not likely associated with behavior, they highlight an example of vocal production in the avian larynx (Anttonen et al., 2025).

We find support for potential morphological differences in the songbird larynx relative to outgroups including cathartids. Arytenoid cartilages in the passerine Corvus brachyrhynchos (Bock, 1978) are rostrally much shorter and have more blunt rostral surfaces; the wings of the cricoid are in contact with each other; and the procricoid is in contact with both the body of the arytenoid and separate dorsal cricoid elements (Figure S3e). While G. gallus, in early developmental stages, shows a pair of separate ossification centers in the dorsal cricoid (Hogg, 1982; King & White, 1975), in C. brachyrhynchos these elements (sometimes referred to as cricoid wings) appear to remain separate in the adult (Bock, 1978; King, 1993). In contrast with C. brachyrhynchos, the arytenoid cartilages in C. corone are elongated rostrally like in Co. atratus, Buteo lagopus, and Tetrao urogallus (Boccius, 1858; Bock, 1978; Figure S3a,b,d,f). More information on laryngeal variation and experimental data on related muscle function is clearly needed.

We describe cathartid vocalizations present in online sound libraries, identifying six types of calls: that is, barks, staccato, boos, grunts, and two types of hisses. Barks, staccatos, boos, and high‐frequency hisses in Cathartidae have a structure including harmonic stacks that implies some level of vocal control (Greenewalt, 1968) and production in Cathartidae (Figure 7). Although this study highlights a variety of vocalization types, most vocalization recordings in online sound libraries do not include data on the sex, age, and behavior of the individual captured in the recordings. These data limitations prevent a better understanding of the behavioral functions of different vocalizations, such as whether certain sound types are produced preferentially in certain contexts by individuals of a particular sex or ontogenetic stage.

There is a significant bias in the online sound libraries toward the two most common North American species, Co. atratus and Ca. aura. Species found exclusively in South America are sparsely represented. G. californianus also has a limited amount of published online recordings, likely due to the species' restricted geographic range, small population size, and conservation status (BirdLife International, 2020; US Fish & Wildlife Service, 2023). While it is possible that the larger repertoire present in Co. atratus is a product of its more vocal nature, we cannot conclude this with certainty. Sounds produced by one species, such as Co. atratus, are not necessarily exclusive to that species and may in fact be produced in all cathartids but remain unsampled. The same is true of other sounds like boos in G. californianus and staccatos in V. gryphus. This is an important consideration because morphological novelties in the tracheobronchial region appear to be conserved in the clade, meaning that all cathartid vultures may be expected to have the structural capability to produce similar calls.

The primary focus of our study is on Ca. aura and Co. atratus. Although these species share a close evolutionary history with other cathartid species (Johnson et al., 2016), investigations into other members of Cathartidae are warranted to understand the clade's variation in tracheobronchial morphology. Nonetheless, by confirming observations made by Beddard (1890, 1898) on V. gryphus in other more distantly related cathartid vultures (Johnson et al., 2016), it can be inferred that the traits described in Ca. aura and Co. atratus may be synapomorphies for the clade. Early surveys of Aves identifying cathartid bronchial membranes and associated muscles as unique (Beddard, 1890, 1898) are corroborated by our own assessment of diceCT data on related regions in other birds (e.g., Chiappone et al., 2024; Legendre et al., 2024), in which bronchial muscles were also not found.

We show that members of Cathartidae have a syrinx, as defined morphologically, and produce vocalizations beyond simple hisses and grunts. It is unclear, and currently untested, whether cathartid vultures use their larynx or their modified bronchi to produce vocalizations. Larynx‐based sounds in birds are seldom understood and explored (Goller, 2016), but the larynx has been identified as the source of some hisses (see Brackenbury, 1978; Chiappone et al., 2024; Thys & Eens, 2025) and whistles (Anttonen et al., 2025) in Aves. Electromyographic experiments are necessary to assess the function of the bronchial muscles and to determine if they have a respiratory and/or sound‐producing role. Histological sections of the bronchial membranes must be examined to assess whether these membranes are involved in vocal production. Mechanical modeling of the cathartid syrinx will provide insights into the relationship between their syringeal anatomy and known vocalizations. Greater sampling is required to understand the variation in both morphology and vocalization in Cathartidae. Such investigations will be crucial in determining whether cathartid vultures have a novel method for producing sound in the bronchi, using the bronchial muscles and membranes, or if the larynx is capable of producing tonal vocalizations.

CONCLUSION

Cathartid species possess traits of the bronchi unlike those in any other avian clade. They may comprise a completely novel form of the syrinx. Cathartid vultures have thin and irregular tracheal rings just cranial to the tracheobronchial juncture, and incomplete, cross‐sectionally round, and differentially robust caudalmost bronchial rings. Membranes insert onto these modified caudal bronchial rings and further expand into the lungs. The TL and ST are present in Cathartidae, with the ST having a novel origin on the midline of the sternum. Furthermore, three pairs of muscles are inserted onto these bronchial membranes and originate on the thoracic wall from the vertebral ribs. These muscles may modify the tension and shape of the bronchial membranes, potentially facilitating vibratory and vocal function. We also demonstrate that New World vultures are not voiceless and in fact have a vocal repertoire including harmonic/tonal sounds. Experimental work and further acoustic and anatomical data collection are required to assess whether cathartid vultures share more of their acoustic repertoire and if modifications of the bronchi, extrinsic muscles, and novel thoracic muscles are involved in vocal production or if these structures are utilized in another function. This research highlights the importance of studying the syrinx and larynx of other understudied avian clades in order to broaden our understanding of the evolution and function of the syrinx and vocal production in birds.
