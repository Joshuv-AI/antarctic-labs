Luminescence meets physical unclonability in fracture engineered porous polymer skeletons for anticounterfeiting
Authors: Shengwei Feng, Lipeng Qin, Lei Guo, Xinjian Xie, Xiongwei Luo, Hongjiao Li, Yijun Zhang, Tao He, Wenqian Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCounterfeiting remains a persistent global challenge, necessitating security technologies that combine robust unclonability with rapid, field-verifiable accessibility. Conventional optical labels with uniform designs are easily replicated, while most physical unclonable functions (PUFs) rely on complex fabrication processes or specialized readout systems, limiting their practical deployment, especially considering the broad accessibility of smartphone-based authentication. Here, we report a materials-driven strategy that unifies luminescence and physical unclonability through fracture-engineered polymer PUFs. Porous polymer skeletons bearing customized patterns are printed via maskless photolithography and subsequently functionalized by surface-initiated ATRP with Ir-based complexes and fluorescent dyes. This design yields Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET)-enabled, intensity-amplified, and spectrally multiplexed emissions under single-wavelength excitation, enabling optical information encryption and straightforward decoding. Crucially, the porous architecture supports fracture-engineered tape transfer that preserves macroscopic luminescent patterns while spontaneously generating stochastic microscale features at each pixel, producing intrinsically unclonable fingerprints. The resulting mechanically durable tags integrate bright, stable luminescence with reliable PUF characteristics and enable field-readable authentication using only a handheld flashlight and smartphone imaging, without the need for specialized instrumentation. By coupling luminescence with luminescence-enhanced intrinsic PUF security on easily readable tags, this work establishes a versatile and scalable platform for on-site customization and on-demand anti-counterfeiting authentication.Subject terms: Polymers, Organic molecules in materials science, Information storage

Introduction

Counterfeiting poses a critical threat to global commerce, public health, and national security1–3. This challenge manifests in falsified pharmaceuticals, forged documents, and substandard components that infiltrate supply chains and compromise critical infrastructure4–7. Conventional safeguards, such as serial numbers and printed security labels, offer limited protection and are increasingly vulnerable to sophisticated replication8–13. This growing challenge underscores the urgent need for materials-based strategies that encode authenticity in a manner resistant to forgery, while enabling scalable deployment of on-site, customized security solutions without reliance on complex instrumentation.

Luminescent security labels address part of this demand by combining scalability, ease of production, and straightforward visual readout14–20. Typically, fluorescent inks are printed onto substrates to produce features that emit visible fluorescence or phosphorescence under ultraviolet light, thereby displaying overt or covert patterns21–24. However, purely luminescent labels remain inherently insecure, as their spectral signatures and emission patterns can be easily analyzed and replicated. Multi-channel fluorescence strategies can improve encoding capacity by combining multiple dyes12,25,26, but typically require multi-wavelength excitation and professional-grade fluorescence microscopy, severely constraining their practicality for routine, field-deployable authentication.

Security labels incorporating physical unclonable functions (PUFs) represent a promising alternative to conventional anti-counterfeiting technologies and are regarded as one of the most viable strategies to address the escalating problem of global forgery27–30. A PUF is a physical entity characterized by intrinsic, unique, and random features that emerge from non-deterministic processes, ensuring code outputs that cannot be replicated. Considerable advances in PUF-based encryption have been achieved in the anti-counterfeiting domain, particularly through the generation of random features such as rough surface morphologies31,32. For example, Sunkook et al. used laser ablation to form random micro-pit structures on photosensitive substrates, creating a physically unclonable anti-counterfeiting tag via binarization and encryption of the optical images33. In related approaches, PUF-based encryption is realized by confining discrete nanoparticle arrays within well-defined patterned regions34–37. Alternatively, PUF anti-counterfeiting labels can be constructed by exploiting random optical scattering in porous media to generate diverse speckle patterns38. Despite their inherent security advantages, most existing silica-based PUFs exhibit vulnerabilities to modeling attacks and suffer from low reliability39. Integrating PUF-based systems with conventional anti-counterfeiting technologies to enhance their overall security level, information storage capacity, and readability remains a significant challenge40–44.

A compelling strategy, therefore, is to unify the accessibility of luminescent labels with the intrinsic unclonability of PUFs, thereby creating security features that combine high output complexity and convenient readout capabilities while remaining fundamentally impossible to replicate. Achieving this balance, however, is nontrivial. The challenge arises from the seemingly contradictory requirements of generating a regular, readable luminescent pattern for optical authentication while simultaneously maintaining an irregular distribution of information for unclonability. As a result, only a handful of anti-counterfeiting methods have been reported to date—such as the stochastic pinning of quantum dots at the three-phase contact line during droplet evaporation—which exemplify these constraints45–47. While capable of producing unique microscale patterns, these methods are still constrained by single-level luminescence, stringent process control, indirect chemical-assisted bonding that reduces stability under external stress, and the need for specialized, costly identifiers that compromise robust readout. These factors severely limit their scalability, practicality, and suitability for real-time or on-demand production of PUF-based security features. Recent work by Zhang48 et al. embedded semiconducting polymer nanoparticles into an SU-8 photoresist and combined this strategy with photolithographic patterning to construct multiscale PUF anti-counterfeiting labels, enabling portable optical readout. Although this work demonstrates the strong potential of luminescent PUFs for high-security authentication, its fabrication still relies on nanoparticle doping, photoresist patterning and subsequent development processes. Moreover, the retention of encoded information under more complex practical conditions, such as mechanical handling and organic solvent exposure, remains to be further improved. Therefore, developing luminescent PUF labels that combine simpler fabrication, field-readable authentication and stable information output under mechanical stress and solvent exposure remains highly desirable.

To address these limitations, we present a facile strategy in this study for generating luminescent PUF tags through fracture-induced randomization of patterned 3D porous polymer skeletons (Fig. 1a). In this approach, a 3D porous skeleton with atom transfer radical polymerization (ATRP) reactivity is printed onto substrates using maskless photolithography, creating a platform for surface-initiated ATRP (SI-ATRP) grafting of fluorescent dyes. Critically, the skeleton enables site-specific binding of Ir-based complexes, whose strong binding establishes efficient Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET) with the grafted dyes, producing bright multicolor emission with a wide spectral gap under a single excitation source. By precisely tuning the mechanical properties of the polymer skeleton, its porous architecture functions as a reusable master template for repeated fracture-engineered film transfer, with successful A4-scale printing and full-frame transfer demonstrating compatibility with scalable production of this class of tag products. During transfer, stochastic fractures arise at individual printed pixels, generating inherently unpredictable local fingerprints that ensure the unclonability of the resulting PUF tags. Statistical evaluation of key metrics, including bit uniformity, inter- and intra-Hamming distances, and correlation coefficients, demonstrates that the proposed PUF system achieves high randomness, robustness, and uniqueness, confirming its suitability for reliable anti-counterfeiting authentication. Strong multi-luminescence, wide emission separation, and an 80 µm fundamental length scale (pixel size) enable reliable readout of both encrypted luminescent information and fracture-engineered PUF features using only a handheld blacklight, a simple optical filter, and standard smartphone imaging, eliminating the need for specialized multichannel microscopes (Fig. 1b-d). By combining intrinsic randomness, hierarchical security, optical invisibility, compatibility with existing anti-counterfeiting strategies, and robust resistance to environmental stressors such as solvents, abrasion, and compression, these fracture-engineered tags provide a durable, reliable, and scalable platform, positioning them for deployment as cutting-edge anti-counterfeiting technology.

Results

Printing ATRP-reactive porous polymer skeletons via maskless lithography

The overall fabrication strategy for porous polymer skeletons, along with subsequent modification with luminescent molecules, is depicted in Fig. 1a. At the molecular level, 2-(2-bromoisobutyryloxy)ethyl methacrylate (BrMA) was chosen as the key functional monomer, as it introduces pendant alkyl bromide groups that act as initiating sites for ATRP18. Together with BrMA, hydroxyethyl methacrylate (HEMA), the cross-linker ethylene dimethacrylate (EDMA), and the photoinitiator phenylbis(2,4,6-trimethylbenzoyl)phosphine oxide were dissolved in a carefully optimized mixture of cyclohexanol and 1-decanol, which acted as inert porogens49,50. Upon light irradiation, photoinitiation triggered rapid radical polymerization, while simultaneous phase separation between the growing polymer and porogen-rich domains induced pore formation, yielding a rough, interconnected network51,52. An important feature of this light-driven polymerization is its compatibility with maskless digital light processing (DLP) lithography53, which enables rapid and arbitrary patterning without the need for photomasks. In our setup, a 405 nm light source was coupled to a digital micromirror device (DMD), allowing computer-designed images to be projected directly into the monomer solution. Polymerization took place exclusively in the illuminated regions, producing patterned porous skeletons with high geometric fidelity, such as the maple-leaf-shaped porous structure shown in Fig. 1e. SEM imaging revealed a highly roughened, interconnected porous network, while EDX elemental mapping confirmed the uniform distribution of bromine, in agreement with the successful incorporation of BrMA (Fig. 1f). The printed polymer pattern exhibited a uniform resolution of ~160 μm, closely matching the 80 μm DMD pixel size, thereby confirming the high fidelity of the maskless photolithography approach (Fig. 1g).

HEMA, acting as a hydrophilic comonomer54, was incorporated as the second monomer to counterbalance the hydrophobicity of BrMA and to modulate the mechanical properties of the resulting porous polymer skeleton. In a representative composition (HEMA:BrMA:EDMA:cyclohexanol:1-decanol = 4:2:4:9:6), the resulting films exhibited an initial water contact angle (WCA) of 28° ± 3°, sufficiently hydrophilic to enable aqueous processing. The surface reactivity of alkyl bromides was assessed via surface-initiated ATRP of 2-(perfluorooctyl)ethyl acrylate (PFOEA) in dimethylformamide (DMF) using a Cu(I)Br/bipyridine catalyst. BrMA-containing films became superhydrophobic (WCA > 150°), whereas BrMA-free controls remained hydrophilic (Fig. 1h), demonstrating that this dual-functional monomer strategy enables straightforward fabrication of porous skeletons with tunable properties, such as wettability (Fig. 1i). All fabricated porous films appeared opaque in the dry state due to light scattering from air-filled pores, but turned transparent upon wetting (Supplementary Fig. 1). Importantly, copolymerization with HEMA also modulated the mechanical properties of the porous structures (Supplementary Fig. 2), as evidenced by their performance in the adhesive-tape-peeling test, a critical factor for subsequent film-transfer steps. In this way, the single-step printing strategy produced a high-resolution porous BrMA–HEMA–EDMA skeleton, with the HEMA:BrMA = 4:2 formulation selected for further studies based on ATRP reactivity, hydrophilicity, and mechanical integrity.

Chemical grafting on polymer skeletons

Alkyl bromide-functionalized BrMA-HEMA-EDMA polymer skeleton enabled versatile ATRP configurations, allowing SI-ATRP to proceed either in DMF with CuBr or in aqueous conditions with the water-soluble ionic catalyst [Cu(I)ME6TREN]+ (Fig. 2a). The versatility of ATRP enables grafting a broad range of functional monomers, including hydrophobic/hydrophilic (meth)acrylates, reactive monomers, and selected luminescent molecules, to be grafted onto the porous polymer skeleton, endowing the surfaces with tailored functionalities. As an example, hydrophilic acrylic acid N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS-acrylate) was grafted onto the surface using either CuBr or [Cu(I)ME6TREN]+ complex, with characteristic NHS peaks in the IR spectrum confirming successful modification. Subsequent amine-substitution with fluorescent Cy5-NH2 produced a uniform fluorescence gradient across the surface (Fig. 2b), reflecting a direct correlation between surface NHS density and ATRP reaction time in both organic and aqueous solvents.

Considering that solution-based ATRP reactions lack spatial control, photo-initiated ATRP provides distinct advantages, including rapid reaction kinetics and precise control over grafting density and localization55. Upon irradiation at 405 nm, the photocatalyst tris(2-phenylpyridine)iridium(III) (Ir(ppy)3) undergoes photoexcitation, generating reactive species capable of activating surface-bound alkyl bromides to produce radicals, thereby initiating ATRP. This method accelerated polymerization dramatically: skeletons grafted with PFOEA reached superhydrophobicity within only 60 s (Fig. 2c, Supplementary Fig. 3). XPS spectra confirmed an increase in fluorine signal with extended irradiation, consistent with progressive PFOEA grafting (Fig. 2d, Supplementary Fig. 4). The operation could be conducted under spatiotemporal control, wherein a DLP system functioned as a programmable and patterned light source, thereby enabling the fabrication of precise wettability patterns within 30 s of exposure, exemplified by hydrophilic–superhydrophobic leaf-vein structures (Fig. 2e).

The success of the above reaction enabled the introduction of luminescent molecules capable of light emission upon excitation. Analogous to the light-induced SI-ATRP of NHS-acrylate, maleimide groups were installed via a furan-protected acrylate precursor to prevent premature polymerization, thereby allowing site-selective functionalization with amine- and thiol-bearing fluorophores (Cy5-NH₂ and Rhodamine-SH) and producing multi-channel fluorescent patterns (Fig. 2f, Supplementary Figs. 5–8). To streamline the workflow, a straightforward single-step approach was further demonstrated: a Cy5 molecule bearing an acrylate terminal group was directly photografted onto the printed porous skeleton using the Ir(ppy)3 catalyst. As shown in Fig. 2g, the complete process, including skeleton printing and luminescent molecule grafting, can be accomplished within 7 min (Supplementary Fig. 9), satisfying the time constraints of practical production. Consistently producing patterned porous polymer skeletons with high uniformity across different operators and fabrication batches (Supplementary Fig. 10) demonstrates the strong reproducibility of this technique.

Robust Luminescence with Amplified Intensity on Porous Skeletons

In addition to fluorescence from grafted Cy5-NH2 and Rhodamine-SH, green phosphorescence was observed under the FITC channel, originating from residual Ir(ppy)3 bound within exposed regions (Supplementary Fig. 11). Given that the Ir content on the polymer backbone is below the instrumental detection limit (Supplementary Fig. 12), we hypothesize that this phenomenon arises from interactions between the highly reactive excited state of Ir(ppy)356,57 and the polymer backbone, leading to its strong immobilization on the surface. Specifically, the complex appears to be stably retained within the porous skeleton and resists detachment even after extensive solvent washing or soaking (Supplementary Figs. 13 and 14). By exploiting the spatial adjustability of the light projector, porous skeletons with multi-channel fluorescence were fabricated through controlled placement of monomers. Pixel arrays exhibiting alternating FITC, Cy3, and Cy5 emissions were produced (Fig. 3a), where hydroxyethyl acrylate (HEA) domains yielded green fluorescence, maleimide domains supported Rhodamine, and NHS domains anchored Cy5. This maskless approach enables simple, rapid, and high-resolution patterning down to single-pixel scales (80 μm, Supplementary Fig. 15). More complex designs were achieved by combining gradient exposure and sequential SI-ATRP. Stepwise illumination produced ladder-like gradients, while repeated grafting yielded intersecting stripes with orthogonal fluorophores, confirming the retained “living” reactivity of alkyl bromides for repeated grafting (Fig. 3b, Supplementary Figs. 16 and 17). By combining gradient and sequential processing, multi-channel fluorescent skeletons with complex motifs such as maple-leaf shapes and gradient emission distributions were realized (Fig. 3c, d, Supplementary Fig. 18), demonstrating rapid prototyping of CAD designs into fluorescent patterns.

Our primary motivation for covalently grafting fluorophores onto porous skeletons lies in the superior uniformity, brightness, and solvent resistance of the resulting patterns. To illustrate this, we compared chemical grafting with physical dye deposition, a conventional approach used in inkjet printing, on porous films and glass substrates. In a petal-shaped design with a superhydrophobic border, Cy5-NH2 was introduced into the hydrophilic region and either covalently grafted onto NHS-functionalized skeleton (Sample 1) or physically deposited by solvent evaporation onto porous BrMA-HEMA-EDMA (Sample 2) or bare glass (Sample 3) (Fig. 3e). Fluorescence imaging revealed that only the covalently grafted films displayed strong, uniform emission (Fig. 3f).

These results highlight two key advantages of chemical grafting: elimination of the coffee-ring effect during solvent evaporation and the robust anchoring of fluorophores throughout the full thickness of the porous skeleton. Compared with planar molecular stacking, i.e. printing luminescent ink on a flat surface, immobilization of molecules onto a micrometer-thick porous skeleton markedly amplifies luminescence intensity. Fluorophores grafted as molecular brushes onto the porous skeleton exhibited ~15× higher emission than identical brushes on a non-porous surface (Supplementary Fig. 19). For the same dye loading, the porous skeleton generates ~5× stronger luminescence than the dye incorporated in a non-porous, transparent polymer film (Supplementary Fig. 20). This enhancement can be primarily attributed to three factors: the synergistic interplay of increased accessible grafting density, interfacial confinement, and suppression of aggregation-induced quenching inherent to the porous polymer architecture. Compared with bulk polymers, the porous skeleton offers a high density of accessible emission sites distributed throughout its three-dimensional structure, resulting in substantially enhanced radiative output relative to planar systems. At air/polymer interfaces, restricted molecular motion and reduced vibrational relaxation pathways suppress non-radiative decay, thereby increasing emission efficiency, whereas solvent infiltration promotes dynamic quenching through enhanced molecular collisions and energy dissipation. In addition, the spatial confinement imposed by the porous network disrupts the π–π stacking and H-aggregation of fluorophores, thereby suppressing H-aggregation-induced excitonic splitting that would otherwise lead to fluorescence quenching58,59. These effects establish a rigid, de-aggregated environment that favors efficient radiative emission, making the porous skeleton an effective platform for the covalent grafting of luminescent molecules and for achieving uniform luminescence amplification.

The stability of fluorescent patterns was further evaluated by solvent rinsing. While chemically grafted films maintained bright and uniform emission after treatment with water or organic solvents, physically deposited patterns degraded significantly (Fig. 3g, Supplementary Figs. 21–23). This contrast, while expected, underscores the practical importance of solvent-resistant, bright, and uniformly distributed luminescence for applications such as anti-counterfeiting tags.

FRET-enabled luminescence

As noted above, in regions where Ir(ppy)3 participates in photopolymerization, its strong spin–orbit coupling facilitates singlet–triplet mixing, enabling efficient room-temperature phosphorescence from Ir(ppy)3 embedded within the polymer skeleton56. Substantial overlap integral between the broad emission of Ir(ppy)3 and the absorption of co-grafted Cy5, together with the close spatial proximity of the grafted molecules, indicates favorable conditions for FRET from excited Ir(ppy)3 (donor) to the longer-wavelength Cy5 acceptors (Fig. 4a). To evaluate the FRET effect on the porous polymer skeletons, we implemented a two-step SI-ATRP modification strategy: Ir(ppy)3-photocatalyzed polymerization of HEA, followed by CuBr-catalyzed polymerization of NHS-acrylate (Fig. 4b). Skeletons produced under uniform Ir-based polymerization exhibited consistent luminescence in FITC channel (Fig. 4c), while increasing the NHS-acrylate polymerization time (Samples 1–4) led to progressively stronger fluorescence in the Cy5 channel after Cy5-NH2 substitution (Fig. 4d).

Despite an identical Ir(ppy)3 content within the porous skeleton, photoluminescence spectra under 365 nm excitation showed a progressive decrease in the Ir(ppy)3 emission (λmax = 515 nm) and a concomitant increase in the Cy5 emission (λmax = 670 nm) (Fig. 4e). These results confirm energy transfer from Ir(ppy)3 triplet excitons to Cy5 molecules, with FRET efficiency tunable via the acceptor polymer chain length. As FRET is a non-radiative energy transfer process mediated by dipole–dipole coupling between donor and acceptor, the presence of the acceptor shortens the donor lifetime, yielding a measured FRET efficiency of 7.76% and an energy transfer rate of 4.6×10−3 ms−1 (Supplementary Fig. 24). It is this weak FRET efficiency that facilitates the concealment of the encrypted fluorescent patterns, effectively masking the genuine pattern from being revealed under conventional irradiation.

Typically, optical devices require multiple excitation wavelengths to activate their respective fluorescent components, owing to the small Stokes shifts characteristic of most grafted fluorophores60,61. In contrast to the barely observable FRET effect on a non-porous surface, luminescence amplification within the porous skeleton enables efficient FRET excitation using a blacklight or even a blue-light flashlight (Supplementary Fig. 25). The large emission gap between Ir(ppy)3 and Cy5 allows a commercial 650 nm long-pass optical filter to effectively suppress shorter-wavelength emissions, visually isolating the Cy5 signal. The influence of donor-acceptor separation on FRET was next investigated using this approach. Square arrays containing identical amounts of Ir(ppy)3 were fabricated via photocatalyzed HEA polymerization, followed by CuBr-catalyzed styrene polymerization to form an inert, transparent polystyrene interlayer, whose thickness—and therefore the donor–acceptor distance—was tuned by varying the polymerization time (Fig. 4f). Although polystyrene interlayers had little influence on the ability of Ir(ppy)3 emission (Supplementary Fig. 26), the same amount of Cy5 immobilized on the film showed progressively weaker fluorescence under 360 nm excitation as the spacer thickened (Fig. 4g). The application of the 650 nm optical filter further confirmed that red emission from Ir(ppy)3-to-Cy5 energy transfer decreases with increasing spacer thickness (Fig. 4h), in agreement with the well-established distance dependence of FRET62. The successful demonstration of robust FRET performance on our porous skeletons led us to turn to FRET-enabled convenient readout for optical encryption and decoding in anti-counterfeiting applications.

Fracture-engineered Polymer Film Transfer as Anti-Counterfeiting Tags

In principle, spatially defined donor–acceptor arrangements enable information to be concealed in one channel (for example, Ir(ppy)3 green emission) and selectively revealed in another (for example, Cy5 red emission) under appropriate excitation or optical filtering. In this way, porous skeletons grafted with multiple luminescent molecules enable anti-counterfeiting tags in which the spatial distribution of luminescence signals encodes unique optical information (Supplementary Fig. 27).

To create portable anti-counterfeiting tags, we developed a fracture-engineered film-transfer strategy that replicates luminescent patterns printed on rigid substrates, which serve as masters, onto adhesive tapes (Fig. 5a). When a commercial pressure-sensitive adhesive tape is applied to the printed master and peeled away, the porous skeleton is subjected to three competing forces: interfacial adhesion between the tape and the porous polymer skeleton, cohesive forces within the skeleton, and adhesion between the skeleton and the underlying substrate. By tuning the prepolymer formulation, fracture is directed to the weakest inter-structural regions, typically the contacts between stacked polymer globules, thereby enabling controlled interlayer separation (Supplementary Figs. 28 and 29). Through this simple press-and-peel operation, a superficial thin layer is transferred from the master to the tape while the bulk porous skeleton remains intact (Fig. 5b, Supplementary Movie 1). The process exhibits high repeatability, as a 50 µm-thick maple-leaf-shaped master withstands at least 40 peel cycles, while a 15 µm-thick porous polymer transfer template enables 20 stable transfer cycles, yielding transferred films approximately 0.7 µm thick (Supplementary Fig. 30). Except for the first transfer, which was macroscopically unsuitable for anti-counterfeiting due to the dense superficial layer of the master, uniform loading of luminescent molecules throughout the porous master ensures that each transferred film retains the original optical characteristics with consistent intensity at the macro level (Fig. 5c, d), yielding a transfer success rate of approximately 95% (Supplementary Fig. 31).

Hidden information in the fabricated tags can be selectively decoded via FRET. For example, under fluorescence microscopy, a flower-shaped master pattern prepared via Ir(ppy)3-catalyzed grafting of HEA and Cy5-NH2 onto the leaves and petals, respectively, exhibited green fluorescence across the entire flower in the FITC channel, while red fluorescence appeared exclusively on the petals in the Cy5 channel, confirming the intended spatial design (Fig. 5e, Supplementary Fig. 32). Notably, when illuminated with a simple blacklight, strong green emission was visible across the printed motif on both the master and the transferred films, whereas application of a 650 nm optical long-pass filter selectively revealed the hidden red fluorescence localized to the petals only (Fig. 5f, g). Likewise, a numerical array exhibited the pattern “8888” in green under flashlight illumination, while the underlying Cy5 pattern “1896” was revealed upon viewing through 650 nm filter (Fig. 5h). Despite the simplicity of the blacklight-plus-filter setup, the weak-efficiency FRET within the porous skeleton yields results comparable to a professional multi-channel fluorescence microscope. This effect critically depends on Ir complexation with the skeleton; in its absence, Cy5 cannot be excited, and anti-counterfeiting fails (Supplementary Fig. 33).

This transferable luminescent anti-counterfeiting film offers several key advantages. The adhesive-back tape transfer preserves the front pattern, enabling lossless incorporation of multiple encrypted features on both sides of the tag (Supplementary Fig. 34). The porous architecture further allows the film to become optically invisible when infiltrated with liquid glue, providing concealment under ambient lighting (Fig. 5i, Supplementary Fig. 35). Upon blacklight excitation, the adhered films, whether visible or invisible under natural light, emit strong luminescence (Fig. 5j, Supplementary Fig. 36). The ease of transfer from the master, mechanical flexibility of the encoded films, and the ability to decode hidden information using a low-cost blacklight-plus-filter reader (costing less than US$ 10) make this approach well suited for producing customized, tamper-resistant anti-counterfeiting stamps. As a demonstration, word marks in the shape of a customer’s handwritten signature were transferred from a printed porous master, and the encrypted digital information embedded within the underline was revealed using the blacklight-plus-filter identifier (Fig. 5k–n, Supplementary Fig. 37). Successful rapid full-surface film transfer from A5-sized printed masters demonstrates the mass-production capability of this strategy (Supplementary Fig. 38), while printing porous masters on flexible substrates and transferring tags via adhesive tape highlights its potential for roll-to-roll continuous production (Fig. 5o, Supplementary Fig. 39).

Luminescent anti-counterfeiting tags with intrinsic PUF security

PUFs are considered the most promising approach to address the global issue of counterfeiting. Remarkably, our porous film transfer inherently generates stochastic microscopic morphologies, simultaneously enabling luminescent anti-counterfeiting and robust PUF encryption. In a representative cloud-based security tag authentication framework, the manufacturer first captures fluorescence images of each PUF tag using a high-resolution microscope (Fig. 6a). These images are preprocessed by adjusting grayscale distributions to generate two-dimensional datasets, which are then used to record the unique features of each PUF pattern. The resulting PUF tags are incorporated into confidential documents or anti-counterfeiting products and distributed to end users. For authentication, users can directly capture the tag’s fluorescence pattern with a smartphone camera, eliminating the need for specialized imaging equipment. The acquired image is uploaded to a cloud server, where an algorithm identifies the most similar reference image in the database. If the similarity exceeds a set threshold, the tag is verified as authentic, and its product category is returned for traceability; otherwise, verification fails, prompting the user to recapture the image for improved accuracy.

Scale bar: 100μm. d Encryption of microscale fluorescent intensity to generate PUF characteristics in the anti-counterfeiting tags. Scale bar: 2 mm. e Comparison of PUF characteristics under bright-field and fluorescence imaging. Scale bar: 2 mm. f Bit uniformity and inter-chip Hamming distance quantified for distinct transferred tags under both fluorescence (FL) and bright-field (BF) conditions (n = 9 tags). g Bit uniformity and histograms of intra- and inter-Hamming distances (HD) calculated from PUF keys extracted from identical regions across 100 fluorescent tags. h Heatmap of Pearson correlation coefficients among PUF keys extracted from identical regions across 20 fluorescent tags. i Heatmap of Pearson correlation coefficients showing similarity between first-order unclonable keys derived from smartphone- and microscope-captured images of a single region. j Heatmap of Pearson correlation coefficients showing similarity between second-order unclonable keys generated from coupled dual-feature regions.

A morphological comparison between the porous polymer master and the transferred tag revealed that the porous skeleton exhibited identical square pixel arrays, originating from the square pixels of the DMD component used during DLP light curing (Fig. 6b). During tape transfer, fracture locations are guided by the DMD pixel geometry (Fig. 6c, Supplementary Fig. 40), while the detailed morphology and spatial distribution of the fractures remain governed by the intrinsic structural randomness of the porous skeleton. In other words, fracture along the 80 µm edge increases the stochasticity of the fracture sites and reduces the characteristic length scale of the resulting irregular patterns, leading to localized non-uniformity and imparting a distinct, non-clonable microstructure to the tags. Meanwhile, the 80-μm pixel size ensures that the encoded microfeatures remain within the resolution limit of a standard smartphone camera. The raw fluorescence images are subsequently converted into binary bitmaps using either commercial software or custom-developed software (see the source code in the Code Availability section) for batch processing, enabling cloud storage and comparison (Fig. 6d).

The encoding capacity of PUF anti-counterfeiting tags is closely related to their security level. To balance encoding capacity and recognition reliability, each pixel unit was divided into 36 binarization sub-units, and a 16 × 16 pixel region was defined as one detection unit. Accordingly, a theoretical encoding capacity of 29216 can be obtained within a 1280 × 1280 μm² detection area. Although the capacity is moderate relative to existing reports (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2), this strategy significantly reduces fabrication time (average fabrication time <10 min) and cost (average cost <US$ 0.003, Supplementary Table 3) while preserving security performance. Most importantly, tag information can be directly captured using a smartphone and authenticated against a cloud-based database, as demonstrated later.

Comparative microscopic analysis of identical regions on transferred anti-counterfeiting tags under bright-field (BF) and fluorescence imaging (FL) reveals a markedly denser microstructure in the fluorescence channel. This arises from the stochastic fracture of the porous polymer master during transfer, which produces an uneven distribution of polymer on the tape and subtle local variations in material content that are largely invisible under bright-field illumination. The incorporation of fluorescent molecules converts these differences in polymer content into corresponding variations in fluorescence intensity, thereby substantially amplifying the spatial heterogeneity of the microstructure (Fig. 6e, Supplementary Fig. 41). Hamming distances (HD) calculated from fluorescent features in the fluorescence channel and intrinsic skeleton features under white-light illumination were 0.4475 and 0.33915, respectively, with noticeable differences in bit uniformity (BF: 26:74; FL: 50:50) (Fig. 6f). These observations demonstrate that the addition of luminescent molecules enhances the robustness and distinguishability of PUF features. When the same processing workflow was applied to non-PUF control samples, such as laser-printed patterns on commercial cellulose paper, the workflow did not impart PUF-like statistical characteristics to arbitrary samples, despite including parameter optimization. This was evidenced by the failure to achieve near-50:50 bit uniformity after identical processing (Supplementary Figs. 42 and 43). This contrast indicates that the processing workflow primarily serves to normalize acquisition-induced signal variations and extract structural features from fluorescence images, while the resulting bit statistics are governed mainly by the intrinsic microstructure of the samples. Thus, the entropy encoded in the binary keys originates from the structural randomness generated by stochastic transfer of the porous skeleton, rather than from the algorithm.

PUF Acquisition Feature Region 1 from 100 transferred anti-counterfeiting tags was analyzed, and key statistical metrics—including bit uniformity, inter- and intra-Hamming distances, and correlation coefficients—were determined, revealing that no two patterns were identical (Supplementary Figs. 44 and 45). Detection regions from identical positions across these tags were binarized via software, producing a bit uniformity close to 0.5. The corresponding inter- and intra-Hamming distances were 0.48534 ± 0.03917 and 0.05538 ± 0.01879, respectively, confirming both the uniqueness and reproducibility of the keys (Fig. 6g). These results demonstrate the intrinsic randomness of the transferred PUF tags. To further assess reproducibility and uniqueness, Pearson correlation analysis was performed across keys from 20 PUFs. The resulting correlation coefficients, visualized as a heatmap in Fig. 6h, exhibited inter-correlation values of 0.1403 ± 0.1102, while the intra-correlation values remained close to 1, demonstrating the reproducibility and uniqueness of the PUF keys.

Each security tag consists of hundreds of micro-units, generating substantial variations in fluorescence patterns between tags and rendering forgery or decryption extremely difficult. For PUF authentication, images of transferred anti-counterfeiting tags were captured using a smartphone under blacklight illumination, with zoomed-in views revealing local intensity fluctuations. To assess the stability of keys derived from smartphone images, the same fluorescent tag was imaged under a range of practical conditions, including different smartphone models, capture angles, and lighting environments (Supplementary Figs. 46–48). Consistency of the resulting keys was quantified by calculating the Hamming distance, indicating that fixing the procedure for smartphone-based acquisition of feature-region images improves acquisition stability (Supplementary Fig. 49).

We further refined the authentication workflow by implementing a cross-platform comparison between PUF features extracted from smartphone images and reference features stored in a cloud database acquired via microscope, closely mimicking practical, real-world scenarios. Identical microstructural regions from 20 distinct PUF tags were imaged using both platforms, and region-encoded PUF keys were extracted and compared through software-based contrastive decoding. On this basis, we evaluated the false acceptance rate (FAR) and false rejection rate (FRR) as functions of the decision threshold based on the intra- and inter-Hamming distance. For both microscope-based and smartphone-based imaging, Hamming distance threshold windows were identified in which no false acceptance or false rejection events were observed. As zero observed errors in a finite dataset do not necessarily imply zero underlying FAR or FRR values, we estimated the corresponding 95% upper confidence bounds using the Rule of Three. For cross-platform authentication, when the similarity threshold was set between 63% and 70%, no false acceptance events were observed across 380 independent impostor trials, corresponding to FAR < 0.79%, while no false rejection events were observed across 20 genuine trials (Supplementary Fig. 50). These results indicate that, within the current experimental scale, the anti-counterfeiting tags exhibit a low risk of false acceptance and false rejection. Considering authentication security during cross-platform image acquisition, a 70% similarity threshold was selected.

In most cases, comparison of keys from a single region produced a clear one-to-one correspondence between smartphone-derived keys and cloud-stored references, completing the authentication process (Fig. 6i, Supplementary Fig. 51). Smartphone-captured keys showed high similarity exclusively with their matching cloud entries, confirming reliable cross-platform matching. For the few cases showing relatively high similarity with multiple candidates in a single region, verification using a second, independent region was performed. Authentication was accepted only when high similarity was consistently observed across both regions (Fig. 6j). This strategy effectively eliminates false matches, ensuring a unique and unambiguous correspondence between smartphone-captured patterns and stored references, thereby guaranteeing reliable PUF authentication for anti-counterfeiting applications. Considering the potential vulnerability of PUF tags to machine-learning attacks and the associated risk to cloud-based databases, we trained a neural network on a subset of genuine keys to simulate adversarial inference. The results indicate that the system shows resistance to a vanilla autoencoder reconstruction attack, while its resistance to stronger generative, stolen-reference or physical-replication attacks remains to be further evaluated (Supplementary Figs. 52 and 53).

As the random features originate from the fracture-engineered transfer process, which introduces spatial correlations among response bits, the raw binarized keys do not satisfy the assumption of independent and uniformly random bits. Consistent with this, the min-entropy estimated using the NIST SP 800-90B non-IID approach was 0.014163 bits per bit. To mitigate the impact of spatial correlations on the effective encoding capacity of the raw keys, future work could employ post-processing approaches, such as randomness extractors or hash-based compression63–65, to generate shorter, more uniform outputs with higher per-bit min-entropy. Further advances in fabrication and algorithm design may enhance the performance and security of this fracture-derived PUF platform.

Discussion

This study seeks to tackle the global challenge of counterfeiting by developing a dual-mode anti-counterfeiting strategy that couples luminant encryption with PUF characteristics, an approach that remains rarely explored. The method relies on grafting Ir(ppy)3 and various fluorophores onto printed, ATRP-reactive porous polymer skeletons, where the solid porous framework serves as both a reaction platform and a medium to initiate and amplify Förster resonance energy transfer under dry conditions for information encryption and decoding. Using these skeletons as transferable master templates, luminescent anti-counterfeiting tags can be fabricated rapidly through a simple tape-transfer process. A particularly striking observation was that a structural imperfection, uneven mechanical strength at the pixel edges induced by the DMD patterning, became a functional advantage. During tape transfer, these mechanical variations caused each pixel to fracture uniquely, generating microscale thickness fluctuations across the transferred film while simultaneously transferring the encoded luminescent information. Owing to the high luminescence of the porous skeleton and the thinness of the transfer layer, these distinctive microfracture patterns were readily discernible even under low-contrast imaging conditions. Luminescence variations arising from ~80 μm pixel-level features further enabled tag analysis and authentication using a standard smartphone camera, allowing intuitive identification of localized fluorescent fingerprints.

This work highlights how the convergence of photonic design, process serendipity, and mechanical specificity establishes a new paradigm for customizable, CAD-to-product anti-counterfeiting technologies. Although software integration remains under development, the current method already demonstrates a robust proof of concept for dual-channel fluorescent tags featuring intrinsically unclonable microarchitectures generated through a simple transfer process. The resulting film tags exhibit exceptional environmental stability (−80 °C to 100 °C; thermal cycling stability; 0-100% RH), chemical resilience (various organic and aqueous solvents, pH 1-14, for 24 h), and mechanical durability (withstanding 1000 cycles of friction and bending; tolerance to crumpling and peel-and-stick cycling) (Supplementary Figs. 54–59). Prolonged exposure to intense, ambient, or UV light can induce photobleaching of the grafted fluorescent molecules; therefore, light-shielded storage is recommended to extend tag service life. Even when fluorescence intensity is reduced due to light exposure, parameter adjustment during smartphone imaging (Auto Mode) effectively compensates for signal loss, enabling reliable extraction of feature-region fluorescence information and preserving PUF readability (Supplementary Figs.60–63). Combined with low fabrication cost and facile field verification using only a handheld flashlight and smartphone, this approach offers a scalable and practical route toward next-generation anti-counterfeiting materials.

Methods

Chemical synthesis

2-(2-Bromoisobutyryloxy) ethyl methacrylate (BrMA)

2-Hydroxyethyl methacrylate (HEMA, 10 mL, 82.0 mmol), triethylamine (TEA, 13.9 mL, 100 mmol), and anhydrous dichloromethane (DCM, 100 mL) were added to a 250 mL round-bottom flask equipped with a magnetic stir bar under a nitrogen atmosphere. The reaction mixture was cooled in an ice–water bath, after which a solution of 2-bromoisobutyryl bromide (21.8 g, 94.8 mmol) in dry DCM (25 mL) was added dropwise over 1 h. The mixture was then allowed to warm to room temperature and stirred for an additional 16 h. The resulting precipitated salt was removed by filtration, and the filtrate was washed successively with deionized water (2 × 100 mL), saturated aqueous sodium bicarbonate solution (2 × 100 mL), and saturated aqueous sodium chloride solution (2 × 100 mL). The organic layer was dried over anhydrous magnesium sulfate, filtered, and concentrated under reduced pressure to remove DCM. The crude yellow residue was purified by column chromatography on silica gel to afford the product as a clear, pale yellow liquid. 1H NMR (400 MHz, CDCl3) δ 6.11 (dt, J = 1.6, 1.0 Hz, 1H), 5.57 (p, J = 1.6 Hz, 1H), 4.45-4.34 (m, 4H), 1.92 (dd, J = 1.6, 1.0 Hz, 3H), 1.91 (s, 6H). 13C NMR (101 MHz, CDCl3) δ 171.45, 167.03, 135.85, 126.18, 63.52, 61.91, 55.34, 30.69, 30.66, 18.24. HRMS (m/z): [M]+ Calcd. for C10H15BrO4, [M + H] + 279.0226, found 279.0221. The structural characterization of the synthesized compounds by ¹H NMR, ¹³C NMR and mass spectrometry is provided in Supplementary Figs. 64.

Furan-protected maleimide methacrylate (Mal-acrylate)

Maleic anhydride was dissolved in diethyl ether, and furan (5 equiv.) was added under stirring. The reaction mixture was stirred at room temperature for 48 h, after which the resulting white precipitate (compound 1) was collected by filtration, washed with ether, and dried. Compound 1 (1 equiv.) was then dissolved in methanol and cooled in an ice bath. Ethanolamine (1.04 equiv.) was added dropwise, and the reaction mixture was stirred at 85 °C for 4.5 h, followed by stirring at room temperature for an additional 12 h. The resulting white solid (compound 2) was collected by filtration, washed with cold isopropanol, and dried. Compound 2 (1 equiv.) and triethylamine (1.2 equiv.) were dissolved in anhydrous dichloromethane and cooled to 0 °C. Acryloyl chloride (1.05 equiv.) was added dropwise, and the reaction mixture was stirred at 2 °C. After completion, the reaction was diluted with dichloromethane and washed sequentially with deionized water (2 × 100 mL), saturated aqueous sodium bicarbonate solution (2 × 100 mL), and saturated aqueous sodium chloride solution (2 × 100 mL). The combined organic layers were dried over anhydrous magnesium sulfate, filtered, and concentrated under reduced pressure. Prior to solvent removal, 3,5-di-tert-butyl-4-hydroxytoluene was added as a polymerization inhibitor. The crude product was purified by short silica gel column chromatography. 1H NMR (400 MHz, DMSO) δ 6.54 (d, J = 1.0 Hz, 2H), 6.26 (dd, J = 17.1, 1.7 Hz, 1H), 6.06 (dd, J = 17.1, 10.4 Hz, 1H), 5.93 (dd, J = 10.4, 1.7 Hz, 1H), 5.11 (s, 2H), 4.17 (t, J = 5.5 Hz, 2H), 3.66 (t, J = 5.4 Hz, 2H), 2.94 (s, 2H). 13C NMR (101 MHz, DMSO) δ 176.35, 165.14, 136.49, 131.77, 128.01, 80.39, 60.44, 47.14, 36.99. Printing porous polymer skeletons

Hydroxyethyl methacrylate (HEMA, 16 mg), 2-(2-bromoisobutylpropoxy)ethyl methacrylate (BRMA, 8 mg), ethylene glycol dimethacrylate (EDMA, 16 mg), cyclohexanol (36 mg), and n-decanol (24 mg) were mixed in a vial to form the prepolymer solution. Phenyl bis(2,4,6-trimethylbenzoyl)phosphine oxide (0.4 mg) was added as the photoinitiator, together with 4-methoxyphenol (0.2 mg) as the polymerization inhibitor. The prepolymer solution was dispensed onto the maskless lithography screen, and polyimide spacers (12 μm or 50 μm thick) were placed on both sides. A pre-modified glass substrate was then positioned on top, and trapped air bubbles were removed to form a uniform liquid film with a thickness of approximately 15 μm. The predefined printing pattern was sliced using software, imported into the maskless lithography system, and projected onto the printing platform using 405 nm UV light. After 60 s of exposure, the glass substrate was carefully separated from the porous film. Unreacted monomers and porogens were removed by washing with anhydrous ethanol, followed by drying with an air gun, yielding the patterned porous polymer skeleton.

Functional molecules grafting via SI-ATRP reaction

ATRP reaction catalyzed by Cu(I)Br in DMF solution

2-(Perfluorooctyl)ethyl acrylate (PFOEA, 1 mmol), CuBr (8 mg), 2,2′-bipyridine (16 mg), and ascorbic acid (48 mg) were dissolved in N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF, 40 mL) in a 50 mL centrifuge tube. A glass substrate (30 × 25 × 1 mm) bearing the polymer film was fully immersed in the solution with the films facing back-to-back, and the mixture was stirred at room temperature under an inert atmosphere. After completion, the sample was thoroughly rinsed with ethanol and dried with an air gun.

ATRP reaction Catalyzed by [Cu(I)ME6TREN]+ in aqueous solution

N-hydroxysuccinimide acrylate (NHS-acrylate, 1 mmol), copper(I) tetrakis(acetonitrile) tetrafluoroborate (24 mg), tris[2-(dimethylamino)ethyl]amine (36 mg), 2,2′-bipyridine (16 mg), and ascorbic acid (48 mg) were dissolved in deionized water (40 mL) in a 50 mL centrifuge tube. A glass substrate (30 × 25 × 1 mm) bearing the polymer film was fully immersed in the solution with the films arranged back-to-back, and the mixture was stirred at room temperature. After completion, the sample was thoroughly rinsed with ethanol and dried with an air gun.

ATRP reaction Catalyzed by Ir(ppy)3

2-(Perfluorooctyl)ethyl acrylate (1 mmol), Ir(ppy)3 (1 mg), N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP, 200 µL), ascorbic acid (1.2 mg), and N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF, 1 mL) were mixed in a vial to prepare the modification solution. An aliquot (100–200 µL) of the solution was dispensed onto the DLP printer screen, and ATRP-reactive porous polymer skeletons were placed onto the solution to ensure complete immersion. Trapped air bubbles were removed, and the sample was positioned according to the predesigned pattern. The print pattern was sliced using software and imported into the DMD-based printer for exposure. After exposure, the substrate was carefully separated using a scalpel, unreacted monomer was removed by washing with ethanol, and the sample was dried with an air gun.

Fluorescent molecule grafting

N-succinimidyl acrylate (1 mmol), tris(2-phenylpyridine)iridium(III) (Ir(ppy)3, 1 mg), N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP, 200 µL), and ascorbic acid (1.2 mg) were dissolved in DMF (1 mL) to prepare the modification solution. An aliquot (200 µL) of the solution was used to wet the surface of patterned ATRP-reactive porous polymer skeletons. The wetted surface was then inverted and placed onto the DLP printer platform, where it was irradiated with 405 nm light at full exposure power for 120 s. Following irradiation, the film was transferred to a sealed 50 mL centrifuge tube and fully immersed in a DMF solution of Cy5-NH2 (0.05 µg mL⁻¹). The mixture was stirred at room temperature for 24 h. The skeleton was then transferred to a clean centrifuge tube and thoroughly rinsed with ethanol to remove unreacted dye. After drying with an air gun, a well-defined red fluorescent pattern was observed under the Cy5 channel of a fluorescence microscope.

A Cy5 dye bearing an acrylate terminal group was directly photografted onto the printed porous skeleton using Ir(ppy)₃ as the photocatalyst. An aliquot (20 µL) of the Cy5-acrylate solution was used to wet the surface of the porous skeleton, which was then inverted and placed onto the DLP printer platform. The sample was irradiated with 405 nm light at full exposure power for 180 s. Following irradiation, the skeleton was thoroughly rinsed with ethanol to remove unreacted dye and subsequently dried with an air gun.

Preparation of anti-counterfeiting tags

A printed porous polymer skeleton was first functionalized with fluorescent molecules to generate a porous polymer master. A commercial adhesive tag was then applied to the master, and upon physical peeling, a thin layer of the porous polymer was transferred onto the tape. This process produces robust anti-counterfeiting tags that simultaneously exhibit luminescent emission and intrinsic PUF characteristics.

Image binarization and mimicking machine-learning attacks

Images of the feature regions were acquired using a smartphone and a fluorescence microscope, and subsequently imported into a program for automatic generation of binarized keys. To assess resistance against machine-learning attacks, a neural network model was constructed to simulate an extreme scenario in which an adversary, after gaining access to a subset of the database, attempts to infer the remaining keys. From a set of 100 genuine keys, 80 were used for model training while the remaining 20 were withheld as a test set. After learning the generative patterns from the training data, the model produced an additional 1000 synthetic keys, which were then individually compared against the 20 reserved genuine keys. Evaluation based on both structural similarity and Hamming distance confirmed the absence of statistical correlation between the model-generated keys and the authentic ones, thereby verifying that an attacker cannot reconstruct the complete cloud database from a partial data leak.
