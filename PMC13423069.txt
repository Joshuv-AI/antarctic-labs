Growth faltering in Indian preterm infants: a prospective cohort study highlighting modifiable post-discharge feeding practices
Authors: Padmasani Venkat Ramanan, Apoorva Kutar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and objectivesPreterm and extremely low birth weight infants are at high risk of postnatal growth restriction. This study was done to evaluate the growth trajectories during the first year of life among preterm infants ≤34 weeks’ gestation and to identify the modifiable factors associated with undernutrition at 12–13 months corrected age (CA).MethodologyThis prospective longitudinal cohort study was done in a hospital in South India. The participants were infants born at ≤34 weeks’ gestation. They were enrolled at 4–6 months and followed up to 12–13 months CA. The birth details, anthropometric parameters at birth and infant feeding practices were noted initially at 6 months CA and again at 12–13 months CA. The primary outcomes were adverse anthropometric statuses at 12–13 months CA. Multivariable logistic regression identified independent risk factors for adverse outcomes.ResultsAmong 201 preterm infants, mean weight, length and head circumference (HC)-for-age Z-scores declined from birth to 12–13 months CA. At 12–13 months CA, 21.9% were underweight, 32.8% were stunted and 48.3% had HC-for-age Z-score (HCAZ) <−2. In multivariable analyses, lower gestational age and extremely low birth weight were associated with higher odds of underweight. Poor complementary feeding practices as indicated by the infant and young child feeding (IYCF) scores were associated with underweight and wasting. Early initiation of complementary feeding (<6 months CA) was associated with higher odds of stunting and HCAZ<−2.ConclusionsThe prevalence of underweight, stunting and HCAZ<−2 increased steadily from birth to 6 months and to 12–13 months. While lower gestational age and birth weight conferred biological risk, early complementary feeding and poor IYCF practices were significant modifiable determinants of adverse growth outcomes.Keywords: Infant, Growth, Child Health, Breastfeeding

Introduction

About 15 million preterm births are recorded each year worldwide.1 In 2020, around 13.4% of the annual childbirths globally were preterm1 and, in India during 2019–2021, approximately 12% of children were born preterm.2 It is estimated that 23.4% of global preterm births are from India, making it the biggest contributor to preterm births worldwide.3 Preterm and extremely low birth weight (ELBW) infants are at high risk of postnatal growth restriction. Early growth faltering in preterm infants is associated with adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes.4 National infant and young child feeding (IYCF) guidelines exist in India, but they are primarily designed for term infants and are inconsistently adapted for preterm populations.

Objectives

The objective of this study was to evaluate growth trajectories during the first year of life among preterm infants ≤34 weeks’ gestation in India and to identify the biological and modifiable post-discharge risk factors associated with adverse anthropometric outcomes at 12–13 months corrected age (CA).

Materials and methods

This prospective longitudinal cohort study was done in a tertiary care university teaching hospital in South India between October 2021 and February 2023. Babies born in our institution were given information about the study at 4–6 months in the preterm follow-up clinic and invited to participate. Those who were willing were enrolled consecutively by the authors.

Study design and sample size

This study was designed as a prospective longitudinal follow-up of a cohort of preterm infants attending the preterm follow-up clinic with the objective of evaluating their growth trajectory and identifying modifiable post-discharge factors. Given the exploratory nature of this study, we did not calculate the sample size.

All eligible preterm infants who met the inclusion criteria were consecutively enrolled and those who completed the follow-up were included in the analysis

Anthropometry at birth was interpreted using the Intergrowth 21st standard application.5 The socioeconomic status (SES) was categorised according to the modified Kuppuswamy Scale.6 Post-neonatal intensive care unit (post-NICU) discharge infant feeding information was collected from the mother by the interview method using a structured data extraction form.

At 6 months (+2 weeks) CA, a complete physical examination of the infant, focusing on nutritional status, was done and the anthropometric measurements of length, weight and head circumference (HC) were noted using standard techniques (online supplemental annexure I). The age- and sex-specific Z-scores for weight, length and HC for CA were noted using the WHO Anthro Nutritional Survey Analyzer.7 The nutritional status at 6 months and 12–13 months was categorised according to WHO classification as described below.8 9

Underweight: weight for age Z-score <–2 WHO charts.

Underweight: weight for age Z-score <–2 WHO charts.

Stunting: length for age Z-score <–2 SD WHO charts.

Stunting: length for age Z-score <–2 SD WHO charts.

Wasting: weight for length Z-score <–2 SD WHO charts.

Wasting: weight for length Z-score <–2 SD WHO charts.

Microcephaly: HC for age Z-score <–3 SD WHO charts.

Microcephaly: HC for age Z-score <–3 SD WHO charts.

The feeding details were noted through 24-hour dietary recall, and the calorie and protein contents of the diet were calculated. The time of introduction of complementary feeding (CF) was noted, and the quality of complementary foods was evaluated in terms of the minimum dietary diversity (MDD), the minimum meal frequency (MMF) and the minimum acceptable diet (MAD). The WHO-IYCF10 indicator score was calculated (online supplemental annexure II). The details of post-enrolment morbidity requiring hospitalisation were noted from the medical records and discharge summaries at follow-up.

At 12–13 months, the anthropometric measurements were again taken for assessment of the nutritional status using the same standard techniques as described earlier. The age and sex specific Z-scores for CA were calculated and feeding details were noted as already described above. The details of post-enrolment morbidity requiring hospitalisation were noted from the medical records and discharge summary at follow-up.

Routine hospital care

The hospital NICU discharge summary provided advice on feeding, providing warmth, KMC, infection prevention, schedule of follow-up in the preterm follow-up clinic and immunisation. The infants were getting feeding advice as part of routine care in the follow-up clinic and, this being an observational study, the authors only evaluated the quality of feeds according to IYCF indicators.

The primary outcomes were adverse anthropometric statuses at 12–13 months CA—underweight, stunting, wasting and reduced HC-for-age Z-score (HCAZ) <−2 using the WHO Child Growth Standards8 (WHO Anthro Survey Analyzer7).

Secondary outcomes included change in anthropometric Z-scores from birth to 6 months CA and from birth to 12–13 months CA.

The exposures were gestation age, birth weight, age of initiation of CF and quality of CF. The confounders considered were SES and maternal education.

Bias

The potential sources of bias were considered. To minimise measurement bias, anthropometric assessments were performed using standardised techniques by trained personnel. Feeding practices were caregiver-reported and may have been subject to recall bias. Infants lost to follow-up were excluded, which may have introduced survivor bias. Given the limited number of extremely preterm infants, estimates for this subgroup may be imprecise. Multivariable models were constructed using a priori selected variables to reduce overfitting and confounding.

Statistical analysis

The collected data were analysed using IBM SPSS V.25.0. Categorical data were described using frequency and percentages. For continuous variables, mean and SD were used to describe normally distributed data and median and IQR were used to describe data with skewed distribution. To find the significance of differences observed in the bivariate analysis, an independent sample t-test was used. For the comparison of two sample means, Mann-Whitney U test and Wilcoxon signed-rank test were used for independent and dependent samples, respectively. Multivariable logistic regression models were constructed separately for each adverse growth outcome (underweight, stunting, wasting and HCAZ<−2). Variables were selected a priori based on biological plausibility and existing literature. Given the limited number of events in certain subgroups, particularly extremely preterm infants, the number of covariates included in each model was restricted to minimise overfitting and preserve events-per-variable considerations. Estimates for small subgroups were interpreted cautiously, and wide CIs were considered reflective of imprecision rather than magnitude of effect. As several outcomes were common (>10%), ORs are presented as measures of association and not as direct estimates of relative risk. In all the above, the probability value (p value) <0.05 was considered significant.

We used the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline to draft this manuscript, and the STROBE reporting checklist when editing.

Lower gestational age and ELBW were associated with increased odds of underweight at 12–13 months CA. Infants born at <28 weeks had higher adjusted odds of underweight compared with those born at 32–34 weeks; however, CIs were wide, reflecting small numbers in this subgroup. ELBW was associated with higher odds of both underweight and stunting. Early initiation of complementary feeding (<6 months CA) was associated with increased odds of stunting and HCAZ<−2. Poor IYCF score was associated with underweight and showed a strong association with wasting. No statistically significant associations were observed between SES and most growth outcomes after adjustment. Given the observational design and the relatively high prevalence of some outcomes, these ORs should be interpreted as measures of association rather than estimates of relative risk.

Discussion

India accounts for nearly one quarter of global preterm births and these infants now represent a growing cohort of high-risk survivors requiring longitudinal care, which is often fragmented and not well integrated into primary health systems.

In this prospective study of infants born at ≤34 weeks’ gestation, we observed a progressive decline in anthropometric Z-scores from birth to 12–13 months CA, with the steepest reduction occurring during the first 6 months CA. By 1 year CA, nearly one-third of infants were stunted and almost half had HCAZs below −2. These findings suggest that despite improvements in neonatal survival, sustained growth in the first year of life remains suboptimal in this high-risk population. Biological vulnerability at birth was strongly associated with poorer growth outcomes. Extremely preterm and ELBW infants had higher odds of underweight and stunting at 1 year CA; these findings are consistent with global literature demonstrating that biological vulnerability at birth strongly influences postnatal growth trajectories.4 11 12 However, estimates for the most immature subgroups were imprecise, with wide CIs reflecting small numbers of extremely preterm infants. These findings should therefore be interpreted as signals of association and replication in larger cohorts is warranted.

The inherent biological risk appears to have been compounded by modifiable feeding practices post-discharge. Early initiation of complementary feeding (<6 months CA) and poorer IYCF scores were associated with adverse anthropometric outcomes after adjustment for key covariates. Nearly 60% of infants initiated complementary feeding before 6 months CA. National IYCF recommendations in India, as in many settings, are primarily structured around chronological age.13 14 Preterm infants require counselling based on CA and may experience feeding challenges that complicate adherence to standard guidance.15 16 The most pronounced decline in growth Z-scores occurred during the first 6 months CA, coinciding with the transition from facility-based to community-based care. Similar implementation gaps in post-discharge nutrition support for preterm infants have been described in other low- and middle-income country (LMIC) settings.17 18

Strengths and limitations of this study

This study has several strengths. It provides context-specific evidence from an LMIC setting, where data on post-discharge growth in preterm infants are limited. It employed a prospective design with longitudinal follow-up to 12–13 months CA in a well-defined cohort of ≤34 week preterm infants. Standardised anthropometric techniques were used. Feeding practices were assessed systematically, including WHO IYCF indicators, allowing identification of modifiable determinants. Multivariable regression analyses enabled adjustment for biological and socioeconomic confounders. Importantly, the study moves beyond descriptive reporting to highlight actionable post-discharge modifiable risk factors relevant to health systems in India.

A few limitations must be acknowledged. A formal sample size calculation was not performed, and recruitment was based on consecutive follow-up attendance, which was affected by the COVID-19 pandemic.

The denominator of all eligible preterm births during the study period was not available, limiting assessment of coverage. Some subgroups (eg, extremely preterm infants) were small, resulting in limited precision of estimates.

The study was conducted at a single tertiary centre; the study population was largely urban and from middle socioeconomic strata. This limits generalisability. Growth faltering may be even more pronounced in rural or lower-resource settings where follow-up systems are weaker. Feeding practices were caregiver-reported and may be subject to recall bias; nutrient intake estimation was based on reported intake rather than weighed records. Infants who did not survive or were lost to follow-up were excluded, introducing potential survivor bias. Follow-up was limited to 1 year CA. The implications of the high prevalence of reduced HC were not fully explored. It is possible that reverse causality influenced the observed association between early complementary feeding and adverse growth outcomes, whereby caregivers introduced complementary feeding in response to perceived poor weight gain. However, multivariable adjustment for gestational age and birth weight reduces this concern. Statistical analysis included multivariable models adjusted for key biological and socioeconomic variables; however, limited event numbers in some subgroups may have constrained model stability and precision of estimates.

Conclusion

In this prospective cohort of Indian preterm infants ≤34 weeks’ gestation, growth faltering increased across the first year of life, with the most pronounced decline occurring during the first 6 months CA. While gestational age and ELBW confer biological vulnerability, suboptimal complementary feeding practices emerged as key modifiable determinants of adverse outcomes. Addressing post-discharge nutrition in preterm infants should therefore be prioritised within the national child health agenda.
