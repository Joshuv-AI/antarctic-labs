Optokinetic stimulation modulates vestibular contribution to postural control
Authors: Antonio S Pierre, Jonathan Dion, Clara Orsini, Assan M Cedras, Maxime Maheu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbstractPostural control is a complex ability that requires multisensory integration. For example, some studies demonstrated that vision modulates vestibular contribution but the exact mechanism responsible for this interaction remains poorly understood. Visual–vestibular interactions play a central role in virtual reality‐based vestibular rehabilitation and are thought to contribute to conditions such as persistent postural–perceptual dizziness. Consequently, elucidating the mechanisms underlying these interactions is essential. This study aims to investigate the influence of visual perturbation on the vestibular contribution during postural control. A total of 22 healthy young participants were recruited and divided into two groups: Opto (with optokinetic stimulation) and No Opto (without optokinetic stimulation). Participants were submitted to static postural tasks under different conditions where vestibular contribution was assessed using stochastic vestibular stimulation (SVS). No measurable modulation in vestibular contribution was found in participants without optokinetic stimulation (No Opto). Interestingly, the results demonstrated a transient increase in vestibular contribution during the first exposure to optokinetic stimulation (Opto). However, this effect was rapidly inhibited in such way that the vestibular contribution returned to baseline following repeated exposure, suggesting a rapid modulation of the visual input. The results are discussed in relation to sensory reweighting and reference‐motion frame models. Key points This study aims to investigate the influence of visual perturbation on the vestibular contribution during postural control.The optokinetic stimulation (Opto) group exhibited a marked increase in postural sway and vestibular gain during the first optokinetic exposure, an effect absent in a group without optokinetic stimulation (No Opto).Vestibular–postural coupling remained stable across all stochastic vestibular stimulation (SVS)‐only conditions and in the No Opto group, indicating that SVS alone did not induce measurable adaptation.The transient gain increase followed by rapid normalization supports the reference‐frame motion model and not classical sensory reweighting.Optokinetic‐induced changes were transient, with both gain and postural behaviour returning toward baseline by the fourth exposure, demonstrating rapid visual–vestibular adaptation. Keywords: multisensory integration, reference‐frame motion, sensory conflicts, sensory reweighting, vision

Introduction

Multisensory integration is essential for daily functions as it enables complex abilities such as postural control, spatial navigation and body representation in space (Borel et al., 2008; Cullen & Taube, 2017; Fitzpatrick et al., 1994; Mergner et al., 2003). It has been proposed that the central nervous system (CNS) plays a major role in modulating the contribution of every sensory input supporting the sensory reweighting model for postural control (Peterka, 2018, 2002). This model proposes that a certain importance (weight) is attributed to somatosensory, visual and vestibular afferents based on the variability of the input (Peterka, 2018, 2002). More specifically, it is proposed that when the variability of the sensory signal is increased, the CNS reduces the weight attributed to this sensory input, therefore improving postural control (Lacour et al., 2016; Peterka, 2018).

Galvanic vestibular stimulation (GVS) has been widely used to investigate sensory reweighting mechanisms during postural control. GVS is a non‐invasive technique that modulates vestibular afferent firing by delivering low‐amplitude electrical currents through electrodes placed over the mastoid processes (Kwan et al., 2019). Because GVS provides controlled and repeatable perturbations of vestibular input, it provides a convenient mean to examine how the CNS adjusts vestibular weighting under different sensory contexts. Several studies have shown that postural responses evoked by GVS are modulated, at least in part, by visual information. In particular, Day and Guerraz (2007) demonstrated that GVS‐evoked postural responses decrease as the magnitude of visually simulated self‐motion increases (Day & Guerraz, 2007), and reduced vestibular‐evoked responses have been observed under binocular compared with monocular viewing conditions (Jessop & McFadyen, 2008). Moreover, Lacour et al. (1981) demonstrated that vestibulospinal reflexes are modulated by visual input during slow falls in such ways that the response energy is approximately proportional to the visual scene speed (Lacour et al., 1981). Collectively, these findings indicate that visual input can modulate vestibular contributions to postural control, consistent with sensory reweighting principles.

Beyond posture, converging evidence demonstrates robust interactions between vestibular signals evoked by GVS and visually induced self‐motion perception. Experimental paradigms combining wide‐field optic flow with binaural bipolar GVS have shown that vestibular stimulation can causally alter perceived vection trajectories, enhance vection magnitude and reduce vection onset latency (Lepecq et al., 2006; Weech et al., 2020; Weech & Troje, 2017). These studies indicate that vestibular inputs continue to influence estimates of self‐motion direction and dynamics even when visual cues are strong and dominant. Reviews of human and clinical research further highlight the importance of visual–vestibular interactions in shaping both egomotion perception and postural stability, including when vestibular inputs are artificially manipulated using GVS (Liu & Zeng, 2025). Together, these findings establish GVS as a powerful tool to probe visual–vestibular integration across perceptual and motor domains. However, despite clear evidence that static visual cues can attenuate vestibular‐evoked postural responses, most postural studies have manipulated vision in ways that enhance spatial orientation or sensory reliability without explicitly inducing an illusion of self‐motion. As a result, it remains unclear how vestibular contributions to postural control are modulated when visual information explicitly signals self‐motion, such as during optokinetic stimulation.

Bronstein (2019) proposed a conceptual framework in which vestibular signals are interpreted in the context of visual estimates of self‐motion, with dynamic sensory weighting that depends on whether vision signals environmental stability or movement (Bronstein, 2019). Within this framework, explicitly driving visual self‐motion provides a powerful approach to examine how visual context recalibrates vestibular gain in motor control. Moreover, repeated exposure to visual–vestibular conflict may engage short‐term adaptation processes, such that changes in vestibular‐evoked postural responses over time reflect adaptive recalibration of sensory weights rather than simple response attenuation.

Stochastic vestibular stimulation (SVS) represents an especially well‐suited tool for probing these adaptive visual–vestibular interactions. Unlike sinusoidal GVS, which delivers predictable and non‐ecological vestibular input, SVS introduces broadband noise that more closely reflects the natural variability of vestibular afferent activity (Pavlik et al., 1999). These properties make SVS particularly valuable for mechanistic investigations of how the CNS manages visual–vestibular conflict and dynamically adjusts sensory weighting during self‐motion perception and balance control. Importantly, visual motion paradigms such as optokinetic stimulation are commonly employed in vestibular rehabilitation to reduce symptoms and improve postural stability. A better understanding of how visual self‐motion influences vestibular contributions to posture – and how these interactions adapt with repeated exposure – could therefore inform the optimization of rehabilitation strategies (Law et al., 2024; Nocini et al., 2024).

Accordingly, the primary objective of the present study was to investigate the influence of visually driven motion cues on vestibular contributions to postural control, with a specific focus on short‐term adaptation of vestibular‐evoked responses following repeated exposure. Since variations in vestibular and in visual cues are known to induce changes in the reference frames (Borel et al., 2001; Day & Guerraz, 2007), we hypothesized that vestibular contribution to postural control would be modulated by visual stimulation. Specifically, for the ‘No Opto’ group (no optokinetic stimulation) we expected a progressive reduction in centre of pressure (COP)–GVS coupling gain in the frequency domain across the trials, reflecting sensory reweighting in response to repeated vestibular perturbation (Cenciarini & Peterka, 2006; Peterka, 2018) while frequency coherence was expected to remain stable. In contrast, for the ‘Opto’ group (with optokinetic stimulation), we anticipated a decrease in frequency coherence due to visually induced vection (Bronstein, 1986). Conversely, we expected an increase in vestibular gain due to visual–vestibular conflict, as opposed to the reduction typically observed when visual cues are reliable (Day & Guerraz, 2007; Jessop & McFadyen, 2008).

Methods

Ethical approval

A total of 22 participants (6 male, 16 females) aged between 18 and 60 years old were recruited and equally divided into two groups: (1) with optokinetic visual stimulation (Opto; n = 11, 3 male, 8 female) and (2) without optokinetic visual stimulation (No Opto; n = 11, 3 male, 8 female). Demographic data from both groups are shown in Table 1. The participants were healthy and did not report a history of vertigo or dizziness episodes, history of neurological or orthopaedic disorders, history of head trauma or uncontrolled diabetes. Every participant had normal or corrected‐to‐normal vision and was able to stand upright without assistance. The present study was approved by the ethics committee for research in rehabilitation and physical disabilities (CER RDP) of the CIUSSS Centre‐Sud‐de‐l’Île‐de‐Montréal (2024‐1996) and all procedures were conducted in accordance with the latest revision of the Declaration of Helsinki. Every subject provided written informed consent before participating. All participants gave written informed consent before the experiment.

Protocol

Prior to the experimental measurements, every participant underwent a vestibular screening, which consisted of a video head impulse test (vHIT) for all six semicircular canals to assess high frequency vestibulo‐ocular reflex (VOR) responses. Second, all participants were submitted to a postural control task where they were asked to stand upright on a force platform with joined feet while maintaining their balance for eight trials in the following order of presentation (Fig. 1): Baseline (1) (eyes closed; No SVS); SVS (1) (eyes closed; SVS); SVS+Vis (1) to SVS+Vis (4) (Opto: Optokinetic stimulation and SVS/No Opto: spatially fixed visual cues and SVS); SVS (2) (eyes closed; SVS); Baseline (2) (eyes closed; No SVS). Every trial lasted 90 s. A break was given between each trial in which they were asked to step off the platform and remain standing or to sit if judged necessary.

Postural control task protocol

A, stochastic vestibular stimulation (SVS) was an electrical signal filtered with a fourth‐order Butterworth filter with a lowpass cutoff at 25 Hz generated using the script provided in Forbes et al. (2014) (5 × 7cm carbon electrodes placed on both mastoids; 3 mA). B, for each condition, the force plate (BMS464508, OR6, AMTI, Watertown, MA, USA) recorded the force applied by the participants and generated an analog signal sampled at 1000 Hz by the acquisition card (PCIe 6343, National Instruments, Austin, TX, USA). When vestibular stimulation was applied (SVS (1), SVS+Vis (1) to SVS+Vis (4) and SVS (2)), the acquisition of the force plate and the vestibular stimulation were synchronized. C, optokinetic stimulation was delivered through a virtual reality headset (Virtualis, Montpellier, France) which provided full field visual immersion and consisted of a tunnel rotating clockwise at 30°/s (∼0.1 Hz).

Video head impluse test

During the vHIT (EyeSeeCamTM; Interacoustics, Middelfart, Denmark), every participant was seated at 1.5 m away from a target fixed on the wall. The participants were asked to maintain gaze on the target while the experimenter performed passive and unpredictable head impulses at an amplitude of rotation ranging from 5 to 20° and a velocity ranging from 150 to 250°/s (Halmagyi et al., 2017). For the six semi‐circular canals, vestibulo‐occular gain (VOR gain) was calculated by the EyeSeeCam software. A gain below 0.8 for the horizontal canals and below 0.7 for the vertical canals or the presence of significant corrective saccades in any canal was considered abnormal (Halmagyi et al., 2017; MacDougall et al., 2013).

Stochastic vestibular stimulation

The SVS was an electrical signal filtered with a fourth‐order Butterworth filter with a lowpass cutoff at 25 Hz generated using the script provided in Forbes et al. (2014). The voltage was converted into current with a peak amplitude of 3 mA through a linear isolated stimulator (STIMISOLA, Biopac Inc., Goleta, CA, USA) and applied to the participant's mastoids using two carbon rubber electrodes (5 × 7 cm) coated with a conductive gel (Spectra 360 electrode gel, Parker laboratories, Fairfield NJ, USA) and fixed with an elastic headband. Every participant received the same vestibular stimulation, and the stimulation remained the same throughout the protocol.

Visual stimulation

Every participant was asked to wear a virtual reality headset (Virtualis, Montpellier, France) which provided full field visual immersion optokinetic for Opto group or without optokinetic stimulation for the No Opto group. The optokinetic stimulation (Opto group) consisted of a tunnel rotating clockwise at 30°/s (∼0.1 Hz). In contrast, the spatially fixed visual cues involved full‐field visual immersion using the same tunnel as in the No Opto group but presented in a static configuration (not rotating).

Postural control

For each trial, the force plate (BMS464508, OR6, AMTI, Watertown, MA, USA) recorded the force applied by the participants and generated an analog signal sampled at 1000 Hz by the acquisition card (PCIe 6343, National Instruments). Then, the data were collected and analysed in MATLAB (MathWorks Inc., Natick, MA, USA). When vestibular stimulation was applied (SVS (1), SVS+Vis (1) to SVS+Vis (4) and SVS (2)), the acquisition of the force plate and the vestibular stimulation were synchronized.

Path length was compared using a two‐way repeated‐measures ANOVA with Group (No Opto; Opto) as a between‐subject factor and Time (Baseline (1); SVS (1); SVS+Vis (1); SVS+Vis (4); SVS (2); Baseline (2)) as a within‐subject factor. The assumption of sphericity was assessed using Mauchly's test, and the Greenhouse–Geisser correction was applied when violated. Post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using Student's t‐test with Bonferroni correction where appropriate. Statistics were performed using JASP (version 0.19.3).

For each participant and experimental condition, we quantified linear coupling between the SVS and platform force (ML) using magnitude‐squared coherence and frequency‐domain gain computed from averaged auto‐ and cross‐spectra. Coherence quantified how consistently the postural response correlates with the SVS input, whereas gain quantified the magnitude of the postural response relative to the stimulus amplitude.

To restrict statistical inferences to frequencies exhibiting reliable stimulus–response coupling, a significance threshold was computed based on trial duration and window length. The threshold was defined as Cth=1−α1/N, where α = 0.05 and N corresponds to the number of independent 1 s time windows over the duration of the trial (90 s).

Between‐ and within‐group differences in coherence and gain were assessed using bootstrap difference of means. Participants were resampled with replacement independently within each condition, and mean spectra were computed for each bootstrap iteration (10,000 iterations). Mean differences were calculated at each frequency, and 95% confidence intervals were derived from the resulting bootstrap distributions. A frequency was considered to show significant difference in coherence and gain if both of the following criteria were met: (1) the 95% bootstrap confidence interval of the difference excluded zero, and (2) mean coherence being compared exceeded the theoretical coherence significance threshold (C th). This dual criterion ensured that reported effects reflected reliable stimulus–response coupling and were not driven by frequencies with weak or unstable coherence.

The analysis revealed no significant difference between the groups as for age, height and weight (Table 1). Additionally, similar proportions of male and females were recruited within each group.

Coherence and gain

Frequency domain analyses showed no significant differences in either coherence or gain between SVS (1) and SVS (2) for both groups (No Opto and Opto) (Fig. 6), and no within group differences in No Opto between SVS+Vis (1) and SVS+Vis (4), indicating stable vestibular–postural coupling in the absence of optokinetic stimulus. In contrast, the analyses revealed a significant attenuation of mean gain for Opto in SVS+Vis (4) compared with SVS+Vis (1), indicating that the presence of an optokinetic stimulus modulates vestibular–postural coupling.

Discussion

The present study investigated how repeated visual perturbations influence the vestibular contribution to postural control. The Opto group showed a significant increase in path length during the first optokinetic exposure (SVS+Vis (1)). The size of this increase varied across subjects. In contrast, the No Opto group did not exhibit such change, suggesting a robust effect of the initial visual motion. These results indicate that optokinetic stimulation modulates the postural response to combined SVS and visual stimulation in a subject‐dependent manner, while preserving the relative ordering of individual SVS sensitivity. The absence of a comparable pattern in the No Opto condition suggests that this effect is specific to optokinetic stimulation. In parallel, we observed an increase in gain only at this first optokinetic exposure, whereas in the absence of visual motion (SVS only conditions) gain and coherence remained stable across time and between groups. By the fourth optokinetic exposure (SVS+Vis (4)), between group differences were no longer significant, indicating rapid normalization. Together, these results suggest two implications: (1) repeated SVS alone does not measurably alter vestibular contribution to sway under our conditions; and (2) optokinetic stimulation transiently increases the apparent vestibular contribution, but this effect rapidly adapts.

First, the absence of modulation with SVS in the No Opto group and during SVS‐only blocks in the Opto group (SVS (1), SVS (2)) indicates that passive SVS does not by itself induce measurable changes in vestibular contribution under the present conditions. At first glance, this may appear to contrast with classical reweighting frameworks (Cenciarini & Peterka, 2006; Peterka, 2018, 2002), which predict that increases in sensory variability, such as those induced by SVS (Fitzpatrick & Day, 2004) should lead to a down‐weighting of vestibular input. However, an alternative and complementary interpretation is that such reweighting processes require the presence of sensory conflict or task‐relevant discrepancies between modalities to be engaged. Indeed, a large body of work shows that adaptive changes in vestibulo‐ocular and vestibulospinal gains are primarily driven by mismatches between visual and vestibular inputs, rather than by increased variability in a single modality alone. From this perspective, the absence of modulation in the No Opto condition is consistent with the lack of visuovestibular conflict in our paradigm. In addition, the stability of responses is in line with previous findings suggesting that vestibular‐evoked postural responses are relatively automatic and resistant to repetition (Guerraz & Day, 2005), and that adaptation depends critically on the structure of the vestibular input, with random (non‐coherent) stimulation failing to induce attenuation in contrast to movement‐coupled signals (Héroux et al., 2015). Together, these findings suggest that both the presence of intersensory conflict and the temporal structure of vestibular input are key determinants of whether sensory reweighting or adaptation is engaged.

Second, the increase in path length observed during the first optokinetic exposure may reflect a general amplification of postural responses arising from concurrent visual and vestibular stimulation, as suggested in previous work (Lacour et al., 1997). From this perspective, visual and vestibular inputs contribute jointly to postural control through convergent pathways, including vestibulospinal and reticulospinal circuits, which are known to integrate multisensory signals involved in the control of antigravity muscles. Such convergence may lead to an increase in motor output when both systems are simultaneously engaged, particularly during strong vection‐inducing stimuli. Importantly, however, this interpretation does not necessarily imply a strictly additive or linear interaction, but rather reflects the combined influence of multiple sensory channels on postural output within a broader multisensory control framework. In addition, non‐specific factors such as arousal or attentional engagement triggered by the initial exposure to a salient and dynamic visual stimulus may have contributed to the observed increase in postural sway. Although the present study was not designed to directly assess these factors, their potential contribution should be considered when interpreting the magnitude of the initial response.

From another perspective, the modulation in postural responses observed between the first and the subsequent exposures to optokinetic stimulation may reflect changes in self‐motion perception. Optokinetic stimulation is known to induce vection, and previous studies have shown that vection can be influenced by factors such as stimuli duration (Seno et al., 2018) and expectation (Guerraz et al., 2001). Given that the parameters of the visual stimulus remained the same across the conditions in the present study the rapid modulation of the postural response to optokinetic stimulus could be related to prior expectations as described in the study of Guerraz et al. (2001). However, repeated exposure may also induce sensory habituation (Howarth & Hodder, 2008) or adaptation processes (Bronstein, 1986) that can affect postural responses. Furthermore, the increase in vestibular contribution in response to first exposure to optokinetic stimulation is partly consistent with Bronstein's conceptual model of the visual control of posture, which proposes a cognitive framework for multisensory integration of visual input (Bronstein, 2019). Indeed, Bronstein (2019) reported that the CNS recreates an internal representation of the visual scene by comparing the different sensory cues (Bronstein & Buckwell, 1997; Wolsley et al., 1996). In this framework, visual cues are decoded by the CNS (visual comparator) and compared with vestibular and proprioceptive inputs (general comparator) before generating a postural response. Consequently, when visual cues conflict with inertial cues (vestibulo‐proprioceptive inputs), the CNS reduces the contribution of visual input to the multisensory estimate of body orientation, effectively shifting the balance of sensory integration toward vestibular and proprioceptive signals through sensory reweighting.

Beyond classical reweighting interpretations, our findings can also be understood within the recently proposed reference‐frame motion (RFM) framework for multisensory integration in postural control (Assländer et al., 2025). According to this model, changes in sensory contributions do not arise from adjustments in sensory weights. Instead, they result from a non‐linear correction applied by the CNS to the visual input whenever the visual reference frame itself moves. These authors proposed that when visual scene velocity exceeds a threshold (∼0.28°/s), the CNS estimates the velocity of the moving reference frame and adds this estimate to the visual signal, effectively removing the world motion component and temporarily reducing the influence of raw visual cues on the multisensory estimate of upright. In our study, the optokinetic stimulus (30°/s) far exceeded this threshold, so the first exposure (SVS+Vis (1)) likely activated the RFM correction path. This would make the uncorrected visual signal momentarily unreliable and drive the multisensory estimate to rely more strongly on the space fixed channels (vestibular + proprioceptive), producing the apparent increase in vestibular gain we observed at (SVS+Vis (1)). Importantly, once the CNS has reconstructed the reference‐frame motion, the corrected visual signal becomes stable again, and the sensory estimate returns toward its baseline balance without requiring explicit reweighting. This dynamic aligns with the rapid normalization of vestibular gain observed by SVS+Vis (4). Thus, the pattern of an initial vestibular gain increase during the first optokinetic exposure followed by rapid stabilization across repeated exposures is consistent with the RFM mechanism and provides an alternative explanation to classical linear sensory reweighting for the transient modulation we observed.

Both Bronstein's model and the RFM framework can account for the initial increase in vestibular contribution observed during the first optokinetic exposure. However, Bronstein's comparator‐based model would predict a persistently reduced visual influence and a sustained elevation of vestibular gain as long as the visuo‐vestibular conflict remains (Bronstein, 2019).

An alternative interpretation could be framed within a Bayesian sensory reweighting framework (Berniker & Kording, 2011; Wolpert et al., 1995). Initially, sensory conflict leads to a compensatory increase in vestibular weighting. With repeated exposure, the CNS learns that the visual stimulus is causally unrelated to self‐motion, reducing intersensory conflict (Berniker & Kording, 2011; Mikula et al., 2018; van Beers et al., 2011; Wolpert et al., 1995). As a result, the need for compensatory vestibular up‐weighting diminishes, and vestibular contributions return toward baseline, while the visual input remains functionally ignored for self‐motion estimation.

While this Bayesian model can, in principle, explain the overall pattern, the RFM framework provides a plausible interpretation of the specific dynamics observed in our study. In particular, the RFM model naturally accounts for the transient increase in vestibular gain and its rapid normalization, as observed in the SVS+Vis (4) condition, through a non‐linear correction of the moving visual reference frame that attenuates visually induced self‐motion signals when they are inconsistent with vestibular input, without requiring a persistent change in low‐level sensory reliability estimates or causal priors. Importantly, the present data do not allow a formal dissociation between Bayesian reweighting and RFM frameworks. Visuo‐vestibular interactions are known to involve both linear and non‐linear mechanisms, depending on task conditions, stimulus properties, and inter‐individual differences in sensory strategies (Fetsch et al., 2013; Lacour et al., 1997). From this perspective, these frameworks should be viewed as complementary rather than mutually exclusive.

More broadly, the present findings can be interpreted in the context of multiple mechanisms contributing to visuovestibular interactions in postural control. These include a quasi‐linear integration of visual and vestibular inputs that modulates response gain, as well as non‐linear mechanisms through which conflicting sensory signals may be selectively suppressed or enhanced depending on perceptual context, such as the direction of vection. While both mechanisms are well supported individually, it is possible that these mechanisms operate differently and interact under normal conditions. However, this remains speculative and warrants further investigation using more ecologically valid paradigms.

While this study provides important insights, certain limitations must be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the sample size was relatively small (n = 22). However, the use of bootstrapping procedures limits the potential effects of the sample size and enhances the robustness of our statistical analyses. In addition, previous studies exploring similar mechanisms or using similar experimental paradigms have reported comparable or smaller sample size (Assländer & Peterka, 2014; Guerraz et al., 2001; Héroux et al., 2015; Zaback et al., 2025), suggesting that this sample size is common for the field. Second, only a stimulus with a velocity of 30°/s was used in this experiment, which largely exceed the velocity threshold required to trigger the RFM (Assländer et al., 2025). Using different velocities would have allowed testing the proposed triggering threshold (0.28°/s). Although this was beyond the primary target of this investigation, exploration of this parameter would be of interest in future studies for further comprehension of the RFM. Third, the present study does not address individual differences in visual reliance and anxiety traits (general comparator bias). Measuring these aspects could have shed light on its role in adaptation to visual perturbation (Bronstein, 2019; Staab, 2023) since it is proposed that such traits may modulate the adaptation mechanism to visual perturbation. Future studies could provide more insight by comparing the velocity‐dependent trigger of the RFM framework with the influence of individual traits such as anxiety and visual reliance. Finally, the present study employed stochastic vestibular stimulation (SVS), which differs fundamentally from conventional sinusoidal galvanic vestibular stimulation paradigms commonly used to investigate phase‐dependent visual–vestibular integration. Unlike sinusoidal stimulation, which allows vestibular and visual motion cues to be programmed as temporally congruent or incongruent and is therefore well suited to studying moment‐to‐moment cue fusion, Bayesian integration or causal inference, SVS introduces broadband, temporally unpredictable vestibular noise that is largely uncorrelated with visual motion signals. As such, the present paradigm is less suited to probing trial‐by‐trial multisensory integration strategies. Instead, it is better interpreted as probing short‐term adaptive reweighting mechanisms, whereby vestibular inputs that are persistently unreliable or task‐irrelevant within a visually driven self‐motion context are progressively down‐weighted. Accordingly, the present paradigm is better suited to examining time‐dependent changes in sensory weighting under conditions of unreliable vestibular input than to probing explicit integration of temporally coherent visual and vestibular signals.

Conclusion

The present study aimed to investigate the influence of visual perturbation on vestibular contribution to posture. We have demonstrated that optokinetic stimulation modulates vestibular weight during the first exposure by increasing the vestibular gain, even in the presence of vestibular perturbation. However, its influence is rapidly inhibited after repeated exposure by the CNS. This result contrasts with both the conceptual model of the visual control of posture (Bronstein, 2019) and classic reweighting frameworks (Cenciarini & Peterka, 2006; Peterka, 2018, 2002), which predicted sensory reweighting in response to sensory conflicts or sensory perturbations. On the other hand, the results are in agreement with the reference‐frame motion proposed by Assländer et al. (2025), which rather suggests a correction of the visual input when the visual scene velocity exceeds the triggering threshold, without requiring explicit reweighting (Assländer et al., 2025).
