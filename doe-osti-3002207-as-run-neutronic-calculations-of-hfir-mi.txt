 ABSTRACT
This report provides guidelines for analysts performing radiation transport calculations for mini-fuel ex-
periments irradiated in the High Flux Isotope Reactor (HFIR). Procedures, practical recommendations, and
modeling conventions which are essential to ensuring consistency and high fidelity in as-run simulations
have been compiled. The content is intended to be a portion of the official training material for new staff in
this area, offering both technical references and practical guidance derived from previous experience. This
report is a living document and will be periodically updated to incorporate new developments, method-
ological improvements, and lessons learned from ongoing and future irradiation campaigns.

 1. PROBLEM DESCRIPTION AND DEFINITIONS
The HFIR at Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) can irradiate material samples, often referred to as
targets, with neutron fluxes as high as 4.6 × 1015 n/(cm2 s). HFIR operation cycles of approximately 26
days of full-power operations at 85 MW are followed by an end of cycle (EOC) outage for refueling, main-
tenance activities, and upgrades (High Flux Isotope Reactor (HFIR) User Guide: A Guide to In-Vessel Ir-
radiations and Experiments 2015). The targets can be placed in many different positions within the reactor
and may be irradiated for several cycles before being extracted.
An "as-run" calculation, also called an "as-built/as-irradiated" calculation, is a radiation transport (RT)
simulation in which a HFIR experiment is modeled as closely as possible to the fabrication and irradia-
tion details of the experiment. Although this type of calculation can be applied to any kind of target mate-
rial, it is most commonly associated with mini-fuel experiments—that is, irradiation campaigns involving
millimeter-sized fissile specimens.
The fabrication details include a complete geometric description of the different parts of the experiment
and associated structures, as well as the isotopic or elemental composition of the corresponding materials.
The irradiation details include the specific HFIR cycles during which the experiment was irradiated, the
length of each cycle, the time between cycles, the positioning of the experiment in the reactor, and the con-
trol element (CE) position as a function of time.
The as-run simulations aim to calculate, as accurately as possible, the following quantities as a function
of time: (1) isotopic content of the targets and other components of the experiments, (2) their associated
total activities, (3) heat generation rates (HGRs), (4) fission rates, and (5) burnup (for fissile targets). Some
quantities may only be requested at the end of the irradiation campaign.
The as-run calculations are often performed after an actual HFIR cycle in order to utilize the most updated
irradiation details. For an N-cycle experiment, as-run calculations should be conducted so that simulations
for cycle N-1 are available when cycle N ends. This approach is more efficient for delivering results and
helps detect possible issues related to the computational models, codes, and resources.
The following sections describe how to perform an as-run calculation. This includes the code descriptions,
the preparation of models and inputs, data extraction and how to implement fabrication and irradiation
details into the models, how to run the codes and extract and post-process the outputs, as well as a list of
good practices and common issues.

 2. COMPUTATIONAL CODES
The complexity of the physical problem addressed in an as-run simulation requires the integration of sev-
eral RT tools. At the beginning of each cycle in HFIR, referred to as beginning of cycle (BOC), fresh re-
actor fuel is used. However, the targets could be new (i.e., in the first irradiation cycle) or previously ir-
radiated, awaiting the next irradiation cycle. Throughout the cycle, the fuel undergoes burnup which al-
ters its isotopic composition. The resulting changes in reactivity are compensated with adjustments to the
CE position, which in turn affect the neutron flux distribution in phase space. The targets also experience
depletion, activation, and burnup (for fissile targets), and are influenced by the core dynamics. Notably,
fuel burnup is not independent of the total target loading in the reactor. Increased absorption in the experi-
ments can affect neutron economy and ultimately cycle duration. These factors make the as-run simulation
a highly coupled and time-dependent problem.
To address these factors, the High Flux Isotope Reactor CONtroller (HFIRCON) code was developed at
ORNL. HFIRCON is a collection of Python routines and C plugins that automate the workflow for fuel
and single- or multi-cycle target depletion analyses for HFIR (Pandya et al. 2024).
The user should incorporate the fabrication and irradiation details of the experiment into (a) a Monte Carlo
N-Particle (MCNP) input and (b) a HFIRCON input. The user should also have prior knowledge of MCNP,
particularly in geometry building and general aspects of the Monte Carlo method (e.g., tally uncertainty
and convergence rate).
The MCNP input is prepared by the RT analyst using a HFIR base model. This input incorporates the ex-
periment’s fabrication details and specific irradiation position (previously classified as an irradiation de-
tail). The remaining relevant irradiation details are included in the HFIRCON input.
A simplified workflow performed by the analyst (i.e., the user) is described as follows:
 • Provide an MCNP model of the HFIR reactor containing the specific targets to be irradiated, along
 with a list of cells to be identified by HFIRCON as targets (i.e., all the cells in which a depletion/ac-
 tivation calculation is requested).
 • Specify a list of cells corresponding to the fuel and two MCNP transformation numbers that allow
 HFIRCON to operate on the CE positions.
 • Specify the number of cycles to run, the duration and time elapsed between cycles, and a set of time
 steps within each cycle with the corresponding CE positions.
HFIRCON produces a modified version of the MCNP input to include the proper CE position and a set
of tallies to calculate the volume-averaged flux spectra and reaction rates (RR) in all the target and fuel
cells previously specified by the user. This information is transmitted to the MXS_deplete module to per-
form all depletion calculations via the Oak Ridge Isotope Generation (ORIGEN) application programming
interface. After each depletion calculation, the materials are updated for the next time step and the new
transport responses are calculated. Photon emissions from the activated materials are also included as cor-
responding photon sources to properly account for the photon contribution to the heating tallies, because
a photon produced in a certain cell will not necessarily deposit its energy locally, thereby necessitating the
transport of those photons. The contribution of charged particles to the heating is considered to be local
because the range of charged particles in most materials is negligible compared to the cell size.

 3. INPUT DATA
The requester of the as-run simulation should provide all required details to successfully build the corre-
sponding computational models. The information is typically transferred through an Electronic Laboratory
Notebook (ELN) or via the NEIT server located here:

smb://ornldata.ornl.gov/NEIT/MiniFuel - As-built as-irradiated neutronics

The dataset should include the following:
 • The overall goal of the calculation, required quantities of interest to be calculated, and intended use
 of the results
 • A design overview
 – Provide context regarding sample type (such as for mini-fuel experiments, whether the fuel
 samples are in disk geometry, spherical kernels, etc.)
 – Irradiation position in HFIR
 – Main characteristics of target material (e.g., 3%-enriched UO2 )
 • Detailed technical drawings of the irradiation basket, capsules, and subcapsules
 • Capsule fabrication request sheets
 • Information on all experiment materials (material IRs)
 • Irradiation history
Typical irradiation positions for mini-fuel experiments include the following: RB-1A, RB-5B, VXF-9, and
VXF-11. The baskets used for experiments in the removable beryllium (RB) have five radial positions (de-
noted R), whereas those in the vertical experiment facilities (VXF) have three. Each radial position can
hold three vertically stacked irradiation targets (i.e., sealed tubes containing subcapsules). The axial po-
sitions are denoted A=1, . . . , 3; where A=1 is the bottom capsule, A=2 is the middle, and A=3 is the top.
Within each axial target there are six subcapsules which contain the mini-fuel samples to be irradiated.
The subcapsule positions, from bottom to top, are denoted S=1, . . . , and 6. The combination of the R, A,
and S numbers is often called the "RAS" position of the experiment. Detailed schemes of the irradiation
basket, targets, and subcapsules can be found in Petrie et al. 2019 and Gorton et al. 2024. Figure 1 shows
plots of a typical MCNP model for a mini-fuel experiment, which will be described in detail in the next
section.
Each subcapsule consists of a metallic holder, a heater tube, a passive thermometry monitor made of sil-
icon carbide, insulating disks, and a cup or dish that secures the fuel specimens in place. The gaps are
filled with inert gases such as argon or helium. The geometry of the cup can vary depending on the type
of specimen: it may be flat to accommodate disk-shaped fuels, or it may contain grooves to hold spherical
TRISO particles or individual fuel kernels. An example of these designs is presented by Petrie et al. 2019.
Detailed information for each component is provided in the capsule fabrication request sheets, including
drawings, material IRs, and masses.
In the following sections, we describe how the input data should be implemented in the MCNP and HFIR-
CON models.

 4. MCNP MODEL
To perform an as-run calculation, the analyst should prepare an MCNP model of the HFIR reactor that in-
cludes the specific target experiment. The starting point should be one of the available HFIR base models,
most notably the Cycle-400 Model (Ilas et al. 2015) or the Representative Model (Chandler et al. 2016).
The latter is based on the former and includes a representative loading of HFIR experiments in the flux
trap and in the large and small VXFs. The positions listed previously for mini-fuel irradiation are, in both
models, populated with dummy elements that should be replaced with the actual basket–target–subcapsule
configuration of the experiment.
At the time of this writing, a new Python-based tool is under development and nearing completion. This
tool takes the unchanged MCNP base models and a set of geometric parameters, and returns a modified
input containing the geometry for the selected R and A positions together with empty S placeholder (i.e.,
void) cells to be filled with the subcapsules using the universe approach. The analyst is expected to start
from such a model and incorporate the subcapsule universes, as explained in the next section. In the mean-
time, the basket-target models can be based on existing models for each of the four typical irradiation posi-
tions. Parametric subcapsule geometry generation is also slated for inclusion in this Python tool.

4.1 SUBCAPSULE GEOMETRY CONSTRUCTION IN MCNP
The subcapsules containing each of the target specimens are modeled in MCNP using a universe approach.
Each axial segment of the holder is filled with a different universe (e.g., u=n, u=n+1, u=n+2, etc.) represent-
ing each individual subcapsule. Successive subcapsules are stacked along the z-axis by means of transfor-
mation cards (TRn)1 , which apply the appropriate horizontal and vertical translations. This modular struc-
ture allows identical geometric descriptions to be reused at multiple radial and axial positions without du-
plication of cell definitions.
Within each subcapsule universe, the various components—such as holders, heater tubes, insulators, ther-
mometry tubes, fuel dishes, fuel specimens, and filling gas—are defined as individual cells. These compo-
nents are often generated using the "like ... but" syntax, which replicates the definition of a base cell
while assigning it to a different universe number.
The fuel space inside each subcapsule is defined as a void cell and then filled with another universe repre-
senting the fuel disk and its surrounding filling gas, argon or helium. This is done using the "like (cell)
but fill=(universe)" syntax, which allows each subcapsule to reference a different fuel-disk universe.
In this way, the fuel geometry can vary from one subcapsule to another while maintaining the surrounding
structure. This enables an efficient representation of the different fuel geometries across the subcapsules.
In the following example, the RB-1A basket model includes predefined empty slots which correspond to
the subcapsule positions. Cells 10019-10025 represent the empty-volume subcapsule slots for position
R=1, A=2, S=1, . . . , 6. These cells are filled with universes 121-126 containing the geometric detail of
each subcapsule, as shown in Figure 1. Transformations TR121-TR126 are applied to place the subcapsules
at their correct positions because the subcapsule universes were built centered at the origin with the bottom
of the subcapsule coincident with the horizontal plane.

 5. HFIRCON INPUT
This section describes a typical HFIRCON input used to perform as-built calculations. The input will be
explained in segments that correspond to the consecutive parts of the input file.
HFIRCON utilizes JavaScript Object Notation (JSON) files as input to define simulation parameters and
configuration data. The JSON input files consist of hierarchical key-value pairs, where keys are strings and
values can be of various types including strings, numbers, arrays, and nested objects. When specifying the
input, users should concatenate all key-value pairs separated by commas, and enclose the entire set in curly
braces, {}, at the beginning and end of the file. Proper placement of commas between pairs is essential to
maintaining valid JSON syntax, thereby ensuring input data can be correctly parsed and validated within
HFIRCON. The key-value pairs are described in detail in the following sections.

5.1 MCNP INPUT AND SDEF FILE
The key "MCNP_inp_name" specifies the name of the MCNP input file to be used in the simulation. The key
"sdef_card_name" refers to the file containing the source definition card (SDEF), which defines the initial
neutron source for the simulation. This source is used to sample initial fission neutrons and is typically
applied during the inactive cycles, until a stationary, converged fission source distribution is established.

Example:
    "MCNP_inp_name": "HBU-0107.i",
 "sdef_card_name": "sdef.txt",

The typical content of the "sdef.txt" is the following:

sdef erg=d1 axs=0 0 1 rad=d2 ext=d3
sp1 -3 0.988 2.249
si2 6.5 21
sp2 -21 1
si3 -25.4 25.4

This source generates neutrons according to a fission Watt energy spectrum, uniformly distributed within
a cylindrical shell defined by radii from 6.5 cm to 21 cm and axial positions from −25.4 cm to 25.4 cm.
These positions reside within the fuel region, as required by the SDEF card when used in conjunction with
KCODE mode.
As a standard practice, the "input_location" folder contains the MCNP input file, the SDEF source defi-
nition file, and the JSON file, which serves as the HFIRCON controller.

5.2 JOB DETAILS AND FOLDER PATHS
The "analysis_name" key is a string specifying the name of the simulation or analysis case. The "num_nodes"
key is an integer representing the number of compute nodes requested for the simulation run; in the exam-
ple below, "num_nodes": 1 means the job will run on a single node. The "procs_per_node" entry spec-
ifies how many processor cores (or processes) to use per node. The "mem_per_proc" value is an integer
giving the amount of memory allocated per processor in megabytes (MB). The "hours_per_job" field
indicates the expected or requested wall time for the job in hours; for example, 96 corresponds to a max-
imum run time of 4 days. The "platform" key names the computing platform or cluster where the job

 8

will run; for example, "helios" is the target HPC system. The "working_dir", "input_location", and
"output_location" entries specify the absolute paths to the working directory, the directory containing
input files, and the directory where final output files should be saved, respectively. Two or all of these loca-
tions can coincide. Finally, the "user_name" key gives the string username (i.e., the ORNL 3-character ID)
of the individual submitting the job.

5.3 CYCLE AND CONTROL ELEMENT INFORMATION
For HFIRCON versions greater than 1.1.0, variables starting with muc_ enable the simulation of multiple
HFIR cycles in a sequence. The key muc_cycle_number is a list of N consecutive integers starting from 0,
where N is the number of cycles to be simulated.
The key muc_depletion_step_times is a list of N sublists, each containing T real numbers starting from
0. These values define the time boundaries (in days) used to discretize each cycle into T − 1 time intervals.
These intervals represent the time steps in which steady-state transport calculations are performed. The last
entry in each sublist must match the total length of the corresponding cycle.
HFIRCON also allows the simulation of decay periods between cycles through the muc_initial_decay_time
key, which is a list of N real values (in days). Each value defines the decay time occurring before the start
of the corresponding cycle. A value of 0 indicates no decay period between the previous and current cycle,
which is typically the case for the first irradiation cycle.
EOC decay-only calculations can be independently specified using the eoc_decay_steps key. This is a list
of decay times (in days) at which decay-only calculations should be performed after each cycle, indepen-
dently of the elapsed time between cycles.
Although HFIRCON supports criticality search calculations to determine CE positions that achieve a criti-
cal reactor configuration (keff = 1), this method is highly sensitive to the MCNP model fidelity—including
accurate representation of reactivity worth from experiments, homogenized fuel, control elements, and
other factors. For as-built calculations, the fixed CE input option is typically preferred because it allows
users to provide the actual measured CE positions recorded after each HFIR cycle. The key muc_fixed
_rod_positions is a list of N sublists, each containing T − 1 real numbers representing the CE positions
(in centimeters) used during the corresponding depletion intervals.
HFIRCON requires that the MCNP input file include transformation cards (TRn) for the inner and outer
control elements. These transformations must be referenced using the keys ICE_transform and
OCE_transform, which specify the corresponding transformation numbers used in the MCNP input file.
The key muc_advantg_steps specifies the time steps during which ADVANTG should calculate or update
weight windows for variance reduction. It is defined as a list of N sublists, each containing the time step

 9

indices when ADVANTG will be executed within the respective cycle. It is recommended to set a higher
density of ADVANTG steps at those timesteps where the rate of change in CE position is highest, namely
at the BOC and the EOC, in order to capture changes in the required weight windows due to flux density
variations.

5.4 HOW TO SELECT TIME STEPS AND CONTROL ELEMENT POSITIONS
CE position data are typically provided in spreadsheet format, wherein each column contains CE positions
(in inches) as a function of time. The time interval between entries is generally 6 h, except when a data
point is missing or incorrect.
As previously described, if t1 , t2 , . . . , tT are the T entries in muc_depletion_step_times and p1 , p2 , . . . , pT −1
are the T −1 entries in muc_fixed_rod_positions, then each pi corresponds to the time interval between ti
and ti+1 . Therefore, users should input representative time-averaged CE positions rather than instantaneous
values.
In general, the requester of the as-built calculation should specify a set of fixed times r j for j = 1, 2, . . . , R
at which the desired responses should be reported, but simply setting ti = ri will not necessarily yield a
sufficiently detailed representation of the CE evolution. Conversely, the total calculation time scales with
the number of depletion steps so an overly fine CE position resolution may lead to impractical run times.
The following procedure directs a user how to select the depletion time steps and corresponding CE posi-
tions based on available time-dependent data and desired response times:

 otherwise, set ti = t.

5.5 TARGET SPECIFICATION
To perform an analysis on target cells, the key "execution_mode" is set to "target_depletion", which
enables depletion in explicitly defined targets. The key "target_cell_list" specifies the list of cell IDs
set to undergo depletion or activation. These target cells have their number densities updated stepwise
based on transmutation and decay.
The key "target_output_groups" is a list of lists, wherein each sublist defines one cell’s inclusion in one
or more output summation groups. The first element of each sublist is the cell ID, which must match an
entry in "target_cell_list", and the remaining elements are group names under which the cell’s re-
sponses will be reported. Importantly, each entry in "target_cell_list" must have exactly one corre-
sponding sublist in "target_output_groups".
For example, cell 10142 is reported under the group names "111 Fuel Kernel" and "Fuel Kernels",
whereas cell 10145 is reported under "111 Thermometry" and "Thermometry cells". This grouping al-
lows for convenient aggregation of data in the output files.

Example:
 "execution_mode": "target_depletion",
    "target_cell_list": [10142,10143,10144,10145,10146],
 "target_output_groups": [[10142,"111 Fuel Kernel", "Fuel Kernels"],

 [10145,"111 Thermometry","Thermometry cells"],
                             [10146,"Other"]],

5.7 CROSS SECTION LIBRARIES
When fuel or target cells contain materials that use the MT card (i.e., they rely on thermal cross section li-
braries), a list of the specific thermal libraries used must be provided via the "fuel_mts" and "target_mts"
keys, respectively. These lists contain the names of the thermal scattering libraries applied to each mate-
rial, such as "lwtr.10t" for light water at 293 K.
In addition, the keys "fuel_nlib" and "target_nlib" specify the neutron cross section library extensions
(e.g., 70c or 80c) used for fuel and target materials, and "fuel_plib" and "target_plib" define the corre-
sponding photon interaction library extensions.
Users should avoid photon libraries with the 04p extension because they contain incorrect Compton broad-
ening data. The recommended photon library version is 84p, in accordance with findings documented in a
Los Alamos National Laboratory internal memo (Hendricks, Solomon, and James 2012).

Example:
    "fuel_mts": ["lwtr.10t"],
    "target_mts": [],
    "fuel_nlib": "80c",
    "fuel_plib": "84p",
    "target_nlib": "80c",
    "target_plib": "84p",

5.8 MONTE CARLO CONVERGENCE CONTROL
The relative errors√of Monte Carlo tallies follow the law of large numbers; that is, they are approximately
proportional to 1/ N, where N is the total number of particle histories. This implies that to reduce the
relative error of a given response by half, the number of simulated particles must be quadrupled.
To adjust the total number of neutron histories used to compute neutron responses—such as neutron-induced
reaction rates and the neutron contribution to heating—the user may tune either the "mc_exec_cycles" pa-
rameter or the "min_mc_particles" keyword.
The number of simulated neutron histories is given by:

 NPS = 30 × slots_per_node × num_nodes × mc_exec_cycles

Here, 30 is the fixed number of active cycles. The value of slots_per_node depends on the computing
environment—for example, it is 8 on Helios, 2 on CADES, and 8 on Lise-II. Importantly, increasing the
number of nodes increases the total number of particle histories proportionally, rather than reducing run
time for a fixed particle count.
When "mc_exec_cycles" is set sufficiently low, the "min_mc_particles" keyword provides a convenient
alternative that specifies the desired total number of neutron histories directly. In this case, HFIRCON in-
ternally adjusts the number of cycles to meet the required particle count, thereby minimizing the need for
manual calculation.
Analogously, the parameter "rod_pos_cycles" governs the number of particles used during control rod
search2 . In this case, 50 active cycles are used instead of 30, and no "min_particles" equivalent is avail-
able.
 2. Even in fixed_rod_positions runs, these calculations are required to evaluate keff and determine the converged criti-
cality source.

5.10 OTHER INPUT KEYS
The "mctal_write" keyword determines whether mctal output files are written after each Monte Carlo
run.
The "volume_calc" parameter controls whether HFIRCON performs a stochastic volume calculation us-
ing LAVAMINT. This is enabled by default and is strongly recommended unless all required cell volumes
are already provided in the MCNP input. The optional "volume_tol" variable sets the desired maximum
relative error for these volume calculations. Additionally, the "lavamint_override" flag instructs HFIR-
CON to ignore MCNP’s analytical volumes and exclusively use LAVAMINT’s stochastic values. Enabling
this setting (True) is recommended when depleting cells that are defined within MCNP universes.
The "problem_bounds" key specifies the spatial bounds of the weight window mesh used by ADVANTG,
defined as a list of six values: [xmin, xmax, ymin, ymax, zmin, zmax].
Finally, the "reactor_power" and "fission_energy" keys allow users to set the reactor’s thermal power
(in megawatt) and the recoverable energy per fission (in megaelectron volt), respectively, which are used to
normalize tallies.
For further details, refer to the HFIRCON manual (Pandya et al. 2024).

Example:
 "mctal_write": true,
 "volume_calc": true,
 "lavamint_override": true,
    "volume_tol": 0.005,
    "problem_bounds": [-350.0, 350.0, -350.0, 350.0, -150.0, 150.0],
    "reactor_power": 85.0,
    "fission_energy": 200.7,

 7. OTHER IMPORTANT REMARKS
7.1 EXSITE
All relevant operational information should be extracted from the EXSITE page (https://home.rrd.ornl.
gov/cfpro/exsite/). It should be noted that the information regarding the irradiation position provided in the
ELN should be verified against the actual operation shown in EXSITE for all irradiation cycles.
The cycle length shown on the website (at https://home.rrd.ornl.gov/cfpro/exsite/CycleHistory.cfm) al-
ready accounts for seasonal daylight-saving time adjustments, which can easily be overlooked in manual
calculations. Although the resulting one-hour difference is negligible compared to the total 25-day cycle
length, relying on the cycle length reported by EXSITE is recommended as the reference.

7.2 ABSOLUTE SUBCAPSULE POSITIONS
Positioning is crucial because the primary parameter governing activation and depletion of samples is the
local neutron flux, which is highly sensitive to position. Other factors, such as the presence of surrounding
absorbent materials, overall core depletion, and the CE position, are also significant contributors. There-
fore, the analyst should ensure that the absolute subcapsule position is correctly modeled.
Although the exact reference should be verified for each case, it is noted that the bottom plane of the sub-
capsule space corresponding to A=2, S=3 coincides with the mid-plane of the core in height. In the MCNP
models, this reference mid-plane is defined at z = 0.

7.3 TROUBLESHOOTING
Although HFIRCON generates an output file (hfircon.out) that records the run progress and any errors
encountered, the messages do not always reveal the underlying issue. Tracing the error back to the out-
put files for the specific cycle and time step is a good practice. Each stage should be checked in sequence:
’lavamint’, ’rod search’, ’advantg’, ’mcnp’, ’deplete’, and ’decay gamma’.

7.3.1 Not a Number (NaNs)
If the total heating output for relative error (RE) contains NaNs, it is typically due to one of the heating
components (e.g., the gamma component) having a zero mean value, which in turn leads to a zero relative
error in MCNP. This issue can usually be resolved by increasing the number of particles used in the simu-
lation for that component.
