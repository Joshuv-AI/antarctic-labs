3. Executive Summary:
 The ENGAGE project, led by the Electric Power Research Institute (EPRI), aims to
 enable behind-the-meter (BTM) distributed energy resources (DERs) to provide grid
 services that maximize benefits for both customers and the grid. This initiative
 addresses the technical, economic, and regulatory barriers that currently prevent
 BTM DERs from participating in grid services at scale. By developing innovative
 control architectures and methodologies, the project seeks to enhance grid reliability,
 economic efficiency, and customer satisfaction.
 Goals
 The project aims to advance the integration of behind-the-meter distributed energy
 resources (BTM DERs) into grid services for both bulk power and distribution
 systems. It focuses on developing and validating control architectures and
 optimization methods to ensure technical effectiveness and economic viability.
 Ultimately, the initiative aims to deliver public benefits through improved grid
 reliability, cost reductions, and enhanced customer engagement.
 Major Accomplishments
 The project delivered a comprehensive Grid Service Guide outlining opportunities for
 distribution-connected DERs in both distribution and bulk power systems. A
 hierarchical control architecture leveraging Model Predictive Control (MPC) was
 developed to optimize DER dispatch, ensuring scalability and adaptability. To
 measure effectiveness, detailed performance metrics were established across
 magnitude, timing, and economic dimensions. A robust techno-economic analysis
 using production cost modeling for the New York Independent System Operator
 (NYISO) region demonstrated the economic viability and reliability benefits of DER
 aggregations. Additionally, a Transmission and Distribution co-simulation platform
 was created to assess the impact of DER-provided grid services across transmission
 and distribution networks. Real-world field demonstrations at sites in Fremont, CA,
 and Georgia Tech campus in Atlanta, GA validated the architecture’s practical
 benefits and economic potential. Finally, the project evaluated measurement and
 verification (M&V) methods, proposing feasible solutions to ensure accurate
 performance validation of DER-provided grid services.
 Contributions to Understanding
 The ENGAGE project significantly advances the understanding of how BTM DERs
 can be effectively integrated into grid services. By developing and validating new
 control architectures and optimization methodologies, the project demonstrates the
 technical and economic feasibility of DER-provided grid services. The project's
 findings provide valuable insights into the potential of DERs to enhance grid
 reliability, reduce costs, and improve customer engagement.
 Technical Effectiveness and Economic Feasibility
 The project's hierarchical control architecture, based on MPC, ensures the technical
 effectiveness of DER dispatch by optimizing resource allocation and maintaining grid
 stability. The techno-economic analysis conducted for the NYISO region
 demonstrates that DER aggregations can significantly reduce system operating
 costs and improve reliability. The field demonstrations further validate the economic

feasibility of DER-provided grid services, showing that DERMS solutions are cost-
effective compared to traditional infrastructure upgrades.
Public Benefit
The ENGAGE project offers substantial benefits to the public by enhancing grid
reliability, reducing energy costs, and promoting customer engagement. By enabling
BTM DERs to provide grid services, the project supports the transition to a more
sustainable and resilient energy system. The project's findings and methodologies
can be leveraged by utilities, regulators, and policymakers to develop effective
strategies for integrating DERs into grid operations, ultimately benefiting consumers
and the broader community

5. Background:
 ENGAGE conducted research on DER-provided grid services, including distribution
 services (also called “non-wires alternatives [NWA]”) provided by individual or
 aggregated DERs (also called “virtual power plants [VPP]”). Grid service definitions
 were a key area of research. For bulk system services, where markets are more
 mature, EPRI’s report titled Grid Services in the Distribution and Bulk Power
 Systems: A Guideline for Contemporary and Evolving Service Opportunities for
 Distributed Energy Resources, published in 2021 out of this project, built on ISO
 product definitions. For distribution services, ENGAGE built on precursor work
 conducted in California 1 and Hawaii 2, as well as emerging definitions in Europe
 jurisdictions [1][2]. EPRI’s work on distribution service attributes was itself widely
 shared with distribution utilities as part of the Distribution Services Working Group
 (DSWG), as well as other research stakeholders including the National Labs [03].
 ENGAGE work on grid services has informed subsequent industry research [04]-
 [06].
 ENGAGE developed an MPC-based hierarchical control architecture for aggregating
 behind-the-meter DERs to provide grid services efficiently. The use of MPC for DER
 scheduling is well-studied in academic and industry literature. Prior studies have
 shown how MPC-based DERMS optimizes DER dispatch [7]. Similar research
 explores centralized, decentralized, and hierarchical control architectures for
 DERMS [8], as incorporated in ENGAGE. Utkarsh et al. developed a hierarchical
 control framework to aggregate and manage BTM DERs, utilizing MPC to optimize
 their operation [9]. Hans et al. introduced a hierarchical distributed MPC strategy for
 operating interconnected microgrids, aiming to optimize energy management across
 multiple microgrids [10].
 ENGAGE builds on existing literature by refining performance metrics such as DER
 response accuracy, grid constraint satisfaction, and flexibility utilization. By
 incorporating peer-reviewed MPC methodologies, the project ensures scalability and
 adaptability in handling large-scale DER fleets without excessive computational
 costs. However, unlike some of these studies that concentrate on front-of-the-meter
 resources or large-scale DERs, ENGAGE specifically targets behind-the-meter
 DERs, addressing unique challenges such as limited visibility and control granularity.
 ENGAGE control algorithm emphasizes the simultaneous integration of various DER
 types whereas some studies focus on a single DER type. ENGAGE prioritized
 practical field demonstrations to validate the hierarchical control strategies
 developed in the project, moving beyond theoretical models or simulations
 commonly found in existing literature.
 The participation of DER Aggregations (DERAs) in US wholesale electricity markets
 is facilitated by the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) Order 2222 [11].

The market participation model developments following this order have
encompassed various aspects, including market service eligibility, sizing and
locational requirements, metering and telemetry capabilities, and market clearing
software considerations [12]-[21]. Although the existing literature has captured the
broader policy, market and technical implementation aspects, the specifics of
modeling DERAs in a full-fledged production cost model (PCM) have not been
explored. The contributions of this project in this regard are manifold. First, it
presents a detailed review of the state-of-the-art market participation options for
DERAs, providing a broader context for the existing considerations, use-cases, and
challenges for DERA usage towards providing grid services. Second, it develops
innovative participation models which help adequately represent DERAs within PCM
simulations, given the constantly updating regulations and challenges around their
adoption. Third, it extensively evaluates the techno-economic value of DERAs for a
representative market clearing model of the New York Independent System
Operator (NYISO) region using the Power System Optimizer (PSO) software tool.
This evaluation accounts for various systems, resource and ratepayer metrics, and
corresponds to a range of operational and resource deployment scenarios. Lastly,
the approach used for the development of participation models and PCM evaluation
provides a general framework that can be leveraged by future studies to model and
benchmark more sophisticated DERA configurations.
Enabling grid services from behind-the-meter DERs in a reliable and economically
efficient manner requires the development of fundamentally new controls and
harmonization of Transmission System Operator (TSO), Distribution System
Operator (DSO), and Aggregator efforts. Research, development, and
demonstration of these concepts require advanced analysis and simulation
capabilities beyond those typically used today.
One of the main challenges in testing DERMS algorithms is the simulation size
needed. This is because to demonstrate how a DERMS algorithm can effectively
provide transmission system (TX) grid services from BTM DERs, the simulation must
be able to include the propagation of dynamics between T&D systems. Furthermore,
it must be capable of simulating hundreds to thousands of devices equipped with
grid support functionalities, as well as the communication links between DERMS and
the devices. In [22], Poudel et al. introduced a modeling environment for testing
DERMS with the GridAPPS-D platform. The platform demonstrates how DERs can
be controlled to provide grid services in real time utilizing the GridAPPS-D platform.
However, the proposed method is limited by the GridAPPS-D capabilities, which is
focused on Distribution (DX) systems, and does not include Transmission (TX)
system dynamics. Moreover, the method was validated in a small scenario (IEEE
13-bus feeder model).
In [23], researchers from NREL compare eight different methods for T&D co-
simulation work, including the method proposed in the ENGAGE project. In
comparison they highlight how ENGAGE work allows the simulation of a very large
grid with high DER penetration while maintaining data exchange and time
synchronization due to the idea of utilizing a communication framework based on the
Message Queuing Telemetry Transport (MQTT) communication protocol. As pointed

 Page 7 of 72

in [23], one uniqueness of the ENGAE work is the real time T&D co-simulation
framework which can be used to test DERMS algorithms in large-scale HIL
simulations. The DERs are represented by DER emulators, which are equipped with
typical grid support functionalities and are integrated into the DX simulator via MQTT
communication protocol [24]. The proposed framework is validated in a large-scale
use case including a section of the Eastern Interconnection Transmission System
(EITS) containing realistic DX feeders from local utilities.
Following the analysis completed as part of ENGAGE on the new complexities,
common approaches, and research needs related to M&V for grid services [25] and
building on technical documentation developed by early adopters in Europe [26],[27],
EPRI has further evaluated the specific M&V needs and potential solutions emerging
in the distribution domain [28]. Follow up work included a comprehensive white
paper [29] analyzing the metering configurations DERs providing wholesale
electricity services, which EPRI presented at the bi-annual CIGRE Session in August
2024.

6. Project Objectives:
 Technical, economic, and regulatory barriers prevent behind-the-meter (BTM)
 distributed energy resources (DERs) from providing grid services at scale. These
 barriers include technology limitations, performance criteria, and economic
 constraints. Bulk Power System (BPS) services face minimum size limits, metering
 requirements, and scan rates due to software and communication limitations.
 Additionally, these services must be economically competitive. Distribution services
 are rarely recognized and lack standardized performance requirements. They are
 not yet packaged into well-defined business products.
 Coordinating BTM DERs to provide various combinations of BPS and distribution
 services while meeting performance criteria and evaluating grid impacts is
 challenging. Effective local control coordination, aggregation techniques, and
 interactions with Distribution System Operators (DSOs) are crucial for enabling BTM
 DERs to meet eligibility requirements and performance standards. Untapped BTM
 DERs can provide services through innovative control methods, interfaces, and
 architecture developed in the ENGAGE project. ENGAGE project objectives include:
 a. Evaluate the technical, regulatory, and performance requirements of existing and
 new Distribution (DX) and BPS services and develop a behind-the-meter (BTM)
 DER grid services guideline to facilitate the provision of grid services and
 address existing participation barriers.
 b. Develop and validate end-to-end aggregation and control architectures,
 interfaces, and optimization methodologies to enable grid service provision by
 BTM DERs while meeting individual performance criteria, supplier and customer
 objectives, and T&D limitations.
 c. Evaluate the economic and reliability impacts of BTM DER-provided services
 from the perspectives of system operator, service provider, and ratepayer.
 d. Assess achievable performance for DER-provided grid services using T&D, and
 customer level co-simulation platforms.
 e. Develop measurement and verification methods for aggregated BTM DERs.
 f. Demonstrate provision and evaluate performance, including economic
 assessment based on measured data, of DER-provided grid services in the field.
 Figure 1 illustrates the concept of the ENGAGE project, highlighting the roles of key
 components. This multi-layer hierarchical control architecture bridges the gap
 between Transmission System Operators (TSOs), Distribution System Operators
 (DSOs), and Aggregators, enabling coordinated control and optimization of
 resources across different scales.

 7.1 Project Management and Technology Transfer (BP1 – Task 1, BP2 – Task 6,
 and BP3 – Task 11)
 Industry Collaboration
 The ENGAGE team facilitated discussions on Transmission System Operator (TSO)
 and Distribution System Operator (DSO) coordination through an industry working
 group. A major drive for this coordination is to reduce the cost and complexity of
 providing grid services from aggregations of Distributed Energy Resources (DER)
 while maintaining reliable system operation. The motivations for coordination can
 vary among the different stakeholders. For instance, TSOs may seek to improve the
 utilization and access of DER for wholesale grid services. On the other hand, DSOs
 might aim to ensure the reliable and safe operation of the distribution grid. For
 aggregators, coordination could open new economic opportunities for DER.
 The core goals of the working group were to
 • Address fundamental needs for TSO/DSO coordination through a menu of
 well-defined technical functions/interactions;
 • Inform functional requirements of operational control systems e.g.,
 DMS/EMS/DERMS/SCADA to address TSO/DSO coordination; and
 • Addressing technical challenges in enabling simultaneous services from DER
 thereby allowing more opportunities for DER to provide grid services.
 This TSO/DSO coordination working group meetings were well attended by
 independent system operators (ISOs), distribution utilities, technology providers,
 national laboratories, academia, consultants, and regulatory entities. The focus of
 these discussions during the ENGAGE project period was primarily driven by the
 requirements in FERC order 2222 3, 4.
 The team developed several TSO/DSO coordination functions, detailed in the report 5
 titled “TSO-DSO Coordination Functions for DER.” With FERC Order 2222
 mandating ISOs to establish rules for DER participation in wholesale services, this
 work has been instrumental in guiding their local rulemaking processes as they
 begin discussions on implementing the order.

7.2 Define Requirements and Metrics to Characterize Grid Services, Grid
 Reliability, and Customer Comfort (BP1 – Task 2)
The ENGAGE project team conducted a comprehensive review of essential
reliability services for the bulk power system (BPS). This review includes a catalog of
both traditional and emerging BPS ancillary services, focusing on potential service
opportunities for aggregated behind-the-meter distributed energy resources (BTM
DERs). The review covers technical definitions and characteristics, requirement
calculation approaches, contemporary performance criteria, and compensation
mechanisms for each identified service. The team surveyed provisions and
opportunities for aggregated DER participation in wholesale electricity markets, both
domestically across selected Independent System Operators (ISOs) and Regional
Transmission Organizations (RTOs), and internationally.
Additionally, this task introduced distribution grid services and defined technical
parameters that distribution operators can use to characterize specific distribution
service opportunities. It also provides examples of distribution services and
discusses possible approaches to develop performance verification plans.

 The ENGAGE project team documented their findings in a report 6 titled “Grid
 Services in the Distribution and Bulk Power Systems: A Guideline for Contemporary
 and Evolving Service Opportunities for Energy Resources”. This report serves as a
 comprehensive guide to existing and emerging grid service opportunities in both the
 distribution and bulk power system (BPS) domains, specifically for distribution-
 connected distributed energy resources (DERs). The report was developed with
 active input and feedback from participants in the Distributed Energy Resources
 Bulk Service Provision Working Group (DERBSP WG) and the Distribution Services
 Working Group (DSWG). Both groups, which meet regularly, were open to any
 interested utility, Independent System Operator (ISO), or Regional Transmission
 Organization (RTO).
 Energy regulatory agencies are continuing to encourage greater participation of
 DERs in the provision of distribution and BPS services. Specifically, in the United
 States, the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission’s (FERC) Order 2222 requires
 Independent System Operators (ISOs) and Regional Transmission Operators
 (RTOs) to allow and enable aggregations of DERs to provide services in the
 wholesale electricity markets, with the participation threshold lowered to 100 kW.
 Similarly, in several jurisdictions, distribution utilities are being encouraged, and
 sometimes required, to consider DER-provided distribution services as a way to
 defer, mitigate, or eliminate traditional network reinforcements. This report also
 identifies several existing challenges and evaluates the technical, regulatory, and
 performance requirements for providing grid services through aggregated behind-
 the-meter distributed energy resources (DERs).
 Distribution services are voluntarily provided by DER assets, for a fee, and in
 response to time- and/or location-specific needs identified by distribution system
 operators in normal, alternate (e.g., due to maintenance), or emergency conditions.
 The concepts of distribution services, virtual power plant (VPP), and non-wires
 alternatives (NWA) are closely related. NWAs are utility-driven solutions that defer or
 eliminate the need for conventional system upgrades to address network
 constraints. Such need may arise from a range of factors, including load growth and
 increased DER penetration. The technical requirements for DER-based NWAs can
 be decomposed and packaged into one or several distribution services, based on
 the specific system needs identified by the distribution utility.

 The architecture emphasizes standardized information exchanges, modular control
 techniques, and harmonization among stakeholders to maximize economic and
 operational benefits. It consists of the following main components:
 • Grid Service Requesting Entity: Entities such as Independent System
 Operators (ISOs) or DSOs issue grid service requests to be fulfilled by
 aggregators, Local DER Management Systems (LDERMS), or standalone
 DERs.
 • Aggregator DER Management Systems (ADERMS): These systems act as
 intermediary controllers, managing groups of LDERMS and standalone
 DERs. ADERMS optimize resource coordination to fulfill grid service requests
 from higher-level entities (e.g., DSOs or ISOs) while ensuring operational
 efficiency. By offering modular combinations of distribution grid (DX) and bulk
 power system (BPS) services, ADERMS facilitate cost-effective participation
 for smaller-scale DERs.
 • Local DER Management Systems (LDERMS): Premise-level controllers
 responsible for managing DERs (e.g., PV, Energy Storage Systems, EVs,
 HVAC systems, Heat Pump Water Heaters, etc.) located behind a single

 • Distributed Energy Resources (DERs): End-user resources that respond to

Control System Workflow
The team developed a service activation process which is a coordinated sequence
of interactions between the Managing Entity, ADERMS, LDERMS, and individual
DERs. As shown in Figure 4, this process ensures seamless execution of grid
services while adhering to operational constraints and objectives at every level. The
steps are outlined as follows:
1. Service Activation Request: The Managing Entity initiates the process by
 sending a service activation request to ADERMS. This request specifies the
 power delivery requirements, including magnitude, duration, and timing of the
 service.
2. Status Monitoring Request: Upon receiving the service request, ADERMS
 requests real-time availability and operational status from the LDERMS it
 manages. This step ensures that ADERMS has up-to-date information on the
 resources available for dispatch.
3. Availability Status Response: LDERMS responds to ADERMS with the
 availability status of the DERs it manages. This includes details such as current
 capacity, operational constraints, and readiness to provide the requested service.
4. ADERMS Optimization and Dispatch Request: Using the information received,
 ADERMS performs optimization to allocate the incoming service request among
 its managed LDERMS. This optimization considers factors such as resource
 availability, contractual obligations, and cost-effectiveness. ADERMS then sends
 a power dispatch request to each LDERMS.
5. LDERMS Optimization and Dispatch to DERs: Each LDERMS, upon receiving
 its power dispatch request from ADERMS, runs a local optimization process. This
 process disaggregates the request further, assigning specific dispatch setpoints
 to the individual DERs it manages. The optimization accounts for DER
 characteristics, local objectives, and utility interconnection requirements.
6. DER Power Dispatch: The individual DERs respond to the dispatch setpoints
 sent by LDERMS, delivering power as per the specified request. This final step
 ensures that the grid service is executed reliably and efficiently.

                                   Page 22 of 72

 Figure 4 Control system architecture workflow.
Model Predictive Control (MPC) was implemented in both ADERMS and LDERMS
control systems, offering significant advantages over traditional corrective control
methods. It enables the effective utilization of BTM DER flexibility and transforms it
into a reliable resource for providing grid services. These benefits can be
summarized as follows:
• Proactive Control: Unlike corrective control methods, MPC anticipates future
 conditions, enabling preemptive adjustments to system operations.
• Constraint Management: It effectively handles multiple, complex operational
 constraints, ensuring system stability and compliance with objectives.
• Real-Time Adaptation: MPC dynamically updates control strategies, responding
 swiftly to sudden changes in system behavior or external conditions.
Figure 5 illustrates the operational framework and workflow of MPC, showcasing
how it integrates forecasted data, measurements, and DER models to compute an
optimized control strategy. The block diagram highlights the process of constructing
the control problem by defining the objectives and constraints. Constraints include
operational limits and the DER model, which captures the system's physical

                                   Page 23 of 72

dynamics through algebraic equations. These equations account for forecasted data
(e.g., PV output) and the latest measurements, ensuring the control actions are
founded in realistic system behavior.
The optimizer solves this problem for the entire prediction horizon (planning phase),
calculating an optimal control strategy that satisfies the objectives, respects the
operational limits, and adheres to the system dynamics. Although MPC plans for the
entire prediction horizon, it applies only the next setpoint (control/Do phase) to the
DER system at each time step, ensuring flexibility and adaptability to updated data.

services from LDERMS and standalone DERs and maximizing profit either by
responding to service requests or by leveraging Locational Marginal Pricing (LMP)
signals. These objectives are implemented through two complementary optimization
strategies, integrated with MPC for dynamic and real-time decision-making.

Based on the above case-study setup, the project made various key findings which
are provided as follows:
 • DERA comprising of rooftop solar and BTM electric storage resources (ESRs)
 were observed to displace conventional generation technologies within the
 daily operational generation mix, particularly during peak demand periods.
 For the system under consideration, the future (early 2030) average hourly
 generation from DERAs could rise to about 12.7% (approximately 2.5 GWh)
 of the maximum average hourly system load, compared to the present 4.2%
       (around 0.85 GWh).
 • The increased integration of DERA and utility-scale renewable resources
 yielded a 27% reduction in system operating costs, equivalent to around $566
 million from the current $2.1 billion.
 • With the increased integration of DERA and utility-scale renewables, average
 real-time LMPs categorized by hour-of-day are expected to experience
 reductions ranging between 31% to 52% (approximately $7.9 to $12.8).
 Moreover, reductions averaging up to 88% are projected for ancillary service
 marginal prices in scenarios considering rooftop solar only and up to 39% in
 scenarios where both rooftop solar and storage are considered.
 • All ratepayers benefit from lower market clearing prices resulting from the
 increased integration of utility-scale renewables and DERAs in the future
 resource deployment scenario, while DERA owners stand to gain substantial
 profits due to reduced operating costs, and revenues earned from value
 stacking of multiple grid services. Notably, payments made by load in
 wholesale electricity markets could decrease by over 43% (about $1.33
 billion) in the future resource deployment scenario due to lower market
 clearing prices, paralleled by the potential for BTM DERA profits to surge by
 up to 86% (about $36.9 million). Additionally, value stacking of services could
 potentially boost BTM DERA profits by up to 37% in the current resource
 deployment scenario, with an anticipated increase of about 11% in the future
 resource deployment scenario.
 • DERAs offering ancillary services leads to higher quantities of cleared
 regulation and operating reserves from these resources necessitating the
 dispatching down or curtailment of DERAs to create headroom for reserve
 provision. To ensure a level playing field, both utility-scale variable renewable
 energy (VRE) resources and DERAs were qualified for reserve provision in
 this study.

                                     Page 34 of 72

 • As more grid services were offered by DERAs within the current resource
 deployment scenario, the overall renewable curtailment, which includes
 curtailments of both utility-scale VRE resources and DERAs, was observed to
 increase by as much as 42%. While this percentage increase with grid
 services provision was less pronounced in the projected future resource
 deployment scenario, the initial curtailment was notably higher by three
 orders of magnitude.
 • While bulk system reliability issues were not observed in the tested system,
 caution is needed when applying these results to systems with different
 resource mixes, limited ramping capabilities, transmission constraints, or
 higher DER penetration, since that may yield different reliability conclusions.
 • The use of zonal modeling to represent the system and the incorporation of
 few but fairly large DER aggregations, as considered in this project, helped
 capture the overall impact of DER integration without running into
 computational modeling issues. However, this approach may lack the
 required granular representation to detect energy and ancillary service-related
 deliverability and scalability challenges.
 • Energy storage proves valuable in offering flexibility through load shifting and
 becomes most beneficial when providing both energy and ancillary services.
Overall, the above-mentioned use of realistic system data and industry-grade
research tool, along with a well-designed study and thorough analysis, helped
provide a rigorous and quantitative understanding of DERA impacts, and thus
accurately inform operator confidence in the ability of DERA to provide grid services.
BPS grid service provision by DERA was found to be economically viable while
ensuring grid reliability, with the impacts much more evident at future resource
deployment, as well as when more ancillary services were offered by DERA and
when the system had greater flexibility through the inclusion of storage resources.

HIL verification 1: Frequency support service by BTM DERs
Investigates the impact of DER on system-wide frequency response in high DER
penetration scenarios. The simulated event is a permanent three-phase ground fault
on the transmission network, followed by the outage of sub-transmission lines,
causing a 400 MW load shedding in central NY. This event trips the main corridors
between southeast and upstate NY and is simulated under three cases.
Base Case: This case represents a traditional power system with no DERs. In this
case, no DER is deployed in the co-simulation.
Case 1: This case represents today's power system where DERs do not provide grid
services. It follows the same events as the Base Case but replaces 2,878 MW of
traditional synchronous generation with DERs that operate without frequency-watt
grid support.
Case 2: This case represents the future power system with DERs providing grid
services. It considers the same events and DER deployment percentage as the
Base Case and Case 1. However, unlike Case 1, DERs' grid services, including freq-
watt, are activated to assess their impact on system frequency response.
Figure 10 compares system frequency at a central NY bus under the three cases.
The Base Case shows stability, while Case 1, lacking DER frequency response,
leads to instability. Despite replacing 2.8 GW of synchronous generation with DERs,
system inertia remains largely unchanged, as seen from the inertial response
between t=50s and 60s. This is because the NY state model is a reduced version of
the entire Eastern Interconnection grid model, incorporating the total inertia from all
synchronous generators in the grid. Case 2 results indicate that DERs with
frequency-droop support can dampen post-event oscillations and help stabilize
frequency closer to the nominal value.

7.6 Performance Requirements, Measurement & Verification Methods for
 Services from DER Aggregations (BP2 – Task 10)
Measurement and Verification (M&V) is an important building block to enable DER-
provided, pay-for-performance grid services. Traditionally, the utility industry
distinguishes between two types of metered data. First, revenue-grade meter data,
traditionally used for after-the-fact verification and settlement. Second, real-time
operational data, depending on the type of grid service, the metering resolution
available, and the frequency at which data is being streamed to the service
requesting entity. For example, operational data may be used to flag in near real-
time a DER service provider not meeting their contractual obligations and activate
contingency measures when warranted. The two types of metering data may be
derived from one or multiple metering infrastructures.
The ENGAGE project identified several challenges introduced by DER-provided grid
services which are motivating system operators to revisit their M&V practices.
• DERs are smaller in size compared to conventional resources, which can make
 traditional metering and telemetry solutions relatively expensive at the scale of a
 DER project.
• DER may also provide services to multiple entities (e.g., DSO and ISO), raising
 new “accounting” challenges, when conventional resources typically operate in a
 single service domain.
• Further, existing M&V practices for Demand Response (DR) programs may not
 be sufficient to verify the performance of DER service providers. Most DR
 programs are primarily focused on resource adequacy and/or system balancing.
 What matters is the aggregated, “statistical” response of the resource portfolio. In
 contrast, some of the emerging DER grid services place a stronger emphasis on
 the quality and certainty of the individual response of each service provider. This
 is particularly true for distribution services which tend to reflect localized grid
 needs.
This project evaluated three possible metering configurations (as shown in Figure
16) which may be considered to support DER-provided grid services moving
forward:
• Option A involves the use of a dedicated meter, i.e., a meter that sits directly in
 between the grid and a single participating resource. This solution, already
 implemented at the transmission level, can also be implemented at the
 distribution level. However, it is only applicable to front-of-the-meter (FTM) DER,
 and not applicable to behind-the-meter (BTM) DER.
• Option B entails the use of a dedicated sub-meter, i.e., a meter that sits directly
 in between the end-user (utility) meter and the DER. This solution is applicable to
 BTM DER, and should support the metering requirements for most, if not all grid
 services. However, it entails complex meter reconciliation calculations to avoid
 duplicative counting between the end-user meter, and the sub-meter, which
 existing AMI cannot currently support.
• Option C applies the approach already in use in DR programs: DER are treated
 as load-modifying DR resources, and baselines are calculated for performance
 evaluation and financial compensation. This solution is workable in the short term

                                   Page 43 of 72

 for BTM DER (treated as DR), but due to its simplicity, it may only support a
 subset of grid service products.

management, highlighting key lessons for optimizing resource allocation, improving
service quality, and ensuring economic viability:
• Effective resource allocation aligned with contract specifications enhances

• Efficient energy storage management during variable demand and uncertain
 solar conditions maintains service quality and economic returns.
• Accurate weather forecasting and integrating physics-based DERMS awareness
 improve performance under uncertainty. This adaptability ensures effective
 responses to dynamic conditions, including solar variability and load fluctuations.
• Time management strategies, such as pre-charging or pre-heating during low-
 demand periods, are critical for addressing demand effectively, especially during
 periods of reduced solar availability or high grid service requests.
• Comprehensive site assessments before deploying DERMS help identify the
 most effective configurations and control strategies suited to the unique needs of
 the site. This customization improves operational outcomes and resource
 utilization.
• Maintaining sufficient reserve capacity ensures resilience during extended
 service demands and peak load periods, reducing the risk of disruptions while
 improving operational reliability.
• Aggregators benefit from well-structured contracts and data-driven modeling
 approaches, boosting confidence in delivering reliable service requests. These

 enhancements position DERMS to function as effective Virtual Power Plants
 (VPPs).
• Expanding DERMS operations or integrating additional DERs supports broader

 Testbed Architecture and Components
 The SPIDER testbed integrates multiple layers into a cohesive co-simulation
 environment, including managing entities (ISO/DSO), control systems (ADERMS,
 LDERMS), devices (PV, ESS, HVAC, WH), circuit simulation, and visualization &
 data analytics. This platform serves as a comprehensive benchmark for evaluating
 DERMS architectures. Figure 27 shows the SPIDER architecture configured to test
 the ENGAGE DERMS. The following sections summarize the key components of the
 SPIDER testbed.

Managing Entity: The managing entity layer emulates the utility back-office role,
which can represent both the ISO and DSO functions. The ISO oversees market
operations and requests bulk power system services, while the DSO manages
distribution operations and requests distribution system services. This layer
communicates grid service requests to the ADERMS for execution.
OpenDERMS: OpenDERMS is a versatile controller framework designed to facilitate
the deployment of control algorithms as ADERMS and LDERMS. It enables the
aggregation, simplification, translation, and optimization of grid service requests from
upstream service-requesting entities into actionable DER commands. OpenDERMS
supports a wide range of communication protocols, including SunSpec Modbus,
DNP3, IEEE 2030.5, and more. It can be configured as OpenDERMS-A, hosting
ADERMS control logic, or as OpenDERMS-L, hosting LDERMS control logic,
providing flexibility and scalability for various deployment scenarios.
Device Layer: Device simulators have built-in open and standard communication
protocols that are required for communication with the upstream managing entity.
• PV and ESS simulator: This is a Windows-based desktop application that
 models a solar smart inverter and an energy storage device. It supports standard
 communication protocols like DNP3 and SunSpec Modbus. The PV and ES
 Simulator can be integrated with circuit simulation software such as OpenDSS,
 CYME, and Synergi via MQTT. Also, it supports IEEE 1547 smart inverter
 functions including Limit Active Power, Volt-Var, Volt-Watt, Frequency-Watt,
 Fixed Power Factor, and Charge/Discharge commands.
• WH simulator: The ENGAGE project contributed to the SPIDER testbed by
 developing a WH Simulator, a Windows-based desktop application that models a
 real-world smart electric water heater. The WH Simulator supports standard
 communication protocols, such as CTA-2045, enabling it to receive commands
 from a managing entity for scheduled events like "shed" and "load-up".
 Additionally, the WH Simulator integrates seamlessly with co-simulation
 components via MQTT, enhancing its flexibility for broader testing environments.

• HVAC simulator: The ENGAGE project contributed an HVAC simulator to the
 SPIDER testbed, which models HVAC systems in buildings using EnergyPlus
 and integrates with co-simulation frameworks via MQTT. It receives temperature
 setpoint commands from managing entities like DERMS and communicates with
 EnergyPlus. The simulator supports integration with circuit simulation tools
 (OpenDSS, CYME, Synergi) and exchanges data like timestamps and real power

 custom API using HTTP over TCP/IP.
Circuit Simulation Layer: The circuit simulator layer models distribution circuits to
emulate real-time feeder states under varying grid conditions and DER outputs. It
supports open-source tools like OpenDSS and commercial software such as CYME
and Synergi for detailed circuit modeling, including topology, unmanaged DER, load
profiles, and controllable device locations. The simulator runs power flow analyses to
determine grid states based on active and reactive power at circuit nodes.
Visualization and Analytics Layer: The visualization and analytics layer enables
users to study grid impacts and benefits of simulated scenarios. It visualizes feeder
attributes and data is logged in a local database after each simulation cycle, allowing
users to access data for post-analysis.

8. Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions:
  The significant accomplishments of the ENGAGE project are summarized below:
  • Published a comprehensive report that can serve as guide to existing and
 emerging grid service opportunities in the distribution and bulk power system
 (BPS) domains that are available to distribution-connected distributed energy
 resources (DERs).
  • Developed common service attributes across three attribute categories: location,
 timing, and magnitude, along with “less common” attributes termed special
 requirements. Collectively, these service attributes form a set of technical
 parameters which distribution operators can utilize to characterize specific
 distribution service opportunities based on distribution needs.
  • Developed an end-to-end aggregation and control architecture, interfaces, and
 model predictive control (MPC) based optimization algorithms to enable grid
 services provisions by BTM DERs. Aggregator DER Management System
 (ADERMS) MPC algorithm developed focuses on minimizing the total cost of
 fulfilling specific grid service requests while ensuring all contractual and
 operational constraints are respected. It also leverages LMP signals to maximize
 revenue by strategically dispatching resources during periods of high electricity
 prices. Local DERMS (LDERMS) utilizes MPC to satisfy the objectives and
 constraints of each DER type manage by it. It effectively manages the facility's
 overall objectives and constraints by optimally coordinating the various DERs
 under its management. It also handles the facility level global objectives and
 constraints via optimally coordinate among those DERs. ADERMS and LDERMS
 specifications and algorithms developed in the ENGAGE project can be
 leveraged to develop successful VPP or NWA programs.
  • Established detailed performance metrics across magnitude, timing, and
 economic dimensions to provide a robust framework for evaluating and

                                      Page 62 of 72

 enhancing ADERMS and LDERMS performance. Stakeholders can utilize the
 ADERMS performance criteria to evaluate how well a managed entity (like
 ADERMS) delivers grid service requests from a higher-level managing entity
 (e.g., ISO, DSO). Similarly, DER aggregators can utilize LDERMS performance
 criteria to evaluate how well disaggregated service requests sent to managed
 entities (like LDERMS) align with contractual obligations between managing and
 managed entities. These criteria assess the performance of LDERMS in fulfilling
 contractual obligations, meeting utility requirements, and achieving local
 objectives.
• Developed innovative participation models which could adequately represent
 DER Aggregations (DERAs) within the production cost modeling (PCM)
 simulations. The PCM and simulation studies conducted in this project utilized
 the Power System Optimizer (PSO) software tool and a representative market
 clearing model of the New York Independent System Operator (NYISO) region.
 The study examined three main factors: DERA configurations (homogeneous or
 heterogeneous), eligibility for different services (including ancillary services), and
 resource mixes (current and future). DERAs with rooftop solar and BTM electric
 storage resources (ESRs) can significantly displace conventional generation,
 especially during peak demand, with future average hourly generation potentially
 rising to 12.7% by early 2030 in NYISO region. Increased integration of DERAs
 and utility-scale renewables could reduce system operating costs by 27% and
 lower real-time LMPs by 31% to 52%. Additionally, all ratepayers benefit from
 lower market clearing prices, while DERA owners see substantial profit increases
 due to reduced operating costs and value stacking of multiple grid services.
• Traditionally, transmission and distribution (T&D) organizations have analyzed
 their systems independently, using simplified representations of each other's
 systems and disparate simulation tools. These traditional methods may not
 suffice for studying DER grid services and their system impacts. The ENGAGE
 project addressed this by developing a T&D co-simulation platform that
 integrates models of T&D system, central generation resources, DERs, and
 DERMS. This platform was to perform hardware-in-loop (HIL) verification of three
 grid services provision by BTM DERs. While BTM DER services can benefit the
 grid, they may also have negative local impacts on the distribution system, such
 as reduced equipment lifespan and operational limit violations. These impacts
 need to be addressed or considered in planning and operational time frames to
 ensure system reliability.
• Measurement and Verification (M&V) is crucial for enabling DER-provided, pay-
 for-performance grid services. The ENGAGE project identified factors introduced
 by DER-provided services that motivate system operators to revisit existing M&V
 practices. Due to their smaller size, DERs make traditional metering and
 telemetry solutions relatively expensive. The project evaluated three metering
 configurations to support future DER-provided grid services.
• End-to-end hierarchical control architecture and ADERMS/LDERMS algorithms
 from the ENGAGE project were tested at: a) Three fire stations in Fremont, CA b)
 Georgia Tech campus in Atlanta, GA. The Fremont demo highlighted effective
 disaggregation of grid service requests, strategic energy storage usage, and

                                     Page 63 of 72

 economic viability, despite solar utilization challenges. The Georgia Tech demo
 focused on price-responsive control of building HVAC loads and simulated
 energy storage to maximize revenue while maintaining user comfort.
• Year-long simulations using SPIDER provide valuable insights into the techno-
 economic viability of DERMS architecture. Customers providing grid services
 face higher energy bills but are incentivized through revenue from energy sales.
 DERMS-based solutions are significantly more cost-effective than traditional
 infrastructure upgrades, particularly for addressing localized congestion issues,
 and offer financial incentives to DER owners, creating a win-win scenario.
 Scaling up DERMS.
The ENGAGE project has demonstrated substantial progress in integrating
distributed energy resources (DERs) to support both distribution and bulk power
systems. Major accomplishments include the development of a standardized
framework for grid service attributes, implementation of advanced model predictive
control strategies for coordinated DER operation, and establishment of rigorous
performance metrics to assess system reliability and efficiency. These efforts
validate the technical feasibility and economic benefits of DER-based solutions,
providing a foundation for scalable virtual power plant initiatives and non-wires
alternatives. Collectively, the project outcomes enhance grid resilience, operational
flexibility, and customer engagement, aligning with DOE objectives for a
modernized, sustainable energy infrastructure.

9. Path Forward:
  Despite capturing the broader market reforms and opportunities with regards to DER
  Aggregations (DERAs), the ENGAGE project has several aspects that may benefit
  from wider information availability, detailed modeling, and extensive testing, among
  other things. Some of the limitations and potential future directions are elaborated
  below:
  • A comprehensive assessment of the impact of non-dispatchable DERAs on
 market operations, particularly at high penetration of renewable resources, would
 require capacity expansion and infrastructure upgrade considerations.
 Specifically, this would entail solving (or knowing the results of) a planning
 problem in addition to the production cost modeling of market operations but
 would allow for a fairer comparison between different use cases.
  • The considered model does not fully realize and quantify the true potential of
 DERAs. This is due to the lack of a nodal model which helps capture the
 locational value of resources. Otherwise, DERA integration may not be much
 different than the more centralized, utility-scale integration of renewable and
 storage resources. A lot of DERA value is locational in nature and should be
 quantified as such to better assess its potential in the grid, particularly at the
 distribution level.
  • The modeling simplifications, including zonal modeling and large resource
 aggregations, are driven by the lack of required system data and in part by the
 need to capture the macro impacts of DERAs, but they subsequently result in
 market optimization problems that do not adequately reflect the at-scale issues
 such as computational tractability. Thus, a more granular model for the system
 and aggregations will be instructive in identifying and characterizing scalability
 issues.
  • The development of distribution and retail markets may improve the existing
 value chains for DERAs, in addition to potentially unlocking new ones. In
 addition, other hybrid market models utilizing the current transmission and
 distribution markets and operations, but requiring enhanced T&D co-ordination,
 may be devised. All these measures would entail overcoming relevant technical,
 economic and policy hurdles, including but not limited to, double counting of
 services, bi-directional and/or reverse power flows, and efficient yet fair market
 outcomes.
  • The resource types considered within the DERA included PV and ESRs, which is
 fairly representative of the current makeup of the DERs in North American
 ISOs/RTOs. However, the inclusion of other resource types, e.g., demand
 response, electric vehicles, and building loads (water heater and air-
 conditioning), may further increase flexibility for DERAs. These other resource
 types may have similarities to PV and ESRs but also some unique operational
 constraints, e.g., an electric vehicle is mobile storage and water heater is thermal
 storage. All of this may translate into new opportunities and challenges for
 resource owners and system operators.
  • The provision of non-market services from DERAs, e.g., voltage regulation, may
 be studied to identify further use cases. Importantly, the contributions and
 potential of DERAs towards providing both local and bulk system services may

                                     Page 65 of 72

 be gauged. In addition, new transactive and non-market models may be explored
 and analyzed for the provision of typical market services across wide range of
 transmission and distribution networks.
• A host of technical implementation issues pertaining to DERAs, including but not
 limited to, data collection, telemetry, and infrastructure upgradation, would likely
 need to be identified and addressed. For example, issues like latency can impact
 market operations and lead to different than expected outcomes for economic
 efficiency, reliability, and other metrics. Moreover, it is important to assess
 privacy issues for market participating DERs in an aggregation and to address
 relevant network and cyber-security vulnerabilities that may lead to information
 leakage, and in extreme cases, even compromise the market and grid
 operations.
The efficacy of DERAs should be quantified more thoroughly, in particular, the
locational and reliability benefits under stress cases such as storms and heat waves.
For example, DERAs comprising of demand response from air-conditioners will likely
not be very effective in terms of reliability in a heat wave, but perhaps electric
vehicles may be. EPRI has ongoing research on microgrid design with vehicle-to-
grid (V2G) to provide resiliency support to facilities during power outage.
The type of metering equipment required to support practical M&V implementation is
another important consideration, as the equipment currently used for large,
transmission-connected market participants may present an economic barrier for
smaller scale DERs. Moving forward, various strategies can be explored to reduce
costs: 1) at lower voltage levels, simpler and less costly instrument transformers
may be used; 2) single-phase meters may be used at the distribution meters, while
poly-phase meters are the norm in the transmission domain; 3) mass-market
adoption of products combining revenue-grade metering and real-time telemetry
capabilities into a single hardware product may generate further cost reductions.
Recently, EPRI launched the Flexible Interoperable Technologies Initiative (FLEXIT),
focusing on the VPP/DER Registry and Integration Interface [30]. This initiative aims
to:
• Enhance visibility of aggregated Distributed Energy Resources (DER) and Virtual
 Power Plants (VPPs) within an electric company's footprint.
• Simplify integration by standardizing service definitions and interfaces.
• Develop a future-proof implementation strategy to ensure long-term viability and
 adaptability.
The groundwork conducted by EPRI as part of ENGAGE on service definitions and
M&V approaches is now being fully leveraged as part of EPRI’s newly launched
FLEXIT initiative [30], which intends to address DOE’s objectives regarding VPP
development highlighted in the 2023 and 2025 VPP Liftoff reports [31]
