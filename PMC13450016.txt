Cavitation-enabled selective goethite crystallization for efficient iron removal and suppressed Cu/Zn loss in hydrometallurgical zinc sulfate systems

Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

As a critical strategic non-ferrous metal, zinc is widely used in key sectors such as alloy manufacturing, batteries, electronics and information technology, metallurgy, and corrosion protection. Its stable supply and green, efficient production are of great significance to industrial development and resource security [1], [2]. Currently, hydrometallurgical zinc production has become the dominant process in global zinc smelting due to its mature process, high metal recovery rates, and relatively low environmental impact [3]. During the acid leaching of zinc concentrates and secondary zinc resources, iron typically enters the zinc sulfate system at high concentrations, while valuable metals such as copper and zinc are often present in the leachate as co-existing ions, forming a complex multi-metal solution system [4], [5]. If the purification process fails to achieve efficient removal of iron while effectively retaining copper and zinc, it will not only lead to reduced current efficiency and increased energy consumption in the subsequent electrowinning process, but will also result in the loss of valuable associated metals, thereby affecting the system’s metal balance, production efficiency, and resource utilization efficiency [6], [7]. Therefore, in the purification stage of the hydrometallurgical zinc production process, achieving efficient and synergistic separation of iron from copper and zinc has become a critical scientific challenge for enhancing the process’s environmental sustainability, minimizing losses, and maximizing value.

Among existing iron removal processes, the goethite method has emerged as one of the most promising industrial approaches in hydrometallurgical zinc smelting systems. Compared to the jarosite method and hematite methods, it offers advantages such as lower slag production, better filterability of the settled sludge, and relatively lower losses of valuable metals [8]. The goethite method oxidizes Fe2+ in solution to Fe3+ by controlling the conditions of oxidation, hydrolysis, and neutralization, and further converts it selectively into structurally stable, highly crystalline α-FeOOH precipitates, thereby achieving effective iron removal [9]. Previous studies have shown that the precipitation of goethite is essentially a complex process governed by the coupled mechanisms of Fe(III) hydrolysis, hydroxyl bridging, multinuclear aggregation, and crystal phase rearrangement. The phase composition, surface hydroxyl distribution, particle structure, and specific surface area of the precipitate not only determine the iron removal efficiency but also directly influence the adsorption, entrapment, and encapsulation of valuable metals such as copper and zinc during the precipitation process [10], [11], [12]. Therefore, fine-tuning the precipitation pathways of goethite, enhancing the selective fixation of iron, and suppressing the loss of copper and zinc are of great significance for improving the metal separation efficiency in the purification process of hydrometallurgical zinc production.

However, the traditional goethite method still faces several key limitations in practical applications. First, the kinetics of Fe2+ oxidation and Fe3+ hydrolysis crystallization in the system are relatively slow, typically requiring higher temperatures, longer reaction times, and the addition of seed crystals, resulting in high energy consumption and increased operating costs [13]. Second, during the hydrolysis and precipitation process, amorphous Fe(OH)3 colloids or other low-crystallinity byproducts are likely to form, leading to problems such as a loose sludge structure, filtration difficulties, and increased carryover of sludge particles. More importantly, traditional goethite leaching processes primarily focus on iron removal, with insufficient attention paid to the migration, enrichment, and fixation patterns of copper and zinc during the iron precipitation process. As a result, it is often difficult to achieve both efficient iron removal and minimal loss of copper and zinc [14], [15], [16]. On the one hand, rapid flocculation and particle coarsening can easily lead to mechanical entrainment of copper and zinc. On the other hand, surface defects, hydroxyl groups, and disordered pore structures on the precipitate can enhance the non-selective adsorption and entrapment of copper and zinc during coprecipitation, ultimately resulting in increased loss of valuable metals [17], [18]. Therefore, the traditional goethite process still has significant shortcomings in terms of the synergistic control of efficient iron removal and low-loss retention of copper and zinc.

In recent years, ultrasonication has attracted widespread attention in the intensification of hydrometallurgical processes due to its unique cavitation, microjet, and mechanical vibration effects [19], [20]. Ultrasonic cavitation can instantaneously create localized microenvironments of high temperature and pressure in a liquid phase, promoting the decomposition of oxidizing agents and the generation of reactive free radicals, thereby significantly enhancing the oxidation rate of Fe2+ [21], [22], [23], [24]. At the same time, microjets and high shear forces can break up colloidal aggregates, compress the mass transfer boundary layer, promote interfacial renewal, and effectively regulate the processes of particle nucleation, growth, and reorganization [25], [26], [27]. Previous studies have shown that ultrasonication can significantly accelerate the precipitation kinetics of iron oxides and iron hydroxides, optimize particle morphology, and improve crystal crystallinity and surface chemical properties [28], [29], [30]. Therefore, the introduction of ultrasound into the goethite process is expected to promote the ordered assembly of Fe–O units and the directed formation of goethite by simultaneously enhancing the processes of oxidation, hydrolysis, mass transfer, and crystal growth, while suppressing disordered flocculation and coarse-particle agglomeration. This approach is thus expected to improve iron removal efficiency while reducing the mechanical entrainment and non-selective coprecipitation losses of copper and zinc in the precipitate residue [31]. However, research on the synergistic separation of iron, copper, and zinc using the ultrasonic-enhanced goethite method remains limited. In particular, there is a lack of systematic understanding regarding the intrinsic relationships among the evolution of local coordination structures during iron precipitation, particle restructuring behavior, and the mechanisms governing the distribution of copper and zinc.

Based on this, this study proposes a novel approach using an ultrasound-enhanced goethite method for the simultaneous separation of iron, copper, and zinc, with the aim of achieving efficient, targeted removal of iron from zinc sulfate solutions while simultaneously minimizing losses of copper and zinc. Using pH, ultrasonic power, temperature, and oxidant dosage as the primary control parameters, this study systematically investigates the precipitation behavior of iron and the loss patterns of copper and zinc under ultrasonic conditions. Combining multi-scale characterization techniques such as XRD, XPS, particle size analysis, EPR, SEM-EDS, TEM, EXAFS, and EPMA, this study provides an in-depth elucidation of the coupling mechanisms among the formation of goethite, radical reactions, particle evolution, and the immobilization/release behavior of copper and zinc under ultrasound. This study is expected to provide a new theoretical basis and process support for the efficient synergistic separation of iron and valuable metals in complex zinc sulfate systems, and to offer new insights into the green intensification and short-process synergistic control of the purification process in hydrometallurgical zinc production.

Materials and methods

Materials

All reagents used in this study were of analytical grade and employed without further purification. The simulated zinc sulfate solution was prepared with deionized water using ferrous sulfate heptahydrate (FeSO4·7H2O), zinc sulfate heptahydrate (ZnSO4·7H2O), and copper sulfate pentahydrate (CuSO4·5H2O), all supplied by Tianjin Zhiyuan Chemical Reagents Co., Ltd. The initial concentrations of Zn, Fe, and Cu in the simulated solution were adjusted to 130 g/L, 10 g/L, and 0.5 g/L, respectively. A 6 wt% H2O2 solution was used as the oxidant, and ZnO powder with a purity higher than 99% was used as the neutralizing agent and pH regulator. To ensure experimental reproducibility, all stock solutions were prepared from the same batch of reagents and thoroughly mixed prior to use. The prepared Fe-containing solution was used immediately after preparation in order to minimize the undesired oxidation of Fe2+ by atmospheric oxygen.

Experimental procedure

Iron precipitation experiments were carried out in a cylindrical glass reactor with an effective volume of 500 mL. The inner diameter and total height of the reactor were 7.5 cm and 12.5 cm, respectively. In each experiment, 200 mL of simulated zinc sulfate solution was added to the reactor, corresponding to a liquid height of approximately 4.5 cm. The ultrasonic horn was placed vertically along the central axis of the reactor to reduce asymmetric cavitation distribution. The probe diameter was 3 cm, and the probe tip was immersed 4 cm below the liquid surface. Accordingly, the distance between the probe tip and the reactor bottom was approximately 0.5 cm, and the radial clearance between the probe edge and the reactor wall was approximately 2.3 cm. The same reactor geometry, liquid volume, liquid height, probe immersion depth, and probe position were maintained for all ultrasonic experiments to ensure the reproducibility of the cavitation environment (Table S1). Ultrasonic irradiation was applied in a continuous mode throughout the precipitation reaction, corresponding to a duty cycle of 100%. The continuous ultrasonic field was introduced to maintain stable cavitation-assisted liquid phase renewal, radical generation, and mass-transfer enhancement during Fe2+ oxidation and Fe3+ hydrolysis. A thermostatic control system with an accuracy of ± 0.5 °C was used to maintain the reactor temperature at the preset level, thereby minimizing the effects of thermal accumulation caused by continuous sonication.

The ultrasonic device, developed by Kunming University of Science and Technology, operated at a fixed frequency of 20 kHz, with an nominal output power setting range of 0–1200 W. To avoid excessive local supersaturation of Fe3+ and the rapid formation of amorphous ferric hydroxide, the oxidant addition rate was controlled so that the Fe3+ concentration in the solution remained below 1 g/L throughout the reaction. Simultaneously, ZnO powder was added to neutralize the acid generated during Fe3+ hydrolysis and to maintain the solution pH within the range of 2.0–4.0. The pH evolution during the reaction was continuously monitored using a pH meter (PHS-3C, Shanghai Yidian Scientific Instrument Co., Ltd.). At the end of the reaction, the slurry was filtered using a vacuum filtration unit (SHZ-D, Shanghai Lichenbangxi Instrument Technology Co., Ltd.) with quantitative filter paper. The filtrate was collected for elemental analysis, and the solid residue was dried at 353.15 K for 12 h prior to further characterization.

Analysis methods

The concentrations of Fe, Zn, and Cu in the filtrate were determined by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES, Thermo Fisher Scientific, iCAP 6000). The phase composition of the precipitated residues was analyzed by X-ray diffraction (XRD, PANalytical, X'Pert3) using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.54 Å), operated at 40 kV and 40 mA. The morphology and microstructural features of the precipitates were characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Phenom ProX), Electron probe microanalysis (EPMA, JXA-8230) and Transmission Electron Microscope (TEM, JEOL JEM-F200). The surface chemical states of the residues were analyzed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, PHI-5000). The specific surface area and pore characteristics were determined using a surface area and porosity analyzer (BET, Micromeritics, ASAP 2020), while the particle size distribution was measured using a laser particle size analyzer (Mastersize-3000). To investigate the generation and evolution of reactive radicals during the reaction, electron paramagnetic resonance spectroscopy (EPR, JEOL, JES-X320) was employed. The EPR spectra were analyzed semi-quantitatively by integrating the characteristic DMPO-OH signal. The integrated peak area of the DMPO-OH signal was normalized to the maximum signal intensity obtained under the highest ultrasonic power, and the normalized value was used as a relative indicator of ·OH generation. The local coordination environment of Fe in the precipitated residues was further analyzed by extended X-ray absorption fine structure spectroscopy (EXAFS). The EXAFS measurements were performed at the Fe K-edge in transmission mode at the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF). Results and discussion

The influence of the neutralizing agent

The neutralizing agent directly determines the local pH distribution in the reaction system, the hydrolysis rate of Fe3+, and the formation pathway of the precipitate phase. Based on the results of the neutralizing agent screening experiments (Fig. 2a − c), significant differences were observed among the various neutralizing agents in terms of iron removal efficiency and copper and zinc loss rates. Among them, ZnO achieved an iron removal rate as high as 99.98%, comparable to CaO (98.51%) and NaOH (99.68%). However, its copper loss rate was 6.03%, far lower than these of CaO (25.41%) and NaOH (23.58%), and its zinc loss rate was only 3.56%. Overall, ZnO significantly reduces copper and zinc losses while ensuring a high iron removal rate. Furthermore, due to its weak alkalinity and slow-release neutralizing properties, it helps maintain a stable pH evolution in the system, thereby preventing explosive hydrolysis and the coprecipitation of valuable metals caused by localized strongly alkaline environments. Therefore, in subsequent single-factor experiments, ZnO was selected as the neutralizing agent to balance iron removal efficiency and the loss rate of valuable metals, and to provide a baseline for process optimization.

Influence of pH

pH is a key parameter that determines the hydrolysis of Fe3+, hydroxyl bridging, and the nucleation and growth of α-FeOOH. The effects of different pH levels on iron precipitation and copper-zinc loss were investigated under conditions of 6 mL of hydrogen peroxide, a temperature of 343 K, and an ultrasonic power of 120 W, as shown in Fig. S1. The results show that as pH increases, the iron precipitation rate continues to rise, while the loss rates of copper and zinc increase simultaneously, copper is more sensitive to changes in pH. At pH 2.5, the iron precipitation rate was only about 11.66% after 60 min, indicating that strong acidity significantly inhibits the hydrolysis of Fe3+. When the pH was raised to 3.0, the iron precipitation rate increased markedly, reaching 57.30%, suggesting that a higher pH promotes the formation of iron hydroxyl species and the precipitation of goethite precursors. However, the increase in pH significantly reduced selective retention of valuable metals. The copper loss rate rose rapidly from 4.54% at pH 3.0 to 28.37% at pH 3.5, and continued to increase within the pH range of 3.5–4.5. In contrast, the rate of zinc loss increased more gradually, reaching 2.87% at pH 3.5. This suggests that the iron hydroxide intermediates and active sites on the surfaces of precipitate particles, which are generated in large quantities under high pH conditions, enhance the adsorption, encapsulation, and co-precipitation of Cu2+ and Zn2+. Among these, Cu2+ exhibits a stronger interfacial affinity with the iron precipitate phase, resulting in more severe loss [6].

Previous studies have shown that conventional goethite processes typically require the use of seed crystals or enhanced mass transfer methods to broaden the stable crystallization window in the low-pH range and improve iron removal efficiency [14]. In contrast, the ultrasonic system used in this study was still able to maintain a high iron removal rate at pH = 3.0. More importantly, under ultrasonic treatment, a significant iron precipitation rate can be achieved at lower pH levels, while avoiding the extensive formation of the amorphous Fe(OH)3 intermediate phase under high pH conditions and the associated Cu and Zn adsorption, mechanical entrapment, and co-precipitation.

Influence of temperature

Temperature has a significant impact on the iron removal process using goethite method. The essence of this is that the temperature increase can simultaneously promote the oxidation of Fe2+, the hydrolysis and polymerization of Fe3+, as well as the rearrangement of the crystal phase of α-FeOOH. The effects of different temperatures on iron precipitation and copper and zinc losses were investigated under conditions of 6 mL of hydrogen peroxide, a pH of 3.0, and an ultrasonic power of 120 W, as shown in Fig. 2d − f. The results show that as the temperature increased from 313 K to 358 K, the iron precipitation rate rose significantly, increasing from 35.15% to 65.63% at 60 min. This indicates that heating effectively promotes the hydrolysis and polymerization of Fe3+ and the formation of goethite precursors. As the temperature continued to rise to 368 K, the iron precipitation rate remained at a relatively high level but did not increase significantly further. The copper loss rate generally decreased as the temperature increased. The copper loss rate was highest at 313 K, reaching 9.20% at 60 min. When the temperature rose to 358 K, the copper loss rate dropped to 1.90%. This suggests that higher temperatures help reduce the accumulation of low crystallinity iron hydroxide intermediates and inhibit the adsorption and colloidal encapsulation of Cu2+ on the surface of the precipitate phase [7]. In contrast, the zinc loss rate increases gradually with rising temperature, rising from 0.34% at 313 K to 1.45% at 358 K, indicating that high temperatures enhance the entrapment or interfacial fixation of Zn2+ in rapidly forming iron precipitation phases [15]. Previous studies have shown that the goethite process under conventional or weakly enhanced conditions generally requires operation within a temperature range of approximately 80–95 °C to ensure sufficient oxidation kinetics and crystallization rates. When the temperature is insufficient, the system is more prone to accumulating low-crystallinity iron hydroxyl intermediate phases, which limits iron removal efficiency and exacerbates slag entrainment [32]. This study achieves iron removal performance comparable to that of conventional high-temperature processes at a lower operating temperature (358 K), thereby reducing energy consumption.

Influence of ultrasonic power

The effects of different ultrasonic power levels on iron precipitation and copper-zinc loss were investigated under conditions of 6 mL hydrogen peroxide, pH 3.0, and a temperature of 358 K, as shown in Fig. 3a − c. The results showed that as the ultrasonic power was increased from 40 W to 160 W, the iron precipitation rate increased significantly, rising from 56.18% to 68.25% at 60 min. When the power was further increased to 200 W, the iron precipitation rate rose only slightly to 68.38%, indicating that high-power ultrasound can significantly promote the hydrolysis of Fe3+ and the formation of the precipitate phase. However, once the power reaches a certain level, the further enhancing effect tends to be limited [33]. At the same time, the loss rates for copper and zinc generally decreased as the ultrasonic power increased. Under 40 W conditions, the loss rates for copper and zinc were highest, at approximately 10.96% and 3.76%, respectively, after 60 min. When the power was increased to 160 W, the loss rates for copper and zinc decreased to 1.35% and 1.18%, respectively. This indicates that the application of enhanced ultrasound helps suppress the non-selective fixation of Cu2+ and Zn2+ in the precipitate. This is because high-power ultrasound intensifies cavitation, microjets, and interfacial renewal, thereby reducing the formation of amorphous iron hydroxide intermediates and agglomerated particles, and decreasing the extent of surface adsorption, colloidal entrapment, and mechanical entrainment, thus improving the selectivity of the iron precipitation process [34]. A comprehensive comparison revealed that 160 W is the optimal ultrasonic power for achieving efficient separation of iron from copper and zinc in this system. Under these conditions, the iron precipitation rate reached a high level, while losses of copper and zinc remained at a minimum, with no significant disadvantage compared to 200 W. Considering the increased energy consumption associated with higher power levels, 160 W is recommended as the optimal ultrasonic power for achieving efficient separation of iron from copper and zinc in this system.

Influence of H2O2 dosage

The effects of varying hydrogen peroxide concentrations on iron precipitation and copper-zinc loss were investigated under conditions of pH 3.0, a temperature of 358 K, and an ultrasonic power of 160 W, as shown in Fig. 3d − f. The results show that as the added amount of hydrogen peroxide increased from 6 mL to 12 mL, the iron precipitation rate rose significantly, from 68.25% to 99.98% at 60 min. This indicates that increasing the amount of oxidizing agent effectively promotes the conversion of Fe2+ to Fe3+ and accelerates the hydrolysis, polymerization, and formation of the precipitate phase of Fe3+. When the dosage was further increased to 14 mL, the iron precipitation rate remained at its highest level, but the increase was relatively modest. The loss rates of copper and zinc showed an upward trend with increasing hydrogen peroxide dosage, rising significantly from 1.35% and 1.18% at 6 mL to 6.03% and 3.56% at 12 mL, indicating that the loss of Zn2+ and Cu2+ is susceptible to adsorption and mechanical entrainment processes [35]. A comprehensive comparison shows that the system exhibits superior separation performance under the 12 mL condition. Under these conditions, iron is almost completely precipitated, and the loss rates of copper and zinc are low. Therefore, 12 mL can be considered the appropriate amount of hydrogen peroxide to achieve efficient separation of iron from copper and zinc in this system.

Conventional and ultrasound comparison

Under optimal conditions (pH 3.0, temperature 358 K, power 160 W, and 12 mL of hydrogen peroxide), a comparison of the separation behavior of iron and copper-zinc using conventional and ultrasonic methods was investigated, as shown in Fig. 3g − i. The results indicate that, compared to conventional conditions, ultrasonication significantly increases the iron precipitation rate while simultaneously reducing the loss rates of copper and zinc, demonstrating superior separation selectivity. At 60 min, the iron precipitation rate under ultrasonication increased by 4.67%, with a more pronounced increase observed during the first 30 min of the reaction, suggesting that ultrasonication significantly accelerates the processes of Fe oxidation, hydrolysis, and precipitation phase formation. At the same time, the copper loss rate under ultrasonic conditions was only 6.03%, significantly lower than the 12.65% observed under conventional conditions (Fig. 3h). Ultrasonic treatment reduced copper co-precipitation loss by more than 50%, substantially improving the copper recovery rate. Under ultrasonic conditions, the zinc loss rate was only 3.56%, whereas under conventional conditions it reached as high as 11.69% (Fig. 3i). Ultrasonic treatment reduced the zinc loss rate by 70%. These results indicate that ultrasonication can effectively reduce the formation of amorphous iron hydroxide intermediates and loose agglomerates, thereby minimizing the adsorption, encapsulation, and mechanical entrapment of Cu2+ and Zn2+ on the surface of the precipitate phase, and consequently improving the selectivity of the iron precipitation process [36]. In summary, ultrasound not only enhances the precipitation kinetics of iron but also improves the microstructural formation pathways of the precipitate phase, thereby achieving synergistic optimization that enables efficient iron removal with minimal copper and zinc losses.

Kinetic analysis

To further elucidate the reaction mechanism underlying the removal of iron and the reduction of copper and zinc losses via the ultrasonic-enhanced goethite method, a macroscopic kinetic analysis of the iron removal process was conducted under conventional and ultrasonic conditions across different temperature ranges. In this study, first-order and second-order kinetic models were used to fit experimental data obtained under various conditions within the temperature range of 313–368 K (Eqs. (2)-(3)) [7], [12], [37].

Fig. 4(a-c) and (e-g) present the equations for conventional and ultrasonic iron removal under different time and temperature conditions, respectively. The results indicate that the iron precipitation process under both conditions is better described by a first-order kinetic model, suggesting that the macroscopic rate of the system is primarily governed by the decay of the concentration of effective iron species, rather than simply by the saturation of surface sites [14]. Compared with conventional conditions, the rate constant is significantly higher under ultrasonic conditions, indicating that ultrasound not only accelerates the oxidation of Fe2+ to Fe3+, but also enhances the subsequent hydrolysis and goethite nucleation processes, transforming the iron removal process from a relatively slow, disordered hydrolysis precipitation into a faster, more ordered, and directed crystallization process [6]. These results indicate that the enhancement of the goethite method by ultrasound is not limited to improvements in apparent mixing, but rather involves a restructuring of the iron precipitation pathway at the macroscopic kinetic level.

Calculations based on the Arrhenius equation (Eq. (4) show that ultrasonic treatment reduces the apparent activation energy of the iron precipitation process from 18.52 kJ/mol to 16.61 kJ/mol, indicating that it significantly lowers the reaction energy barrier and enhances the diffusion-controlled nature of the reaction (Fig. 4d and h) [38]. This kinetic response reveals the innovative nature of ultrasound-assisted processing, which, through the coupling of cavitation, microjet effects, and localized high-energy fields, compresses the mass transfer boundary layer, homogenizes the Fe3+ concentration field, and suppresses the instantaneous aggregation of amorphous Fe(OH)3, thereby promoting the rapid evolution of Fe species along the α-FeOOH crystallization pathway [39]. More importantly, this kinetic restructuring narrows the time window during which the low-crystallinity intermediate phase exists, thereby reducing the likelihood of Cu and Zn being mechanically entrained, co-precipitated, or non-selectively adsorbed [8]. Consequently, it achieves the simultaneous and unified acceleration of iron precipitation and suppression of copper and zinc loss.

Mechanism analysis

The influence of ultrasound on the crystal morphology of precipitated residues

In this study, systematic X-ray diffraction (XRD) analyses were performed on the precipitates under both conventional and ultrasonic conditions (Fig. 5a). The results indicate that the precipitate residues obtained from both the conventional goethite method and the ultrasonic-enhanced goethite method consist primarily of FeOOH, accompanied by small amounts of Fe(OH)3 and ZnSO4. This suggests that iron precipitates mainly in the form of goethite, but that the residues still contain partially converted iron hydroxide intermediates and small amounts of residual zinc salts. Compared with the conventional precipitated residues, the characteristic peak of FeOOH was more pronounced in the ultrasonic precipitated residues, while the signals associated with Fe(OH)3 and ZnSO4 were relatively weaker. This indicates that ultrasonication significantly promoted the conversion of Fe(OH)3 to FeOOH, improved the crystallinity of the precipitate phase, and reduced the entrapment of zinc salts. These results indicate that ultrasonic cavitation and microjet action enhance mass transfer and local mixing in the liquid phase, inhibit the disordered hydrolysis of Fe3+ into low-crystallinity Fe(OH)3 colloids, and promote the ordered nucleation and crystal growth of goethite, thereby resulting in precipitate residues with higher phase purity and structural stability [40]. Since low-crystalline Fe(OH)3 typically serves as a key carrier for the adsorption, encapsulation, and entrapment of Cu and Zn, a decrease in its concentration under ultrasonic conditions indicates that the non-selective fixation pathways for these valuable metals are being suppressed [41]. In summary, the XRD results demonstrate that ultrasound optimizes the structure of the iron precipitation phase by promoting goethite formation, reducing the residual intermediate phase, and minimizing zinc salt entrapment, thereby providing a crucial structural basis for the highly selective separation of iron from copper and zinc.

To determine the elemental composition, chemical valence states, and bonding environment of the precipitate residues, systematic XPS analyses were performed on the precipitate residues obtained under conventional and ultrasonic conditions (Fig. 5 b − h). The full spectrum indicates that the surfaces of both types of precipitate are primarily composed of Fe and O. The detection of Zn, Cu, and S suggests that the precipitates consist mainly of the ferric hydroxyl phase, with some residual zinc and copper. Compared with conventional precipitated residues, the Zn and Cu signals in the ultrasonic precipitated residues were significantly weaker, while the Fe and O signals were relatively stronger. This indicates that, following ultrasonic treatment, the surface of the precipitate residue became more dominated by FeOOH, and the non-selective enrichment of valuable metals on the surface was significantly reduced. Fe2+ analysis shows that iron on the surfaces of both precipitated residues exists primarily in the Fe3+ form. However, the peak shape of the ultrasonic precipitated residues is more regular, indicating a more uniform coordination environment for Fe3+ on its surface and a higher degree of structural order. This suggests that ultrasonication promotes the conversion of Fe(OH)3 intermediates into the FeOOH main phase, thereby reducing the residual content of low-crystallinity iron hydroxide species [42]. The Zn2+ spectrum indicates that Zn is present in the form of Zn2+ in both precipitated residues. However, the Zn peak is generally weaker in the ultrasonic precipitated residues, suggesting that ultrasonic treatment reduces the adsorption and entrapment of Zn on the surface of the precipitate. The differences in the Cu2+ spectrum were even more pronounced: a distinct Cu2+ peak was observed in the conventional precipitated residues, whereas the ultrasonic precipitated residues showed almost no discernible characteristic Cu2+ peak, indicating that ultrasonic treatment has a highly significant inhibitory effect on copper surface enrichment. Therefore, the XPS results demonstrate that ultrasound promotes the ordered precipitation of iron, reduces surface defects and active sites, and inhibits the adsorption and encapsulation of Zn and Cu on the precipitate surface. This leads to preferential iron precipitation and the retention of valuable metals in the melt, thereby significantly improving the separation selectivity between iron and zinc/copper.

To further elucidate the functional group environment and the structure of goethite, the precipitates were analyzed using Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (Fig. 6a). The results indicate that ultrasonication did not alter the primary chemical bond types or the main phase composition of the precipitate, but it significantly modulated the local coordination environments of hydroxyl, sulfate, and Fe−O groups. Both conventional and ultrasonic precipitated residues exhibited distinct O−H, H−O−H, S−O, and Fe−O/Fe−OH vibrational features, indicating that the precipitate consists primarily of hydroxyl-containing iron oxides, accompanied by some adsorbed water and residual sulfate ions. Compared to conventional conditions, the resolution of the sulfate-related vibrational peaks in the 1100–1000 cm−1 region and the Fe−O vibrational peaks in the low-wavenumber region is higher under ultrasonic conditions. This indicates that ultrasonication promotes the ordered assembly of Fe−O octahedral structural units and results in a more uniform binding environment for hydroxyl and sulfate groups within the precipitate phase [15]. The mechanism involves the localized high temperatures, high pressures, microjets, and intense agitation generated by ultrasonic cavitation, which enhance mass transfer within the solution and interfacial renewal, thereby promoting the hydrolysis of Fe(III), hydroxyl bridging, and phase transformation, while simultaneously suppressing disordered flocculation and coarse particle aggregation [43]. Consequently, iron tends to precipitate in the form of well-structured goethite-type ferrihydroxides. The ordered structure of the precipitate phase further reduces the mechanical entrainment, non-selective surface adsorption, and encapsulation coprecipitation of Cu and Zn, thereby improving iron removal efficiency while simultaneously reducing the loss of copper and zinc.

The BET analysis of the precipitates from the conventional goethite method and the ultrasonic-enhanced goethite method is shown in Fig. 5b − f. The results indicate that the precipitates obtained from both the conventional goethite method and the ultrasonic-enhanced goethite method exhibit N2 adsorption–desorption characteristics typical of mesoporous materials, suggesting that their pore structures primarily consist of interparticle and agglomeration pores. Compared to the conventional method, the specific surface area of the ultrasonic precipitation residue increased from 46.268 m2/g to 66.166 m2/g, the pore volume increased from 0.119 cm3/g to 0.157 cm3/g, and the average pore diameter decreased from 10.334 nm to 7.595 nm. These results indicate that ultrasonication does not merely alter particle size. On the contrary, through cavitation, microjets, and localized high shear forces, it inhibits disordered flocculation and the formation of loose aggregates, thereby promoting the formation of a more open and stable multi-level pore structure in precipitated particles during the processes of disaggregation and restructuring [44]. Therefore, the BET results demonstrate that ultrasound enhances the structural stability and porosity of the precipitate by optimizing the assembly of precipitate particles and their pore structure, thereby providing a crucial foundation for the highly selective separation of iron from copper and zinc.

The particle size distribution analysis of the precipitates from the conventional goethite method and the ultrasonic-enhanced goethite method is shown in Fig. 6g. The results indicate that the precipitates from both the conventional goethite method and the ultrasonic-enhanced goethite method exhibit a multi-peak particle size distribution, suggesting that the precipitate particles underwent complex evolutionary processes such as nucleation, growth, and secondary aggregation. The main peak of the conventional precipitated residues is higher and shifted toward the larger particle size range, indicating that it is more prone to forming coarse, soft agglomerates resulting from the disordered aggregation of fine primary particles. Such agglomerates typically have a loose structure with numerous internal pores, making them prone to entrapping mother liquor and salts, and providing conditions for the adsorption, encapsulation, and mechanical entrapment of Cu and Zn [45]. In contrast, under ultrasonic conditions, cavitation and microjet effects enhance mass transfer in the liquid phase and the renewal of particle surfaces, thereby inhibiting the formation and continued coarsening of loose flocs and promoting the evolution of particles toward a more ordered aggregation state during the processes of disaggregation and re-aggregation [46]. Therefore, the particle size distribution results indicate that the significance of ultrasonic enhancement lies not only in altering the particle size composition of the precipitate residue, but also in reducing the non-selective loss of copper and zinc caused by soft agglomeration and entrapment in pores by optimizing particle aggregation behavior and structural stability, thereby providing an important particle-scale basis for the efficient separation of iron from copper and zinc.

Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) was used to perform in situ trapping and analysis of the original solution, as well as solutions subjected to conventional and varying levels of ultrasonic power (Fig. 6h). The results showed that no distinct DMPO-OH characteristic signal was detected in the original solution or in the reaction mixture obtained by the conventional goethite method. However, under ultrasonic conditions, a clear hydroxyl radical trapping peak appeared, and the peak intensity increased continuously as the ultrasonic power was raised from 40 W to 200 W. This indicates that ultrasonic cavitation significantly promotes the formation of liquid-phase ·OH. The formation of ·OH in the liquid phase plays a key role in promoting the iron precipitation process. Ultrasound-induced highly reactive radicals accelerate the oxidation of Fe2+ to Fe3+, increasing the formation rate and uniformity of Fe3+ distribution. This shortens the residence time of the low-crystallinity Fe(OH)3 intermediate and promotes the formation of FeOOH along a more ordered pathway [47]. In contrast, under conventional conditions, due to the absence of free-radical promotion, the oxidation and hydrolysis of iron are more prone to being limited by local mass transfer, leading to the accumulation of amorphous intermediates and further causing the adsorption, encapsulation, and entrapment of Cu and Zn. Therefore, the EPR results demonstrate that ultrasound induces the generation of reactive radicals through cavitation, creating a more intense liquid-phase oxidative environment that accelerates the iron precipitation reaction at its source and inhibits the non-selective loss of valuable metals, thereby providing key evidence for achieving highly selective separation of iron from copper and zinc.

The DMPO-OH signal was further analyzed using the normalized integral peak area as a semi-quantitative descriptor of relative ·OH generation, thereby avoiding overinterpretation of the EPR results. The normalized DMPO-OH intensity increased consistently with the apparent rate constant of iron precipitation. This indicates that the increased generation of free radicals is closely related to the accelerated oxidation of Fe2+ and the formation of Fe3+ hydrolysis products. Meanwhile, the decrease in apparent activation energy under ultrasonic conditions further supports that ultrasound reduces the apparent reaction barrier by coupling radical oxidation, mass-transfer intensification, and interfacial renewal.

In this system, ·OH mainly contributes to accelerating Fe2+ oxidation and improving the uniformity of Fe3+ supply, while the subsequent hydrolysis, hydroxyl bridging, nucleation, and crystal growth of goethite are also controlled by pH, temperature, oxidant dosage, and mass-transfer conditions. Therefore, the semi-quantitative EPR analysis, combined with the kinetic results and structural characterization confirmed that ultrasonic cavitation promotes a faster and more ordered precipitation pathway, thereby shortening the lifetime of poorly-crystallinity Fe(OH)3 intermediates and reducing the adsorption, encapsulation, and mechanical entrainment of Cu and Zn.

Microstructure analysis

Field-emission scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was used to reveal morphological differences between the precipitates under conventional and ultrasonic conditions (Fig. 7). The results show that the precipitate residue obtained by the conventional goethite method primarily exhibits an irregular lumpy and agglomerated morphology, with blurred particle boundaries and a relatively loose structure. In contrast, the ultrasound-treated precipitated residues contained a large number of well-developed needle-like or short rod-shaped particles exhibiting the typical characteristics of goethite crystals. These results indicate that ultrasonic enhancement does not simply accelerate the precipitation rate, but rather alters the formation pathway of the precipitate phase, causing iron precipitation to transition from disordered aggregation to oriented crystal growth [48]. The scan results show that Fe and O are widely distributed in both precipitated residues, indicating that the precipitate residue consists primarily of ferric hydroxyl phases. In the conventional precipitated residues, Zn and Cu signals are relatively dispersed throughout the matrix, whereas in the ultrasonic precipitated residues, Zn and Cu are mainly concentrated in localized areas. EDS spot scan indicates that the Fe content in the ultrasonic precipitate increased, while the Cu and S contents decreased. This suggests that ultrasonic treatment significantly suppressed the non-selective retention of copper, as well as some zinc and sulfate species, in the precipitate. Therefore, the SEM-EDS results demonstrate the advantages of the ultrasonic-enhanced goethite method in terms of both morphology and elemental distribution. Specifically, by promoting the ordered growth of goethite crystals and suppressing the formation of loose aggregates and amorphous coatings, this method reduces the adsorption, entrapment, and entrainment of Zn and, in particular, Cu in the precipitated residues, thereby improving the purity of the iron phase and the selectivity of iron separation from copper and zinc.

Electron probe microanalysis (EPMA) was performed on the conventional precipitates and the ultrasonic-enhanced precipitates (Fig. 8). The results indicate that the distribution of Fe, O, and Zn in the conventional precipitated residues is more heterogeneous, with pronounced elemental enrichment and interfacial coupling observed in localized regions. In contrast, the ultrasound-enhanced precipitated residues exhibit a more uniform distribution of Fe and O at the microscale, while the Zn and Cu signals are generally weaker and primarily manifest as localized residual features. These results indicate that the conventional goethite method is more prone to forming precipitate microdomains with pronounced compositional segregation, whereas ultrasonic enhancement can significantly improve the spatial compositional homogeneity of the precipitate [7]. The differences in the distribution of Zn and Cu are particularly telling. In conventional precipitated residues, Zn exhibits a more pronounced associated distribution around certain Fe/O-enriched zones, indicating that Zn is more likely to enter the iron precipitation phase through adsorption, encapsulation, or local coprecipitation [49]. Although the overall Cu content is relatively low, there is still a tendency for it to accumulate in localized heterogeneous regions within conventional precipitated residues. In contrast, Zn and Cu in the ultrasonic precipitated residues primarily exhibited low-intensity, localized distributions, indicating that ultrasonic treatment significantly weakened the micro-scale coupling between the valuable metals and the ferric hydroxide matrix. Therefore, the EPMA results demonstrate the advantages of the ultrasonic-enhanced goethite method from the perspective of micro-area distribution [50]. Specifically, by promoting the uniform transformation of iron species and the continuous formation of precipitate phases, this method reduces the formation of amorphous intermediates and localized segregation zones, thereby suppressing the structural entrapment of zinc and copper within the iron precipitate phases and improving the separation selectivity of iron from copper and zinc.

(a-f) Electron microprobe analysis of precipitated residues under conventional. (g-l) Electron microprobe analysis of precipitated residues under ultrasonic.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was performed on both the conventional precipitates and the ultrasonic-enhanced precipitates (Fig. 9). The results indicate that the precipitate obtained via the conventional goethite method primarily consists of large, highly compacted aggregates, with only a small number of needle-like crystals forming at the edges. This suggests that the precipitation process is more prone to the accumulation of low-crystallinity intermediates and disordered aggregation. In contrast, the residual precipitate after ultrasonic treatment clearly shows a large number of well-developed needle-like or short rod-like nanocrystals. This indicates that ultrasound significantly alters the formation pathway of the precipitate phase, causing the iron precipitate to undergo an ordered transition from disordered agglomeration to crystalline FeOOH [14]. The HRTEM images indicate that under normal conditions, only local short-range ordered lattice stripes were observed, with poor continuity and disordered orientation, suggesting that a relatively high proportion of low-crystalline or amorphous iron hydroxide intermediate phases still exist. In contrast, the ultrasonic precipitated residue can clearly distinguish continuous and regular lattice stripes, indicating that ultrasound significantly enhances the crystalline order of the precipitated phase and promotes the formation of the α-FeOOH nanocrystalline phase [8]. Overall, the TEM results indicate that ultrasound, through cavitation, microjets, and localized high-energy fields, reshapes the nucleation-growth path of iron precipitation, transforming it from a disordered agglomeration under normal conditions to a directional crystallization and ordered assembly process. This structural evolution not only promoted the stable formation of goethite crystals, but also significantly weakened the encapsulation, inclusion and non-selective co-precipitation of Cu and Zn in the amorphous aggregates, thereby providing direct nanoscale evidence for the high selectivity separation of iron and copper-zinc.

Expand X-ray absorption fine structure spectroscopy (EXAFS)

This study conducted Fe K-edge synchrotron radiation analysis on precipitated residues under conventional and ultrasonic conditions (Fig. 10 and Table 1). The results show that the EXAFS spectra of the precipitates formed under conventional and ultrasonic conditions both exhibit an absorption edge at 7132.06 eV. It is indicated that iron mainly exists in the form of Fe(III), and ultrasound does not alter the oxidation state of iron. Instead, it mainly affects the nucleation, growth and aggregation processes of iron. EXAFS fitting revealed that under ultrasonic conditions, the Fe–O bond length shortened from 1.987 Å to 1.978 Å, the Fe–O coordination number increased from 4.485 to 5.018. In contrast, the apparent Fe–Fe coordination number decreased from 1.401 to 0.955, while the Fe–Fe bond length remained almost unchanged. This indicates that the local Fe–Fe distances in the goethite lattice are well preserved. Ultrasonic treatment reduces the degree of disordered Fe–Fe clustering and increases the proportion of surface or edge Fe sites in small particles. This is consistent with the particle size and BET results, which show that ultrasound suppresses the formation of loose aggregates and promotes particle deagglomeration and structural reorganization. Therefore, ultrasonic treatment promotes a more ordered Fe–O first coordination shell and a more phase-pure goethite structure, while reducing excessive second-shell Fe–Fe connectivity associated with disordered aggregation. The R-factor of the ultrasonic sample was 0.0186, which was higher than that of the conventional sample. This may be attributed to the smaller particle size, increased surface-to-volume ratio, local structural heterogeneity, and the coexistence of minor iron hydroxide intermediates in the ultrasonic precipitate.

Speciation differentiation of Cu and Zn in the precipitates

To rigorously distinguish whether the Cu and Zn retained in the precipitates are surface-adsorbed or structurally incorporated into the goethite lattice, we performed a comprehensive cross-validation analysis integrating subsurface and bulk (SEM-EDS, EPMA), and local-structural (EXAFS, HRTEM) characterization data.

EPMA and SEM-EDS elemental mapping (Fig. 7, Fig. 8), which provide micro-scale spatial resolution (1–3 μm penetration depth), show that under conventional conditions, Cu and Zn are broadly distributed throughout the Fe/O matrix, indicating extensive surface adsorption, pore entrapment, or coprecipitation with the amorphous iron hydroxide phases. In striking contrast, under ultrasonic conditions, Cu and Zn signals are primarily concentrated in localized, discrete patches, rather than being uniformly distributed within the goethite matrix. This indicates that ultrasonic treatment effectively suppresses the bulk-scale entrapment and coprecipitation of Cu and Zn.

Critically, Fe-EXAFS analysis (Fig. 10, Table 1) provides decisive evidence regarding the possible structural incorporation of Cu and Zn into the goethite lattice. If Cu2+ (ionic radius ∼ 0.73 Å) or Zn2+ (0.74 Å) were to be isomorphically substituted into the Fe3+ (0.64 Å) octahedral site, the Fe–O bond length would be expected to increase measurably due to the larger ionic radii of the substituting cations. However, our Fe-EXAFS results show that the Fe–O bond length remains essentially unchanged (1.987 Å → 1.978 Å) and the Fe–O coordination number actually increases (4.485 → 5.018). These observations strongly argue against substantial structural incorporation of Cu/Zn into the goethite crystal lattice. Instead, the increased Fe–O CN reflects the formation of more complete and ordered FeO6 octahedra—the fundamental building blocks of pure goethite. This conclusion is further supported by HRTEM lattice imaging (Fig. 9), which reveals well-resolved and continuous lattice fringes without evidence of local lattice distortion, strain, or amorphous intergrowths that would be expected if hetero-cations were incorporated into the crystalline framework.

Therefore, the combined multi-scale evidence consistently demonstrates that under conventional conditions, Cu and Zn losses occur primarily through mechanical entrainment, surface adsorption, and coprecipitation with amorphous iron hydroxide intermediates, rather than through actual structural substitution into the goethite lattice. Under ultrasonic conditions, the suppression of amorphous Fe(OH)3 formation, the enhancement of crystallinity, and the reduction of surface defects collectively minimize non-selective surface adsorption and entrapment, while the absence of structural incorporation is confirmed by the invariant Fe–O bond length and the pristine lattice fringes. This speciation differentiation provides a robust mechanistic basis for understanding the selective separation achieved by the ultrasonic-enhanced goethite method.

Mechanism of ultrasonic-enhanced selective separation of Fe, Cu and Zn from zinc sulfate solution

In conclusion, the mechanism indicates that the regulation of the separation behavior of iron, copper and zinc in the zinc sulfate system by the ultrasonic-enhanced goethite method essentially stems from the reconfiguration of the iron precipitation pathway. In the conventional goethite method, the Fe2+ that is oxidized forms Fe3+, which is prone to local supersaturation hydrolysis. It preferentially generates amorphous Fe(OH)3 and low-crystalline iron hydroxyl intermediate phases, and coexists with goethite during the subsequent aging process, eventually forming loose and irregular aggregates. This type of precipitation has a high density of surface defects, strong ability to encapsulate the mother liquor, and significant non-selective adsorption characteristics. Therefore, it is prone to cause mechanical entrainment, surface adsorption and co-precipitation of Cu2+ and Zn2+. In contrast, the ultrasonic effect significantly enhanced the uniform oxidation of Fe2+ to Fe3+ through cavitation effect, microjet effect and free radical oxidation, weakened local concentration polarization and explosive hydrolysis, and inhibited the excessive formation of amorphous Fe(OH)3. Meanwhile, ultrasound promotes the directional nucleation and ordered growth of the Fe−O structural units, causing the precipitation to gradually evolve into a more crystalline and more regular α-FeOOH nanorod/nanofilm aggregate. Due to the improvement in the purity of the precipitate phase and the change in the aggregation mode from disordered flocculation to directional assembly, the structural encapsulation and non-selective fixation of Cu and Zn by the precipitate particles have significantly weakened, thereby enabling copper and zinc to be preferentially retained in the liquid phase. From this, it can be seen that the core of the ultrasonic-enhanced goethite precipitation method lies in enhancing the selective crystallization fixation ability of iron precipitation through uniform oxidation, directional nucleation and ordered assembly mechanisms, while inhibiting the micro-coupling between Cu/Zn and the iron precipitation phase, thereby achieving efficient iron removal and low-loss retention of copper and zinc. This is consistent with the above experimental and macroscopic kinetic results, as shown in Fig. 11.

Conclusions

This study proposes a novel method for the synergistic separation of iron, copper, and zinc from zinc sulfate solutions using an ultrasound-enhanced goethite precipitation process, and systematically reveals the intrinsic relationship between process conditions, precipitation behavior, and microstructural evolution. The results indicate that factors such as pH, temperature, ultrasonic intensity, and oxidant dosage collectively determine the oxidation, hydrolysis, and crystallization processes of iron species, and further influence the migration, enrichment, and immobilization of copper and zinc during the precipitation process. Compared with the conventional goethite process, under optimized conditions, ultrasonic intensification can increase the iron precipitation rate by 4.67% and reduce the copper and zinc loss rates by 6.62% and 8.13%, respectively. This demonstrates that ultrasound significantly enhances the directionality and selectivity of the iron removal process, achieving a synergistic balance between efficient iron removal and the retention of valuable metals with minimal loss.

Multiscale characterization results indicate that ultrasound not only improves macroscopic mixing conditions but also reshapes the phase transformation pathways of iron precipitates and the formation mechanism of goethite through cavitation, microjet effects, and radical oxidation. Ultrasonication promotes the stable formation of the Fe−O coordination structure and the ordered growth of α-FeOOH, while suppressing disordered connections between Fe polyhedra and coarse particle agglomeration, thereby reducing the mechanical entrapment and non-selective coprecipitation of copper and zinc by loose, flocculent precipitates. Overall, this study elucidates the fundamental mechanisms underlying the synergistic separation of iron and copper-zinc using the ultrasonically enhanced goethite method from the perspectives of process intensification and structural evolution. It provides new insights into the efficient separation of iron from valuable metals in complex zinc sulfate systems and offers theoretical support for the green intensification and short-process synergistic control of the zinc hydrometallurgical purification process.
