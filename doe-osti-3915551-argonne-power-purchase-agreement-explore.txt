1 Introduction

This document provides a complete technical reference for the Excel-based Power Purchase
Agreement (PPA) Calculation Tool developed at Argonne National Laboratory (ANL). The tool
prices electricity from nuclear reactors under distinct commercial structures and is calibrated to
Hanna et al. (2024) and the National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR) Annual Technology Baseline
(ATB) financial methodology. A Coal-to-Nuclear (C2N) scenario extension is described in Sec-
tion 9.

1.1 Scope and Purpose

APEX is designed to be flexible enough to cover a range of reactors and data center configurations,
including regional constraints. For illustrative purposes, the documentation addresses a specific
market context: a multi-unit SMR plant (330 MWe per unit, 3–8 units) supplying a large data-center
campus (1,000 MWe peak load). Four pricing modes are implemented:

 Mode 1. Standard LCOE — Levelized cost over the full project lifetime.

 Mode 2. Two-Part PPA — Separate fixed-capacity and variable-energy charges.

 Mode 3. Shared-Risk PPA — Net cost after crediting grid export revenue.

 Mode 4. Mankala Structure — Blended price from partial data-center equity ownership.

1.2 Key Design Principles

A Power Purchase Agreement (PPA) fundamentally serves as a revenue guarantee for an op-
erational asset. Traditional lenders and infrastructure investors will generally only underwrite a
PPA if the underlying technology is proven, licensed, and ready for construction. Consequently,
a PPA alone is insufficient to secure the substantial capital required for R&D, Nuclear Regulatory
Commission (NRC) licensing, and pre-Final Investment Decision (FID) site development. To ap-
propriately contextualize the APEX models, we must explicitly define the analytical boundaries of
our framework and the mechanisms by which these pre-construction barriers are resolved.
Our model adopts a BOAK (Between First- and Nth-of-a-Kind) cost basis. While we acknowledge
the uncertainties inherent in early-stage deployment, our financial parameters are derived from
publicly available, capacity-scaled literature rather than reactor-specific FOAK (First-of-a-Kind) es-
timates. To justify this BOAK methodology, our analysis is predicated on two explicit assumptions:

 • Empirically driven OCC and O&M formulas: Capacity- and unit-number-responsive formu-
 las from Hanna et al. (2024).

 • NLR ATB compliance: All financial factors (WACC, FCR, ProFinFactor, CFF) replicate the
 exact NLR formulation.

 7

 • Multi-unit phasing: Construction can be staggered across two or three phases, or unit-by-
 unit, with a user-selectable interval.

 • Learning curves: OCC declines with cumulative unit count via a Wright’s-Law power func-
 tion.

 • Full DCF: A 100-column (year) discounted cash-flow sheet computes effective levelized
 prices that account for the exact timing of capital expenditure, O&M, and tax credits.

 • Lease-equivalent residual value: A user-configurable residual value parameter enables
 NPV-neutral PPA pricing for any contract term 𝜏 ≤ 𝐿 (see Section 7).

 • C2N extension: Six parameter modifications adapt the baseline tool to the Coal-to-Nuclear
 conversion context (see Section 9).

2 Financial Parameter Derivation

This section derives every financial factor in the order they are computed in the spreadsheet, from
raw inputs through to the Fixed Charge Rate (FCR) that enters the LCOE formula. As a founda-
tional metric in energy project finance, the FCR translates massive, upfront capital expenditures
into a normalized, annualized revenue requirement. It acts as the mathematical bridge between a
project’s engineering costs and its commercial viability by encapsulating the cost of capital, corpo-
rate tax liabilities, and depreciation tax shields into a single, cohesive multiplier. Ultimately, applying
the FCR to the total capital investment determines the exact annual cash flow required to satisfy
all debt and equity investors over the asset’s lifespan.

Impact of Hyperscaler Capital Injections
While the NLR ATB provides baseline financial parameters, the APEX framework can accommo-
date alternative capital structures, specifically modeling the impact of hyperscaler capital injections
across different project phases.

A. Equity Investment (Direct Capital to Developer)

 • Mechanism: Hyperscalers take direct equity stakes in Small Modular Reactor (SMR) devel-
 opers to fund reactor design, licensing, and early-stage development.

 • APEX Adjustments: The model allows for adjustments to the debt-to-equity ratio (𝑤𝑑 /𝑤𝑒 ),
 increasing the overall equity share while applying a lower required rate of return on equity
 (𝑅𝑒 ). This reflects the influx of strategic, rather than purely financial, investors.

 • Financial Impact: Because equity is typically the most expensive component of the capital
 stack, lowering the equity return requirement drastically reduces the Weighted Average Cost
 of Capital (WACC). This reduction cascades through the financial model, yielding a signifi-
 cantly lower Levelized Cost of Energy (LCOE) and reducing the required PPA strike price.

B. Development Capital (Pre-FID Funding)

 • Mechanism: Hyperscalers provide early funding for site selection, environmental permitting,
 and early works prior to financial close (Final Investment Decision, or FID).

 • APEX Adjustments: The framework allows users to shorten the modeled construction du-
 ration and reduce the baseline Construction Financing Cost (CFC) parameters to reflect de-
 risked early development.

 • Financial Impact: This lowers the project’s overall Interest During Construction (IDC) expo-
 sure. By preventing pre-FID capital expenditures from compounding at high construction loan
 rates, the total capitalized cost of the facility is significantly reduced.

 11

2.2 Present Value of Depreciation (PVD)

accounting metric, recognizing these asset write-downs allows the project to aggressively offset
its taxable income during the early years of operation. The PVD translates this future stream of
statutory tax savings into a single, upfront present-value metric, which effectively lowers the net
capital burden that must be recovered from the off-taker. To reflect that these tax deductions are
strictly based on historical, uninflated dollars, the MACRS schedule is discounted at the nominal
operational WACC, consistent with NLR practice:

Automatic MACRS Selection

The MACRS schedule tracks the ITC/PTC Section without user intervention. Existing-nuclear
projects claiming the §45U PTC use the 15-year schedule; new-nuclear projects claiming either
the §45Y PTC or the §48E ITC use the 5-year schedule that IRC §168(e)(3)(B)(vi) grants to qual-

2.4 Intuition: What ProFinFactor Does

 ProFinFactor — What It Represents

 Before introducing the formal equation, it helps to understand the ProFinFactor as a tax-
 adjustment multiplier that translates a project’s raw capital requirement into the pre-tax annual
 revenue needed to recover that capital.
 • If there were no taxes, no depreciation shield, and no investment tax credit (ITC), the
 project would need to collect more revenue from electricity sales to meet investor returns.
 • If the project can use Modified Accelerated Cost Recovery System (MACRS) depreci-
 ation and possibly the ITC, the plant needs less revenue because the tax system provides
 part of the return.
 • Therefore, a smaller ProFinFactor means the tax system is helping the project recover
 capital more efficiently.

 ProFinFactor — Role Inside APEX
 Mechanically, ProFinFactor answers:
 “For every $1 of capital invested, how much pre-tax revenue must the project collect
 once taxes, depreciation, and credits are accounted for?”
 In APEX, ProFinFactor is especially important because it determines how strongly tax benefits

 lower the effective LCOE or PPA price. The pricing mode does not change the tax arithmetic
 itself; it changes how that lower capital burden is transferred to the data center — by lower
 reference price (Mode 1), by lower capacity charge (Mode 2), by lower net cost before export
 credits (Mode 3), or by partial ownership participation (Mode 4) (see Section 6).

3.3 Learning Rate and Batch-Level Learning

In the cost estimation of advanced nuclear reactors, it is critical to distinguish between cost re-
ductions achieved through the Learning Rate (LR) and those achieved through Multi-Unit/Bulk
Savings (Section 3.4). While both reduce the OCC of Nth-of-a-kind (NOAK) deployments, they
are driven by entirely different economic mechanisms.
The learning curve captures chronological improvements gained from experience over successive
construction phases (learning-by-doing). It reflects the efficiency gained by the workforce and the
supply chain as they repeat manufacturing and construction processes over time. According to
Hanna et al. (2024), the learning rate is capacity-dependent:
 Learning Rate (Excel cell B54)

                                      LR(𝑃) = 0.145 × 𝑃 −0.083                                 (16)

This formulation indicates that smaller reactors (lower 𝑃) benefit from a steeper learning curve
than large, complex monolithic reactors because they can be factory-fabricated and deployed more
rapidly, allowing for faster accumulation of experience.
The learning rate is applied strictly at the batch level, representing chronological deployment
phases. The OCC of a subsequent batch 𝑏 is reduced via Wright’s Law:

 OCCbatch (𝑏) = OCCBOAK × 𝑏 log2 (1−LR) (17)

Crucially, units constructed simultaneously within the same batch do not benefit from learning-by-
doing from one another, as their construction schedules overlap, preventing the transfer of sequen-
tial lessons learned. Batch-level learning only reduces the base cost of subsequent batches.

3.4 Multi-Unit Cost Reduction — Sequential Bulk Savings

Independent of the chronological learning curve, deploying multiple units at a single site or procur-
ing them in a single large order book yields immediate physical and logistical savings. These
Sequential Bulk Savings, introduced by Abou-Jaoude et al. (2023, 2024), arise from amortizing
fixed costs—such as site preparation, licensing, shared administrative buildings, centralized control
rooms, and bulk-discounted factory equipment—across multiple reactor modules 𝑢.

6.2 Mode 2: Two-Part PPA

 Mode 2 — Interpretation and Scope

 Mode 2 is designed for the scenario where the data center wants to secure firm capacity

 predictable cash flows to service debt.

6.3 Mode 3: Shared-Risk PPA

 Mode 3 — Interpretation and Scope

 Mode 3 is designed for a strong bilateral/cooperative arrangement between the data center
 and nuclear supplier, centered on excess electricity disposal and value sharing.
 Core idea and justification: The data center underwrites the nuclear facility’s entire gross
 fixed cost. This full-underwrite structure is justified by three complementary rationales:

 1. Bankability requirement: Nuclear projects carry high upfront capital costs and long con-
 struction timelines. Lenders and investors require a creditworthy off-taker to guarantee
 the full fixed-cost recovery before committing capital. A partial off-take agreement leaves
 residual merchant risk on the nuclear developer, which increases the cost of capital (higher
 WACC) and, paradoxically, raises the electricity price for the data center itself. By under-
 writing 100% of fixed costs, the data center enables the lowest possible financing rate,
 which flows back as a lower PPA price.

 2. Capacity reservation premium: The data center secures firm, dispatchable capacity
 sized to its peak demand — a capability that intermittent renewables cannot guarantee
 without expensive storage. The full underwrite is effectively the price of this optionality:
 guaranteed power availability 24/7/365, which is essential for hyperscale AI and cloud
 workloads where even brief outages carry enormous economic penalties.

 3. Excess generation as a financial hedge: Because data centers exhibit variable load fac-

 26

 tors (𝜆DC < 1.0), the nuclear plant generates excess baseload power during off-peak peri-
 ods. This surplus can be sold into the wholesale grid, and the resulting revenue (𝑅export )
 acts as a direct financial credit that offsets the data center’s gross capital burden. The
 data center thus benefits from a natural hedge: when its own demand is low, grid ex-
 port revenue compensates; when its demand is high, it consumes low-cost nuclear power
 directly.

Underwriting the full fixed cost was economically rational because (a) it secured the lowest
possible unit cost of electricity through reduced financing risk, (b) it guaranteed firm capacity
unavailable from alternative sources at comparable cost, and (c) any excess generation could
be monetized through grid sales or capacity markets, partially offsetting the commitment.
Current APEX implementation. The equations below model a symmetric wholesale settle-
ment on the difference between nuclear generation and DC consumption. Surplus generation
is exported as revenue credited to the DC (gain-share); shortfall generation, such as during a
refueling outage, is procured from the grid as a backup cost charged to the DC (pain-share on
generation availability). A single wholesale price 𝑝 grid applies to both directions. The gain-share
pathway typically dominates in load-matched configurations at steady state; the pain-share
pathway becomes material for fleets with 𝜆DC > CFnuc or during refueling-heavy windows.
Cost-overrun pain-share extension (out of scope). Explicit sharing of construction cost over-
runs or O&M cost variance between developer and offtaker via a target-cost band with a sharing
coefficient 𝛼 is not currently modeled; see Section 6.3.1 for the intended future formulation. The
current settlement covers generation-availability variance only, not cost-baseline variance.

7 Residual Value Correction: Lease-Equivalent PPA Pricing

7.1 Motivation: The Lease Problem

 Correcting the Capital Recovery Logic — The Lease Analogy

 The original Mode 2 and Mode 3 formulations recover the entire Total Capital Investment over
 the PPA term 𝜏 via FCR𝜏 = CRF(𝜏) × ProFinFactor. This is financially incorrect when 𝜏 < 𝐿 (the
 plant life), because it implicitly assumes the nuclear asset has zero residual value at the end
 of the PPA term.
 The correct analogy a property lease between a landlord and a tenant:
 • The nuclear developer (landlord) borrows to build the plant.
 • The data center (tenant) agrees to pay rent for 𝜏 years.
 • At the end of the lease, the plant still has 𝐿 − 𝜏 years of productive life — the nuclear
 developer retains this residual value and can re-lease or operate the asset at market
 rates.
 • The data center should only pay for the capital consumed during the lease, not the full
 mortgage.
 As an analogy from consumer finance: when you lease a car, you pay the difference between
 the price (present value) of the new car and the future value of the used car that you return at
 lease end. You are responsible for all O&M costs during the term of the lease, but you do not
 pay off the entire purchase price. The same principle applies to a nuclear PPA: the data center
 should pay for the capital value consumed during the contract term, not the full plant cost.
 From the landlord’s (supplier’s) perspective, a shorter PPA term is actually preferable if the
 tenant pays off the capital faster — the owner then holds the asset free and clear sooner and
 can re-lease at market rates. From the tenant’s (DC’s) perspective, if you only need power for
 10 years, you would never agree to pay off the entire 60-year plant cost in 10 years.
 NPV neutrality condition: For any PPA term 𝜏, the lease-equivalent price is the unique price
 that sets the supplier’s NPV to zero:

8.2 Use Case Narratives

To ground each pricing mode in a concrete business context, the following narratives describe
representative scenarios in which each mode would be the natural first choice. These cases are
stylized for illustrative purposes but reflect the commercial logic underlying recent nuclear-data
center transactions and proposals.
 Mode 1 — Integrated Owner / Full-Cycle Cost Reference

 A hyperscale company decides to develop a dedicated campus with colocated nuclear gener-
 ation. The same parent entity controls both the data center and the generating asset. Mode 1
 is useful because it gives the cleanest long-run reference cost of electricity over the plant’s
 full 60-year life. It is especially useful for internal investment screening, strategic planning, and
 comparison against grid supply or gas-fired alternatives.

 Mode 2 — Capacity Security Without Full Volume Commitment

 A data center developer needs reliable peak capacity but expects its actual consumption to
 vary. It does not want to pay for every MWh the nuclear plant could generate. The nuclear

 38

 supplier needs a stable revenue floor to raise debt. Mode 2 is useful. as the capacity payment
 makes the project bankable, while the energy payment reflects actual usage. With the lease-
 equivalent residual value correction (Section 7), the DC pays only for capital consumed during
 the PPA term, making shorter-term contracts financially fair to both parties.

 Mode 3 — Cooperative DC–NPP with Export Flexibility

 A colocated nuclear plant and data center are developed as strategic partners. Because the
 data center’s load factor is below unity, the nuclear plant routinely generates more electricity
 than the data center consumes. Rather than curtailing this surplus, both parties want a contract
 that monetizes excess generation through grid sales and credits the resulting revenue against
 the data center’s cost. Mode 3 is useful because it explicitly models the export revenue offset,
 reducing the effective price paid by the data center in proportion to grid sales. The current
 APEX implementation captures the gain-share side of this mechanism; future extensions could
 also allocate pain-share from construction cost overruns (see Section 6.3.1)

 Mode 4 — Equity-Based Strategic Partnership

 A large technology company is willing to invest equity directly in the nuclear project to reduce
 long-term power cost and gain strategic control over supply. Mode 4 is useful, as it explicitly
 models the blended cost of capital and ownership-weighted pricing that arise when the buyer
 accepts governance complexity in exchange for lower expected lifetime cost and potentially
 better financing terms.

8.3 Which Pricing Model to Use?

In practice, the choice of pricing mode is driven by the primary commercial question the analyst
or decision-maker seeks to answer. The following decision logic provides a first-order mapping
from business question to pricing mode. In many real-world transactions, multiple modes may be
evaluated in parallel to bracket the range of plausible outcomes.
 Practical Selection Logic

 • “What is the all-in long-run cost?” → Mode 1
 • “How do we secure firm capacity without full volume risk?” → Mode 2
 • “How do we value excess generation cooperatively?” → Mode 3
 • “What if the DC directly co-invests?” → Mode 4

10 Nuclear Power Uprate Scenario Extension

The Uprate scenario models the incremental capacity addition achieved by increasing the licensed
thermal power of an existing nuclear reactor, with the resulting additional generation dedicated to
serving a colocated or contracted data-center load. Unlike the Greenfield and C2N scenarios—
which price the entire plant output—the Uprate extension prices only the incremental megawatt-
electric (MWe) capacity gained through the uprate modification. This distinction is fundamental:
the existing plant’s sunk capital is excluded, and only the marginal investment required to unlock
additional capacity enters the LCOE and PPA calculations.
Power uprates have been employed to enhance the output of nuclear power plants for over four
decades. In the United States alone, the Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) has approved
more than 170 uprates totaling over 8,000 MWe since 1977. Internationally, uprate programs have
been implemented in Sweden, Finland, Switzerland, Spain, Belgium, and Ukraine, among others.
The process is widely recognized as one of the most cost-effective methods of adding nuclear
generating capacity, typically at a fraction of the cost of new construction.
  Uprate vs. Greenfield/C2N: Key Distinction

 • Greenfield/C2N: Prices the entire plant output. TCI covers the full reactor construction
 cost.
 • Uprate: Prices only the incremental MWe from the uprate. TCI covers only the modifi-
 cation cost applied to the additional capacity. The existing plant’s capital is fully amor-
 tized and excluded.
  This means a 10% uprate of a 1,000 MWe unit prices 100 MWe of new capacity, not
  1,100 MWe. However, O&M economies of scale are computed using the post-uprate to-
  tal plant capacity (1,100 MWe), reflecting the operational reality that the uprated plant shares
  infrastructure, staffing, and maintenance across all output.

increase and the scope of required plant modifications. APEX adopts these categories and extends
them with a fourth category (EPU+) to capture the upper range of extended uprates that require
more extensive balance-of-plant modifications. The four-tier classification provides a structured
framework for associating uprate magnitude with expected capital cost, regulatory complexity, and
implementation risk.

10.1.1 Measurement Uncertainty Recapture (MUR)

MUR uprates are the smallest category, typically increasing licensed thermal power by less than
2%. They are achieved by implementing state-of-the-art feedwater flow measurement devices
(typically ultrasonic flow meters) that more precisely measure feedwater flow, which is the primary
input for calculating reactor thermal power. The reduction in measurement uncertainty from a
standard assumption of 2–3% to a plant-specific value (typically 0.3–0.5%) allows the plant to
operate closer to its actual thermal design limit without exceeding the safety analysis assumptions.
MUR uprates involve minimal physical plant modifications—primarily the installation of the new flow
measurement instrumentation—and have limited effect on plant safety analyses and equipment
qualification. The NRC has approved MUR uprates for both PWR and BWR designs, and the
regulatory review process is streamlined relative to larger uprates via Regulatory Issue Summary
(RIS) 2002-03.

10.1.2 Stretch Power Uprate (SPU)

SPU uprates typically range from 2% to 7% (though the precise upper bound is plant-specific)
and are implemented within the original design capacity of the plant. The term “stretch” reflects
the use of conservative margins that were built into the original plant design but were not credited

 45

in the initial licensing basis. These margins exist in areas such as reactor coolant system flow
rates, steam generator heat transfer capacity, and turbine-generator output capability. SPU imple-
mentations usually involve changes to instrumentation setpoints, control system adjustments, and
potentially minor equipment modifications, but do not require major plant equipment replacements.
The regulatory review focuses on confirming that the existing safety analyses remain bounding or
can be reanalyzed to demonstrate adequate margins at the higher power level.

10.1.3 Extended Power Uprate (EPU)

EPU uprates represent the largest category of power increases, ranging from 7% to 20% for PWRs
and up to 25% for BWRs. These uprates require significant modifications to major balance-of-plant
equipment, including:
 • High-pressure turbine rotor/blading replacement or modification
 • Condensate pump and motor upgrades
 • Main generator rewinding or replacement
 • Main power transformer upgrades
 • Feedwater heater modifications
 • Moisture separator reheater upgrades
EPU implementations require comprehensive safety reanalysis, including accident analyses, con-
tainment response, radiological assessments, and environmental qualification reviews. The NRC’s
Review Standard RS-001 provides detailed guidance for EPU reviews. Operating experience has
demonstrated that EPU implementations require careful attention to flow-induced vibration, steam
dryer integrity (particularly in BWRs), and secondary plant system performance.
 BWR Steam Dryer Experience

 Kang (2008) documents the Quad Cities Unit 2 steam dryer failure following an 18% EPU in
 2002, where a section of the steam dryer outer bank hood cover plate separated into mul-
 tiple pieces due to flow-induced high-cycle fatigue. This event led to significantly increased
 attention to vibration instrumentation, test procedures, and power ascension plans for sub-
 sequent EPU implementations. The incident illustrates why BWR EPU costs may differ from
 PWR EPU costs and why the APEX uprate OCC values should be treated as screening-level
 estimates subject to plant-specific refinement.

10.1.4 Extended Power Uprate Plus (EPU+)

The EPU+ category is an APEX extension that captures the upper range of extended uprates: 20–
25% for PWRs and 25–30% for BWRs. These uprates push beyond the typical EPU range and may
require modifications to reactor internals, containment reanalysis, and more extensive balance-of-
plant upgrades. The higher default OCC for PWR EPU+ ($6,400/kWe vs. $5,200/kWe) reflects
the additional engineering complexity and regulatory burden associated with these larger modifi-
cations. For BWRs, the EPU+ OCC ($5,000/kWe) is lower than the PWR value because BWR
designs have historically demonstrated greater uprate potential due to their direct-cycle steam sys-
tem architecture.

 same. As more granular data becomes available, users should differentiate these values.
 • The EPU+ premium for PWR ($6,400/kWe) reflects the additional complexity of push-
 ing PWR designs beyond 20% uprate, which may require steam generator modifications,
 pressurizer capacity increases, and more extensive containment reanalysis.
 • The lower EPU+ cost for BWR ($5,000/kWe) reflects the historically demonstrated ability
 of BWR designs to accommodate larger uprates with relatively less complex modifications
 to the nuclear steam supply system.
 Important: All OCC values are user-configurable. The defaults serve as starting points for
 screening-level analysis. Plant-specific feasibility studies should replace these defaults with
 detailed cost estimates that account for the specific reactor design, vintage, current condition,
 and regulatory environment. Kang (2008) emphasizes that “the final decision on a particular
 target level and proceeding with the PU would be made by the utility management based on
 their assessment of the optimum balance of the risks, costs, and benefits associated with the
 project.”
 Decommissioning treatment (Uprate): The uprate OCC represents the incremental capital
 required to increase output of an already-operating plant; it does not include a decommissioning
 line item. Decommissioning of the host unit is assumed to be pre-committed under the plant’s
 original capital-recovery basis (the same treatment applied to C2N in Section 9), a reasonable
 simplification given historical U.S. nuclear-project cost-recovery practice (Refs. [4]–[5]).

 Two user-selectable adjustment multipliers are provided to modify the baseline O&M and fuel

 core-flow and CPR-margin constraints, and higher-burnup spent-fuel handling. Offsets come

 core loading patterns, and analysis methods have been employed to enable plants to safely

 Using the post-uprate capacity for O&M estimation reflects the operational reality that the
 uprated plant shares its fixed cost base across all output, including the incremental up-
 rate generation. This produces a lower per-MWh O&M estimate than would be obtained by

 56

  treating the uprate capacity as a standalone plant, which is appropriate because the uprate
  leverages the existing plant’s operational infrastructure. The capacity-weighted formulation
  also correctly handles the case where only a subset of units are uprated: the non-uprated
  units contribute their original 𝑃 0.72 term to the site average, while uprated units contribute
  (𝑃 + Δ𝑃) 0.72 . This ensures that the O&M estimate varies smoothly with the number of units
  uprated and the uprate magnitude.

  Uprate Strategy Selection: Practical Considerations

  The optimal uprate strategy depends on factors beyond the APEX cost model:
 • Regulatory complexity: MUR uprates follow a streamlined NRC review process (RIS
 2002-03), while EPU uprates require comprehensive review under RS-001. Distribut-
 ing the load across more units to stay within the SPU range may significantly reduce
 regulatory timeline and cost.
 • Outage scheduling: Each unit’s uprate modifications are typically implemented during
 refueling outages. Uprating fewer units minimizes the number of extended outages
 required. Kang (2008) notes that “the training for the operating personnel can be a
 time-limiting constraint for the project” because “changes to the simulator need to be
 done well in advance of implementation in the real plant.”
 • Equipment margins: Not all units at a multi-unit site have identical design margins.
 Some units may accommodate larger uprates than others based on their specific equip-
 ment condition and operating history.
 • Operating experience: The NRC and IAEA have documented numerous events re-
 lated to power uprates, including steam dryer failures, flow-induced vibration, and sec-

 58

 ondary plant performance issues. Larger uprates carry higher implementation risk.
 • Fuel considerations: Kang (2008) notes that “for a BWR, fuel performance can still
 be the limiting parameter for the achievable power level” and that “a WWER reactor
 is limited to an uprate of 8%, due to fuel restrictions,” illustrating that fuel design con-
 straints may limit the achievable uprate percentage independently of balance-of-plant
 considerations.

A.2 Risk Allocation Summary

Table A.2 maps the principal risk categories to their allocation under each pricing mode. Unlike a
qualitative risk matrix, the allocation here is grounded in the specific APEX equations: each risk
type corresponds to one or more model parameters or formula terms whose placement within the
pricing architecture determines which party bears the exposure.
The following discussion explains, for each risk category, which equations govern the exposure
and how the four pricing modes assign it differently.

A.2.1 Construction Cost Overrun

Construction cost overrun risk arises when the realized Overnight Capital Cost exceeds the base-
line estimate OCCBOAK (Eq. 14). In APEX, the capital cost enters every pricing mode through the
Total Capital Investment TCI (Eqs. 21–22), which is then annualized via the Fixed Charge Rate

 69

FCR (Eqs. 7–8) and the Construction Finance Factor CFF (Eq. 20). Any increase in OCC propa-
gates multiplicatively through TCI = (OCC + GCC) × CFF and into the annualized capital charge
TCI × FCR.
 • Mode 1 (Eq. 30): The integrated owner absorbs the full overrun because TCIall × FCR60
 appears entirely in the numerator of the LCOE. There is no counterparty to share the cost
 with.
 • Mode 2 (Eq. 33): The supplier bears most of the overrun. The fixed charge (TCInet ×
 FCR30 +FOMannual )/𝐸 contracted is set at contract signing. If actual construction costs exceed
 the assumed TCI, the supplier’s margin is compressed because the contracted price does
 not adjust upward.
 • Mode 3 (Eq. 38): The gross fixed cost TCInet × FCR30 +FOM is underwritten by the data
 center, so a cost overrun increases the data center’s obligation. However, the pain-share
 extension (Eq. 43) allows explicit splitting via the coefficient 𝛼: the buyer absorbs 𝛼 · Δ𝐶𝑡 and
 the supplier absorbs (1 − 𝛼) · Δ𝐶𝑡 .
 • Mode 4 (Eq. 46): The overrun is shared in proportion to the ownership stake 𝑠. The data
 center’s share of the capital charge is 𝑠 × TCIall × CRFblended ×ProFinFactor; the supplier bears
 the (1 − 𝑠) fraction through the LCOE term in Eq. 47.

A.2.2 Under-Utilization Risk

Under-utilization risk occurs when the data center’s actual consumption falls below the nuclear
plant’s available generation. In APEX, this is governed by the relationship between total annual
generation 𝐸 annual = 𝑃 total × 8,760 × CF and the data center’s consumption 𝐸 DC = 𝑃 total × 8,760 × 𝜆DC
(Eq. 35), where 𝜆DC < CF implies surplus generation.
 • Mode 1 (Eq. 30): Fully internalized. The denominator 𝐸 annual is the plant’s total output; the
 integrated owner consumes or curtails as needed.
 • Mode 2 (Eq. 33): The data center is partly protected because the fixed charge is normalized
 by 𝐸 contracted (Eq. 32), which can be set below 𝐸 annual . The data center pays the capacity
 charge regardless of usage but only pays VOMtotal on electricity actually consumed.
 • Mode 3 (Eqs. 36–37): Shared via the export mechanism. Surplus generation 𝐸 export =
 𝐸 annual − 𝐸 DC is sold at 𝑝 grid , generating revenue 𝑅export that directly offsets the data cen-
 ter’s net fixed cost (Eq. 38). Lower DC utilization increases 𝐸 export and thus 𝑅export , providing
 a natural hedge.
 • Mode 4 (Eq. 47): Shared by owners. The data center pays 𝑠 × Base price on its ownership
 share regardless of consumption; the supplier bears (1 − 𝑠) at the LCOE rate.

A.2.3 Energy-Volume Risk

Energy-volume risk is the exposure to mismatches between contracted or expected energy vol-
umes and actual delivery. This is distinct from under-utilization: it concerns whether the plant
delivers the expected MWh (driven by capacity factor CF, Eq. 27), not whether the data center
consumes them.

 70

 • Mode 1: Internalized. If CF falls below the assumed value, 𝐸 annual decreases and the effective
 LCOE rises, but the integrated owner bears this internally.
 • Mode 2: Reduced for the DC. The capacity charge (Eq. 33) is paid regardless of actual
 generation (𝑄𝑛 = 0 during outages still triggers the capacity payment). The DC’s volume
 exposure is limited to the VOMtotal component, which is only paid on delivered MWh.
 • Mode 3: Reduced if exports are monetized. Lower plant output reduces both 𝐸 DC and 𝐸 export ,
 but the net fixed cost (Eq. 38) is partially buffered by the export credit structure.
 • Mode 4: Shared. Both parties bear volume risk in proportion to 𝑠 and (1 − 𝑠) through the
 weighted-average price (Eq. 47).

A.2.4 Fuel and Variable O&M Escalation

Fuel and VOM costs enter all four pricing modes as the additive term VOMtotal = VOM + Fuel (see
Section 5). In the current APEX implementation, these are deterministic inputs; escalation risk
refers to the possibility that realized costs exceed the assumed values.
 • Mode 1 (Eq. 30): Internalized. The VOMtotal term is added directly to the LCOE; any escala-
 tion is absorbed by the integrated owner.
 • Mode 2 (Eq. 34): Mostly pass-through. The VOMtotal term in the PPA price is typically indexed
 or renegotiated periodically, allowing the supplier to pass fuel cost increases to the buyer.
 • Mode 3 (Eq. 39): Can be shared. The VOMtotal term appears in the final price; whether
 escalation is absorbed by the supplier or passed through depends on the contractual terms
 governing the gain/pain-share mechanism.
 • Mode 4 (Eq. 46): Shared. The VOM term appears in the Base price (Eq. 46), which is
 weighted by 𝑠, and in the LCOE term, weighted by (1 − 𝑠). Both parties bear escalation in
 proportion to ownership.

A.2.5 Grid-Price Risk

Grid-price risk is the exposure to wholesale electricity price volatility. In APEX, this risk is concen-
trated in the export revenue term 𝑅export = 𝐸 export × 𝑝 grid (Eq. 37), which appears only in Mode 3.
 • Mode 1: Not central. The LCOE (Eq. 30) is independent of 𝑝 grid .
 • Mode 2: Secondary. The two-part PPA price (Eq. 34) does not include grid export revenue.
 Grid prices matter only if the supplier independently sells excess generation outside the PPA.
 • Mode 3 (Eq. 38): Explicitly included. The net fixed cost is TCInet × FCR30 +FOM − 𝑅export .
 If 𝑝 grid falls, 𝑅export decreases and the data center’s effective price rises. Conversely, high
 grid prices reduce the DC’s cost. This is the natural hedge described in Section 6: the DC
 benefits from high grid prices (through larger export credits) and from low grid prices (through
 cheaper alternative supply if needed). Note that in the revised formulation, TCInet replaces
 TCIall ; when 𝑅𝑉 % = 0, TCInet = TCIall and the expression is identical to the original (see
 Section 7).
 • Mode 4: Depends on structure. If the Mankala entity sells excess generation, grid-price
 exposure is shared by ownership fraction. In the current APEX formulation (Eq. 47), no
 explicit export term is included.

A.2.6 Tax-Benefit Realization

Tax benefits in APEX are captured through two mechanisms: the Investment Tax Credit (ITC, Eq. 9)
embedded in the ProFinFactor (Eq. 6), and the Production Tax Credit (PTC, Eq. 10) subtracted
directly from the PPA price. The risk is that the asset owner lacks sufficient tax liability to fully
monetize these credits.
 • Mode 1 (Eq. 30): Internalized. The ProFinFactor reduces FCR60 , and PTCeff is subtracted
 from the LCOE. The integrated owner must have sufficient tax appetite to realize both bene-
 fits.
 • Mode 2 (Eq. 34): Supplier side. The supplier claims MACRS depreciation (via PVD in Eq. 4)
 and any ITC. The benefit reaches the buyer indirectly as a lower FCR30 in the fixed charge.
 • Mode 3 (Eq. 39): Supplier side. Same mechanism as Mode 2: the ProFinFactor reduces
 the gross fixed cost, and PTCeff is subtracted from the final price. The data center does not
 directly claim any tax benefits.
 • Mode 4 (Eq. 46): Shared via ownership. The data center claims a fraction 𝑠 of the MACRS
 depreciation and ITC through the ProFinFactor term in the Base price. As discussed in
 Section Appendix C, direct tax-benefit capture at the buyer’s lower discount rate 𝑑𝐵 < 𝑑𝑆
 yields approximately 3.7% more value per dollar of ITC compared to indirect pass-through in
 Modes 1–3.

A.2.7 Residual Value and PPA-Term Risk

When the PPA contract duration 𝜏 is shorter than the plant lifetime 𝐿, the question of who retains the
residual asset value at PPA expiry becomes a material risk factor. In the original APEX formulation
(prior to the lease-equivalent correction of Section 7), Modes 2 and 3 implicitly assumed zero
residual value, forcing the DC to recover the entire TCI over the PPA term regardless of 𝜏. The
revised formulation introduces TCInet (𝜏) (Eq. 52), which credits the present value of the residual
asset back to the supplier, ensuring NPV-neutral pricing for any contract term.
 • Mode 1 (Eq. 30): Not applicable. The LCOE recovers capital over the full 60-year plant life
 (𝜏 = 𝐿), so 𝑅𝑉 (𝐿) = 0 by construction.
 • Mode 2 (Eq. 33): Directly affected. The fixed charge uses TCInet as the capital basis. When
 𝑅𝑉 % = 0, TCInet = TCIall and the original formulation is recovered. When 𝑅𝑉 % > 0, the DC
 pays only for capital consumed during the lease term, and the supplier retains the residual
 asset value. The supplier bears the risk that the actual residual value at PPA expiry may differ
 from the assumed 𝑅𝑉 %.
 • Mode 3 (Eq. 38): Same mechanism as Mode 2. The gross fixed cost uses TCInet as the
 capital basis. The residual value risk is borne by the supplier, who retains the plant at PPA
 expiry.
 • Mode 4 (Eq. 46): Shared via ownership. The data center’s ownership stake 𝑠 entitles it to a
 proportional share of the residual value. In the current APEX formulation, Mode 4 uses the
 full 60-year project life for capital recovery, so the residual value correction is not applied.

Appendix B Coupling Options and Pricing Mode Applicability

The physical and electrical architecture connecting a nuclear power plant, a data center, and the
external grid fundamentally constrains which pricing modes are commercially meaningful. A fi-
nancially settled PPA through the transmission system (Option 1) implies a very different risk and
revenue structure than an islanded microgrid with no grid connection at all (Option 4). This sec-
tion, based on Stauff et al. (2025), describes five coupling topologies and maps each to the APEX
pricing modes that are most naturally applicable. The three distinguishing factors across options
are:
  1. Whether the NPP and DC are geographically colocated (sharing a site boundary and po-
 tentially a common switchyard).
  2. Whether the data center has its own independent grid interconnection (affecting backup
 supply and export capability).
  3. Whether the nuclear plant has its own grid interconnection (affecting the ability to sell excess
 generation).

B.1 Coupling Option Descriptions

The five options span a spectrum from fully grid-integrated, geographically separated facilities to
completely islanded, colocated microgrids. Each option implies different physical power flows,
regulatory treatment, and commercial flexibility, which in turn determine the feasibility and attrac-
tiveness of each APEX pricing mode.

 Option 1: No Colocation, Interconnected with the Grid. The NPP and DC are geograph-
 ically separated. Both maintain full, independent grid connections. The DC pur-
 chases power financially via a PPA, but physical delivery occurs through standard
 transmission lines.

 Option 2: Colocation with Direct Connection and Power Export. The NPP and DC are
 colocated with a direct physical connection. Both also retain full grid connections.
 The NPP can export excess power; the DC can draw full backup from the grid.

 Option 3: Colocation with Direct Connection Behind the Meter (BTM). The NPP and DC
 are colocated and directly connected. The NPP is grid-connected, but the DC has
 no substantial independent grid connection. The DC sits behind the nuclear meter;
 excess power is exported by the NPP.

 Option 4: Colocation, Islanded Microgrid. The NPP and DC are colocated and directly
 connected. Neither facility is connected to the external grid. This is a completely
 closed-loop, off-grid system.

 Option 5: Colocation, Microgrid with Backup Interconnection. The NPP and DC are colo-
 cated and directly connected. The DC maintains a full grid connection for backup;
 the NPP has only a limited connection for safety-critical house loads.

 76

B.2 Should Excess Electricity Be Sold?

A recurring question in colocated nuclear-data center configurations is whether surplus generation
should be exported to the grid or curtailed. The answer depends on interconnection capacity,
wholesale market conditions, and the physical coupling architecture between the nuclear plant and
the data center. The following guidance summarizes the key considerations and identifies Mode 3
as the APEX pricing structure that explicitly addresses this question.
 Excess Electricity Disposal — Practical Guidance

 Whether excess electricity should be sold depends on interconnection rights, market access,
 and the coupling architecture:
 • If the nuclear plant has meaningful grid access, selling excess electricity is usually
 economically preferable to curtailment.
 • If the DC has highly variable load, export capability increases the value of the nuclear
 asset and lowers the effective DC-served price.
 • If grid prices are weak, congestion is severe, or interconnection export limits are binding,
 excess sales may be limited or unattractive.
 • In isolated or islanded configurations (Coupling Option 4; see Section B.1), excess elec-
 tricity may not be saleable at all; curtailment, storage, or flexible loads become central.
 In APEX, Mode 3 is the primary pricing mode for explicitly crediting excess electricity
 sales. If no sales are possible, the export-price and export-volume assumptions should be set
 to zero.

B.3 Pricing Mode Applicability by Coupling Option

Table B.1 maps each coupling option to the APEX pricing modes that are most commercially rel-
evant. The mapping reflects the physical constraints of each architecture: options with full grid
export capability favor Mode 3 (export-credit sharing), while islanded configurations favor Mode 1
(integrated cost reference) or Mode 4 (direct co-ownership). Options where the DC lacks indepen-
dent grid access tend to favor Mode 2 (capacity-secured contracting) because the DC’s depen-
dence on the nuclear plant makes firm capacity guarantees especially valuable.

Appendix C NPV Framework and ProFinFactor Benefit Distribution

While the LCOE and PPA pricing formulas in Section 6 determine the price of electricity, they do
not directly reveal whether a given contract structure is financially attractive to each party. The Net
Present Value (NPV) framework provides this assessment by discounting all future cash flows—
revenues, costs, tax benefits, and debt service—to a single present-value metric for both the sup-
plier and the buyer.

A positive supplier NPV indicates that the project exceeds the developer’s required return; a pos-
itive buyer NPV indicates that the contract delivers electricity at a cost below the data center’s al-
ternative supply options. This section presents simplified NPV expressions for each pricing mode,
explains how the ProFinFactor’s tax benefits are distributed between parties, and identifies the
structural reason why direct equity participation (Mode 4) can be financially superior for hyperscale
data center operators.

C.1 Intuition: What the NPV Comparison Means

 Why NPV Matters?

 Each pricing mode changes who pays when, who bears which risks, and who captures
 tax benefits. The NPV framework is simply a way to compare those streams on a consistent
 present-value basis.
 • A supplier-side NPV asks whether the nuclear project is financeable and attractive to the
 developer/utility.
 • A buyer-side NPV asks whether the DC receives enough value and price stability to justify
 the contract.
 • If a pricing mode lowers the supplier’s revenue volatility, it generally improves bankability.
 • If a pricing mode lets the DC avoid paying for unused MWh, it reduces DC volume-risk
 exposure.
 • If a pricing mode allows direct ownership, it may shift tax benefits and financing advan-
 tages toward the DC.

ments made under the contract. The discount rates 𝑑𝑆 and 𝑑𝐵 reflect each party’s opportunity cost
of capital and may differ significantly. Such distinction may become critical in Mode 4.

impact of Mode 4 also depends on governance costs, operational complexity, regulatory re-
quirements for nuclear asset ownership, and the buyer’s ability to fully monetize the tax credits
against its own tax liability.

Appendix D Discounted Cash Flow (DCF)

D.1 Overview

The Cash Flow DCF sheet implements a 100-column annual DCF model that serves as the compu-
tational backbone of APEX. While the analytical pricing formulas in Section 6 provide closed-form
LCOE and PPA prices under the simplifying assumption that all units start simultaneously, the DCF
engine computes effective levelized prices that account for the exact timing of each unit’s construc-
tion start, capital drawdown, operational commencement, and tax credit eligibility. Each column
represents one project year (1 through 100); rows represent distinct cost and revenue streams.
The effective LCOE from the DCF (Eq. D.10) converges to the analytical LCOE (Eq. 30) when all
units start in year 1, providing an internal consistency check.

H.3 Sheet 3: APEX ACCERT Interface

External cost estimates from ACCERT flow into APEX through a dedicated APEX ACCERT Interface
sheet in every workbook (Greenfield, C2N, and Uprate). The sheet exposes six per-parameter
rows (OCC, fixed O&M, variable O&M, fuel, grid connection, contingency), each with an Enable?
toggle and an External Value field, and defines a matched pair of workbook names for each row:

 94

EXT OCC ENABLED / EXT OCC VALUE, EXT FOM ENABLED / EXT FOM VALUE, and so on. Any formula in the
workbook wraps its internal calculation as IF(EXT X ENABLED="Yes", EXT X VALUE, internal ).
When the OCC or FOM toggle is Yes, the flat ACCERT value is applied to every unit without the
learning-curve, multi-unit bulk-savings, or (0.53/N + 0.47) reductions that the internal formulation
layers on top. Three formula sites are gated: the 1st-unit OCC (learning collapses to 1), the fleet-
average factor 𝑚 all of Eq. 21 (collapses to 1), and the per-unit DCF OCC row (learning × multi-unit
collapses to 1). The FOM analog gates the (0.53/N + 0.47) term in the “Estimated Total O&M”
cell and, for the Uprate workbook, in the strategy-table capacity-weighted site average. When
the toggle is No, every formula falls through to the internal expression, so the interface layer is
transparent to any workflow that does not enable it.
