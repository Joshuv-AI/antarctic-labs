Few-Step Boltzmann Generators via Scalable Likelihood Flow Maps
RuiKang OuYang ∗, Hanlin Yu †, Xinyue Ai †, Yutong He, Nicholas M. Boffi, Pradeep Ravikumar, José Miguel Hernández-Lobato, Max Simchowitz, Benjamin Kurt Miller, Omar Chehab

Abstract
Recent progress in flow-based generative modeling has led to models that output high-quality samples while using only a small number of function evaluations. However, at present, there is a lack of similar advances in estimating the model likelihood. In particular, most existing methods either rely on restrictive architectures that enable exact calculations, or use stochastic approximations such as Hutchinson’s trace estimator that introduce substantial variance. In this work, we introduce SCAlable LikeLihood distillation of flOw maPs ( SCALLOP ). SCALLOP builds on the recently proposed F2D2, a likelihood flow map model that can generate samples and their densities in a small number of function evaluations. While F2D2 uses Hutchinson’s estimator during training, we introduce an alternative and more scalable likelihood distillation objective that is Hutchinson-free and admits a vectorized formulation. Empirically, we demonstrate the effectiveness of SCALLOP as a Boltzmann generator in molecular science, and further validate its benefit on image datasets. SCALLOP significantly reduces both training variance and training time while consistently improving performance compared to F2D2, and is competitive with the state-of-the-art while achieving up to 10 × 10\times inference speedup over the fastest baseline.

Introduction

While the generative modeling community’s focus has indexed on improving the empirical quality of generated samples and the speed of generation
(
Song et al. 2023
;
Song and Dhariwal 2024
;
Lu and Song 2025
;
Geng et al. 2025
;
Boffi et al. 2026
)
, numerous applications, notably in the sciences, require access to the model likelihood, e.g., for free energy estimation and enhanced sampling methods
(
Torrie and Valleau 1977
;
Grubmüller 1995
;
Laio and Parrinello 2002
;
Barducci et al. 2008
;
Noé et al. 2019
;
Rissanen et al. 2025
;
Nam et al. 2025
;
Xie et al. 2026
)
.
To satisfy this requirement, early approaches restricted model architectures to ensure strict invertibility
(
Papamakarios et al. 2021
)
, which inherently limits expressivity. Conversely, continuous flow- and diffusion-based models—along with their few-step distilled counterparts—offer far greater expressivity but do not trivially admit scalable likelihood evaluation. Computing their likelihoods remains bottlenecked by either prohibitively expensive Jacobian calculations
(
Chen et al. 2019
;
Rehman et al. 2026
)
or high-variance stochastic estimators
(
Grathwohl et al. 2018
)
.

Current Approaches and Limitations

Overall, the generative modelling literature has focused on learning a transport map from Gaussian samples to data samples, and then using the change-of-variables formula to compute the corresponding likelihood. Using that formula is often problematic: it requires computing costly quantities based on the neural network, during inference or training.

Likelihoods of normalizing flows
(
Rezende and Mohamed 2015
;
Papamakarios et al. 2021
)
have limited expressivity and can be slow to compute. Normalizing flows parameterize transport maps as compositions of invertible neural network layers and compute likelihoods through the static change-of-variables formula, which requires evaluating their Jacobian log-determinants. To make this computation cheap, namely linear in the data dimension, the neural network has strong architectural constraints. These constraints can substantially reduce expressivity
(
Gao et al. 2020
)
, particularly in applications with important symmetries such as molecular and materials modeling, where autoregressive layers are often unsuitable.
For practical applications, expressive architectures require many neural network layers. For example, normalizing flows based on scalable transformer-based architectures

(
Zhai et al. 2025
)
have been successfully applied to atomic systems
(
Tan et al. 2026a
;
Tan et al. 2026b
;
Schebek et al. 2025
)
. Still, at inference time, they often suffer from a trade-off in speed between generating samples and computing likelihoods.

Computing likelihoods from continuous normalizing flows (CNFs)
(
Chen et al. 2019
)
typically suffers from high variance. CNFs remove the architectural constraints of normalizing flows by parameterizing the velocity field of a continuous transport process. Likelihood computation then requires integrating the divergence of the velocity field along trajectories through the instantaneous change-of-variables formula
(
Chen et al. 2019
)
. In practice, this divergence is typically estimated using the Hutchinson’s trace estimator
(
Grathwohl et al. 2018
)
, whose variance can become prohibitively large in high dimensions: maintaining constant precision may require computational budgets scaling quadratically with the data dimension. While variance-reduction techniques have been developed
(
Meyer et al.
;
Liu et al. 2025
)
, they are not mainstream.

Recent CNF distillation methods struggle to reconcile fast sampling with scalable and tractable likelihood evaluation. For instance, while flow map models
(
Boffi et al. 2025
)
successfully compress sampling into a single forward pass, they do not natively support tractable likelihood evaluation. Unlike standard normalizing flows, these models lack explicit architectural constraints and are not guaranteed to be bijective. To address this, subsequent works attempt to enforce model invertibility via regularization techniques
(
Draxler et al. 2024
;
Rehman et al. 2026
)
; however, likelihood evaluation under these models remains computationally expensive—scaling cubically with the data dimension (
\mathcal{O}(d^{3})
) due to the required Jacobian log-determinant calculation. To bypass this bottleneck,
Ai et al. 2026
frame likelihood evaluation as a parallel distillation task, jointly amortizing both the sampling process and the likelihood estimation into a few network evaluations. Nevertheless, training this joint architecture still relies on Hutchinson-based divergence estimators, thereby inheriting their characteristic high variance. Alternatively, energy-based parameterizations of CNFs
(
Aggarwal et al. 2025
;
OuYang et al. 2026a
;
Plainer et al. 2026
;
He et al. 2026
;
Guth et al. 2026
)
enable likelihood estimation in a single network evaluation, while they are not capable to generate samples in few steps.

Contributions.

In this work, we build on F2D2
(
Ai et al. 2026
)
that extends flow maps into
likelihood flow maps
that distill both the sampling procedure and the likelihood computation into a single neural network evaluation. We propose
SCAlable LikeLihood distillation of flOw maPs
(

SCALLOP
), which replaces the likelihood distillation loss in F2D2 with an alternative, based on reformulating the change-of-density along the flow. Our contributions are as follows:

•

Our new training objective makes likelihood distillation more scalable by design. It has lower-variance because it is
Hutchinson-free
and faster to optimize given the “vectorized" formulation of our objective.

•

Empirical improvements over the F2D2 model, on which
SCALLOP
is based, are substantial:
SCALLOP
can reduce training variance by up to
100\times
, converges faster, and consistently obtains better test set performance.

•

In molecular domains,
SCALLOP
is much faster than state-of-the-art Boltzmann Generators at inference time:
10\times
faster than normalizing flows
(
Tan et al. 2026a
)
while achieving better performance, and
100\times
faster than the comparable FALCON
(
Rehman et al. 2026
)
.

•

These empirical gains translate to higher-dimensional image domains, where
SCALLOP
estimates more accurate likelihoods than F2D2, while being faster and more stable to train.

Background

We motivate our study with a common setting: A practitioner is interested in sampling from an unnormalized distribution
\pi
to approximate some macroscopic property like free energy differences, denoted
O
.
\pi
takes on a Boltzmann distribution and the observable
O
is computed in expectation:

\displaystyle\pi(x)=\frac{1}{Z}\exp({-U(x)}),\quad\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[O(X)]=\int O(x)\pi(x)\,dx,

(1)

where
U:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the energy and
Z
is a normalizing constant. Since drawing unbiased samples from the Boltzmann distribution is difficult, Boltzmann Generators
(
Noé et al. 2019
)
fit a generative model
p^{\theta}
to a potentially biased, accessible distribution
p_{\text{data}}
with similarities to
\pi
. Then
p^{\theta}
approximates
O
with self-normalized importance sampling (SNIS):

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[O(X)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p^{\theta}}\left[\frac{\pi(X)}{p^{\theta}(X)}O(X)\right]\approx\frac{\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_{n}x_{n}}{\sum_{m=1}^{N}w_{m}},\quad\text{where}w_{n}=\frac{\exp(-U(x_n))}{p^{\theta}(x_{n})},\,x_{n}\sim p^{\theta}.

(2)

SNIS requires evaluating the probability density of the generated sample
x
under
p^{\theta}
, commonly referred to as the model likelihood. Provided no ambiguity arises, we use the terms
sample density
and
model likelihood
interchangeably throughout this paper.
Presently, the most expressive generative architectures, such as continuous-time flow models
(
Song et al. 2021
;
Lipman et al. 2023
)
, require computationally expensive simulations to evaluate exact densities. Prior works either restrict themselves to less expressive models with tractable likelihoods, or rely on the Hutchinson trace estimator during training or inference. In this work, we present an alternative to the latter:
a novel distillation objective for likelihood flow maps that enables simultaneous sample generation and density approximation from continuous-time models without requiring Hutchinson’s trace estimator at any stage
. Beyond our primary motivation, efficient likelihood estimation is of broad interest across machine learning, with applications extending to reward tilting and model composition.

Continuous Flows as Boltzmann Generators.

A continuous flow is an Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE) that transports samples from a base distribution
p_{0}=\mathcal{N}(0,I)
to the data distribution
p_{1}=p_{\text{data}}
, through a sequence of intermediate distributions
(p_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
. It can be used to generate samples and their densities by integrating

\displaystyle\frac{\differential}{\differential t}\begin{bmatrix}x_{t}\\
\log p_{t}^{\theta}(x_{t})\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}v_{t}^{\theta}\\
-\nabla\cdot v_{t}^{\theta}(x_{t})\end{bmatrix},\quad\text{with }x_{0}\sim p_{0}\text{ and }\log p_{0}^{\theta}(x_{0})=\log p_{0}(x_{0}),

(3)

where
\nabla\cdot
is the divergence operator. The upper equation for generating samples is called the
Probability Flow ODE
(PF-ODE) and the lower equation for generating their log-densities is commonly called the
instantaneous change-of-variables
equation
(
Chen et al. 2019
)
.

In many machine learning applications, the intermediate distributions
(p_{t})_{t}
are defined by the user, for example, using a
linear interpolation
between samples,
x_{t}=(1-t)x_{0}+tx_{1}
with
x_{0}\sim p_{0}
and
x\sim p_{1}
.
We will use this choice as a default throughout this paper. Flow Matching (FMs)
(
Lipman et al. 2023
;
Liu 2022
)
learn a corresponding velocity field
v_{t}^{\theta}(x)
and Diffusion Models (DMs)
(
Hyvärinen 2005
;
Ho et al. 2020
;
Song et al. 2021
)
learn
marginal scores
(\nabla\log p_{t}^{\theta})_{t}
which are a simple reparameterization of the velocity field, following

\displaystyle v_{t}(x_{t})=\frac{x_{t}}{t}+\frac{1-t}{t}\nabla\log p_{t}(x_{t}).

(4)

Note that evaluating the density using
Equation

3
requires the exact computation of
\nabla\cdot v_{t}^{\theta}
, which is infeasible in high-dimensional spaces. A popular approximation is to use Hutchinson’s trace estimator
(
Hutchinson 1990
)
, which estimates
\nabla\cdot v_{t}^{\theta}(x_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon}[\epsilon^{\top}\nabla v_{t}^{\theta}(x_{t})\epsilon]
with some zero-mean random variable
\epsilon
, where
\nabla v_{t}^{\theta}(x_{t})\epsilon
can be efficiently computed using the Jacobian-Vector Product (JVP) operation. However, this estimator suffers from high variance, and the JVP still introduces some computational overhead.

Flow Maps for Few-step Sampling.

Generating samples and their densities by numerical integration of
Equation

3

requires hundreds of function evaluations. To reduce this computational inefficiency, flow maps
(
Frans et al. 2025
;
Boffi et al. 2025
;
Geng et al. 2025
)
directly integrate the
sample generation
given by the upper part of
Equation

3
. In practice, flow map models are neural networks, trained to satisfy the consistency constraint

\displaystyle\Phi(x_{t},t,s)=x_{t}+\int_{t}^{s}v_{\tau}(x_{\tau})\differential\tau=x_{s}.

(5)

Training objectives for the flow map are decomposed into two terms. For
t=s
,
\Phi(x_{t},t,t)=v_{t}(x_{t})
degenerates to the classic FM objective; for
t<s
, the consistency is imposed via (1) the Lagrangian equation:
\differential_{s}\Phi(x_{t},t,s)=v_{s}(x_{s})
, (2) the Eulerian equation:
\differential_{t}\Phi(x_{t},t,s)=0
, or (3) the semigroup property:
\Phi(\Phi(x_{t},t,r),r,s)=\Phi(x_{t},t,s)
for any
t<r<s
.
For a trained flow map with parameter
\theta
, the generation process is solved by iteratively applying
\hat{x}_{t_{i+1}}=\Phi_{\theta}(\hat{x}_{t_{i}},t_{i},t_{i+1})
with
0=t_{0}<...<t_{N}=1
and
\hat{x}_{t_{0}}\sim p_{0}
, involving
N\ll 1000
Number of Function Evaluations (NFEs).

Extending flow map models so that they simultaneously integrate the
likelihood generation
given by the bottom part of
Equation

3
was recently proposed by
Ai et al. 2026
.

3
Scalable Likelihood Distillation in Flow Maps

We focus on learning a flow map-based, few-step generative model with co-evaluation of the model likelihood. Following the terminology of
Ai et al. 2026
, we call such a family
likelihood flow maps
and introduce a scalable training objective after reviewing the recent
F2D2

(
Ai et al. 2026
)
.

Throughout the paper, we use the linear interpolation
x_{t}=(1-t)x_{0}+tx_{1}
with
x_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
to construct the corrupted samples. This defines a sequence of marginal distributions
(p_{t})_{t}
that could be induced by a velocity field
(v_{t})_{t}
through solving the ODE
\differential x_{t}=v_{t}(x_{t})\differential t
.
Although
v_{t}
is typically intractable, we assume a distillation setting with access to an approximate velocity field
v_{t}^{\phi}
. We omit
\phi
for simplicity and assume
v_{t}^{\phi}\approx v_{t}
.

3.1
Likelihood Flow Map Enables Few-steps Likelihood Evaluation

We first define the
likelihood flow map
, which is an operator that outputs both the generated samples and the associated log-density change jointly.

Definition 1

(Likelihood Flow Map)

Given a density-augmented PF-ODE as described as
Equation

3
, the likelihood flow map
\Psi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1]^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
is the solution operator that maps any state at time
t
to its corresponding state at time
s
, with
s>t
, as well as the log-density change:

\displaystyle\Psi(x_{t},t,s)=\begin{bmatrix}x_{t}\\
0\end{bmatrix}+\int_{t}^{s}\begin{bmatrix}v_{\tau}(x_{\tau})\\
-\nabla\cdot v_{\tau}(x_{\tau})\end{bmatrix}\differential\tau=\begin{bmatrix}x_{s}\\
\log p_{s}(x_{s})-\log p_{t}(x_{t})\end{bmatrix}.

(6)

Ai et al. 2026
propose to parameterize the likelihood flow map through the skip-connection mechanism

\displaystyle\Psi_{\theta}(x_{t},t,s)=\begin{bmatrix}x_{t}\\
0\end{bmatrix}+(s-t)\begin{bmatrix}u_{\theta}(x_{t},t,s)\\
D_{\theta}(x_{t},t,s)\end{bmatrix}\Longrightarrow\begin{cases}u_{\theta}(x_{t},t,t)=v_{t}(x_{t})\\
D_{\theta}(x_{t},t,t)=-\nabla\cdot v_{t}(x_{t})\end{cases},

(7)

where
u_{\theta}
and
D_{\theta}
are neural networks that share a subset of their parameters. When
s\rightarrow t
, this formulation naturally recovers the instantaneous velocity and instantaneous log-density-change.
For clarity, we will use
f_{\theta}(x_{t},t,s)=\begin{bmatrix}u_{\theta}(x_{t},t,s)\\
D_{\theta}(x_{t},t,s)\end{bmatrix}
and
b_{t}(x_{t})=\begin{bmatrix}v_{t}(x_{t})\\
-\nabla\cdot v_{t}(x_{t})\end{bmatrix}
.

3.2
How to Train a Likelihood Flow Map?

According to the instantaneous behaviour as described by
Equation

7
, training a likelihood flow map
(
Ai et al. 2026
)
is analogous to training a standard flow map
(
Boffi et al. 2025
)
: we simply allocate an extra dimension for the log-density. Under the skip-connection parameterization,
training a likelihood flow map is essentially learning to satisfy
(s-t)f_{\theta}(x_{t},t,s)\approx\int_{t}^{s}b(x_{\tau},\tau)\differential\tau
.
The training objective integrates the following
three losses
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{F2D2}}(\theta)=\int_{0}^{1}w_{v}(t)\mathcal{L}_{v}(\theta;t)+w_{\mathrm{DM}}(t)\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DM}}(\theta;t)\differential t+\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{s}w(t,s)\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SD}}(\theta;t,s)\differential t\differential s,

(8)

where

w:[0,1]^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
w_{v},w_{\mathrm{DM}}:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
are weighting functions. The first two losses
\mathcal{L}_{v}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DM}}
are instantaneous losses written as

\displaystyle(\text{Flow Matching})\quad

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{v}(\theta;t)=\mathbb{E}_{x_{0},x_{1}}\Bigl[{\left\|u_{\theta}(x_{t},t,t)-v_{t}(x_{t})\right\|^{2}}\Bigr],

(9)

\displaystyle(\text{Divergence Matching})\quad

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DM}}(\theta;t)=\mathbb{E}_{x_{0},x_{1}}\Bigl[{\left|D_{\theta}(x_{t},t,t)+\nabla\cdot v_{t}(x_{t})\right|^{2}}\Bigr],

(10)

with
x_{t}=(1-t)x_{0}+tx_{1}
. The third loss
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SD}}
is called the self-distillation loss and has three different instantiations:
(1) the Lagrangian equation:
\differential_{s}\Phi(x_{t},t,s)=v_{s}(x_{s})
, (2) the Eulerian equation:
\differential_{t}\Phi(x_{t},t,s)=0
, or (3) the semigroup property:
\Phi(\Phi(x_{t},t,r),r,s)=\Phi(x_{t},t,s)
for any
t<r<s
. Adapted to the likelihood flow map setting, these three consistency constraints read:

(1)

the Lagrangian self-distillation (LSD),
i.e.

\differential_{s}\Psi(x_{t},t,s)=b_{s}(x_{s})

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LSD}}(\theta;t,s)=\mathbb{E}_{x_{0},x_{1}}\left[\bigl\|f_{\theta}(x_{t},t,s)+(s-t)\mathrm{sg}\bigl(\partial_{s}f_{\theta}(x_{t},t,s)\bigr)-\mathrm{sg}\bigl(f_{\theta}(x_{s},s,s)\bigr)\bigr\|^{2}\right];

(11)

(2)

the Eulerian self-distillation (ESD),
i.e.

\differential_{t}\Psi(x_{t},t,s)=0

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ESD}}(\theta;t,s)=\mathbb{E}_{x_{0},x_{1}}\biggl[\Bigl\|f_{\theta}(x_{t},t,s)-\mathrm{sg}\bigl(f_{\theta}(x_{t},t,t)\bigr)-(s-t)\mathrm{sg}\bigl(\nabla f_{\theta}(x_{t},t,s)\cdot b_{t}(x_{t})+\partial_{t}f_{\theta}(x_{t},t,s)\bigr)\Bigr\|^{2}\biggr];

(12)

(3)

the progressive self-distillation (PSD),
i.e.

\Psi(\Psi(x_{t},t,u),u,s)=\Psi(x_{t},t,s)

\forall u\in[t,s]

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{PSD}}(\theta;t,s)=\int_{t}^{s}\mu(u)\mathbb{E}_{x_{0},x_{1}}\biggl[\Bigl\|f_{\theta}(x_{t},t,s)-\mathrm{sg}\biggl(\frac{u-t}{s-t}f_{\theta}(x_{t},t,u)+\frac{s-u}{s-t}f_{\theta}\bigl(\Psi_{\theta}(x_{t},t,u),u,s\bigr)\biggr)\Bigr\|^{2}\biggr]\differential u,

(13)

where
\mathrm{sg}(\cdot)
denotes stop-gradient and
\mu:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
is another weighting function for
u
which is usually chosen as a midpoint
\mu(u)=\delta(u-\frac{t+s}{2})

(
Frans et al. 2025
)
.

Ai et al. 2026
proposed F2D2, a likelihood flow map that trained with the above objectives. Despite the mathematical simplicity, F2D2 relies on the Hutchinson’s trace estimator
(
Hutchinson 1990
)
to estimate the divergence term
\nabla\cdot v_{t}
in
b_{t}
to replace the computationally expensive
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DM}}
term, namely
Hutchinson Divergence Matching
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{HDM}}(\theta;t)=\mathbb{E}_{x_{0},x_{1}}\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon}\left[\left|D_{\theta}(x_{t},t,t)+\epsilon^{\top}\nabla v_{t}(x_{t})\epsilon\right|^{2}\right],

(14)

where
\epsilon
is sampled from a 0-mean distribution such as
\mathcal{N}(0,I)
, and it can be shown that
\arg\min_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{HDM}}(\theta;t)=\arg\min_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DM}}(\theta;t)
. Though
\epsilon^{\top}\nabla v_{t}(x_{t})
can be implemented through
JVP
, making it more efficient than the exact divergence calculation, it remains computationally expensive during training, especially in large-scale systems, and suffers from high variance.

3.3
Scalable Likelihood Distillation via Conditional Divergence Matching

The computational bottleneck in
F2D2
is introduced by having to estimate
-\nabla\cdot v_{t}(x_{t})=\differential_{t}\log p_{t}(x_{t})
whenever
b_{t}(x_{t})
needs to be calculated, which also suffers from high variance. Inspired by the success of Flow Matching
(
Lipman et al. 2023
;
Liu 2022
)
, which leverages a conditional objective
to bypass the intractable velocity
v_{t}(x_{t})
during training, we raise the following question:

Is there any conditional objective that allows us to bypass the divergence calculation/estimation?

Our answer is positive. The key derivation is given by the chain rule: given
x_{t}
generated by a known velocity field
v_{t}
, the chain rule reads as

\displaystyle\differential_{t}\log p_{t}(x_{t})=\nabla\log p_{t}(x_{t})\cdot v_{t}(x_{t})+\partial_{t}\log p_{t}(x_{t});

(15)

this identity is also used by
Choi et al. 2021
for density ratio estimation.

Remark 1

Alternatively,
Equation

15
can be derived from the continuity equation as stated in
Section

6.3
.

It is well known that the Stein score
\nabla\log p_{t}
can be learned via
Denoising Score Matching

(
Hyvärinen 2005
;
Vincent 2011
;
Ho et al. 2020
;
Song et al. 2021
, DSM,)
, while the time score
\partial_{t}\log p_{t}
can be learned via
Conditional time Score Matching

(
Yu et al. 2025
;
Guth et al. 2026
, C
t
SM,)
. Combining both, we propose the
Conditional Divergence Matching
(CDM) objective, which bypasses the divergence operator:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CDM}}(\theta;t)=\mathbb{E}_{t,z,x_{t}}\Bigl[\bigl|D_{\theta}(x_{t},t,t)-v_{t}(x_{t})\cdot\nabla\log p_{t}(x_{t}|\xi)-\partial_{t}\log p_{t}(x_{t}|\xi)\bigr|^{2}\Bigr]

(16)

where
\xi
is a conditioning variable such that conditional time and space scores are available in closed-form. For example, when using the interpolation
x_{t}=(1-t)x_{0}+tx_{1}
, then conditioning on
\xi=x_{1}
leaves only one source of randomness in
x_{0}
, which is Gaussian. It follows that
p_{t}(x_{t}|\xi)
becomes tractable, and plugging it into the above yields the following proposition:

Proposition 2

(Conditional Divergence Matching)

Let
(p_{t})_{t}
be the laws of
x_{t}=(1-t)x_{0}+tx_{1}
, where
x_{0}\sim p_{0}=\mathcal{N}(0,I)
and
x_{1}\sim p_{1}
.
Let
(v_{t})_{t}
be the velocity field that generates
(p_{t})_{t}
, and
D_{\theta}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1]^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
the parameterized divergence predictor.
The Conditional Divergence Matching objective

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CDM}}(\theta;t)=\mathbb{E}_{x_{0},x_{1}}\Bigl[\bigl|D_{\theta}(x_{t},t,t)+\frac{x_{0}}{1-t}\cdot\bigl(v_{t}(x_{t})-(x_{1}-x_{0})\bigr)-\frac{1}{1-t}d\bigr|^{2}\Bigr]

(17)

equals to the Divergence Matching objective
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DM}}(\theta;t)
up to a constant independent of
\theta
, for all
t\in[0,1]
. Hence,
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CDM}}(\theta;t)=\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DM}}(\theta;t)
.

Remark 3

The CDM objective can also be derived by first expressing the velocity as the posterior expectation over the conditional velocity, and then manipulating the divergence operator to obtain the divergence-operation-free training objective. Details are provided in
Section

6.4
.

Remark 4

While the velocity
v_{t}
and the Stein score
\nabla\log p_{t}
are connected by
Equation

4
when
p_{0}=\mathcal{N}(0,I)
and
x_{t}=(1-t)x_{0}+tx_{1}
, the mapping between the two becomes nontrivial for the general Stochastic Interpolants cases, as stated in
Equation

28
. Hence, we stick to treating
\nabla\log p_{t}
as intractable. This can introduce additional variance to the training objective in the
\mathcal{N}(0,I)
base cases. However, we found that it almost does not degrade performance in practice
.

3.4
Vectorized Divergence Matching

Because the divergence and log-density are scalar quantities, learning to predict them directly is more data-intensive than learning from a vector signal.
To regress vectors instead of scalars, we propose the following vectorized variants of the divergence matching objectives: (1)
Vectorized Divergence Matching
(DM-v), (2)
Vectorized Hutchinson Divergence Matching
(HDM-v), and (3)
Vectorized Conditional Divergence Matching
(CDM-v). In particular, the effective training target changes from the negative divergence
-\nabla\cdot v_{t}
to the negative diagonal entries of the Jacobian
-\mathrm{diag}(\nabla v_{t})
. For notational clarity, we use the symbolic notation
\nabla\odot v_{t}
to denote
\mathrm{diag}(\nabla v_{t})
. In particular, the divergence predictor is now parameterized to output a vector, i.e.
D_{\theta}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1]^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DM\text{-}v}}(\theta;t)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x_{0},x_{1}}\left[\left\|D_{\theta}(x_{t},t,t)+\nabla\odot v_{t}(x_{t})\right\|^{2}\right],

(18)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{HDM\text{-}v}}(\theta;t)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x_{0},x_{1},\epsilon}\left[\left\|D_{\theta}(x_{t},t,t)+\epsilon\odot\nabla v_{t}(x_{t})\epsilon\right\|^{2}\right],

(19)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CDM\text{-}v}}(\theta;t)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x_{0},x_{1}}\biggl[\Bigl\|D_{\theta}(x_{t},t,t)+\frac{x_{0}}{1-t}\odot\bigl(v_{t}(x_{t})-(x_{1}-x_{0})\bigr)-\frac{1}{1-t}\mathbf{1}_{d}\Bigr\|^{2}\biggr].

(20)

Proposition 5

(Vectorized Divergence Matching)

Let
(v_{t})_{t}
and
(p_{t})_{t}
be defined as in
Proposition

2
, and
D_{\theta}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1]^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the parameterized
vectorized
divergence predictor.
The vectorized learning objectives defined in
Equations

18
,
19
and
20
are related as

\displaystyle\arg\min_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{HDM\text{-}v}}(\theta;t)

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DM\text{-}v}}(\theta;t)\quad\text{and}\quad\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CDM\text{-}v}}(\theta;t)=\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DM\text{-}v}}(\theta;t),

(21)

for all
t\in[0,1]
. In particular, the optimal
\theta^{*}=\arg\min_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DM\text{-}v}}(\theta;t)
recovers

\displaystyle D_{\theta^{*}}(x_{t},t,t)

\displaystyle=\nabla\odot v_{t}(x_{t})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{1}_{d}\cdot D_{\theta^{*}}(x_{t},t,t)=\nabla\cdot v_{t}(x_{t}).

(22)

The same vectorization technique is also used by
Ou et al. 2025
for learning the covariance of the denoising kernel in Diffusion models, and
Yu et al. 2025
for learning the time score.

Connection to Second-Order Score Matching.

Our CDM-v objective is related to the second-order Score Matching objective
(
Meng et al. 2021
)
. To understand this connection recall the relationship between the velocity and score described in
Equation

4

\displaystyle v_{t}(x_{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{x_{t}}{t}+\frac{1-t}{t}\nabla\log p_{t}(x_{t})\Longrightarrow\nabla v_{t}(x_{t})=\frac{I}{t}+\frac{1-t}{t}\nabla^{2}\log p_{t}(x_{t}).

(23)

With access to
\nabla\log p_{t}
,
\nabla^{2}\log p_{t}
can be learned with a conditional loss
(
Meng et al. 2021
)
.
Hence, learning the diagonal entries of
\nabla^{2}\log p_{t}(x_{t})
only is equivalent to our vectorized conditional divergence matching up to an independent constant.
We leave the detailed background and discussion in
Section

6.7
.

3.5
SCALLOP

Combing the training objectives from
Section

3.2
with our
Conditional Divergence Matching
objective (
Section

3.3
) and the vectorization technique (
Section

3.4
), we propose
SCALLOP
, a scalable and efficient pipeline for training a likelihood flow map. The complete training objective is written in
Equation

24
.
Again, we consider a distillation setting following
Ai et al. 2026
, where a pretrained velocity field
v_{t}^{\phi}\approx v_{t}
is available. When no ambiguity arises, we denote
v_{t}
as
v_{t}^{\phi}
for notational simplicity. We focus on modelling molecular systems, where specific symmetries must be respected.
Related works are discussed in
Section

1
and expanded on in
Section

7
.

Parameterization.

We stick to using the vectorized objective, CDM-v, to learn the log-density-change part in likelihood flow map. Therefore, we parameterize
\Psi_{\theta}(x_{t},t,s)=[x_{t};0]+(t-s)f_{\theta}(x_{t},t,s)
, where

f_{\theta}(x_{t},t,s)=[u_{\theta}(x_{t},t,s);D_{\theta}(x_{t},t,s)]
,

u_{\theta},D_{\theta}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1]^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

x_{t}+(s-t)u_{\theta}(x_{t},t,s)
gives the generated samples, and
(s-t)D_{\theta}(x_{t},t,s)
gives the vectorized log-density-change. We employ shared encoding layers for feature extraction, while specifying two readout-heads for the samples and log-density-change respectively, analogous to
Ai et al. 2026
. As a byproduct of the vectorization trick, we employ the same architecture for these two readout-heads. In particular, we follow
Rehman et al. 2026
to use the standard Diffusion Transformer block
(
Peebles and Xie 2023
, DiT,)
with an additional time embedding head to adapt to the flow map setting. We use 6 shared DiT blocks, and 3 DiT blocks for each of the sample readout-head and log-density-change readout head.

Enforcing Soft Symmetries for Molecular Systems.

The generated samples should be equivariant to rotation and translation of the input, namely SE(3)-equivariant, and the predicted log-density-change should be SE(3)-invariant. Following
Tan et al. 2026a
;
Rehman et al. 2026
, we subtract the mean of the data to enforce translational equivariance, while employing random rotation to softly impose equivariance of the rotations. After training, both
u_{\theta}
and
D_{\theta}
are SE(3)-equivariant. And again, by summing over the elements of
D_{\theta}
, the predicted log-density-change is SE(3)-invariant.

Learning Objective and Stabilizing Training.

Following
Boffi et al. 2025
;
Ai et al. 2026
, we use learned weights
\exp(-w_\psi(s, t))
as
w_{\mathrm{SD}}(s,t)
and
\exp(-w_\psi(t, t))
as
w_{v}(t)
, where
\psi
denotes a shallow scalar-valued neural network. However, for the divergence matching part, the objective has high variance when
t\rightarrow 1
as studied by
Song et al. 2021
;
Yu et al. 2025
as well as a singularity at
t=1
. To stabilize training, we uniformly sample
t
from
[0,1-\mathrm{eps}]
with
\mathrm{eps}=10^{-5}
, as well as employ a linear weighting
w_{\psi}(t,t)(1-t)
for the divergence matching part. For the self distillation part,
(s,t)
is sampled uniformly on the upper triangle
t<s
of the square
[0,1-\mathrm{eps}]^{2}
. Overall, the practical training objective reads

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{\hbox{$\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{}{}{{\hbox{{\includegraphics[height]{figs/scallop-yellow.png}}}}}}}$}\penalty\ \texttt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}SCALLOP}}}(\theta)=\int_{0}^{1}\Bigl[e^{-w_{\psi}(t,t)}\bigl(\mathcal{L}_{v}(\theta;t)+(1-t)\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CDM\text{-}v}}(\theta;t)\bigr)+w_{\psi}(t,t)\Bigr]\differential t\\
+\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{s}\Bigl[e^{-w_{\psi}(t,s)}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SD}}(\theta;t,s)+w_{\psi}(t,s)\Bigr]\differential t\differential s.

(24)

Fast Generation and Likelihood Evaluation.

Given a discrete time schedule
0=t_{0}<...<t_{K}=1
, inference in
SCALLOP
is analogous to that in a standard flow map, with a small amount of additional overhead due to calling the log-density-change readout-head. In particular, we start by sampling
x_{0}\sim p_{0}
and evaluate
\log p_{0}(x_{0})
, then we iteratively update
x_{t_{i+1}}=x_{t_{i}}+(t_{i+1}-t_{i})u_{\theta}(x_{t_{i}},t_{i},t_{i+1})
and
\log p_{t_{i+1}}(x_{t_{i+1}})=\log p_{t_{i}}(x_{t_{i}})+(t_{i+1}-t_{i})D_{\theta}(x_{t_{i}},t_{i},t_{i+1})
, see
Algorithm

1
for details. It is notable that, unlike methods with approximate invertibility
(
Draxler et al. 2024
;
Rehman et al. 2026
)
,
SCALLOP
does not require calculating or estimating the log-determinant of the Jacobian of the generator for likelihood evaluation, but a single forward pass of the neural network.
Overall, the few-steps and high-order-free nature of
SCALLOP
enables efficient data generation alongside their density evaluations.

We optimize

Molecular Systems.

We conduct experiments on several alanine systems of varying lengths, including Alanine Dipeptide (ALA-2), Alanine Tripeptide (ALA-3), Alanine Tetrapeptide (ALA-4), and Alanine Hexapeptide (ALA-6). Data are obtained from simulating Molecular Dynamics in implicit solvent, with the AMBER-14 force field. Details of the simulation are provided in

.
Following
Tan et al. 2026a
;
Rehman et al. 2026
, models are trained on the biased dataset. The goal is to correct the model using self-normalized importance sampling, enabling it to sample equilibrium conformations corresponding to the Boltzmann distribution.

Baselines.

We benchmark
SCALLOP
against the most promising discrete- and continuous- flow baselines identified by
Rehman et al. 2026
: SBG
(
Tan et al. 2026a
)
and FALCON
(
Rehman et al. 2026
)
. Specifically, to better focus on likelihood evaluation, we compare against the SBG with standard SNIS (denoted as SBG-IS). Although an SMC-based variant (SBG-SMC) exists and can be seamlessly integrated with both FALCON and
SCALLOP
,
we exclude it from our current benchmark to ensure fair comparison with SNIS, leaving the exploration of
SCALLOP
-SMC for future work.

Metrics.

We evaluate
SCALLOP
and the baselines using standard SNIS. To measure sampling efficiency, we report the Effective Sample Size (ESS). To quantify the effectiveness of the reweighted samples, we compute the 2-Wasserstein distance for both the distributions of energy (
\mathcal{E}\text{-}\mathcal{W}_{2}
) and torsional angle (
\mathbb{T}\text{-}\mathcal{W}_{2}
). Specifically,
\mathcal{E}\text{-}\mathcal{W}_{2}
is sensitive to extreme energy values—often caused by atoms being too close to one another—and thus reflects local structural refinements. Conversely, because torsional angles act as the primary collective variables capturing the most information in alanine systems,
\mathbb{T}\text{-}\mathcal{W}_{2}
measures global structural quality. Further details are provided in Appendix
8
.

SCALLOP as a Boltzmann Generator

Comparable Performance against SOTA.

The performance of
SCALLOP
and the other baselines are reported in
Table

. Across different systems,
SCALLOP
consistently outperforms F2D2 across all considered metrics, while achieving highly competitive results against the state-of-the-art FALCON and FALCON-A. Specifically,
SCALLOP
excels at capturing the global conformational space, achieving the best
\mathbb{T}\text{-}\mathcal{W}_{2}
on the ALA-3 and ALA-6 systems. Furthermore, it consistently yields a high ESS, indicating substantial overlap with the target distribution and a strong resistance to mode collapse—a conclusion further corroborated by its strong
\mathbb{T}\text{-}\mathcal{W}_{2}
performance.

While FALCON generally achieves a lower
\mathcal{E}\text{-}\mathcal{W}_{2}
,
SCALLOP
drastically improves upon F2D2, nearly halving the
\mathcal{E}\text{-}\mathcal{W}_{2}
error in larger systems like ALA-4 and ALA-6. More importantly, higher
\mathcal{E}\text{-}\mathcal{W}_{2}
values do not undermine the overall validity of the drawn samples, as the Wasserstein-2 distance is notoriously sensitive to energy outliers caused by microscopic structural deviations. The low
\mathbb{T}\text{-}\mathcal{W}_{2}
demonstrates that
SCALLOP
correctly captures the global torsional angle structure, confirming that the macroscopic metastable states and their statistical weights are accurately recovered. Because the underlying conformational macrostates are correct, the remaining local structural noise that inflates the
\mathcal{E}\text{-}\mathcal{W}_{2}
can be efficiently mitigated by applying a computationally cheap local relaxation to align the local structures.

5
Discussion

In conclusion, we proposed
SCAlable LikeLihood distillation of flOw maPs
(

SCALLOP
), an efficient procedure for training few-step models to jointly generate samples and their densities. This capability allows
SCALLOP
to serve as a Boltzmann generator for sampling from unnormalized densities, where it is competitive with the state-of-the-art while being significantly faster to evaluate at inference time.

Limitations.

Despite its empirical success,
SCALLOP
has two main limitations. First, the conditional divergence matching objective relies on the assumption that the velocity is perfectly learnt,
while the theoretical gap and resulting bias
currently remain unquantified.
Second, while
SCALLOP
can generate samples and their densities in
1
step, it practically uses
\sim 10
steps for high-dimensional tasks.

\beginappendix

Notation and Terminology.

Throughout this appendix, we operate within the framework of generative modeling via continuous-time paths. Here,
x_{0}
and
x_{1}
denote the endpoint data variables,
x_{t}
represents the intermediate state at time
t\in[0,1]
, and
z
is the latent noise variable driving the process. Unless otherwise specified, conditional expectations of the form
\mathbb{E}[\cdot|x_{t}]
are taken over the joint posterior distribution
p(x_{0},x_{1}|x_{t})
of the endpoints given the intermediate state. Because the noise
z
is uniquely determined once
(x_{0},x_{1},x_{t})
are known, expectations involving the noise (such as
\mathbb{E}[z|x_{t}]
) are implicitly evaluated under this same posterior.

Furthermore, we adopt a slight abuse of terminology for convenience: we frequently refer to analytical formulas containing exact population expectations as “estimators.” Strictly speaking, these mathematical expressions represent theoretical population limits. In practice, it is understood that these exact expectations are substituted with their corresponding unbiased finite-sample estimators via empirical Monte Carlo averages over mini-batches during training.

Conditional Estimators for Flow-based Model’s Likelihood: Proofs and Connections

In this section, we provide conditional estimators for estimating the model density, i.e. likelihoods, of samples generated by the following ODE in

Remark 7

While the velocity field generating a given marginal probability path

and applying Tweedie’s formula to the Gaussian noise term, we can express the marginal velocity as:

\displaystyle v_{t}^{*}(x_{t})=f_{t}(x_{t})-\dot{\gamma}_{t}\gamma_{t}\nabla\log p_{t}(x_{t}).

(30)

(x_{u})_{u\in[0,t]}

25

6.1
Desiderata for a Consistent Objective

We would like to obtain an estimator for
\differential_{t}\log p_{t}(x_{t})
using the marginalization trick
(
Lipman et al. 2023
)
, so as to avoid forming the objective using gradients. We rely on the following identity
(
Tong et al. 2024
;
Yu et al. 2025
)

Theorem 8

Consider two functions,
f_{t}(x_{t}|\xi)
and
g_{t}(x_{t})

1
1

1

By a slight abuse of notation, we write
f_{t}(x_{t}|\xi)
instead of
f_{t}(x_{t},\xi)
for convenience.

, satisfying the property that
g_{t}(x_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(\xi|x_{t})}\left[f_{t}(x_{t}|\xi)\right]
. The two loss functions

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{f}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p(t,\xi,x_{t})}\left[\lambda(t)\norm{ f_{t}(x_{t}|\xi) - s_{\theta}(\xi,t) }^{2}\right],

(31)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{g}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p(t,x_{t})}\left[\lambda(t)\norm{g_{t}(x_{t}) - s_{\theta}(x_{t}, t) }^{2}\right],

(32)

have the same gradient with respect to
\theta
.

The proof can be found in
Yu et al. 2025
. We provide a proof here for completeness.

Proof.

Note that

\displaystyle g_{t}(x_{t})=\mathbb{E}\left[f_{t}(x_{t}|\xi)\big|x_{t}=x_{t}\right]=\int\frac{p_{t}(x|\xi)p(\xi)}{p_{t}(x)}f(x,t|\xi)\differential\xi.

(33)

We first express the gradients of the two loss functions,

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{f}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{p(t),p(\xi),p_{t}(x_{t}|\xi)}\left[\lambda(t)\norm{ f_{t}(x_{t}|\xi) - s_{\theta}(x,t) }^{2}\right]

(34)

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{p(t),p(\xi),p_{t}(x_{t}|\xi)}\left[\lambda(t)\left(\norm{ f_{t}(x_{t}|\xi) }^{2}-2\langle f_{t}(x_{t}|\xi),s_{\theta}(x_{t},t)\rangle+\norm{s_{\theta}(x_{t},t)}^{2}\right)\right]

(35)

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{p(t),p(\xi),p_{t}(x_{t}|\xi)}\left[\lambda(t)\left(\norm{s_{\theta}(x_{t},t)}^{2}-2\langle f_{t}(x_{t}|\xi),s_{\theta}(x_{t},t)\rangle\right)\right],

(36)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{g}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{p(t),p_{t}(x_{t})}\left[\lambda(t)\norm{ g_{t}(x_{t}) - s_{\theta}(x_{t},t) }^{2}\right]

(37)

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{p(t),p_{t}(x_{t})}\left[\lambda(t)\left(\norm{ g_{t}(x_{t}) }^{2}-2\langle g_{t}(x_{t}),s_{\theta}(x_{t},t)\rangle+\norm{s_{\theta}(x_{t},t)}^{2}\right)\right]

(38)

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{p(t),p_{t}(x_{t})}\left[\lambda(t)\left(\norm{s_{\theta}(x_{t},t)}^{2}-2\langle g_{t}(x_{t}),s_{\theta}(x_{t},t)\rangle\right)\right].

(39)

Observe that these two expressions are the same when

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(x_{t})}\norm{s_{\theta}(x_{t},t)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p(\xi)p_{t}(x_{t}|\xi)}\norm{s_{\theta}(x_{t},t)^{2}}

(40)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(x_{t})}\langle g_{t}(x_{t}),s_{\theta}(x_{t},t)\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p(\xi),p_{t}(x_{t}|\xi)}\langle f_{t}(x_{t}|\xi),s_{\theta}(x_{t},t)\rangle.

(41)

The first equality trivially holds. For the second, observe that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(x_{t})}\langle g_{t}(x_{t}),s_{\theta}(x_{t},t)\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(x_{t})}\left\langle\int\frac{p_{t}(x_{t}|\xi)p(\xi)}{p_{t}(x_{t})}f_{t}(x_{t}|\xi)\differential\xi,s_{\theta}(x,t)\right\rangle

(42)

\displaystyle=\int p_{t}(x_{t})\left\langle\int\frac{p_{t}(x_{t}|\xi)p(\xi)}{p_{t}(x_{t})}f_{t}(x_{t}|\xi)\differential\xi,s_{\theta}(x_{t},t)\right\rangle\differential x

(43)

\displaystyle=\int\left\langle\int p_{t}(x_{t}|\xi)p(\xi)f_{t}(x_{t}|\xi)\differential\xi,s_{\theta}(x,t)\right\rangle\differential x

(44)

\displaystyle=\int\int\langle f_{t}(x_{t}|\xi),s_{\theta}(x_{t},t)\rangle p_{t}(x|\xi)p(\xi)\differential\xi\differential x

(45)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p(\xi),p_{t}(x_{t}|\xi)}\langle f_{t}(x_{t}|\xi),s_{\theta}(x_{t},t)\rangle.

(46)

As such,
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{f}(\theta)=\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{g}(\theta)
.

∎

Leveraging this theorem, establishing a consistent learning objective for
\differential_{t}\log p_{t}(x)
simply requires constructing a tractable conditional estimator. Specifically, we need a function
f_{t}(x_{t}|\xi)
whose conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}_{p(\xi|x_{t})}[f_{t}(x_{t}|\xi)]
recovers the target marginal quantity. This can be achieved either by designing an estimator whose expectation strictly matches
\differential_{t}\log p_{t}(x)
, or by formulating a combination of component estimators whose expectations sum to the desired target.

6.2
Derivation through Total Derivative

For clarity, we define the following notation throughout the paper:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\tilde{v}_{t}(x_{t}|x_{0},x_{1}):

\displaystyle=\dot{\alpha}_{t}x_{1}+\dot{\beta}_{t}x_{0}+\dot{\gamma}_{t}z,\quad{\text{where }}z=\frac{x_{t}-(\alpha_{t}x_{1}+\beta_{t}x_{0})}{\gamma_{t}},

(47)

and
v_{t}(x_{t})=\mathbb{E}[\tilde{v}_{t}(x_{t}|x_{0},x_{1})|x_{t}]
.

To estimate
\differential_{t}\log p_{t}(x_{t})
, one could expand it through total derivative

\displaystyle\differential_{t}\log p_{t}(x_{t})

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\log p_{t}(x_{t})+\nabla\log p_{t}(x_{t})\cdot\differential_{t}x_{t}

(48)

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\log p_{t}(x_{t})+\nabla\log p_{t}(x_{t})\cdot v_{t}(x_{t}),

(49)

where both
\nabla\log p_{t}(x_{t})
and
\partial_{t}\log p_{t}(x_{t})
can be expressed as conditional expectations
(
Song et al. 2021
;
Yu et al. 2025
)
, respectively, as follows
:

\displaystyle\nabla\log p_{t}(x_{t})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\nabla_{x_{t}}\log p(x_{t}|x_{0},x_{1})|x_{t}\bigr]

(50)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\gamma_{t}}\mathbb{E}\bigl[z|x_{t}=x_{t}\bigr],

(51)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\log p_{t}(x_{t})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\partial_{t}\log p(x_{t}|x_{0},x_{1})|x_{t}\bigr]

(52)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\gamma_{t}}\mathbb{E}\biggl[-d{\dot{\gamma_{t}}}+z\cdot\left(\dot{\alpha}_{t}x_{1}+\dot{\beta}_{t}x_{0}+{\dot{\gamma_{t}}}z\right)\Big|x_{t}\biggr]

(53)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\gamma_{t}}\mathbb{E}\biggl[-d{\dot{\gamma_{t}}}+z\cdot\tilde{v}_{t}(x_{t}|x_{0},x_{1})\Big|x_{t}\biggr].

(54)

Therefore, the total derivative can be expressed as a conditional expectation

\displaystyle\differential_{t}\log p_{t}(x_{t})

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}\biggl[\frac{z}{\gamma_{t}}\cdot\Bigl(v_{t}(x_{t})-\tilde{v}_{t}(x_{t}|x_{0},x_{1})\Bigr)+\frac{\dot{\gamma}_{t}}{\gamma_{t}}d\Big|x_{t}\biggr],

(55)

which can be learned efficiently without any high-order derivatives once having access to
v_{t}
.

On the other hand, we could then expand the Jacobian of the velocity,

\displaystyle\nabla v_{t}(x_{t})

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\Bigl(\tilde{J}_{v}(x_{t}|x_{0},x_{1})\Bigr)

(65)

\displaystyle\mathrm{diag}\bigl(\nabla^{2}\log p_{t}(x_{t})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\gamma_{t}^{4}}\Bigl(\mathbb{E}[\alpha_{t}^{2}\odot^{2}x_{1}|x_{t}]-\gamma_{t}^{2}\mathbf{1}_{d}-\odot^{2}x_{t}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\alpha_{t}^{2}\odot^{2}x_{1}|x_{t}]

(86)

\displaystyle=\odot^{2}x_{t}-2\gamma_{t}x_{t}\odot\mathbb{E}[z|x_{t}]+\gamma_{t}^{2}\mathbb{E}[\odot^{2}z|x_{t}]

(87)

\displaystyle\overset{(i)}{=}\odot^{2}x_{t}+2\gamma_{t}^{2}x_{t}\odot\nabla\log p_{t}(x_{t})+\gamma_{t}^{2}\mathbb{E}[\odot^{2}z|x_{t}],

(88)

where
(i)
is by Tweedie’s formula. The diagonal of hessian can be simplified as

(89)

Further notice that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\biggl[\odot^{2}\biggl(\frac{1}{\gamma_{t}}z\biggr)\Big|x_{t}\biggr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\biggl[\odot^{2}\biggl(\frac{1}{\gamma_{t}}z+\nabla\log p_{t}(x_{t})\biggr)\Big|x_{t}\biggr]

(90)

\displaystyle\overset{(i)}{=}\mathbb{E}\biggl[\odot^{2}\biggl(\frac{1}{\gamma_{t}}z+\nabla\log p_{t}(x_{t})\biggr)\Big|x_{t}\biggr]+\odot^{2}\nabla\log p_{t}(x_{t}),

(91)

where
(i)
is again by Tweedie’s formula. We have the simplification

\displaystyle\mathrm{diag}\bigl(\nabla^{2}\log p_{t}(x_{t})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\biggl[\odot^{2}\biggl(\frac{1}{\gamma_{t}}z+\nabla\log p_{t}(x_{t})\biggr)\Big|x_{t}\biggr]-\frac{1}{\gamma_{t}^{2}}\mathbf{1}_{d}

(93)

\displaystyle=\frac{\dot{\alpha}_{t}}{\alpha_{t}}\mathbf{1}_{d}+c_{t}\gamma_{t}^{2}\mathbb{E}\biggl[\frac{z}{\gamma_{t}}\odot\biggl(\frac{z}{\gamma_{t}}+\nabla\log p_{t}(x_{t})\biggr)\Big|x_{t}\biggr]-c_{t}\mathbf{1}_{d}

(94)

\displaystyle\overset{(i)}{=}\mathbb{E}\biggl[\frac{z}{\gamma_{t}}\odot\biggl(c_{t}\gamma_{t}z+v_{t}(x_{t})-\frac{\dot{\alpha}_{t}}{\alpha_{t}}x_{t}\biggr)\Big|x_{t}\biggr]+\frac{\dot{\gamma}_{t}}{\gamma_{t}}\mathbf{1}_{d}

(95)

where
(i)

(ii)

(97)

Remarkbly, it recovers the estimator derived from the conditional total derivative,

Expanded Discussion of Related Works

The following are three dominant approaches used in computational chemistry for training neural networks that support efficient sampling
and
likelihood.
Once such neural networks are trained, they can be used as a
proposal
distribution, known in computational chemistry as a Boltzmann generator, for self-normalized importance sampling of a Boltzmann distribution.

Discrete and Continuous Normalizing Flows

Boltzmann Generators (BGs) are tractable proposal distributions used in importance sampling, allowing both efficient sampling and likelihood evaluation. They were first applied to molecular systems by
Noé et al. 2019
, who parameterized them using neural networks in the form of Normalizing Flows (NFs).
Subsequent work has evolved along two main directions. The first replaces NFs with their continuous counterpart, Continuous Normalizing Flows
(
Chen et al. 2019
, CNFs)
that can be parameterized using more flexible model architectures. For instance,
Köhler et al. 2020
;
Klein et al. 2023
develop equivariant CNFs tailored to molecular systems. However, this approach does not scale well: computing the log-likelihood requires integrating the divergence of the velocity field (as in Eq.
3
), which becomes prohibitively expensive in high dimensions.
The second direction instead focuses on improving the expressivity and sampling efficiency of standard NFs. Recent work, such as TarFlow
(
Zhai et al. 2025
)
, introduces scalable transformer-based architectures, achieving state-of-the-art performance. Building on this line,
Tan et al. 2026a
;
Tan et al. 2026b
;
Schebek et al. 2025
pushed the boundaries of NFs for atomic systems. In particular,
Tan et al. 2026a
also proposes an inference-time scaling strategy to further improve sampling efficiency.

Energy-based Diffusion Models.

Training diffusion models with energy parameterization acts a bridge to alleviate the computational overhead of solving the divergence integration in CNFs, where the learned energies define Boltzmann distributions on different noise levels. Recent works focus on improving energy-based diffusion models training beyond Denoising Score Matching.
Aggarwal et al. 2025
;
OuYang et al. 2026a
incorporate Noise-Contrastive Estimation
(
Gutmann and Hyvärinen 2012
)
, where
Aggarwal et al. 2025
treats the learned energy of clean data as the approximated normalized likelihood and
OuYang et al. 2026a
leverages the learned intermediate energies for further inference-time control.
He et al. 2026
improves the training via imposing the Bayes’ rule constraint, with an application of the EBMs for free energy estimation.
Plainer et al. 2026
regularizes the EBMs to enforce the Fokker-Planck Equation, and uses the learned EBMs as a force field.
Yu et al. 2026
proposes a unified framework for learning EBMs. In contrast to the standard data-driven setting,
OuYang et al. 2026b
proposes an energy matching objective for learning a Boltzmann neural sampler when data is unavailable, relying instead on the unnormalized target density.

Flow Maps with Approximate Invertibility.

NFs enables exact likelihood evaluation through its invertibility, which also limits its flexibility. To enable the usage of more flexible network architecture, Free-form Flows
(
Draxler et al. 2024
)
propose to softly enforce the invertibility through an additional reconstruction loss. To incorporate with the recent advanced few-steps generative models, FALCON
(
Rehman et al. 2026
)
proposes to make flow map invertible, by additionally imposing reconstruction losses on the jump between two times
t<s
. FALCON enables flow map to have fast yet accurate likelihood estimation and shows its effectiveness and scalability on molecular systems. However, FALCON requires the exact log-determinant of the Jacobian of the flow map for likelihood evaluation; over
T
steps, this is computationally expensive, resulting in
\mathcal{O}(Td)
function evaluations and an additional
\mathcal{O}(Td^{3})
cost.

8.4
Details of Metrics

Effective Sample Size.

ESS
(
Kish 1957
)
quantifies the resampling efficiency of SNIS. Given
N
generated samples
x_{1:N}
with associated unnormalized importance weights
w_{1:N}
, the ESS is defined as:

\displaystyle\mathrm{ESS}(w_{1:N})=\frac{(\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_{n})^{2}}{N\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_{n}^{2}}\in[0,1],

(99)

where a higher ESS generally reflects a better performance of the SNIS. Therefore, ESS can be treated as a proxy of evaluating how accurate the estimated log densities of samples are, given that the model can generate high quality samples.

2-Wasserstein Distance.

The 2-Wasserstain distance (
\mathcal{W}_{2}
) is a measure of difference between two probability distributions
p
and
q
defined on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
defined as follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{2}(p,q)^{2}=\min_{\pi\in\Pi(p,q)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}}c(x,y)\differential\pi(x,y),

(100)

where
\Pi
is the set of admissible couplings and
c:\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the cost function.

•

2-Wasserstein Distance of energy (
\mathcal{E}\text{-}\mathcal{W}_{2}
) measures the energy histogram difference between generated samples and reference ones, which admits
m=1
and uses
c(x,y)=|x-y|
.

•

2-Wasserstein Distance of torus (
\mathbb{T}\text{-}\mathcal{W}_{2}
) measures the torsional-angle histogram difference between generated samples and reference ones. For a ALA-N system, there are
2(N-1)
torsional angles in total. Let
\mathrm{Deheral}(x)=(\phi_{1},\psi_{1},...,\phi_{N-1},\psi_{N-1})\in[-\pi,\pi]^{2(N-1)}
be the torsional angles of a sample
x
.
To account for the fact that these torsional angles live in a torus
[-\pi,\pi]
, we use:

\displaystyle c(x,y)=\sum_{i=1}^{2(N-1)}\left[(\mathrm{Dehedral}(x)_{i}-\mathrm{Dehedral}(y)_{i}+\pi)\mathrm{mod}2\pi-\pi\right]^{2}

(101)

as the cost function.

LSD Model Training.
For the vanilla LSD baseline, we follow the experimental setup of
Boffi et al. 2026
. We adopt the batch-allocation strategy used in
Ai et al. 2026
: during the first 200k iterations, 75% of each batch is used for the flow-matching loss and the remaining 25% is used for the self-distillation loss; during the subsequent 150k iterations, the allocation is changed to 50% for flow matching and 50% for self-distillation. For the self-distillation objective, we sample two time steps independently and uniformly from
[0,1]
, without imposing an ordering constraint between them. Equivalently, the full time-step pair is sampled as
(t,s)\sim\mathcal{U}([0,1]^{2})
, which allows the model to learn both forward and backward estimators.

SCALLOP Training.
For
SCALLOP
, we follow the same image-generation training setup as
Ai et al. 2026
and initialize the model from the pretrained LSD model, adding only the divergence-prediction head. We train the model for 60k iterations with a initial learning rate of
10^{-4}
. At each iteration, 50% of the batch is used for the flow-matching loss, while the remaining 50% is used for the self-distillation loss. The vectorized divergence head is implemented on the decoder of the image UNet: instead of producing a single scalar divergence estimate, it applies a
1{\times}1
convolution to predict a spatially resolved divergence field with the same shape as the noisy image state, namely
(B,C,H,W)
.

FID Evaluation.
We compute FID using 50k images generated by each model.

BPD Evaluation.
We compute bits per dimension (BPD) on the full CelebA-64 test set, following the procedure of
Lipman et al. 2023
. For numerical integration, we discretize the reverse-time interval from

to
