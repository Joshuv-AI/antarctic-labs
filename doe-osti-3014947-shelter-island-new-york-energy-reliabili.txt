 Executive Summary
 Shelter Island is in the Peconic Bay between the North and South Forks at the eastern end of
 Long Island and is only accessible by ferry. The community faces energy reliability challenges
 due to its isolation and limited connectivity to the mainland.

 Shelter Island’s Green Options Committee (GOC), an all-volunteer committee tasked with
 considering all environmental conservation issues for the island, requested technical assistance
 under the U.S. Department of Energy-funded Energy Technology Innovation Partnership Project
 (ETIPP). The primary goals of this technical assistance project included:

 1. Informing the GOC about energy use trends within the community, as well as distributed
 solar, wind, and energy storage opportunities.
 2. Providing additional resources and technical feasibility information for geothermal,
 agrivoltaics, and tidal energy.
 3. Supporting the development of a community engagement plan.
 4. Supporting collaboration between the GOC and the local electrical utility Public Service
 Enterprise Group Long Island (PSEGLI) to find mutually beneficial follow-on projects.
 Assistance from the National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR) to help the GOC meet these
 overall project goals was provided through the following primary tasks:

 1. Collaborate with the local utility to collect energy use data and inform the community on
 energy use patterns through a baseline assessment.
 2. Provide technical analysis of distributed local energy generation and reliability
 improvement opportunities (solar, wind, and storage) that best align with the
 community’s energy priorities.
 3. Provide high-level feasibility support for future local energy generation scenarios that
 include agrivoltaic solutions, tidal energy, and geothermal projects on Shelter Island.
 4. Integrate all findings into a community outreach presentation to help the GOC engage
 with community stakeholders to build support and awareness of chosen strategies to
 improve reliability and responsiveness to outages.
 5. Summarize all project work and key findings in a final report.

 Baseline Assessment
 To understand Shelter Island’s existing energy use, the team first completed a baseline
 assessment that revealed Shelter Island’s electricity was primarily generated from fossil-fuel-
 fired power plants, with the residential sector accounting for most (88.5%) of the island’s annual
 electricity consumption. Additionally, summer usage is nearly two times that of winter, likely
 due to the seasonal influx of residents and tourism (Figure ES-1).

 Given that the vast majority of Long Islanders—nearly 90%—heat their homes with fossil fuels,
 PSEGLI does a lot of work on electrification with a push towards heat pumps. However, to date,
 only a small percentage of homeowners have installed them. PSEGLI has also focused on
 increasing energy reliability on Shelter Island by providing dedicated crews on-site during
 storms to expedite electricity restoration. Outage data provided by PSEGLI show that over the
 past five years, Shelter Island averaged one to four outages per year ranging in duration from 0.6
 to 3.7 hours, depending on the circuit.

 Techno-Economic Analysis of Distributed Local Energy Generation
 and Reliability Opportunities
 The Shelter Island project team selected eight sites (e.g., municipal, commercial, and residential)
 on Shelter Island to analyze the cost-effectiveness of behind-the-meter solar and battery storage
 systems, as well as how the systems could improve reliability during power outages. Payback
 periods between 7 and 11 years were found across all sites, indicating a potentially significant
 opportunity for cost savings (Table ES-1). Residential buildings, which comprise the bulk of the
 island load, provide the best case for solar PV deployment. Battery storage with solar is also
 cost-effective, depending on the PSEGLI rate structure chosen.

 One of the main conclusions, in terms of reliability, is that the optimal battery size needed in
 order to provide 3 versus 14 days of reliability is very similar, highlighting that the battery
 energy storage can be charged using the PV system throughout an outage, depending on critical
 loads and solar resource over the duration of the outage. Additionally, the school was found to be
 a good option for providing energy reliability as an emergency shelter—the system is also
 economically favorable for the reliability scenario (up to 14 days). The analysis indicates that a
 large solar and battery storage system, which would be able to accommodate emergency loads, is
 cost-optimal to meet the school’s large electric load and, given the large footprint (when
 compared to other analyzed buildings), the facility could be an ideal site to serve as an
 emergency shelter. The batteries can also be operated for cost reduction during most of the year
 and charged and operated for energy reliability before extreme events, such as hurricanes or
 Nor’easters.

 Finally, because the meters from the Recycling Center are owned by the Town of Shelter Island,
 one attractive option is to link them with other meters throughout the Town via the Remote Net-
 Metering program. Maximizing the available rooftop space at the recycling center could offset
 electricity usage for the recycling buildings, police department, and legion hall.

 Feasibility of Tidal Energy, Agrivoltaic Solutions, and Geothermal
 Projects
 There has been interest within the community in marine tidal energy technology, in part due to
 efforts to demonstrate the technology in Eastport, Maine, another ETIPP community. To address
 this interest, NLR provided a presentation on the state of tidal energy technology and past
 preliminary tidal energy resource assessments completed around the Island. While a potentially
 usable resource was identified, the community representatives concluded that the technology is
 insufficiently developed (i.e., a low technology readiness level or TRL) to be a viable near-term
 solution.

 viii

 Agrivoltaics were deemed a potentially viable option for deployment at Sylvester Manor. The
 NLR team provided the GOC with five deployment options, each with various benefits and
 drawbacks for the community to consider in the event of possible future installations.

 Geothermal for heating and cooling systems was heavily studied for this effort. Due to concerns
 around the freshwater aquifer, the community determined that a horizontal “slinky” closed-loop
 system at a depth of just 10 feet was ideal. NLR modeled a closed-loop system for Fiske Field, a
 4-by-4-acre field adjacent to the high school, to determine potential offsets that could be
 achieved at the school. Complete data on the school’s heating and cooling loads were not
 available for this study, so proxies were used. Dependent on actual load data, NLR found that a
 geothermal installation under Fiske Field could offset between 51% and 127% of the school’s
 heating and cooling needs.

 1 Overview
 The U.S. Department of Energy’s (DOE’s) Energy Technology Innovation Partnership Project
 (ETIPP) provides strategic energy planning and technical assistance for remote and island
 communities to address local energy needs and enhance the long-term reliability of their energy
 systems. In 2024, the National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR) partnered with the island
 community of Shelter Island, New York, to provide technical assistance as part of the ETIPP’s
 third cohort of communities.

 1.1 Community Background
 Shelter Island is in the Peconic Bay between the North and South Forks at the eastern end of
 Long Island and is only accessible by ferry (Figure 1). The year-round population is
 approximately 3,200, swelling to more than 12,000 during the summer (from Memorial Day
 through Labor Day).

 Figure 1. Shelter Island, New York.
 Photo courtesy of Shelter Island Chamber of Commerce

 Shelter Island’s electric utility is the Long Island Power Authority (LIPA), with grid connection
 managed by Public Service Enterprise Group Long Island (PSEGLI) via underwater cables that
 run about 0.6 miles from Greenport on the North Fork and 0.4 miles from North Haven on the
 South Fork. Prior to Superstorm Sandy in 2012, Shelter Island was served by two distribution
 lines from the North Fork and one from the South Fork. During the storm, one of the North Fork
 feeders failed. A replacement cable was laid in 2018 in one of three new underground conduits,
 with two for future backup.

 1

 The proportion of elderly residents is significantly higher on Shelter Island than the national
 average, with 36.1% of the population 65 years or older (BFJ Planning 2024). The Island’s aging
 population is dependent on reliable electric power for water, heating, and communication.

 In addition to a reliable energy supply, local energy generation is a priority for Shelter Island. To
 date, only a few homeowners have installed solar projects, and there are no community- or
 utility-scale energy generation projects on the Island (BFJ Planning 2024).

 1.2 ETIPP Objectives
 The ETIPP team was led by Shelter Island’s Green Options Committee (GOC)—an all-volunteer
 committee tasked with considering environmental conservation issues for the island. Energy
 research and the development of recommendations to increase the Town’s use of locally
 generated energy is one of the primary functions of the GOC. An additional priority is ensuring
 new locally generated capacity meets current and future energy needs in the event of major
 weather or other external impacts to reliability.

 The primary goals of this technical assistance project included:

 • Informing the GOC about energy use trends within the community, as well as distributed
 solar, wind, and storage opportunities.
 • Providing additional resources and technical feasibility information for geothermal,
 agrivoltaics, and tidal energy.
 • Supporting the development of a community engagement plan.
 • Supporting collaboration between the GOC and PSEGLI to find mutually beneficial
 follow-on projects.
 Assistance from NLR to help the GOC meet these overall project goals was provided through the
 following five primary tasks:

 • Collaborate with the local utility to collect energy use data and inform the community on
 energy use patterns through a baseline assessment.
 • Provide technical analysis of distributed local energy generation and reliability
 opportunities (solar, wind, and storage) that best align with the community’s energy
 priorities.
 • Provide high-level feasibility support for future local energy generation scenarios that

 2 Baseline Assessment
 Through multiple conversations with the project team and PSEGLI, NLR completed a baseline
 assessment to better understand how the community utilizes energy—including electricity costs
 and consumption patterns, as well as reliability opportunities. Data on thermal consumption in
 the community were not readily available and therefore not included.

 2.1 Electricity Analysis
 2.1.1 Electricity Consumption and System Peaks
 The data provided by PSEGLI indicate that in December 2023, Shelter Island had 2,867
 residential electric service meters, 355 commercial and industrial meters, and 5 street lighting
 meters. 1 As shown in Figure 2, between January 2019 and December 2023, the average
 distribution of meters was 88.5% residential, 11.3% commercial and industrial, and just 0.2% for
 street lighting. As shown in Figure 3 and consistent with the meter data, the majority of
 electricity consumption occurs in the residential sector, followed by the commercial and
 industrial sector. On average from 2019 to 2023, annual electricity consumption for Shelter
 Island was approximately 33.5 GWh, with spikes in the summer months due to increased
 demand from a seasonal influx of residents and summer tourism-related activities

 2.1.2 Tariff and Bill Summary
 Meter-level load data were not available, so an average approach was taken. The average
 consumption for a residential customer (i.e., meter) was estimated by dividing total monthly
 residential consumption by total residential meters. The average monthly usage across the 5-year
 period was about 814 kWh per month. Monthly and annual bills were calculated using average
 monthly consumption data and 2023 electric tariff information from LIPA for Service
 Classification 1, Rate Code 180, and annual adjustment factors, as shown in Error! Reference
 source not found.. 2 These data are provided as an estimate and do not accurately reflect actual
 bills because not all customers are on this rate code, some charges are excluded (e.g., taxes, New
 York state adjustment factor), and customers will use more or less than the average monthly
 consumption. This analysis also assumes all residential customers take both energy and delivery
 service from LIPA and do not contract with competitive energy service companies.

 The LIPA tariff generally consisted of energy charges, delivery charges, and other charges (e.g.,
 riders, taxes, and adjustments):

 • Energy charges are the “power supply charges” associated with energy generation and
 capacity, with rates that adjust every month.
 • Delivery charges are the inclining block rate delivery charges that increase as total usage
 increases.
 • Other charges include the state sales tax, merchant function charge and distributed energy
 charge that are applied volumetrically to overall usage, fixed daily charge, and
 decoupling and delivery service adjustment riders that are applied as a percentage of total
 delivery service revenues. 3
 As shown in Figure 5, monthly bill costs increase in the summer season in a similar pattern to
 total peak usage. The highest-cost months are in the summer, followed by the winter and then
 shoulder seasons. As shown in Figure 6, the increase in usage in the summer coupled with higher
 summer delivery charge rates boosts delivery-related charges to compose the largest portion of
 the sample customer’s bill in summer months. Conversely, energy charges represent the largest
 portion of the total bill in the winter months. The highest power supply rate in this period was in
 January (11.95 cents/kWh), then July (11.85 cents/kWh) and February (11 cents/kWh). These
 data indicate energy charges are pulled from a winter and summer peaking system, while
 delivery charges are summer peaking.

 On March 29, 2023, the LIPA board of trustees voted to modernize delivery and system charge
 rate design by moving to time-of-day (TOD) rates, while power supply charges will continue to
 fluctuate by month (LIPA 2025b, 2025c; PSEGLI 2025b). Starting in 2024, LIPA began
 automatically migrating certain residential and small business customers to standard TOD rates,
 while there is also a voluntary enrollment option (PSEGLI 2025f). Customers can opt out of the

 6

 automatic migration or choose to go back to the previous rate plan (PSEGLI 2025e). The two
 TOD rate options include the standard TOD rate and the super off-peak TOD rate. Whereas the
 previous flat rate structure (e.g., Rate Code 180) had different seasonal prices and a price change
 based on total consumption, the new TOD rates are different based on the season and time of day
 electricity is consumed. In general, TOD rate prices increase during times of the day when the
 system is peaking, therefore sending a price signal to customers to encourage energy
 conservation, the shifting of energy use, and efficiency. For both of the TOD rate plans, the daily
 peak period when prices are highest is from 3 to 7 p.m. The super off-peak TOD plan has a
 slightly higher peak period price, but lower prices from 10 p.m. to 6 a.m. (the super off-peak
 period) (LIPA 2023a). All TOD rates are provided in detail in Appendix A.2. LIPA asserts that
 80% of customers will pay less on TOD rates without changing their routines because most
 consumption currently occurs off-peak (LIPA 2023a). There is also a Guaranteed Bill Protection
 program for eligible customers who are moved to or voluntarily choose to switch to the TOD rate
 plan that ensures total electric bills under TOD rates will not be higher than they would have
 been under the previous flat rate plan (PSEGLI 2025f). Additional information on LIPA can be
 found in Appendix B.1.

 2.2 Reliability Needs
 The GOC requested power system reliability data from PSEGLI to better understand outage

 3 Techno-Economic Analysis of Distributed Local
 Energy Generation and Reliability Opportunities
 (REopt Analysis)
 The Shelter Island project team selected eight sites (e.g., municipal, commercial, and residential)
 on Shelter Island to analyze the cost-effectiveness of behind-the-meter solar and battery storage
 systems, as well as how the systems could improve reliability during power outages, The NLR
 software REopt® was used to optimally size distributed solar and battery storage equipment by
 minimizing life cycle costs. A preliminary analysis was conducted for all eight sites, providing
 solar and storage sizes that minimize cost. Three sites were subsequently chosen by the project
 team for a “deep-dive” analysis—a residential home, school, and recycling center—for which
 additional results were generated.

 3.1 Introduction to REopt
 REopt is a techno-economic optimization model used to support distributed energy systems
 planning decisions (NLR 2025d). Formulated as a mixed-integer linear program, REopt can
 identify the cost-optimal sizes of various behind-the-meter distributed energy resource
 technologies, along with strategies to dispatch these technologies to minimize life cycle costs.
 REopt assumes a perfect foresight of future loads, generation, and market conditions over the
 simulation horizon. Life cycle costs include estimated capital costs of new on-site generation and
 storage capacity; the present value of all estimated operating expenses such as electricity, fuel,
 and other operations and maintenance costs; and the present value of any financial incentives and
 depreciation. Results from REopt can be used to inform a detailed technical feasibility analysis
 of distributed energy resource technologies. This study utilizes REopt to determine the system
 sizes and dispatch strategies for behind-the-meter solar photovoltaic (PV) and battery energy
 storage technologies, which can provide electric bill cost savings and reliability against outages.

 The following are the key inputs to REopt:

 • Site information: Site location is used to determine the solar resource availability for
 solar PV sizing, while rooftop/land area restrictions and existing on-site distributed
 energy resource systems are used as constraints.
 • Electric load data: Ideally, a REopt analysis would include the electric load (interval) data
 for a site in a time series format. These interval data consist of measured power
 consumption at regular intervals (e.g., 15 minutes, 30 minutes, 1 hour). Interval data are
 key because they can capture the daily and seasonal variation of a site’s electric loads,
 which informs in REopt’s system sizing and dispatch strategy. Lack of interval data
 results in a loss of resolution in REopt analyses, which can directly impact sizing and
 operations estimation. However, there can be cases where interval data for a site may not
 be readily available or complete. In these cases, REopt can use DOE’s commercial
 reference buildings load profiles to simulate a site’s electric loads. These simulated loads
 are intended to be representative of the site’s loads but are only gross estimates. When
 available, monthly data of electricity use (kWh) and peak demand (kW) should be used to
 normalize the commercial reference building profiles.
 • Electric tariff: Details of the electricity demand and related charges levied on the site are
 also critical, as electric bill savings are measured using the electric tariff. Both demand

 9

 charges and electricity charges can vary by time of day or month. A comprehensive
 database of utility rates was used to provide a good initial point for the analysis but
 should always be verified with current information from the utility.
 The REopt workflow is presented in Figure 9. Different types of inputs are represented in blue,
 while the main results are in green. Only solar PV, battery energy storage, and air-source heat
 pumps (ASHPs) were considered for the Shelter Island analysis per community request.

 3.2.3 Net-Metering Policy
 The solar billing mechanism was identified as a net energy metering policy, as described in the
 LIPA tariff (LIPA 2023b). Residential customers are constrained to a PV rated capacity of 25
 kW or less and 110% of site load (annual kilowatt-hours). Excess generation is paid monthly as
 credit on the next bill (with the same rate as customers without generation) with an annual true-
 up. The annual true-up was not modeled in the analysis because its impact depends on which
 month of the year it happens, which varies by customer. In general, annual true-ups happening
 right after summer months with high production are less beneficial to the customer because the
 excess annual generation is usually rewarded under a lower rate. Commercial customers are
 constrained to a PV rated capacity of 750 kW or less and 110% of site load (annual kilowatt-
 hours). Excess generation is paid in the same way as described for residential. Larger systems
 (>750 kW or 110% annual kilowatt-hours) are allowed as “Large Onsite/Offsite” customers, but
 paid differently. Remote net-metering allows generation from a “host” PV array (location/meter)
 to offset other “satellite” meters, where the same customer or corporation must own or lease the

 13

 property designated to the host and satellite accounts. Remote net-metering also eliminates the
 demand ratchet (75% of the highest demand over previous year) for host and satellite meters,
 which is very beneficial for customers.

 The last option is community solar, in which customers sign up for the community solar project
 with a certain subscription percentage and pay a fee to a generator facility. Community solar
 projects feed electricity directly to the grid, production credits are distributed to the customers
 based on their subscriber percentage, and the allocated kilowatt-hours are included on the
 customer’s bill as a credit. Although community solar was not directly evaluated in this study, it
 is an option that the community may consider in the future. However, in conversations with
 PSEGLI, it was noted that anything greater than 300 kVA would require infrastructure upgrades
 for interconnection. The costs for these upgrades would fall on the developer and/or owner of the
 land and are not covered by PSEGLI. Per LIPA tariff, a customer can only participate in one of
 the above categories.

 Next, battery storage + solar was found to be cost-effective overall, with some considerations.

 In general, TOD rates are not cost-effective for commercial buildings (solar and/or battery)

 not present a time-of-use component and were thus found to be less valuable when deploying
 battery energy storage with solar. This finding aligns with PSEGLI standard rates, which are new
 TOD rates for residential customers and classic rates for commercial customers.

 One of the main conclusions, in terms of reliability, is that the optimal battery size to provide
 3 versus 14 days of reliability is very similar, highlighting that the system is self-sufficient and
 self-sustaining in the sense the battery energy storage can be charged using the solar system
 throughout the outage. It must be noted, however, that solar energy generation is stochastic by
 nature, and while the analysis considers some elements of the worst-case scenario (e.g., by
 applying the outage when the site load is the highest), in reality, variations in solar generation
 could result in gaps of reliability.

 The school building was found to be a good option for providing reliability as an
 emergency shelter—the system is also economically favorable for the reliability scenario (up to
 14 days). Its large electric load and footprint (compared to other analyzed buildings) yield a large
 solar and battery storage system, which would be able to accommodate emergency loads. This
 site is also a good location for deploying large backup batteries, which can be operated for cost
 reduction during most of the year and charged and operated for reliability before natural weather

 18

 hazards. A possible downside is that its critical load may be large during long outages, assuming
 that the school would be operating, which would reduce its capacity to address other emergency
 loads.

 All scenarios provided significant cost savings with payback periods of 7 years or less. Rate

 4 Agrivoltaics Scenario Assessment
 Agrivoltaics is defined as “a land use configuration where solar energy generation and sunlight-
 dependent agricultural activities are directly integrated” (Macknick et al. 2022). Agricultural
 activities can span crop production, livestock production, solar greenhouse operations, and
 pollinator habitat establishment (Figure 15). The concept was introduced to help mitigate the
 land use conflict between solar energy and agricultural production and to provide mutual benefits
 to agricultural practitioners and solar developers. As a working farm, Sylvester Manor on Shelter
 Island, New York, is interested in exploring these potential benefits and adding an on-farm,
 ground-mounted solar array that incorporates small livestock, shade-tolerant crops, and
 pollinator habitat.

 Sylvester Manor determined that the land outline in the red square was the optimal location for a

 4.1 Methodology
 NLR’s System Advisor Model™ (SAM) was used to evaluate the following techno-economic
 variables of different agrivoltaic systems at Sylvester Manor: annual PV generation, upfront
 installation costs, net present value, payback period, and sunlight availability to the crops grown
 within the solar array (NLR 2025e). A scalable 1-acre site was modeled due to uncertainties
 surrounding local community solar regulations and potential increase of on-site energy load.
 Utilizing a consistent land size for all scenarios allowed for a trade-off analysis of different
 designs that could be scaled up or down based on land size or desired annual energy generation.

 4.1.1 Modeling Assumptions
 Capital costs for different agrivoltaic designs were estimated from a 2020 NLR technical report
 (Horowitz et al. 2020). Cost estimates were updated in 2022 for inflation and supply chain
 issues. The system was assumed to be purchased in cash and utilize financial incentives available
 at the time of this analysis. Operating costs were estimated at $16/kW per year. Analysis
 assumes a $0.16/kWh power purchase agreement based on a recent estimate from Harvest Power
 LLC, a local solar developer to Shelter Island. All designs incorporated 3.5-by-7.5-ft, 450-W

 22

 bifacial panels in 1-portrait orientation. Local solar resource was estimated for Shelter Island,
 New York, from the National Solar Radiation Database (NLR 2025c). SAM modeling runs
 assumed a 14% total system loss due to soiling, shading, snow, mismatch, and wiring, as well as
 a 0.5% annual AC degradation rate (NLR 2025a).

 5 Geothermal Scenario Assessment
 Geothermal energy refers to the heat stored beneath the Earth’s surface, which can be harnessed
 for various applications including power generation, thermal energy storage, and heating and
 cooling through geothermal heat pump (GHP) systems (Figure 20). GHPs operate as either open-
 loop or closed-loop systems, with closed-loop configurations more applicable for sensitive
 coastal ecosystem areas like Shelter Island to protect underlying aquifers. These closed-loop
 systems are sealed and depend entirely on conductive heat transfer with the surrounding ground.
 A typical setup includes a heat pump, circulation pump, and high-density polyethylene piping
 installed in vertical boreholes that are grouted with materials like bentonite to improve heat
 exchange efficiency. GHP systems are highly efficient, with COPs ranging from 3 to 4,
 significantly outperforming conventional heating and cooling technologies. They also offer long
 service lives, with heat pump units typically lasting more than 20 years and underground loops
 lasting between 25 and 50 years, all while requiring minimal maintenance. Together, these
 characteristics can result in substantial long-term energy savings.

 Figure 20. Geothermal technologies.
 Source: Joelynn Schroeder, NLR

 5.1 Site-Specific Subsurface Considerations
 Shelter Island relies on a shallow freshwater aquifer for its drinking water (Rozell and Wong
 2010), making the protection of this resource a top community priority. The same subsurface
 conditions that sustain the island’s water supply also present an opportunity for energy-efficient
 heating and cooling through GHP systems. Closed-loop GHP systems considered for Shelter
 Island require thermally conductive and hydraulically stable ground formations to operate
 efficiently. Shelter Island’s geology is dominated by sandy glacial outwash deposits in the Upper
 Glacial aquifer, which offers favorable thermal properties and sufficient saturated thickness to

 27

 support such systems. However, careful planning regarding borehole depth, aquifer protection,
 and system design is essential to ensure that these systems deliver energy savings without
 compromising water resources. This section provides an overview of the hydrogeological
 conditions, water table, aquifer thickness, thermal properties, and regulatory framework relevant
 to GHP installation on Shelter Island, highlighting how proper engineering practices can ensure
 effective system function while maintaining the integrity of the freshwater resource.

 5.1.1 Hydrogeological Overview
 Shelter Island’s groundwater is primarily recharged by local precipitation, with an estimated 4.4
 billion gallons infiltrating annually, significantly surpassing the 260 million gallons extracted for
 potable use (Bebon et al. 2020). The Upper Glacial aquifer, composed of stratified clay, silt,
 sand, gravel, cobbles, and boulders, serves as the island’s sole source of freshwater (Soren 1978).
 Beneath this aquifer lie marine and nonmarine clays extending to approximately 300 feet below
 the surface. (Nelson, Pope & Voorhis LLC 2014). Water table elevations on Shelter Island
 typically range from 3 to 5 feet above sea level (Figure 21). Seasonal precipitation patterns can
 lead to fluctuations in the water table, potentially affecting the position of the freshwater–
 saltwater interface.

 The Upper Glacial aquifer on Shelter Island is approximately 76–90 feet thick in the central

 5.1.4 Groundwater Protection and Closed-Loop System Design
 To assess the feasibility of implementing closed-loop GHP systems on Shelter Island while
 protecting groundwater aquifers, we can refer to the expert analyses described in the following
 subsections. They describe a case study from a major geothermal company assessing aquifer
 contamination.

 Concerns about aquifer contamination during the installation of GHP systems are valid and have
 been addressed through industry standards and best practices. Properly constructed boreholes are
 essential to prevent potential contamination of pathways between surface and subsurface
 environments or across different aquifers.

 Terry Proffer, a geologist and geothermal expert, has significantly contributed to this question
 (California Geothermal Heat Pump Association 2020).

 Below are insights from his industry experience on installation processes, working fluids,
 regulatory perspectives, professional standards, performance of bentonite as grout, and historical
 context.

 5.1.4.1 Installation Process
 • Borehole drilling: A borehole is drilled to the required depth for the geothermal system.
 • Ground loop insertion: A U-bend ground loop is immediately inserted into the borehole,
 followed by a grout injection hose to the bottom.
 • Grouting: Bentonite grout is pressure-injected from the bottom to the surface, filling all
 voids and creating a seal to prevent external water migration (Figure 23).
 Bentonite-based grouts are preferred due to their natural properties and their ability to swell or
 contract with subsurface conditions, maintaining a continuous seal. This prevents any
 contamination of aquifers.

 30

 5.1.4.2 Working Fluid
 For some closed-loop systems, a nominal amount of food-grade propylene glycol is necessary to
 protect the mechanical components should the ground heat exchanger (GHE) fluid approach
 freezing temperatures. Typically, 15% to 20% by volume is sufficient for freeze protection when
 using food-grade propylene glycol for closed-loop GHE installations. Propylene glycol is a type
 of glucose (sugar) used in ice cream, toothpaste, and other food products and is biodegradable. It
 breaks down quickly if spilled, typically within a few days to a week, depending on conditions.
 Colorado’s Division of Water Resources also recognizes propylene glycol as an acceptable
 working fluid used in closed-loop GHEs.

 5.1.4.3 Regulatory Oversight
 In Colorado, the Division of Water Resources regulates all closed-loop GHE installations.
 Vertical closed-loop boreholes are classified as non-consumptive wells, termed “geoexchange
 systems.” These systems are distinguished from high-temperature geothermal wells and are
 subject to specific regulations to protect groundwater resources.

 5.1.4.4 Professional Standards
 The International Ground Source Heat pump Association (IGSHPA), in collaboration with
 organizations like the National Ground Water Association and ASHRAE, has established
 standards for the installation of vertical closed-loop systems. These standards emphasize the
 importance of grouting to prevent water movement between aquifers and to preserve
 groundwater quality (IGSHPA 2017).

 5.2.1 Annual Heating and Cooling Load Profile
 Understanding heating and cooling loads of the school building is crucial for properly sizing and
 designing the GHP energy system. In this project, annual heating and cooling load profiles of the
 Shelter Island school were modeled using the EnergyPlus building energy modeling tool. For the
 school energy modeling, this study utilized the DOE prototype secondary school model (DOE
 2025b). It consists of two floors with a total floor area of 210,900 ft2, representing a typical U.S.
 secondary school with approximately 1,200 students (Figure 24(a)), and typical meteorological
 year weather data (A4) for the nearby Westhampton-Gabreski airport.

 33

 Figure 24. School building energy modeling: (a) the prototype model for secondary school and (b)
 annual heating and cooling load profile
 Figure 24(b) shows annual heating and cooling load of the school. Due to internal heat gains
 from lighting, equipment (e.g., electrical appliances and devices used inside the building), and
 occupant density, as well as solar heat gain through windows, the building has higher annual
 cooling loads than heating loads (approximately three times greater in annual total)—even
 though Shelter Island is located in a heating-load-dominant climate. In addition, the high
 ventilation requirements for maintaining indoor air quality in the school further contribute to
 cooling demands, as outdoor air must often be conditioned and dehumidified during occupied
 hours. These factors combined lead to a cooling load profile that exceeds heating, highlighting
 the importance of considering internal and latent loads in heating, ventilating, and air-
 conditioning system design, even in colder regions.

 5.2.2 Ground Heat Exchanger Design
 There are various types of GHEs, including borehole heat exchangers, horizontal or slinky loops
 in shallow trenches, and open-loop systems that utilize groundwater or surface water sources
 such as lakes or ponds. Among these GHEs, the slinky loop configuration was selected for this

 34

 project due to a combination of factors, including regional geological conditions, land
 availability, and the community’s preference. The slinky loop GHE system was designed in
 GLHEPro, a widely used commercial software for GHE design. Regional ambient conditions,
 site-specific soil thermal conductivity, and the modeled heating and cooling load profiles of the
 prototype school were incorporated into the model. Other design parameters, such as trench
 spacing of 3.05 m (10 ft), were kept at default values to reflect typical industry practice. The
 specific configuration was then modeled using GLHEPro for installation within the available
 land area (Figure 25), which is approximately 125 m × 125 m (3.86 acres), with potential
    extension up to 127.3 m × 127.3 m (4 acres).

 5.2.3 Geothermal Analysis Key Takeaways
 The subsurface conditions on Shelter Island—including aquifer thickness, water table levels, and
 favorable thermal conductivity—make it feasible to deploy a closed-loop GHP system. Industry
 best practices, regulatory frameworks, and successful installations at depths below 300 ft in
 shallow aquifers (see Table 14) demonstrate that groundwater resources can be protected while
 achieving high energy efficiency using shallow geothermal systems. The project evaluated
 several types of GHEs, including vertical boreholes, shallow horizontal or slinky loops, and
 open-loop configurations that use surface or groundwater. Ultimately, a slinky loop
 configuration was selected. This decision was based on a combination of regional geologic
 conditions, available land area, and client preference. System design was performed using
 GLHEPro, a commercial modeling tool that incorporated local ambient conditions, site-specific
 soil thermal conductivity, and heating/cooling load profiles based on a DOE prototype secondary
 school.

 The design assumed a typical trench spacing of 3.05 meters (10 feet) and fit within a 3.86-acre
 (125 m × 125 m) land parcel, with flexibility to expand to 4 acres. Results showed that the
 system could meet approximately 51% of the prototype school’s thermal load. However, because
 the actual Shelter Island school is significantly smaller at 77,000 ft2 compared to the 210,900-ft2
 prototype, the designed system could meet 100% of its real-world heating and cooling needs.
 The prototype is about 2.74 times larger, meaning the 51% load coverage translates to full
 coverage for the actual building. Nonetheless, building-specific factors such as insulation quality,
 window-to-wall ratio, and construction materials can affect actual energy demands, highlighting
 the limitations of using floor area alone for performance projections.

 To assess the system’s potential further, a hypothetical scenario was modeled where the system
 operated at its maximum thermal capacity within the same land area, independent of specific
 building loads. In this case, annual heating output rose to 0.83 GWh, a significant increase over
 the 0.18 GWh in the prototype-based case. However, cooling output decreased slightly from 0.53
 GWh to 0.46 GWh to achieve a more balanced thermal load. Maintaining this balance is crucial,
 as long-term imbalances, especially in cooling-dominated systems, can reduce subsurface heat
 transfer efficiency over time due to soil temperature drift.

 Key performance figures for the maximum-capacity scenario included a peak heating capacity of
 2 MMBtu/h (166.4 tons, or 43.5 tons per acre) and a peak cooling capacity of 155.7 tons (40.7
 tons per acre). These values illustrate the system’s upper operational limits and potential for
 sustainable performance, provided that thermal loads remain relatively balanced. Finally,
 trenching costs for the slinky loop system were estimated at $67,000–$200,000, depending on
 trench unit costs ranging from $4 to $12 per foot. These estimates support the system’s economic
 feasibility when evaluated alongside its performance and land use efficiency.

 6 Tidal Energy Overview
 Marine energy generates electrical energy from the movement of water—such as waves, tides,
 and river and ocean currents (DOE 2025a). Each type of resource requires different technology
 to capture the energy in that resource. Generally, however, marine energy devices require a
 minimum flow speed (cut-in speed) to start operating. This minimum flow speed ranges from 0.5
 m/s to 1 m/s depending on the specific device (Haas et al. 2011). While still under development,
 marine energy technology is a promising source of local and predictable electricity for both
 remote communities and regional grids. Figure 27 shows the extent of U.S. marine energy
 resources. For Shelter Island, tidal energy is the most promising marine energy resource,
 described in detail in Section 6.4.

 Figure 27. U.S. marine energy resources.
 Source: Kilcher, Fogarty, and Lawson (2021)

 Tidal currents occur when specific geographical features, such as a narrow strait, or certain
 bathymetrical attributes, such as a deep channel that becomes shallower, accelerate existing tidal
 flows. Tides are driven by the gravitational pull of the moon, so these flows are highly
 predictable. High tides occur every 12 hours and 25 minutes, with low tides in between. Other
 marine resources are more variable, as they depend on wind (waves) or precipitation (riverine
 kinetic energy). Ocean currents, such as the Gulf Stream, are much farther offshore and more
 difficult to exploit for energy generation. Therefore, tidal current energy is an excellent resource
 for coastal communities seeking a reliable and local source of energy.

 6.3 Siting and Regulatory Information
 Permitting and licensing marine energy devices can be a lengthy process. A good first step in
 determining if a marine energy device is a fit for each site of interest in the waters around Shelter
 Island is to perform both desktop and in-water data collection to characterize the potential for
 each site. In-water data collection typically needs federal (U.S. Army Corps of Engineers and
 U.S. Coast Guard) and state (all applicable New York agencies that regulate state waters)
 permits, which can take 30–120 days to obtain.

 Once the community has determined a suitable site to test a marine energy device or devices,
 there are three different types of permitting and pathways to consider:

 • The “Verdant Exception” (Water Power Technologies Office 2020). The Federal Energy
 Regulatory Commission (FERC) defined a process where a marine energy device can be
 installed in the ocean, tested for up to 18 months, not connected to the grid. In this
 scenario, the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers is the lead agency, and a general permit or
 nationwide permit must be obtained, along with any other applicable local, state, and
 federal permits. It typically takes 90–120 days to obtain a permit from the U.S. Army
 Corps of Engineers.

 44

 • FERC staff developed a licensing process for marine energy (i.e., hydrokinetic) pilot
 projects tailored to meet the needs of entities (including communities) interested in
 testing new technology, including connection with the interstate grid, while minimizing
 the risk of adverse environmental impacts (FERC 2025a). The goal of the pilot process is
 to allow grid-connected testing of marine energy devices to determine appropriate siting
 of these technologies, and to confirm their environmental effects, while maintaining
 FERC oversight and agency input. Currently, there are two projects in the United States
 that have a FERC hydrokinetic pilot license: the Igiugig Hydrokinetic Project and the
 Bourne Tidal Hydrokinetic Test Site. Both projects encountered delays related to funding
 and developing environmental monitoring plans with state and federal agencies.
 • FERC also has a commercial license available for projects greater than 5 MW in scale.
 There are several different processes: integrated, traditional, and alternative (FERC
 2025b). The most common process is the integrated licensing process, intended to
 streamline FERC’s licensing process by providing a predictable, efficient, and timely
 licensing process that continues to ensure adequate resource protections. Currently, the
 PacWave South test site is the only project in the United States that has a commercial
 FERC license to test wave energy devices (PacWave 2025).

 8 Conclusion
 The Town of Shelter Island offers numerous opportunities for local energy generation to bolster
 energy reliability. Of the technologies assessed during this analysis:

 • Solar was found to be cost-effective for all analyzed buildings, with optimal sizes
 maximizing either rooftop area or net-metering limit (110% of annual energy load).
 Payback periods between 7 and 10 years were found across all sites.
 • An agrivoltaic deployment and Sylvester Manor may be viable however each scenario
 presents pros and cons. For example, Scenario 1 will provide the highest annual energy
 generation per acre at the least cost per watt installed but will come with the agricultural
 trade-offs of east-west planting rows, higher levels of shading, lower agricultural
 equipment and crop compatibility, and reduced mobility for farmers throughout the array
 due to the tighter row spacing and lower hub height. It will be up to the community to
 determine which scenario makes the most sense for future needs at the farm.
 The subsurface conditions on Shelter Island—including aquifer thickness, water table levels, and
 favorable thermal conductivity—make it feasible to deploy a closed-loop GHP system. Industry
 best practices, regulatory frameworks, and successful installations at depths below 300 ft in
 shallow aquifers demonstrate that groundwater resources can be protected while achieving high
 energy efficiency using shallow geothermal systems. Assuming a typical trench spacing of 3.05
 meters (10 feet) and fit within a 3.86-acre (125 m × 125 m) land parcel, results showed that a
 horizontal closed loop system could potentially meet a majority of the school’s thermal load.

 • Conditions are amenable to tidal power around Shelter Island however output is not
 enough to warrant the investment. Tidal energy is also not yet fully commercialized, and
 other local energy generation options will be more cost effective for Shelter Island’s
 needs.
 More local data is needed to determine the ideal system to be deployed on the island. Next steps
 for the Town and the GOC are outlined throughout the document, and the community
 engagement plan will help Shelter Island build awareness of this work among long stakeholders
 and drive further action.

 A.4 LIPA Background and Resources
 LIPA is one of the largest publicly owned power utilities in the United States, serving more than
 1.2 million customers (LIPA 2025a). In 2013, the LIPA Reform Act was signed into law,
 reorganizing LIPA and putting operational responsibility under PSEGLI (New York State
 Department of Public Service 2025). PSEGLI is a private, investor-owned utility selected to run
 LIPA via competitive bid. The current contract between LIPA and PSEGLI expires Dec. 31,
 2025 (LIPA 2025a). LIPA and PSEGLI are under the regulatory oversight of the New York
 Department of Public Service, including overseeing electricity rates, resource plans, and system
 improvement plans (New York State Department of Public Service 2025). LIPA is governed by a
 local board of trustees generally responsible for contracting, policy, infrastructure finance, and
 other duties (PSEGLI 2023). National Grid owns and operates the natural gas system service and
 business for the majority of Long Island. In select cases, such as with Shelter Island, gas use is in
 the form of propane not supplied by the local utility.

 According to its 2023 integrated resource plan, LIPA owns its electricity transmission and
 distribution system, along with 250 MW of nuclear generation. LIPA contracts with National
 Grid for 3,550 MW of generation capacity and with other providers for 1,750 MW of generation
 on Long Island and 990 MW of transmission cable capacity to facilitate electric market
 purchases from the New York Independent System Operator (NYISO), ISO New England (ISO-
 NE), and PJM Interconnection. In 2022, about 43% of Long Island’s electricity resources were
 from local baseload fossil-fuel-fired power plants, 34% from market-based imports, 14% from
 nuclear, 5% from solar, and 4% from fossil fuel peaking plants (Figure A-14) (PSEGLI 2023).
