Nonlinear Lissajous orbits and particular superintegrability
A. M. Escobar-Ruiz, R. Azuaje

Abstract
We investigate the geometry of classical trajectories generated by separable two-dimensional polynomial potentials of the form V ⁡ ( x , y ) = 1 2 ​ ( x 2 ​ N + A ​ y 2 ​ N ) V(x,y)=\tfrac{1}{2}\big(x^{2N}+A\,y^{2N}\big) , where N = 1 , 2 , … , N=1,2,\ldots, and A > 0 A>0 . Special emphasis is placed on the emergence of nonlinear Lissajous figures and on the distinction between global and particular superintegrability in the Liouville sense. In the harmonic case ( N = 1 N=1 ) closed periodic orbits are a consequence of an additional global integral of motion whenever the frequency ratio is rational, rendering the system maximally superintegrable. In contrast, for anharmonic oscillators, already in the quartic case ( N = 2 ) (N=2) , the oscillation frequencies depend on the partial energies, so periodic Lissajous-type trajectories occur only under nonlinear resonance conditions fixed by the initial data. Accordingly, the extra conserved quantities that characterize these closed orbits are not global invariants but particular (trajectory-dependent) integrals that emerge only on the resonant trajectories. For higher-degree potentials N ≥ 3 N\geq 3 , the resonant trajectories are naturally described by hyperelliptic phase constraints rather than by a universal polynomial orbit equation. Keywords: Lissajous figures, nonlinear oscillators, integrability, superintegrability.

Introduction

Lissajous figures were introduced in 1857 by Jules Antoine Lissajous while studying the composition of two mutually perpendicular harmonic vibrations; such figures were made visible by attaching a mirror to two perpendicular tuning forks and bouncing light off them
[
20
]
. In Classical Mechanics, Lissajous figures emerge as the configuration-space trajectories of a two-dimensional anisotropic harmonic oscillator
[
1
,
13
,
19
,
8
]
; they remain central objects in several modern research studies in Classical and Quantum Mechanics
[
7
,
17
,
11
,
26
,
12
,
29
]
. The distinction between rational and irrational frequency ratios anticipates
the resonance structure that plays a central role in the theory of nearly
integrable Hamiltonian systems
[
16
,
24
,
2
]
.

Lissajous figures constitute a paradigmatic example of ordered motion arising from the superposition of oscillations with commensurate frequencies. For the two-dimensional anisotropic harmonic oscillator, closure of the
configuration-space orbit is a consequence of global superintegrability: whenever the frequency ratio
\omega_{x}/\omega_{y}
is rational, an additional integral of motion exists throughout phase space, and the bounded trajectories belong to closed Lissajous families determined by their amplitudes and relative
phases
[
25
,
23
]
. The closed trajectories are therefore not exceptional features of the dynamics but a systematic consequence of an underlying symmetry algebra
[
6
,
14
,
18
,
22
,
21
,
9
]
. This global structure is well understood, and the harmonic case serves as the standard reference point for all subsequent generalizations.

Considerably less attention has been paid to the nonlinear counterparts of Lissajous figures, generated by anharmonic potentials. Anharmonic oscillators occupy a central place in nonlinear Classical and Quantum Mechanics
[
5
,
3
,
4
,
27
,
28
]
, arising naturally in molecular physics, nonlinear optics, and the perturbative treatment of field theories. In the anharmonic setting the frequencies of oscillation are no longer fixed by the potential alone but depend on the energy of the motion. As a result, two trajectories with different energies generically oscillate at incommensurate frequencies even when the potential is isotropic, and the simple geometric picture of Lissajous figures appears to be destroyed. Nevertheless, closed orbit-like trajectories do persist in anharmonic systems, provided the initial data satisfy a precise nonlinear resonance condition. The natural questions are then: what geometric structure do these nonlinear closed orbits possess, and what conserved quantities, if any, are associated with them?

These questions touch on a fundamental issue in the theory of Hamiltonian systems: the relationship between the closure of trajectories and the existence of conserved quantities. For integrable systems in the Liouville sense, the existence of sufficiently many global integrals of motion constrains the dynamics to invariant tori. Superintegrable systems — those possessing more independent integrals than degrees of freedom — exhibit the stronger property that every bounded trajectory is periodic
[
23
]
. The isotropic harmonic oscillator, and more generally the resonant
anisotropic harmonic oscillator, belongs to this class. However, superintegrable systems are exceptional, and once the potential departs from the harmonic form no additional global integral is typically available. The persistence of closed orbits in non-superintegrable systems therefore demands an explanation that goes beyond the standard framework.

The previously developed notion of particular superintegrability provides such an explanation. A particular integral of motion is a phase-space function whose Poisson bracket with the Hamiltonian vanishes not throughout phase space but only along certain trajectories
[
32
]
. While not a global symmetry, a particular integral encodes the fact that a specific trajectory — or a lower-dimensional invariant manifold — possesses additional conservation laws invisible to the rest of the dynamics
[
10
]
. The concept of particular superintegrability, in which the number of such trajectory-dependent integrals exceeds the Liouville bound on a given manifold, has begun to receive systematic attention
[
30
,
31
]
, but its geometric content and its connection to nonlinear resonance have not been fully developed.

In this study, we analyze the classical dynamics of systems described by the Hamiltonian

H(x,y,p_{x},p_{y})=\frac{1}{2\,m}\left(p_{x}^{2}+p_{y}^{2}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left(x^{2N}+A\,y^{2N}\right),

(1)

N=1,2,\ldots,
and
A>0
. We focus on the emergence of nonlinear Lissajous orbits and the structure of the associated
conserved quantities
. The separability of the potential implies the existence of two global partial-energy integrals in all cases. Beyond these, the structure of conserved quantities depends critically on
N
.

We contrast two cases: the integrable anisotropic harmonic oscillator (
N=1
), which is globally superintegrable for rational frequency ratios, and the quartic anisotropic anharmonic oscillator
[
15
]
(
N=2
), where superintegrability beyond Liouville’s theorem becomes strictly trajectory–dependent. This last feature has been studied in previous work within the framework of particular (super)integrability, which describes systems where only a part of the dynamics satisfies the conditions for complete (super)integrability
[
10
]
. The conserved quantities in such cases are called particular integrals
[
32
]
, which may exist only along certain trajectories; namely, a particular integral for a Hamiltonian system is a quantity on phase space that is conserved possibly only for certain trajectories of the system.

Our main contributions are threefold. First, we derive the nonlinear
resonance conditions for the separable polynomial potentials and describe the
corresponding closed configuration-space orbits. In the quartic case, where
the dynamics is elliptic, the resonant orbits can be written explicitly as
algebraic curves by using Jacobi elliptic multiplication formulas. For

N\geq 3
, the motion is governed by hyperelliptic phase variables, and the
resonant orbits are described by implicit phase constraints rather than by a
universal polynomial equation
F(x,y)=0
.

Second, we construct the associated particular integrals of motion: algebraic
orbit constraints in the quartic case and hyperelliptic phase constraints in
the higher-degree cases. In each case, these quantities are conserved only
after restriction to the corresponding resonant invariant manifold and do not
define global integrals on phase space.

Third, we clarify why such trajectory-dependent invariants cannot be promoted
to global conserved quantities. Taken together, these results show how
integrability reorganizes itself in nonlinear separable systems: global
superintegrability is lost, but closed resonant trajectories persist through
particular, trajectory-dependent invariants that define the resonant
manifolds on which closure occurs.

Global integrals of motion and orbit equations

3.1
General solutions

The conserved quantities
{\cal H}^{(N=2)}_{x}=E_{x}

{\cal H}^{(N=2)}_{y}=E_{y}

(25)

\dot{x}^{\,2}=\frac{2E_{x}-x^{4}}{m},\qquad\dot{y}^{\,2}=\frac{2E_{y}-Ay^{4}}{m}.

(26)

The turning points are

x_{\max}=(2E_{x})^{1/4},\qquad y_{\max}=\left(\frac{2E_{y}}{A}\right)^{1/4}.

(27)

The solutions may be written in terms of Jacobi elliptic functions as

\displaystyle x(t)

\displaystyle=x_{\max}\,\mathrm{cn}\!\left(\alpha_{x}t+\varphi_{x},k\right),

(28)

\displaystyle y(t)

\displaystyle=y_{\max}\,\mathrm{cn}\!\left(\alpha_{y}t+\varphi_{y},k\right),

(29)

with fixed modulus

(30)

Here
\varphi_{x}
and
\varphi_{y}
are determined by the initial data.
The coefficients of the elliptic arguments are

\alpha_{x}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{m}}\,x_{\max}=\frac{2^{3/4}E_{x}^{1/4}}{\sqrt{m}},\qquad\alpha_{y}=\sqrt{\frac{2A}{m}}\,y_{\max}=\frac{2^{3/4}(AE_{y})^{1/4}}{\sqrt{m}}.

(31)

Indeed, since

\frac{d^{2}}{du^{2}}\mathrm{cn}\!\left(u,\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)+\mathrm{cn}^{3}\!\left(u,\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)=0,

(32)

Eqs. (
28
)–(
29

\ddot{x}+\frac{2}{m}x^{3}=0,\qquad\ddot{y}+\frac{2A}{m}y^{3}=0.

(33)

The real period of

\Omega_{x}=\frac{\pi}{2K(k)}\,\alpha_{x},\qquad\Omega_{y}=\frac{\pi}{2K(k)}\,\alpha_{y}.

Equivalently,

Consequently, the nonlinear frequency ratio is

3.2
Nonlinear resonance and orbit equations

In contrast to the harmonic case, the ratio (

\frac{\Omega_{x}}{\Omega_{y}}=\frac{p}{q},\qquad p,q\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0},\qquad\gcd(p,q)=1.

This is a nonlinear resonance condition fixed by the initial data

(x_{0},p_{x0};y_{0},p_{y0})

We now specialize to equal partial energies

E_{x}=E_{y}=1

(38)

A=n^{4},\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}.

(39)

Thus

3.3
Particular integrals of motion

For the quartic oscillator the globally defined Liouville integrals are the
two partial energies

{\cal H}^{(2)}_{x}=\frac{p_{x}^{2}}{2m}+\frac{x^{4}}{2},\qquad{\cal H}^{(2)}_{y}=\frac{p_{y}^{2}}{2m}+\frac{Ay^{4}}{2},

(57)

with

{\cal H}^{(2)}={\cal H}^{(2)}_{x}+{\cal H}^{(2)}_{y}.

(58)

For generic values of
A
, no additional globally defined independent
integral exists. Nevertheless, on resonant invariant manifolds one can
construct additional quantities whose Poisson bracket with the Hamiltonian
vanishes only after restriction to the corresponding resonant motion. These
are the particular integrals considered below.

More precisely, let
\mathcal{R}_{p:q}
denote the resonant set on which
the nonlinear frequency ratio satisfies

\frac{\Omega_{x}}{\Omega_{y}}=\frac{p}{q},\qquad p,q\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0},\qquad\gcd(p,q)=1.

For a fixed relative phase
\Delta
, let

\mathcal{R}^{(\Delta)}_{p:q}\subset\mathcal{R}_{p:q}
denote the
corresponding phase-locked resonant invariant manifold, and let

\gamma^{(\Delta)}_{p:q}\subset\mathcal{R}^{(\Delta)}_{p:q}
be a
trajectory contained in this manifold. A phase-space function

I_{3}(x,y,p_{x},p_{y})
is called a particular integral associated with

\mathcal{R}^{(\Delta)}_{p:q}
if

\left.\left\{H^{(2)},I_{3}\right\}\right|_{\mathcal{R}^{(\Delta)}_{p:q}}=0,

(59)

whereas

\left\{H^{(2)},I_{3}\right\}\not\equiv 0

(60)

as an identity on the full phase space. Equivalently,
I_{3}
is conserved
along the resonant trajectories contained in the phase-locked component

\mathcal{R}^{(\Delta)}_{p:q}
, but it does not define a global integral
of the quartic Hamiltonian.

Remark.
The definition (
59
)–(
60
) should not be understood as
the arbitrary construction of a function that vanishes on a single
parametrized trajectory. In the present problem the particular integrals
arise from the nonlinear phase-locking condition on a resonant invariant
manifold. The coordinate-space constraints below are therefore
representatives of an underlying resonant phase invariant, not merely
a posteriori orbit equations.

We now specialize to the equal-energy resonant family

{\cal H}^{(2)}_{x}=1,\qquad{\cal H}^{(2)}_{y}=1,\qquad A=n^{4},\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}.

(61)

Then

\frac{\Omega_{x}}{\Omega_{y}}=\frac{1}{n}.

(62)

The turning points are

x_{\max}=2^{1/4},\qquad y_{\max}=\frac{2^{1/4}}{n},

(63)

and we use the dimensionless variables

\xi=\frac{x}{2^{1/4}},\qquad\eta=\frac{y}{y_{\max}}=\frac{n\,y}{2^{1/4}}.

(64)

For the phase-locked resonant orbit
\Delta=0
, Section 3.2 gives

\xi=\mathrm{cn}(u,k),\qquad\eta=\mathrm{cn}(nu,k),\qquad k=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}.

(65)

Using the multiplication theorem for Jacobi elliptic functions, this relation
can be written as

\eta Q_{n}(\xi)-P_{n}(\xi)=0,

(66)

where

\mathrm{cn}(nu,k)=\frac{P_{n}(\mathrm{cn}(u,k))}{Q_{n}(\mathrm{cn}(u,k))},\qquad k=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}.

(67)

Thus the coordinate-space orbit constraint

F^{(0)}_{n^{4}}(x,y)\equiv\eta Q_{n}(\xi)-P_{n}(\xi)

(68)

satisfies

\left.\frac{d}{dt}F^{(0)}_{n^{4}}(x(t),y(t))\right|_{\gamma_{1:n}}=\left.\left\{{\cal H}^{(2)},F^{(0)}_{n^{4}}\right\}\right|_{\gamma_{1:n}}=0.

(69)

However, the same Poisson bracket does not vanish identically in phase space.
Hence
F^{(0)}_{n^{4}}
, or any phase-space representative obtained from it
using the energy-shell constraints, defines a particular integral associated
with the chosen resonant orbit.

The energy-shell constraints

{\cal H}^{(2)}_{x}=1,\qquad{\cal H}^{(2)}_{y}=1

(70)

imply

x^{4}=2-\frac{p_{x}^{2}}{m},\qquad Ay^{4}=2-\frac{p_{y}^{2}}{m}.

(71)

These identities hold only on the specified energy shell. They may be used to
rewrite the coordinate orbit constraint as a trajectory-dependent phase-space
function.

Lowest resonances.

•

n=1
,
A=1
,
1\!:\!1
resonance.

For the phase-locked orbit one has

F^{(0)}_{1}(x,y)=y-x=0.

(72)

Equivalently, the trajectory is the invariant line

x=y,\qquad p_{x}=p_{y}.

(73)

On this invariant line the angular momentum

L_{z}=xp_{y}-yp_{x}

(74)

vanishes and is conserved. In the full phase space, however,

\left\{{\cal H}^{(2)},L_{z}\right\}=2xy\left(y^{2}-x^{2}\right),

(75)

which is not identically zero. Thus
L_{z}
is not a global integral of the
quartic oscillator, although it becomes conserved on the invariant lines

x=\pm y
.

•

n=2
,
A=2^{4}
,
1\!:\!2
resonance.

The phase-locked orbit equation is

F^{(0)}_{2^{4}}(x,y)=2^{3/4}y\left(2+2\sqrt{2}\,x^{2}-x^{4}\right)-x^{4}-2\sqrt{2}\,x^{2}+2=0.

(76)

Using
x^{4}=2-p_{x}^{2}/m
, an equivalent phase-space representative on the
energy shell is

I^{(2)}_{3}(x,y,p_{x})=2^{3/4}y\left(\frac{p_{x}^{2}}{m}+2\sqrt{2}\,x^{2}\right)+\frac{p_{x}^{2}}{m}-2\sqrt{2}\,x^{2}.

(77)

It satisfies

\left.\left\{{\cal H}^{(2)},I^{(2)}_{3}\right\}\right|_{\gamma_{1:2}}=0,

(78)

but the Poisson bracket is not zero as a global identity.

•

n=3
,
A=3^{4}
,
1\!:\!3
resonance.

The phase-locked orbit equation is

F^{(0)}_{3^{4}}(x,y)=3y\left(4+12x^{4}-3x^{8}\right)-x\left(x^{8}+12x^{4}-12\right)=0.

(79)

Using
x^{4}=2-p_{x}^{2}/m
, this may be written on the energy shell as

I^{(3)}_{3}(x,y,p_{x})=3y\left[16-3\left(\frac{p_{x}^{2}}{m}\right)^{2}\right]-x\left[16-16\frac{p_{x}^{2}}{m}+\left(\frac{p_{x}^{2}}{m}\right)^{2}\right].

(80)

Again,

\left.\left\{{\cal H}^{(2)},I^{(3)}_{3}\right\}\right|_{\gamma_{1:3}}=0,

(81)

while the bracket does not vanish on the full phase space.

For higher resonances the construction is analogous: one first obtains

P_{n}
and
Q_{n}
from the
\mathrm{cn}
-multiplication formula at

k=1/\sqrt{2}
, forms the orbit constraint

F^{(0)}_{n^{4}}(x,y)=\eta Q_{n}(\xi)-P_{n}(\xi),

and, if desired, uses the energy-shell identities

x^{4}=2-\frac{p_{x}^{2}}{m},\qquad Ay^{4}=2-\frac{p_{y}^{2}}{m},

to obtain a phase-space representative. These functions are conserved only on
the corresponding resonant invariant manifold. They are therefore particular,
not global, integrals of motion.

4
General case
N\geq 3

Let us consider the general Hamiltonian function

{\cal H}^{(N)}=\frac{1}{2m}\left(p_{x}^{2}+p_{y}^{2}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left(x^{2N}+Ay^{2N}\right),

(82)

for
N\geq 3
, where
A>0
is the anisotropy parameter.
As in the previous cases, separability implies the existence of the two global
integrals

{\cal H}^{(N)}_{x}=\frac{p_{x}^{2}}{2m}+\frac{x^{2N}}{2},\qquad{\cal H}^{(N)}_{y}=\frac{p_{y}^{2}}{2m}+A\frac{y^{2N}}{2},

(83)

with
{\cal H}^{(N)}={\cal H}^{(N)}_{x}+{\cal H}^{(N)}_{y}
.

4.1
Equations of motion and first integrals

Hamilton’s equations yield

\ddot{x}+\frac{N}{m}x^{2N-1}=0,\qquad\ddot{y}+A\frac{N}{m}y^{2N-1}=0.

(84)

Using the conserved partial energies, the dynamics may be written in first–order
form as

\dot{x}^{2}=\frac{2}{m}E_{x}-\frac{1}{m}x^{2N},\qquad\dot{y}^{2}=\frac{2}{m}E_{y}-\frac{A}{m}y^{2N}.

(85)

The turning points are

x_{\max}=(2E_{x})^{1/(2N)},\qquad y_{\max}=\left(\frac{2E_{y}}{A}\right)^{1/(2N)}.

(86)

4.2
Frequency scaling and nonlinear resonance

Systems with
N\geq 3
are non–isochronous. From
Eq. (
85
), the period of the
x
–motion is

T_{x}=4\sqrt{m}\int_{0}^{(2E_{x})^{1/(2N)}}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{2E_{x}-x^{2N}}}.

(87)

After the change of variables

x=(2E_{x})^{1/(2N)}u,

one obtains

T_{x}=4\sqrt{m}\,(2E_{x})^{\frac{1}{2N}-\frac{1}{2}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{du}{\sqrt{1-u^{2N}}}.

(88)

Hence the characteristic angular frequency scales as

\Omega_{x}=C_{N}\,\frac{E_{x}^{(N-1)/(2N)}}{\sqrt{m}},

(89)

where
C_{N}>0
is a numerical constant depending only on
N
.

Similarly, for the
y
–motion,

T_{y}=4\sqrt{m}\int_{0}^{(2E_{y}/A)^{1/(2N)}}\frac{dy}{\sqrt{2E_{y}-Ay^{2N}}}.

(90)

Using

y=\left(\frac{2E_{y}}{A}\right)^{1/(2N)}v,

we find

T_{y}=4\sqrt{m}\,A^{-1/(2N)}(2E_{y})^{\frac{1}{2N}-\frac{1}{2}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{dv}{\sqrt{1-v^{2N}}}.

(91)

Therefore

\Omega_{y}=C_{N}\,\frac{A^{1/(2N)}E_{y}^{(N-1)/(2N)}}{\sqrt{m}}.

(92)

Consequently, the frequency ratio is

\frac{\Omega_{x}}{\Omega_{y}}=A^{-1/(2N)}\left(\frac{E_{x}}{E_{y}}\right)^{(N-1)/(2N)}.

(93)

Closed configuration–space orbits occur only when this ratio is rational,

\frac{\Omega_{x}}{\Omega_{y}}=\frac{p}{q},\qquad p,q\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0},\qquad\gcd(p,q)=1.

(94)

Thus, unlike the harmonic oscillator, the resonance condition depends on the
partial energies as well as on the anisotropy parameter
A
.

Equivalently, for a prescribed
1:n
resonance, Eq. (
93
)
can be solved for the anisotropy parameter:

A=n^{2N}\left(\frac{E_{x}}{E_{y}}\right)^{N-1}.

(95)

This expression shows explicitly that the nonlinear resonance is selected
jointly by the anisotropy parameter and the partial-energy ratio. Figures

and
4

illustrate the energy dependence of the nonlinear resonance condition.

shows the frequency ratio

4.3
Resonant condition and orbit equation

We now fix equal partial energies,

E_{x}=E_{y}=1,

(96)

and impose a
1\!:\!n
nonlinear resonance,

\frac{\Omega_{x}}{\Omega_{y}}=\frac{1}{n},\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}.

(97)

Using Eq. (
95
) on the equal-energy shell

E_{x}=E_{y}=1
, one obtains

A=n^{2N}.

(98)

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we shall denote

A_{n}=n^{2N}.

(99)

For
E_{x}=E_{y}=1
and
A=A_{n}
, the turning points are

x_{\max}=2^{1/(2N)},\qquad y_{\max}=\left(\frac{2}{A_{n}}\right)^{1/(2N)}=\frac{2^{1/(2N)}}{n}.

(100)

We introduce the dimensionless variables

\xi=\frac{x}{2^{1/(2N)}},\qquad\eta=\frac{y}{y_{\max}}=\frac{n\,y}{2^{1/(2N)}}.

(101)

For
N\geq 3
, the separated one-dimensional motions are described by
hyperelliptic integrals. Let

K_{N}=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{ds}{\sqrt{1-s^{2N}}},

(102)

and define the generalized cosine function
\mathrm{C}_{N}(u)
as the
real
4K_{N}
-periodic solution of

\left(\frac{d\,\mathrm{C}_{N}}{du}\right)^{2}=1-\mathrm{C}_{N}^{2N},\qquad\mathrm{C}_{N}(0)=1,\qquad\mathrm{C}_{N}^{\prime}(0)=0.

(103)

Equivalently, on the monotonic branch
0\leq u\leq K_{N}
,

u=\int_{\mathrm{C}_{N}(u)}^{1}\frac{ds}{\sqrt{1-s^{2N}}}.

(104)

With these definitions, the resonant solutions can be written as

x(t)=2^{1/(2N)}\,\mathrm{C}_{N}(u),\qquad y(t)=\frac{2^{1/(2N)}}{n}\,\mathrm{C}_{N}(nu+\Delta),

(105)

where

u=\frac{2^{(N-1)/(2N)}}{\sqrt{m}}\,t+\varphi_{x},\qquad\Delta=\varphi_{y}-n\varphi_{x}.

(106)

The constant
\Delta
is the relative phase and determines the particular
member of the closed resonant family.

Eliminating the time variable gives the implicit orbit relation

\eta=\mathrm{C}_{N}\left(n\,\mathrm{C}_{N}^{-1}(\xi)+\Delta\right),

(107)

with the appropriate branch of
\mathrm{C}_{N}^{-1}
chosen along each
monotonic segment of the motion. Equivalently, on a monotonic branch,

\int_{\eta}^{1}\frac{ds}{\sqrt{1-s^{2N}}}=n\int_{\xi}^{1}\frac{ds}{\sqrt{1-s^{2N}}}+\Delta\qquad\mathrm{mod}\;4K_{N}.

(108)

Equations (
107
) and
(
108
) define the configuration-space orbit

F_{n}^{N}(x,y;\Delta)=0.

(109)

Thus, for
N\geq 3
, the orbit equation is naturally expressed in terms of
hyperelliptic phase variables. In special low-order cases the implicit
relation may reduce to an algebraic equation, but there is no universal
polynomial formula of the form

y^{2}(2+x^{2N})^{n}-C_{n}^{N}x^{2}(2-x^{2N})^{n-1}=0

for the general
N\geq 3
system. The mass
m
fixes only the time scale
and drops out of the orbit geometry.

4.4
Particular integrals of motion

For
N\geq 3
, the resonant orbit is not described, in general, by a
universal algebraic polynomial relation in
x
and
y
. The construction
of particular integrals must therefore be formulated in terms of the
hyperelliptic phase variables introduced above.

We work on the equal-energy resonant shell

{\cal H}^{(N)}_{x}=1,\qquad{\cal H}^{(N)}_{y}=1,\qquad A=A_{n}=n^{2N}.

(110)

The corresponding turning points are

x_{\max}=2^{1/(2N)},\qquad y_{\max}=\frac{2^{1/(2N)}}{n}.

(111)

We use the dimensionless variables

\xi=\frac{x}{2^{1/(2N)}},\qquad\eta=\frac{n\,y}{2^{1/(2N)}}.

(112)

Let
{\rm C}_{N}(u)
be the generalized cosine defined by

\left(\frac{d{\rm C}_{N}}{du}\right)^{2}=1-{\rm C}_{N}^{2N},\qquad{\rm C}_{N}(0)=1,\qquad{\rm C}^{\prime}_{N}(0)=0,

(113)

with real period
4K_{N}
, where

K_{N}=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{ds}{\sqrt{1-s^{2N}}}.

(114)

On the resonant shell (
110
), the motion may be
written as

x(t)=2^{1/(2N)}{\rm C}_{N}(\theta_{x}),\qquad y(t)=\frac{2^{1/(2N)}}{n}{\rm C}_{N}(\theta_{y})\ .

(115)

Away from the turning points, the phases
\theta_{x}
and
\theta_{y}
are
understood as phase-space functions obtained by inverting
{\rm C}_{N}
, with
the branch selected by the signs of
p_{x}
and
p_{y}
, respectively. At the
turning points they are defined by continuity.
The phase variables evolve according to

\theta_{x}=\omega_{N}t+\varphi_{x},\qquad\theta_{y}=n\omega_{N}t+\varphi_{y},\qquad\omega_{N}=\frac{2^{(N-1)/(2N)}}{\sqrt{m}}.

(116)

Thus

\dot{\theta}_{y}=n\dot{\theta}_{x}.

(117)

It follows that the phase combination

J^{(N,n)}=\theta_{y}-n\theta_{x}\qquad\mathrm{mod}\;4K_{N}

(118)

is constant along the resonant motion.

Equivalently, for a fixed relative phase

\Delta=\varphi_{y}-n\varphi_{x},

(119)

the resonant invariant manifold is defined by

J^{(N,n)}-\Delta=0\qquad\mathrm{mod}\;4K_{N}.

(120)

A single-valued representative of this particular integral is

I^{(N,n)}_{\Delta,c}=\cos\!\left[\frac{\pi}{2K_{N}}\left(\theta_{y}-n\theta_{x}-\Delta\right)\right],

(121)

or, equivalently,

I^{(N,n)}_{\Delta,s}=\sin\!\left[\frac{\pi}{2K_{N}}\left(\theta_{y}-n\theta_{x}-\Delta\right)\right].

(122)

Locally on each phase branch, these functions satisfy

\left.\left\{{\cal H}^{(N)},I^{(N,n)}_{\Delta,c}\right\}\right|_{{\cal H}^{(N)}_{x}={\cal H}^{(N)}_{y}=1,\;A=n^{2N}}=0,\qquad\left.\left\{{\cal H}^{(N)},I^{(N,n)}_{\Delta,s}\right\}\right|_{{\cal H}^{(N)}_{x}={\cal H}^{(N)}_{y}=1,\;A=n^{2N}}=0.

(123)

They are not conserved as global functions on the full phase space. They are
therefore particular integrals associated with the resonant invariant
manifold.

On a fixed monotonic branch, this construction can be written directly in
terms of hyperelliptic integrals. Define

{\cal A}_{N}(z)=\int_{z}^{1}\frac{ds}{\sqrt{1-s^{2N}}}.

(124)

Then the phase constraint becomes

{\cal A}_{N}(\eta)-n\,{\cal A}_{N}(\xi)-\Delta=0\qquad\mathrm{mod}\;4K_{N}.

(125)

Equation (
125
) is the hyperelliptic analogue
of the algebraic orbit constraint used in the harmonic and quartic cases.

Lowest resonances.

For
n=1
, one has
A=1
. The particular phase integral is

J^{(N,1)}=\theta_{y}-\theta_{x}\qquad\mathrm{mod}\;4K_{N}.

(126)

For the phase-locked choices
\Delta=0
and
\Delta=2K_{N}
, this includes
the invariant lines
x=y
and
x=-y
, respectively. Along these invariant
lines the angular momentum

L_{z}=xp_{y}-yp_{x}

(127)

vanishes and remains constant. In the full phase space, however,

\left\{{\cal H}^{(N)},L_{z}\right\}=Nxy\left(y^{2N-2}-x^{2N-2}\right),

(128)

which is not identically zero for
N\geq 2
. Thus
L_{z}
is not a global
integral of the nonlinear oscillator, although it becomes a particular
integral on the special invariant lines.

For
n=2
, the resonant anisotropy is

A=2^{2N},

(129)

and the corresponding particular integral is

J^{(N,2)}=\theta_{y}-2\theta_{x}\qquad\mathrm{mod}\;4K_{N}.

(130)

For the sextic oscillator
N=3
, this gives
A=2^{6}=64
.

For
n=3
, the resonant anisotropy is

A=3^{2N},

(131)

and the corresponding particular integral is

J^{(N,3)}=\theta_{y}-3\theta_{x}\qquad\mathrm{mod}\;4K_{N}.

(132)

For the sextic oscillator
N=3
, this gives
A=3^{6}=729
.

Thus, for
N\geq 3
, the particular integrals are naturally expressed as
hyperelliptic phase constraints rather than as universal polynomial functions
of
x
and
y
. The mass
m
fixes only the common time scale and does
not enter the orbit geometry or the resonant phase constraint.

5
Restriction to resonant trajectories and loss of globality

A natural question arising from the previous sections is whether the
trajectory-dependent integrals of the nonlinear systems can be related to the
global integrals of the harmonic oscillator. Such a comparison must be made
with care. The quantities

{\cal H}^{(1)}_{x},\qquad{\cal H}^{(1)}_{y},\qquad I^{(1)}_{3}

are global integrals for the harmonic Hamiltonian
{\cal H}^{(1)}
, but
they are not, in general, conserved by the nonlinear Hamiltonians

{\cal H}^{(N)}
with
N\geq 2
. Thus they cannot be used as global
constants of motion for the anharmonic systems.

Nevertheless, after restriction to a resonant invariant manifold, functional
independence among phase-space functions is reduced. On such a lower
dimensional set, additional trajectory-dependent relations may appear. These
relations are useful for describing the resonant orbit, but they do not define
global identities on phase space and do not imply the existence of an
additional global symmetry.

5.1
General setting

For the harmonic oscillator
(N=1)
, the system is globally
superintegrable for rational frequency ratios. Besides the Hamiltonian,

{\cal H}^{(1)}=\frac{1}{2m}(p_{x}^{2}+p_{y}^{2})+\frac{1}{2}(x^{2}+Ay^{2}),

(133)

one may choose the partial energies

{\cal H}^{(1)}_{x}=\frac{p_{x}^{2}}{2m}+\frac{x^{2}}{2},\qquad{\cal H}^{(1)}_{y}=\frac{p_{y}^{2}}{2m}+\frac{Ay^{2}}{2},

(134)

together with an additional resonant integral
I^{(1)}_{3}
. These quantities
are conserved throughout phase space for the harmonic flow.

For the anharmonic systems
N\geq 2
, the globally defined Liouville
integrals are instead the nonlinear partial energies

{\cal H}^{(N)}_{x}=\frac{p_{x}^{2}}{2m}+\frac{x^{2N}}{2},\qquad{\cal H}^{(N)}_{y}=\frac{p_{y}^{2}}{2m}+\frac{Ay^{2N}}{2}.

(135)

Additional quantities associated with closed nonlinear Lissajous trajectories
are conserved only after restriction to the corresponding resonant invariant
manifold. Thus, if
\mathcal{R}^{(\Delta)}_{1:n}
denotes the phase-locked resonant manifold selected by the
1:n
nonlinear resonance condition and the relative phase
\Delta
, then the associated particular
integrals satisfy

\left.\left\{{\cal H}^{(N)},I^{(N)}_{\mathrm{part}}\right\}\right|_{\mathcal{R}^{(\Delta)}_{1:n}}=0,\qquad\left\{{\cal H}^{(N)},I^{(N)}_{\mathrm{part}}\right\}\not\equiv 0

(136)

as an identity on the full phase space. Equivalently, these quantities are
conserved on the selected resonant manifold, but they do not define global
integrals of the anharmonic Hamiltonian. These are the particular integrals
discussed above.

5.2
Nontriviality of the particular integrals

It is important to distinguish the present construction from the tautological fact that an arbitrary function vanishing on a single trajectory is constant along that trajectory. The particular integrals considered here are not introduced in this way. They arise from the nonlinear phase variables of the separated motions and from the resonance condition imposed by the frequency map. On a resonant invariant manifold the phases satisfy
\dot{\theta}_{y}=n\dot{\theta}_{x}
, and hence the phase combination
J^{(N,n)}=\theta_{y}-n\theta_{x}
is conserved modulo the appropriate period. This conservation law is intrinsic to the resonant dynamics and is independent of a posteriori choices of a defining function for an individual orbit.

Therefore, the nontrivial content of the particular integral is twofold. First, it gives a constructive phase-locking invariant that characterizes an entire resonant family rather than a single parametrized curve. Second, it fails to be a global integral: its Poisson bracket with the Hamiltonian vanishes only after restriction to the resonant invariant manifold, and not as an identity on the full phase space. In the quartic case this phase-locking invariant can be converted, through Jacobi elliptic multiplication formulae, into algebraic orbit constraints. For
N\geq 3
, the same mechanism produces hyperelliptic phase constraints. The particular integral therefore encodes the localization of superintegrable behavior on nonlinear resonant manifolds, rather than a hidden global symmetry of the anharmonic oscillator.

5.3
Why no global promotion is possible

A trajectory-dependent relation obtained on a resonant manifold cannot be
promoted to a global conservation law unless its Poisson bracket with the
nonlinear Hamiltonian vanishes identically. This fails for the quartic and
higher-degree systems considered here. The corresponding additional
quantities are tied to the resonance condition and to the chosen invariant
manifold; away from that manifold, their Poisson brackets with

{\cal H}^{(N)}
are nonzero.

Thus the obstruction is not merely algebraic. It is dynamical: the closed
orbits are selected by energy-dependent resonance conditions, and the
associated constraints are conserved only on the selected resonant sets.

5.4
Restriction to resonant trajectories

The preceding obstruction disappears only in a limited and
trajectory-dependent sense. Once the dynamics is restricted to a fixed
resonant invariant manifold
\mathcal{R}^{(\Delta)}_{1:n}
, functional independence
among phase-space functions is reduced. In particular, on a trajectory

\gamma_{1:n}\subset\mathcal{R}^{(\Delta)}_{1:n}
, the motion is effectively
one-dimensional, and additional relations may hold along that trajectory.
Such relations are not identities on the full phase space and should not be
interpreted as global conservation laws.

In particular, for the equal-energy normalization used throughout the
nonlinear examples one has

{\cal H}^{(N)}_{x}={\cal H}^{(N)}_{y}=1,

whereas the resonance condition is imposed through the anisotropy parameter,
namely
A=n^{4}
for the quartic case and
A=n^{2N}
for the general

N\geq 3
case. Thus the
1\!:\!n
resonance should not be represented by
relations such as
{\cal H}^{(N)}_{x}=n{\cal H}^{(N)}_{y}
. Likewise, except
for the
1\!:\!1
phase-locked orbit, the resonant trajectories are not
straight lines such as
y=x/\sqrt{n}
.

Example 1: Angular momentum for the quartic
1\!:\!1
resonance.

For the isotropic harmonic oscillator, the angular momentum

L_{z}=xp_{y}-yp_{x}

(137)

is a global integral of motion. For the isotropic quartic oscillator

{\cal H}^{(2)}=\frac{p_{x}^{2}+p_{y}^{2}}{2m}+\frac{1}{2}(x^{4}+y^{4}),

one instead finds

\left\{{\cal H}^{(2)},L_{z}\right\}=2xy\left(y^{2}-x^{2}\right),

(138)

with the convention
\dot{f}=\{f,{\cal H}\}
or, equivalently,

\{{\cal H},f\}=-\dot{f}
depending on the sign convention for the Poisson
bracket. In either convention the bracket is not identically zero. Hence

L_{z}
is not a global integral of the quartic oscillator.

Nevertheless, on the invariant lines

x=y,\qquad p_{x}=p_{y},

(139)

or

x=-y,\qquad p_{x}=-p_{y},

(140)

the angular momentum vanishes and remains constant. Thus
L_{z}
is a
particular integral on these special invariant manifolds. This is a
trajectory-level statement: it does not imply the existence of a global
rotational symmetry for the quartic oscillator.

Example 2: Quartic oscillator,
1\!:\!2
resonance.

For the quartic system with

A=2^{4},\qquad{\cal H}^{(2)}_{x}={\cal H}^{(2)}_{y}=1,

(141)

the nonlinear frequency ratio is

\frac{\Omega_{x}}{\Omega_{y}}=\frac{1}{2}.

The phase-locked resonant orbit is not the straight line

y=x/\sqrt{2}
. It is the algebraic curve obtained in Sec. 3,

F^{(0)}_{2^{4}}(x,y)=2^{3/4}y\left(2+2\sqrt{2}\,x^{2}-x^{4}\right)-x^{4}-2\sqrt{2}\,x^{2}+2=0.

(142)

Using the energy-shell identity

x^{4}=2-\frac{p_{x}^{2}}{m},

one may equivalently use the phase-space representative

I^{(2)}_{3}(x,y,p_{x})=2^{3/4}y\left(\frac{p_{x}^{2}}{m}+2\sqrt{2}\,x^{2}\right)+\frac{p_{x}^{2}}{m}-2\sqrt{2}\,x^{2}.

(143)

On the resonant trajectory
\gamma_{1:2}^{(0)}
this function satisfies

I^{(2)}_{3}\big|_{\gamma_{1:2}^{(0)}}=0,\qquad\left.\left\{{\cal H}^{(2)},I^{(2)}_{3}\right\}\right|_{\gamma_{1:2}^{(0)}}=0.

(144)

However,

\left\{{\cal H}^{(2)},I^{(2)}_{3}\right\}\not\equiv 0

on the full phase space. Thus
I^{(2)}_{3}
is a particular integral
associated with the chosen resonant orbit, not a global integral.

If one evaluates the harmonic partial energies

{\cal H}^{(1)}_{x}=\frac{p_{x}^{2}}{2m}+\frac{x^{2}}{2},\qquad{\cal H}^{(1)}_{y}=\frac{p_{y}^{2}}{2m}+\frac{Ay^{2}}{2}

along this quartic trajectory, they are merely auxiliary functions of the
quartic motion; they are not conserved by
{\cal H}^{(2)}
. Therefore a
relation such as

I^{(2)}_{3}=C_{2}\left({\cal H}^{(1)}_{x}-2{\cal H}^{(1)}_{y}\right)^{2}

is not a valid trajectory-independent identity and should not be used.

Example 3: Sextic oscillator and the
N\geq 3
case.

For the sextic oscillator
N=3
, the
1\!:\!3
resonance with equal
partial energies is obtained by choosing

A=3^{2N}=3^{6}=729,\qquad{\cal H}^{(3)}_{x}={\cal H}^{(3)}_{y}=1.

(145)

The resonant manifold is not described by

{\cal H}^{(3)}_{x}=3{\cal H}^{(3)}_{y},\qquad y=\frac{x}{\sqrt{3}},\qquad p_{y}=\frac{p_{x}}{\sqrt{3}}.

Rather, it is described by the hyperelliptic phase constraint derived in
Sec. 4. In the notation of that section, the dimensionless variables satisfy

\eta={\rm C}_{3}\left(3\,{\rm C}_{3}^{-1}(\xi)+\Delta\right),

(146)

or equivalently, on a monotonic branch,

\int_{\eta}^{1}\frac{ds}{\sqrt{1-s^{6}}}=3\int_{\xi}^{1}\frac{ds}{\sqrt{1-s^{6}}}+\Delta\qquad\mathrm{mod}\;4K_{3}.

(147)

The associated particular integral is therefore the conserved phase
combination

J^{(3,3)}=\theta_{y}-3\theta_{x}\qquad\mathrm{mod}\;4K_{3},

(148)

where
\theta_{x}
and
\theta_{y}
are the hyperelliptic phase variables
defined by the inverse of the generalized cosine function.

More generally, for
N\geq 3
and
A=n^{2N}
, the resonant invariant
manifold is characterized by

J^{(N,n)}=\theta_{y}-n\theta_{x}\qquad\mathrm{mod}\;4K_{N}.

(149)

This phase constraint is the appropriate analogue of the algebraic
trajectory constraint in the quartic case. It does not reduce, in general, to
a polynomial identity involving only
x
,
y
, and the harmonic oscillator
integrals.

Consequently, trajectory-restricted relations do exist, but they are
non-unique and depend on the chosen resonant manifold. They express the loss
of functional independence after restriction to a particular orbit; they do
not reveal a hidden global symmetry of the nonlinear oscillator.

5.5
Interpretation

The preceding examples show that, after restriction to a resonant invariant
manifold, additional trajectory-dependent relations may appear among the
phase-space variables. In the quartic case these relations can be expressed
as algebraic orbit constraints, while for
N\geq 3
they are naturally
written as hyperelliptic phase constraints.

Such relations reflect the loss of functional independence caused by
restricting the dynamics to a lower-dimensional invariant manifold. They do
not signal the presence of an additional global symmetry, nor do they imply
that the nonlinear system inherits the global superintegrability of the
harmonic oscillator.

In this sense, particular integrability in nonlinear separable systems is a
genuinely trajectory-level phenomenon. It is generated by nonlinear resonance:
the resonance condition selects special invariant manifolds, and the
corresponding particular integrals define those manifolds rather than
providing global labels for all trajectories.

6
Discussion and Conclusions

Our results show that nonlinear Lissajous orbits arise naturally in separable
anharmonic systems through nonlinear resonance. In the harmonic oscillator,
closed trajectories are consequences of global superintegrability: additional
integrals exist throughout phase space and organize entire families of
periodic motions. In anharmonic systems, by contrast, the oscillation
frequencies depend on the partial energies, and closure occurs only on
resonant invariant manifolds selected by the initial data.

The central point of this work is therefore not merely the construction of
closed orbits, but the clarification of how integrability is reorganized once
global superintegrability is lost. For
N=1
, rational frequency ratios lead
to global additional integrals and hence to closed Lissajous trajectories
throughout the relevant bounded region of phase space. For
N\geq 2
, no
analogous global symmetry algebra exists in general. The system remains
Liouville integrable by separability, but the additional structures associated
with closed motion become trajectory-dependent.

In the quartic case, the resonant orbits can be written as algebraic
constraints obtained from elliptic multiplication formulas. For
N\geq 3
,
the corresponding constraints are naturally expressed in terms of
hyperelliptic phase variables. In both cases, the associated particular
integrals should not be interpreted as global conserved charges labeling all
motions. Rather, they define the resonant invariant manifolds on which the
closed trajectories occur.

From this viewpoint, the fact that a particular integral may vanish on its
associated trajectory is not a defect or a triviality. Its significance lies
in the restricted conservation law: its Poisson bracket with the Hamiltonian
vanishes on the selected resonant manifold, but not as an identity on the
full phase space. The particular integral therefore encodes the closure of
the dynamics on a lower-dimensional invariant set.

Thus, integrability in nonlinear separable systems does not disappear when
global superintegrability is lost; it becomes localized on resonant
manifolds. Global integrals continue to provide Liouville integrability,
while trajectory-dependent invariants describe the exceptional closed
orbits selected by nonlinear resonance.
