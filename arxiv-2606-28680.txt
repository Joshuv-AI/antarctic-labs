Persistent Subdivisions of Coxeter Permutahedra
Timothy Blanton, Jesús A. De Loera, and Melissa Sherman-Bennett

Abstract
. We investigate the realizations of Coxeter permutahedra which are also Coxeter matroid polytopes; these are polytopes of the form conv ⁡ ( W ⋅ 𝐚 ) \conv(W\cdot\mathbf{a}) where W W is a finite Coxeter group acting on ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} and 𝐚 \mathbf{a} is generic. Our main focus is how the geometric properties of conv ⁡ ( W ⋅ 𝐚 ) \conv(W\cdot\mathbf{a}) change as 𝐚 \mathbf{a} changes, with particular attention to persistent simplices, triangulations, and subdivisions.



1.
Introduction

The permutahedron is a classical polytope associated with the symmetric group, first studied by Schoute in 1911
[
27
]
. Its vertices are labeled bijectively by the elements of
S_{n}
and its geometry reflects the combinatorics of
S_{n}
. Its one-skeleton is the Hasse diagram of the weak order. Its face lattice is isomorphic to the lattice of cosets of parabolic subgroups (ordered by inclusion), or equivalently, to ordered set partitions of
[n]
(ordered by coarsening). The best known realization of the permutahedron is the convex hull of all permutation vectors
\{(w(1),\dots,w(n)):w\in S_{n}\}
, which we call the
regular permutahedron
. This polytope has a myriad of interpretations and connections to other objects in algebraic and geometric combinatorics (see
[
7
,
18
,
17
,
22
,
20
]
and the references therein). The regular permutahedron is the graphical zonotope associated to the complete graph; the Minkowski sum of the type A roots; the flag matroid polytope of the uniform complete flag matroid; the secondary polytope of a simplicial prism
[
14
]
; and the moment polytope of a generic torus orbit in the complete flag variety.
The normal fan of the regular permutahedron is the famous braid arrangement, providing a connection to algebraic geometry. For instance,
[
12
]
constructs the permutohedral variety—the toric variety whose fan is the braid arrangement—which has played an important role in the Chow theory of matroids.

The permutahedron is a “type A” object which can be generalized to arbitrary finite Coxeter groups
W
. Such groups act on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
by reflections across hyperplanes, and the normal vectors to the hyperplanes form the corresponding (not necessarily crystallographic) root system
\Phi
. The union of the reflecting hyperplanes forms the
W
-braid arrangement (see
[
7
,
18
]
for more detail). The
W
-permutahedron again has vertices labeled by
W
; one-skeleton equal to the Hasse diagram of the weak order on
W
; and a face lattice isomorphic to the lattice of cosets of parabolic subgroups of
W
. Many of the connections mentioned above between the permutahedron and other topics in algebraic and geometric combinatorics extend to the
W
-permutahedron. For example, there is a
regular
W
-permutahedron
, which is the Minkowski sum of the positive roots of
\Phi
, or alternately, the convex hull of the orbit
W\cdot\mathbf{a}
where
\mathbf{a}
is the sum of positive roots. The regular
W
-permutahedron has normal fan equal to the
W
-braid arrangement. The regular
W
-permutahedron is a Coxeter matroid polytope for
W
, and when
W
is crystallographic, it is the moment polytope of a generic torus orbit in
G/B

[
9
]
.

There are many other realizations of the
W
-permutahedron besides the regular one. Recall that the
realization space

\mathcal{R}(P)
of a polytope
P
is a semi-algebraic set inside
(\mathbb{R}^{\dim P})^{\text{Vertices}(P)}
encoding (the vertices of) all polytopes with the same face lattice as
P
(see
[
8
,
25
]
for formal definitions). The entire
\mathcal{R}(P)
is a mysterious, difficult object even for well-studied polytopes such as the
W
-permutahedron. However, since the face lattice of the
W
-permutahedron is preserved under the
W
-action, it is natural to consider the realizations where the vertices themselves are invariant under
W
-action. We call such realizations
orbit
W
-permutahedra
, and the corresponding subset of the realization space the
orbit realization space
; these realizations are the central focus of our results.

It is not hard to show that the
W
-orbit permutahedra are exactly the polytopes
P^{W}(\mathbf{a}):=\conv(W\cdot\mathbf{a})
, where
\mathbf{a}
is a point in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus H^{W}
. That is, orbit permutahedra are exactly the convex hulls of generic
W
-orbits. The orbit realization space
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{O}}^{W}
is the subset of
\mathcal{R}(P^{W}(\mathbf{a}))
consisting of orbit permutahedra. This class of realizations has been studied before. When
W=S_{n}
, their facet inequalities are described by a classical result of Rado
[
23
]
, and their volume and Ehrhart polynomials were studied in the beautiful work of Postnikov
[
22
]
. Other combinatorial properties when
W=S_{n}
are described in
[
17
,
20
]
. For general
W
, orbit permutahedra are exactly the realizations of the
W
-permutahedron which are Coxeter matroid polytopes in the sense of
[
9
]
; they are in bijection with points in the interior of the
W
-symmetric
\Phi
-submodular cone
[
1
]
.

Our results:

Now we summarize our results.
We first show in (
4.1
) is that the orbit realization space
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{O}}^{W}
is homeomorphic to an open ball. This
opens up the central question of this paper: how do geometric properties of
P^{W}(\mathbf{a})
change as
\mathbf{a}
varies continuously in one chamber of
H^{W}
? We investigate this question with a particular eye towards properties that
persist
as
\mathbf{a}
varies. In particular we investigate which triangulations persist. Triangulations of permutahedra is a topic that has been studied for several years (see, e.g.,
[
2
]
).

Our initial motivation for this question was
[
15
, Theorem 1.5]
, which provided many triangulations of
P^{W}(\mathbf{a})
that persist for all choices of
\mathbf{a}
. We characterize the persistent triangulations of
P^{W}(\mathbf{a})
: they are exactly the triangulations whose individual simplices are persistent (
5.1
). We then study persistent simplices from a number of perspectives, providing some sufficient conditions and asymptotics. Persistent simplices are closely related to the behavior of the
oriented matroid

[
8
]
of the point configuration
W\cdot\mathbf{a}
as
\mathbf{a}
varies, which turns out to be quite subtle (see Section
3
).

We also present some results on persistent subdivisions and triangulations. As we mentioned above,
P^{W}(\mathbf{a})
is a Coxeter matroid polytope. We show that all
matroidal
subdivisions of
P^{W}(\mathbf{a})
are persistent (and in fact one can relax
P^{W}(\mathbf{a})
to an arbitrary Coxeter matroid polytope
P
for
W
). When
P
is a type A flag matroid polytope, these subdivisions are closely related to the tropical flag variety and the flag Dressian
[
10
]
; specifically, the
regular
matroidal subdivisions of
P
correspond to cones in the flag Dressian.

Structure of the paper:

We begin with Section
2
on some general reasons why persistence of structures is interesting from a combinatorial perspective. In Section
3
, we turn to our specific case of interest, and give definitions and an exploration of
W
-permutahedra for small
n
. This is an experimental investigation of what is preserved or persists under different choices of
\mathbf{a}
, focusing on simplices and coplanarity. We provide some numerics, including the number of different oriented matroids possible for the point configurations
W\cdot\mathbf{a}
. Next, in Section
4
we discuss the contractibility of the orbit realization space, showing that for orbit realizations the space is homeomorphic to an open cone. In Section
5
we give a sufficient and necessary condition for a triangulation to persist, and some sufficient conditions for simplices to persist. We also briefly discuss the case of “generic” triangulations and simplices, which persist for most
\mathbf{a}
rather than all
\mathbf{a}
. In Section
6
we give some asymptotics on the number of generic and persistent simplices using a “lifting from facets” argument. In Section
7
we show that matroidal subdivisions of
W
-matroid polytopes are persistent.

2.
Interlude: Why Do Persistent Structures matter in Combinatorics?

The reader may wonder, why should I care about persistent subdivisions, triangulations or normal fans? While the focus of our paper is specifically about persistence for Coxeter realizations of permutahedra and generalized permutahedra, we outline two good reasons why researchers may care about persistent triangulations and subdivisions, or persistent normal fans in general polytopes.

Persistence of triangulations always yields volume and integral formulas

First, the existence of persistent triangulations is in fact very useful to combinatorially compute not just the volume but the value of any integral of a polynomial over a persistent triangulation.

In order to find the formulas or expressions for the volume of any realization of
P_{n}
, we triangulate it with a persistent triangulation and find the volume of
each simplex. For example, when the face lattice does not change, a persistent triangulation is the
barycentric triangulation
. This is a classical and widely used triangulation in computational and combinatorial geometry
[
14
]
. Intuitively, after determining the faces of
P
(this is done through their supporting hyperplanes that define facets), first each two-dimensional face is subdivided by introducing its barycenter and coning to the edges.
k
-dimensional faces are triangulated inductively by coning to the barycenters of all its
k-1
-dimensional bounding faces. This produces the canonical barycentric subdivision of
P
. Once we have a triangulation that is invariant, we can compute the volume or integral of the polytope by computing them in each separate simplex. Note that already the volume formula of a simplex can be directly read from its vertices:

(1)

\operatorname{vol}(\Delta)=\frac{1}{(d-1)!}\det\left(\left[s_{2}-s_{1},s_{3}-s_{1},\dots,s_{d}-s_{1},\frac{a}{\|a\|}\right]\right),

where
a
is any vector orthogonal to the affine span of the simplex. With two different realizations of
P
the simplices do not change, thus the above formula does not change and the volume of
P
is then the sum of the volumes of these persistent simplices.

More generally, to compute the integral of a polynomial
f
, our approach again is to use any persistent triangulation as outlined before.
For each simplex in the triangulation, we compute formulas for the integral over that simplex using
[
3
, Lemma 8]
, which provides a closed-form expression for the integral of a power of a linear form over a simplex. Let
M\in Z_{\geq 0}
, and let
\Delta=\conv(s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{n+1})
be an
n
-dimensional simplex in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For any linear form
\ell
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

(2)

\int_{\Delta}\ell^{M}\,\mathrm{d}x=n!\,\mathrm{vol}(\Delta)\,\frac{M!}{(M+n)!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{N}^{n+1}\\
|k|=M\end{subarray}}\langle\ell,s_{1}\rangle^{k_{1}}\cdots\langle\ell,s_{n+1}\rangle^{k_{n+1}},

where we integrate with respect to the standard Lebesgue measure.
In the case of
M=0
, the integral in the formula above, reduces to computing the volume of a simplex, which we saw only depends on the coordinates of the vertices of the persistent simplex. Finally, to integrate an arbitrary polynomial
f
, we simply need to rewrite it as a sum of powers of linear forms.

When the normal fan is preserved, Ehrhart formulas are preserved.

In our case, for orbit permutahedra, not only the face lattice is preserved but in fact they have the same
normal fan
, or equivalently, the same
tangent cone
. We recall that for a convex polytope
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For each face
F\leq P
, the
normal cone
of
F
is

N_{P}(F)=\{c\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\langle c,x\rangle\geq\langle c,y\rangle\text{ for all }x\in F,\,y\in P\}.

Equivalently,
N_{P}(F)
is the set of linear objectives
c
for which

F\subseteq\arg\max_{P}\langle c,\cdot\rangle
, i.e., every point of
F
is optimal.

The
normal fan
of
P
is the complete polyhedral fan

\mathcal{N}(P)=\{N_{P}(F):F\leq P\},

whose cones are exactly the normal cones of all faces of
P
(including
P
itself, whose normal cone is
\{0\}
, and the empty face, whose normal cone is all of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
), but the most important cones are the normal cones at vertices because we can recover the others from there. Two polytopes
P
and
Q
have the same normal fan if and only if they are
normally equivalent
, which holds, in particular, when
Q
is a Minkowski summand of a dilate of
P
. This happens, for instance, in the case of generalized permutahedra. Similarly, for a vertex
v
of a polytope
P
, the
tangent cone
of
P
at
v
is defined as

Cone_{P}(v):=\left\{\,v+w\;|\;v+\epsilon w\in P\text{ for some }\epsilon>0\,\right\}.

The key fact is that if one knows the tangent cone at a vertex one can recover the normal cone at a vertex, and vice versa, using polarity. A motivating example of families of polytopes with the same tangent normal fans are matroid polytopes
[
13
]
and more generally for generalized W-permutahedra
[
7
,
20
,
11
]
.

For our purposes, having same fan structure within a set of realizations (like Coxeter realizations) is notable because then one can conclude, from the theory developed by Barvinok, Stanley and others
[
4
,
5
,
28
]
, that their
Ehrhart polynomials
are, in fact, recoverable in a single parametric formula. Recall that for

P\subseteq^{n}
be a rational polyhedron. The
multivariate
generating function
of
P
is defined as the formal Laurent series in
{\mathbb{Z}}[[z_{1},\ldots,z_{n},z_{1}^{-1},\ldots,z_{n}^{-1}]]

\tilde{g}_{P}(\mathbf{z})=\sum_{\alpha\in P\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}}\mathbf{z}^{\alpha},

where we use the multi-exponent notation
z^{\alpha}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}z_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}
. This is sometimes called the
integer-point transform

[
6
]
as its monomials are in bijection with the integer points of
P
. If
P
is bounded,
\tilde{g}_{P}
is a Laurent polynomial, which we consider as a rational function
g_{P}
.
If
P
is not bounded but is pointed (i.e.,
P
does not contain a
straight line), there is a non-empty open subset
U\subseteq{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
such
that the series converges absolutely and uniformly on every compact
subset of
U
to a rational function
g_{\lx@paragraphsign}
(see
[
4
]
and
references therein). If
P
contains a straight line, we set
g_{\mathrm{Po}}=0
. The rational function
g_{P}\in{\mathbb{Q}}(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})
defined in this
way is called the
multivariate rational generating function

of
P\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
. Barvinok
[
5
]
proved that in polynomial
time, when the dimension of a polytope is fixed,
g_{P}
can be represented
as a short sum of rational functions

g_{P}(z)=\sum_{i\in I}\epsilon_{i}\frac{z^{a_{i}}}{\prod_{j=1}^{n}(1-z^{b_{ij}})},

where
\epsilon_{i}\in\{-1,1\}
.
The multivariate generating function formula for the lattice points can be computed from the tangent cones. Moreover, the Ehrhart polynomial can be recovered directly from the rational function expression using special substitutions. See
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
for details.

Thus, we can apply the above algorithms to obtain formulas for the volumes or Ehrhart polynomials of permutahedra, matroid polytopes, generalized permutahedra, or any other family of polytopes for which the triangulation, tangent cone or normal fan do not change.
An illustrative example is the celebrated paper by Postnikov stating formulas the volumes and Ehrhart polynomials of orbit permutahedra
[
22
]
. Another example: to obtain formulas for Ehrhart polynomials of matroid polytopes,
[
13
]
used the fact that the tangent cones were determined by the combinatorial information alone.

In summary, if a family of polytopes admits a triangulation whose simplices persist across all realizations, then volumes and any other polynomial integrals can be computed simplex-by-simplex using the same combinatorial subdivision in every realization. Similarly, persistence of the normal fan, or its tangent-cone structure, leads to formulas for Ehrhart and lattice-point formulas for all polytopes with the same fan.

3.
Definitions and warmup

As in the introduction, let
W
be a finite reflection group acting on a Euclidean space
V
. We follow the conventions of
[
18
]
.

For each
w\in W
, we may choose a vector
\alpha\in V
such that
w
sends
\alpha
to
-\alpha
. We thus say that
w=s_{\alpha}
and
\alpha,-\alpha
are called
roots
for
W
. The collection of all roots is called a
root system
for
W
, labeled by
\Phi
.

We call a subset of roots
positive
if it consists of exactly the roots on one side of some hyperplane in
V
. A set of positive roots is denoted
\Phi_{+}
. We call a subset
\Delta
of roots
simple
if all
\alpha\in\Phi
are linear combinations of the elements of
\Delta
with either all coefficients nonpositive or all coefficients nonnegative. Each positive root system contains a unique simple root. Similarly, each simple root system is contained in a unique positive root system. For a fixed choice of positive root system
\Phi_{+}
(equivalently, simple system
\Delta
), each positive root system can be written as
w\cdot\Phi_{+}
for a unique
w\in W
.

Note also that each
s_{\alpha}\in W
defines a hyperplane
H_{\alpha}
, consisting of points orthogonal to
\alpha
.

Definition 3.1
.

The hyperplane arrangement
\bigcup_{\alpha\in\Phi}H_{\alpha}
is the
W
-braid arrangement
. The
chambers
of this arrangement are in bijection with positive subsets of roots. For a set of positive roots
\Phi_{+}
, the corresponding chamber is

B_{\Phi_{+}}=\{c\in V\mid\langle c,\alpha\rangle>0\text{ for all }\alpha\in\Phi_{+}\}.

We will fix throughout a chamber
B
as the fundamental chamber; with this choice, the chambers
B_{\Phi_{+}}
are in bijective correspondence with the elements of
W
and so we also write
B_{w}
.

As in the introduction, we denote
P^{W}(\mathbf{a})=\conv(W\cdot\mathbf{a})
for
\mathbf{a}\in B
. We first justify that regardless of
\mathbf{a}\in B
,
P^{W}(\mathbf{a})
deserves the name of “
W
-permutahedron”.

Lemma 3.2
.

For any
\mathbf{a}\in B
, the normal fan of
P^{W}(\mathbf{a})
is the
W
-braid arrangement.

Proof.

Let
v\in W
and
\mathbf{v}=v\cdot\mathbf{a}
. We start by showing the normal cone of
\mathbf{v}
is
\overline{B_{v}}
.
Recall that the normal cone of
\mathbf{v}
,
N_{\mathbf{v}}
, is given by

N_{\mathbf{v}}:=\left\{c\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\ast}\mid\langle c,\mathbf{v}\rangle=\underset{y\in P^{W}(\mathbf{a})}{\max}\langle c,y\rangle\right\}

Let
\Delta_{v}
be the set of simple roots defining
B_{v}
. We have that for any
w\in W
,

\mathbf{v}-w\cdot\mathbf{v}=\underset{\alpha\in\Delta}{\sum}k_{\alpha}\alpha

where
k_{\alpha}\geq 0
. (see
[
18
, Page 22]
). For all
c\in\overline{B_{v}}
, dotting both sides with
c
gives us that

\langle c,\mathbf{v}-w\cdot\mathbf{v}\rangle=\left\langle c,\underset{\alpha\in\Delta}{\sum}k_{\alpha}\alpha\right\rangle=\underset{\alpha\in\Delta}{\sum}k_{\alpha}\langle c,\alpha\rangle

Since
k_{\alpha}\geq 0
and
\langle c,\alpha\rangle\geq 0
, the entire sum is non-negative and so
\langle c,\mathbf{v}\rangle\geq\langle c,w\cdot\mathbf{v}\rangle
. As it suffices to compute maximums over the vertices of a polytope,
c\in N_{\mathbf{v}}
. Thus
\overline{B_{v}}\subseteq N_{\mathbf{v}}
.

Let
c\in N_{\mathbf{v}}
. By definition,
\langle c,\mathbf{v}\rangle\geq\langle c,y\rangle
for all
y\in P
, in particular it holds for
y=w\cdot\mathbf{v}
. Let
w=s_{\alpha}
for some choice of
\alpha\in\Delta
. Then
w\cdot\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{v}-2\frac{\langle v,\alpha\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}\alpha
. Substituting this into the original inequality gives us that

\langle c,\mathbf{v}\rangle\geq\left\langle c,\mathbf{v}-2\frac{\langle v,\alpha\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}\alpha\right\rangle

0\geq-2\frac{\langle\mathbf{v},\alpha\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}\langle c,\alpha\rangle

Since
\mathbf{v}\in B_{v}
,
\langle\mathbf{v},\alpha\rangle>0
. As
\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle>0
, we must have that
\langle c,\alpha\rangle\geq 0
. Since this holds true for all
\alpha\in\Delta
, we have that
c\in\overline{B_{v}}
by definition. Thus
N_{\mathbf{v}}=\overline{B_{v}}
.

Since the normal cone of each vertex is
\overline{B_{v}}
, the normal fan of
P^{W}(\mathbf{a})
is the
W
-braid arrangement and does not depend on the choice of
\mathbf{a}\in B
. As the normal fan of a polytope determines the combinatorial type, the combinatorial type of
P^{W}(\mathbf{a})
does not depend on the choice of
\mathbf{a}\in B
.
∎

As the vertices of
P^{W}(\mathbf{a})
are identified with
W
, the vertices of
P^{W}(\mathbf{a})
and
P^{W}(\mathbf{a}^{\prime})
are in natural correspondence and it makes sense to talk about structure “persisting” for different choices of base point. To streamline notation, we introduce the following.

Definition 3.3
.

For
\mathcal{S}\subseteq W
of size
d+1
and
\mathbf{a}\in B
,
\mathcal{S}
is called an
\mathbf{a}
-simplex
if
\conv(\mathcal{S}\cdot\mathbf{a})
is a
d
-dimensional simplex and is called
\mathbf{a}
-coplanar
otherwise. A collection
\{\mathcal{S}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{p}
of
\mathbf{a}
-simplices is called an
\mathbf{a}
-triangulation
if
\{\conv(\mathcal{S}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{a})\}_{i=1}^{p}
is a triangulation of
P^{W}(\mathbf{a})
. That is,
P^{W}(\mathbf{a})=\cup_{i}\conv(\mathcal{S}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{a})
and for all
i,j
,
\conv(\mathcal{S}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{a})
and
\conv(\mathcal{S}_{j}\cdot\mathbf{a})
intersect in a common face (possibly empty).

We note that the set of
\mathbf{a}
-simplices is exactly the set of bases of the
matroid
of
P^{W}(\mathbf{a})
. As we shall see in subsequent sections, the set of
\mathbf{a}
-simplices and of
\mathbf{a}
-triangulations is heavily dependent on the choice of
\mathbf{a}
. So we are more interested in the
following notion.

Definition 3.4
.

We say that
\mathcal{S}\subseteq W
of size

is a
persistent simplex
(respectively, is
persistently coplanar
) if it is an
\mathbf{a}
-simplex for every choice of
\mathbf{a}\in B
(respectively, is
\mathbf{a}
-coplanar for all
\mathbf{a}\in B
). A collection
\{\mathcal{S}_{i}\}
of persistent simplices is a
persistent triangulation
if
\{\mathcal{S}_{i}\}
is an
\mathbf{a}
-triangulation for each
\mathbf{a}\in B
.

Through several examples we illustrate these concepts in the next three subsections.

3.1.
The 2-dimensional Case

For
W
whose associated vector space

-permutahedron is a convex

-gon. Notice that

The realization space of the

-permutahedron is the realization space of a convex

-gon, which is contractible. However, not all combinatorial permutahedra are orbit permutahedra. One can easily find hexagons in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
which are not invariant under permuting coordinates. For emphasis, we record this in a proposition.

Proposition 3.5
.

The realization space of the

-permutahedron is strictly larger than the orbit realization space.

4.
Topology of orbit realization space

Now we would like to give some context for our results.
If
P
is a 2 or 3-dimensional polytope,
\mathcal{R}(P)
modulo affine transformations is contractible
[
30
]

[
29
]
. But in general,
\mathcal{R}(P)
can be extremely poorly behaved. Indeed, N. Mnëv
[
19
]
proved that realization spaces of convex polytopes (indeed, of oriented matroids)
can be as complicated as
any
semialgebraic set.
More precisely, for every primary semialgebraic set
V
defined over the integers, there exists a (sufficiently high-dimensional) convex polytope
P
whose realization space
\mathcal{R}(P)
is “stably equivalent” (in homotopy type, and rationally equivalent) to
V
. In particular, questions about solutions to polynomial equations over
\mathbb{R}
can be encoded as instances of polytope realization problems. Later work by J. Richter-Gebert
[
24
,
25
,
26
]
greatly strengthened the universality phenomenon by showing the complicated topology already occurs in 4-dimensional polytopes.

However, the situation for the orbit realization space is much simpler, as the following result shows.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
B
be a choice of fundamental chamber for some finite reflection group
W
and let
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{O}}^{W}=\{\conv(W\cdot\mathbf{a}):\mathbf{a}\in B\}
be the orbit realization space. Then
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{O}}^{W}
is homeomorphic to
B
.

Proof.

Fix some arbitrary ordering on
W
such that the identity is first. We think of
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{O}}^{W}
as a set of matrices living in
\mathbb{R}^{n\cdot|W|}
where each row is a vertex of the polytope. As the vertices are naturally in bijection with elements of
W
, we identify each row with some
w\in W
. As
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{O}}^{W}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n\cdot|W|}
, we endow it with the subspace topology.

Consider the function
f:B\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n\cdot|W|}
which sends
\mathbf{a}
to a matrix where the
w
th row is given by
w\cdot\mathbf{a}
. This is a linear function in the input variables, so it is continuous. By construction, we have that
\mathrm{im}(f)=\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{O}}^{W}
.

For the inverse, note that the map sending a matrix
P\in\mathbb{R}^{n\cdot|W|}
to its first row is a projection, so it is continuous. After restricting the domain to
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{O}}^{W}
, the restricted map and
f
are inverses. As
f
is a continuous map with continuous inverse, it is a homeomorphism and
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{O}}^{W}\cong B
.
∎

We note that
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{O}}^{W}
is clearly in bijection with
B
; the content of the above theorem is that this bijection is topologically well-behaved.

Corollary 4.2
.

The orbit realization space
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{O}}^{W}
is contractible and thus path-connected.

Proof.

Since
B
is a open cone, it is contractible and thus path-connected
[
16
]
. As
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{O}}^{W}
is homeomorphic to
B
, it is also contractible and path-connected.
∎

5.
Results on persistence of triangulations, simplices, and coplanar sets

The starting point for our investigation of persistence is the following result.

Theorem 5.1
.

An
\mathbf{a}
-triangulation is a persistent triangulation if and only if it consists of persistent simplices.

By definition, a persistent triangulation consists of persistent simplices, so one direction of the above theorem is clear. The converse direction is the more interesting one. It tells us that we just have to check that a collection of persistent simplices forms a triangulation for some
P^{W}(\mathbf{a})
, and then we know that they form a triangulation for all orbit permutahedra. The main tool for this result is the following proposition from
[
15
]
:

Proposition 5.2
.

[
15
, Proposition A.1]

Let
(P_{\tau})_{\tau\in[0,1]}
be a collection of
d
-dimensional polytopes in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Let
\mathfrak{J}_{1},\dots,\mathfrak{J}_{m}
be subsets of the vertex set of
P_{0}
. Suppose that for each
\tau\in[0,1]
, there is bijection
\varphi_{\tau}
from the vertex set of
P_{0}
to the vertex set of
P_{\tau}
. Define
Q_{\tau}^{i}:=\conv(\varphi_{\tau}(\mathfrak{J}_{i}))
. Assume the following conditions hold:

•

\{Q_{0}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
is a subdivision of
P_{0}

•

The map
\varphi_{0}
is the identity map.

•

For each vertex
v
of
P_{0}

is a continuous function from

•

For every

to the face lattice of

Proof of Theorem
5.1
.

Choose
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\in B
. We will first define a family of polytopes interpolating between
P^{W}(\mathbf{a})
and
P^{W}(\mathbf{a}^{\prime})

\tau\in[0,1]

\mathbf{a}_{\tau}\in B
and we define

Now we define the maps
\varphi_{\tau}

\mathbf{v}=v\cdot\mathbf{a}

\mathbf{v}^{\prime}:=v\cdot\mathbf{a}^{\prime}

\varphi_{\tau}(\mathbf{v}):=\tau\mathbf{v}^{\prime}+(1-\tau)\mathbf{v}

\varphi_{\tau}(\mathbf{v})
moves along the line segment between

and
\mathbf{v}^{\prime}

\varphi_{\tau}(\mathbf{v})=\tau\mathbf{v}^{\prime}+(1-\tau)\mathbf{v}=\tau(v\cdot\mathbf{a}^{\prime})+(1-\tau)(v\cdot\mathbf{a})=v\cdot(\tau\mathbf{a}^{\prime}+(1-\tau)\mathbf{a})=v\cdot\mathbf{a}_{\tau},

so
\varphi_{\tau}(\mathbf{v})
is a vertex of
P_{\tau}
. Thus
\varphi_{\tau}
is a bijection from
P_{0}=P^{W}(\mathbf{a})
to
P_{\tau}
.

For
\tau=0

\varphi_{\tau}(\mathbf{v})=0\mathbf{v}^{\prime}+(1-0)\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{v}
which is the identity map. As

is just a parametrization of a line segment, it is continuous.
As
\varphi_{\tau}
sends vertices of

to those of

6.
Asymptotics on persistent and generic simplices

Set

\mathrm{GenS}(W):=\#\{\mathcal{S}\subseteq W\mid\mathcal{S}\text{ is a }\mathbf{a}\text{-simplex}\}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{PersistS}(W):=\#\{\mathcal{S}\subseteq W\mid\mathcal{S}\text{ is a persistent simplex}\}.

In this section, we obtain some asymptotics on these quantities.
Clearly,

\mathrm{PersistS}(W)\leq\mathrm{GenS}(W)\leq\binom{|W|}{d+1}

where the last binomial coefficient is the number of

(d+1)
-subsets of vertices.
To obtain asymptotics, our key observation is that persistent simplices in a facet can be lifted to
persistent simplices in the whole polytope.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
W_{s}
be the maximal parabolic subgroup of
W
generated by removing a simple reflection
s
. Let
F
be a facet of
P^{W}(\mathbf{a})
corresponding to a coset of
W_{s}
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{S}\cdot\mathbf{a}\subseteq wW_{s}\cdot\mathbf{a}
is a simplex in
this facet for every
\mathbf{a}\in B
. Then
\conv(\mathcal{S}\cdot\mathbf{a})
has
d
vertices and spans the facet hyperplane of

F
. If
v\in W\setminus wW_{s}
, then

\mathcal{S}\cup\{v\}
is a persistent
\mathbf{a}
-simplex.

Proof.

For every
\mathbf{a}\in B
, the vertices indexed by
\mathcal{S}
are affinely
independent and lie in the facet hyperplane of
F
. Since

v\notin wW_{s}
, the vertex
v\cdot\mathbf{a}
is not contained in this facet hyperplane.
Consequently the
d
vertices of
\mathcal{S}
span a hyperplane and the additional
vertex
v\cdot\mathbf{a}
lies outside that hyperplane. Hence the
d+1
vertices indexed
by
\mathcal{S}\cup\{v\}
are affinely independent for every
\mathbf{a}\in B
.
∎

Corollary 6.2
.

We have that

\mathrm{PersistS}(W)\geq\frac{1}{d+1}\sum_{s\in S}\frac{|W|}{|W_{s}|}\left(|W|-|W_{s}|\right)]\mathrm{PersistS}(W_{s})

with base case

\mathrm{PersistS}(\{e\})=1.

Proof.

Fix a simple reflection
s
. The facets of type
s
are indexed by the
|W|/|W_{s}|
left
cosets
wW_{s}
. Each such facet has exactly
|W_{s}|
vertices, so there are
|W|-|W_{s}|
vertices not in the facet.

Each facet
F
is equal to
\conv(wW_{s}\cdot\mathbf{a})
for some
s
and some
w
. Since
\conv(wW_{s}\cdot\mathbf{a})
is a translate of an orbit permutahedron for
W_{s}
, any persistent simplex for
W_{s}
will give a persistent
(d-1)
-simplex in the facet.
Applying Lemma
6.1

to each such simplex and each vertex outside the facet gives

\frac{|W|}{|W_{s}|}\left(|W|-|W_{s}|\right)\mathrm{PersistS}(W_{s})

persistent
d
-simplices, counted with multiplicity. Summing over
all simple reflections gives the raw count

R=\sum_{s\in S}\frac{|W|}{|W_{s}|}\left(|W|-|W_{s}|\right)\mathrm{PersistS}(W_{s}).

It remains to control multiplicity. Suppose a fixed persistent
d
-simplex

\mathcal{S}\subset W
, with
|\mathcal{S}|=d+1
, occurs in this raw count. One occurrence is
determined by a choice of the outside vertex
v\in\mathcal{S}
, together with a facet
containing the remaining
d
-element set
\mathcal{S}\setminus\{v\}
. There are only

d+1
choices for
v
. For a fixed
v
, the set
\mathcal{S}\setminus\{v\}
is
affinely independent and spans a unique hyperplane. If it lies in a facet and
spans that facet hyperplane, then that facet is uniquely determined: two
facets of a full-dimensional polytope with the same supporting hyperplane are
the same facet. Therefore, each persistent
d
-simplex is counted at most

d+1
times in
R
. Hence the number of distinct persistent
simplices is at least
R/(d+1)
, which is the asserted bound.

The base case is the rank-zero Coxeter group. Its Coxeter permutahedron is a
single point, and it has exactly one top-dimensional simplex, namely that
point.
∎

The facet recurrence gives a large, explicit, recursively generated family of
persistent simplices, but it need not account for all persistent simplices.

For an explicit example, take
W=S_{n}
the symmetric group, with orbit permutahedron

P_{n}=\conv\{(a_{\sigma^{-1}(1)},\dots,a_{\sigma^{-1}(n)}):\sigma\in S_{n}\},\quad\text{where}\quad a_{1}<a_{2}<\cdots<a_{n}.

Write

\mathrm{PersistS}_{n}=\mathrm{PersistS}(S_{n}),\qquad\mathrm{GenS}_{n}=\mathrm{GenS}(S_{n}).

In this case, the upper bound for
(n-1)
-simplices becomes

\mathrm{GenS}_{n}\leq\binom{n!}{n}.

Lemma 6.3
.

For the type
A_{n-1}
Coxeter permutahedron,

((n-1)!)^{n}\leq\mathrm{PersistS}_{n}\leq\mathrm{GenS}_{n}\leq\binom{n!}{n}.

Consequently,

e^{-n+\frac{1}{2}\log n+O(1)}\binom{n!}{n}\leq\mathrm{PersistS}_{n}\leq\mathrm{GenS}_{n}\leq\binom{n!}{n}.

In particular,

\log\mathrm{PersistS}_{n}=n^{2}\log n-n^{2}-\frac{1}{2}n\log n+O(n).

Proof.

The upper bound is the trivial bound by the total number of
n
-element
subsets of the vertex set.

For the lower bound, let

G_{i}=\{\sigma\in S_{n}:\sigma(i)=1\}.

Thus
|G_{i}|=(n-1)!
. For each tuple

\mathcal{S}=(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{n})\in G_{1}\times\cdots\times G_{n}
, consider the

n
-element set of vertices

\mathcal{S}\cdot\mathbf{a}=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\},\qquad v_{i}=(a_{\sigma_{i}^{-1}(1)},\dots,a_{\sigma_{i}^{-1}(n)}).

The vertex
v_{i}
is the unique vertex in this set whose
i
-th coordinate
is
a_{1}
. Hence the vertices
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
are distinct, and the tuple

(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{n})
can be recovered from
\mathcal{S}\cdot\mathbf{a}
. Therefore
this construction gives exactly
((n-1)!)^{n}
distinct
n
-subsets of the
vertex set.

It remains to show that each such subset is counted by
\mathrm{PersistS}_{n}
, i.e.
that its affine-dependence determinant is not identically zero as a polynomial
in the chamber parameters. Fix real numbers

b_{2}<\cdots<b_{n}
, and set

a^{(T)}=(-T,b_{2},\dots,b_{n})

for
T
sufficiently large, so that
a^{(T)}
lies in the open chamber.
For the associated vertices
v_{i}(T)
, we have

\frac{1}{T}v_{i}(T)\longrightarrow-e_{i}\qquad\text{as }T\to\infty,

because the
i
-th coordinate of
v_{i}(T)
is
-T
, while all other
coordinates are fixed. The points

-e_{1},\dots,-e_{n}
are affinely independent. Affine independence is an open
condition, and scaling all points by
1/T
preserves affine independence.
Therefore, for all sufficiently large
T
, the vertices

v_{1}(T),\dots,v_{n}(T)
are affinely independent. Thus the determinant
polynomial for
\mathcal{S}\cdot\mathbf{a}
is not identically zero, and

\mathcal{S}
contributes to
\mathrm{PersistS}_{n}
.

Using Stirling’s formula and basics of binomials we can justify the statement on asymptotics.

Since
\mathrm{PersistS}_{n}
lies between
((n-1)!)^{n}
and
\binom{n!}{n}
, its
logarithm differs from
\log\binom{n!}{n}
by at most
O(n)
. This gives

\log\mathrm{PersistS}_{n}=n^{2}\log n-n^{2}-\frac{1}{2}n\log n+O(n).

∎

Remark 6.4
.

From the above result we have

\mathrm{PersistS}_{n}=\exp\left(n^{2}\log n-n^{2}-\frac{1}{2}n\log n+O(n)\right).

Thus, the

. On the

.

In type

7.
Subdivisions of
W
-matroid polytopes into
W
-matroid polytopes are persistent

In this section, we turn from persistent triangulations to persistent subdivisions. Rather than fixing our attention on orbit permutahedra
P^{W}(\mathbf{a})
, we will consider
orbit polytopes
, which are polytopes of the form
P^{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbf{a}):=\conv(\mathcal{S}\cdot\mathbf{a})
for
\mathcal{S}\subset W
and
\mathbf{a}\in B
. However, the combinatorial type of an orbit polytope may depend on
\mathbf{a}
. To remove this complication, we will consider orbit polytopes
P^{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbf{a})
that are also generalized permutahedra.

Definition 7.1
.

A polytope
P
is a
generalized
W
-permutahedron
if all of its edges are parallel to roots of
W
, or equivalently, if its normal fan coarsens the
W
-braid arrangement. Following
[
9
, Section 6.4]
, an orbit polytope
P^{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbf{a})
which is also a generalized
W
-permutahedron is called a
W
-matroid polytope
1
1

1

[
9
]
would use the terminology “Coxeter matroid polytopes for
(W,1)
”. In type A, if we choose
\mathbf{a}=(1,2,\dots,n)
,
W
-matroid polytopes
P^{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbf{a})
are often called (complete) flag matroid polytopes.
.

For the equivalence of the two definitions of
W
-generalized permutahedra, see e.g.
[
1
, Proposition 2.6]
. Generalized permutahedra and matroid polytopes have been extensively studied, particularly in type A
[
21
,
22
]
.
By
[
9
, Theorem 6.4.1]
,
P^{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbf{a})
is a
W
-matroid polytope if and only if
\mathcal{S}
is a
Coxeter matroid
for
(W,1)
. So in particular, whether or not an orbit polytope is a
W
-matroid polytope depends only on
\mathcal{S}
and not on
\mathbf{a}
.
As the next lemma shows, the combinatorial type of a
W
-matroid polytope
P^{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbf{a})
also depends only on
\mathcal{S}
and not on
\mathbf{a}\in B
.

For any orbit polytope
P^{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbf{a})
, the vertices
\mathcal{S}\cdot\mathbf{a}
are in natural bijection with
\mathcal{S}
. We label the maximal cone
N_{v\cdot\mathbf{a}}
of the normal fan with the element
v\in\mathcal{S}
, and obtain in this way the
labeled normal fan
of
P^{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbf{a})
.

Lemma 7.2
.

Fix
\mathcal{S}\subseteq W
and
\mathbf{a}\in B
. If
P^{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbf{a})
is a
W
-matroid polytope, its labeled normal fan does not depend on the choice of
\mathbf{a}\in B
. In particular, its combinatorial type does not depend on
\mathbf{a}\in B
.

Proof.

Let
v\in W
and
\mathbf{v}=v\cdot\mathbf{a}
. Let
\Phi_{v}=\{\alpha\in v(\Phi_{+})\mid s_{\alpha}v\in\mathcal{S}\}
. We claim that

N_{\mathbf{v}}=\{c\in(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\ast}\mid\langle c,\alpha\rangle\geq 0\text{ for all }\alpha\in\Phi_{v}\}.

Take
c
such that
\langle c,\alpha\rangle\geq 0
for all
\alpha\in\Phi_{\mathbf{v}}
. We have that for any
y\in P^{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbf{a})
,
y
is in the vertex span of
\mathbf{v}
. Thus we have that

\mathbf{v}-y=\underset{\alpha\in\Phi_{\mathbf{v}}}{\sum}k_{\alpha}\alpha

where
k_{\alpha}\geq 0
. Dotting both sides with
c
gives us that

\langle c,\mathbf{v}-y\rangle=\left\langle c,\underset{\alpha\in\Phi_{\mathbf{v}}}{\sum}k_{\alpha}\alpha\right\rangle=\underset{\alpha\in\Phi_{\mathbf{v}}}{\sum}k_{\alpha}\langle c,\alpha\rangle.

Since
\langle c,\alpha\rangle\geq 0
and
k_{\alpha}\geq 0
, we have that
\langle c,\mathbf{v}\rangle\geq\langle c,y\rangle
. Thus
c\in N_{\mathbf{v}}
.

Now let
c\in N_{\mathbf{v}}
. We have that
\langle c,\mathbf{v}\rangle\geq\langle c,y\rangle
for all
y\in P
. Let
\alpha\in\Phi_{\mathbf{v}}
and consider
s_{\alpha}\cdot\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{v}-2\frac{\langle\mathbf{v},\alpha\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}\alpha
. Substituting this into the original equation gives us that

\langle c,\mathbf{v}\rangle\geq\left\langle c,\mathbf{v}-2\frac{\langle\mathbf{v},\alpha\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}\alpha\right\rangle

0\geq-2\frac{\langle\mathbf{v},\alpha\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}\langle c,\alpha\rangle.

Since
\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle>0
and
\langle\mathbf{v},\alpha\rangle>0
, we must have that
\langle c,\alpha\rangle\geq 0
. Since this holds true for all
\alpha\in\Phi_{\mathbf{v}}
, we get that

N_{\mathbf{v}}=\{c\in(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\ast}\mid\langle c,\alpha\rangle\geq 0\text{ for all }\alpha\in\Phi_{\mathbf{v}}\}.

We have just shown that the normal cone of the vertex
v\cdot\mathbf{a}
depends only on
v
and not on the choice of
\mathbf{a}\in B
. Thus the labeled normal fan of
P^{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbf{a})
does not depend on
\mathbf{a}
. As the normal fan of a polytope determines the combinatorial type, the second sentence of the lemma follows.
∎

Remark 7.3
.

One well-studied example of orbit polytopes
P^{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbf{a})
which are also
W
-matroid polytopes are
Bruhat interval polytopes
, where
\mathcal{S}
is a Bruhat interval in
W

[
31
]
. So in particular the results in this section apply to Bruhat interval polytopes.

Suppose
P^{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbf{a})
is a
W
-matroid polytope.
We denote by

\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{O}}^{\mathcal{S}}:=\{\conv(\mathcal{S}\cdot\mathbf{a}):\mathbf{a}\in B\}

the realization space of
W
-matroid polytopes which have the same normal fan as
P^{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbf{a})
.

Theorem 7.4
.

Let
B
be a choice of fundamental chamber for some finite reflection group
W
and suppose for
\mathbf{a}\in B
,
P^{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbf{a})
is a
W
-matroid polytope. Then the realization space
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{O}}^{\mathcal{S}}
is homeomorphic to
B
.

Proof.

Fix some arbitrary ordering on
\mathcal{S}
and let
w^{\prime}
be the first element. We think of
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{O}}^{\mathcal{S}}
as a set of matrices living in
\mathbb{R}^{n\cdot|\mathcal{S}|}
where each row is a vertex of the polytope. As the vertices are naturally in bijection with elements of
\mathcal{S}
, we identify each row with some
w\in\mathcal{S}
. As
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{O}}^{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n\cdot|\mathcal{S}|}
, we endow it with the subspace topology.

Consider the function
f:B\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n\cdot|\mathcal{S}|}
which sends
\mathbf{a}
to a matrix where the
w
th row is given by
w\cdot\mathbf{a}
. This is a linear function in the input variables, so it is continuous. By construction, we have that
\mathrm{im}(f)=\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{O}}^{\mathcal{S}}
.

For the inverse, note that the map sending a matrix
P\in\mathbb{R}^{n\cdot|\mathcal{S}|}
to its first row is a projection, so it is continuous. Also note that acting on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
by
w^{\prime}
is a reflection, so it is continuous. After restricting the domain to
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{O}}^{\mathcal{S}}
, the composition of these maps and
f
are inverses. As
f
is a continuous map with continuous inverse, it is a homeomorphism and
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{O}}^{\mathcal{S}}\cong B
.

∎

Corollary 7.5
.

The orbit realization space
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{O}}^{\mathcal{S}}
is contractible and thus path-connected.

These generalize the results of Theorem
4.1
and Corollary
4.2
to the setting of
W
-matroid polytopes.

We now turn to subdivisions. A collection of

if
\cup_{i=1}^{r}Q_{i}=P
and for all

,

is a face of both
Q_{i}
and
Q_{j}
. We are interested in subdivisions of a
W
-matroid polytope

that “persist” for different choices of
\mathbf{a}\in B
.

Definition 7.6
.

Fix
\mathcal{S}\subset W
and a collection
\Theta=\{\mathcal{S}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{p}
of subsets of
W
. We say that
\Theta
is a
\mathbf{a}
-subdivision
of

if

is a subdivision of
P^{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbf{a})
. We say
\Theta
is a

of

if it is a
\mathbf{a}
-subdivision of
\mathcal{S}
for every
\mathbf{a}\in B
.

We now turn to
\mathbf{a}
-subdivisions of a
W
-matroid polytope into
W
-matroid polytopes. Such subdivisions, particularly the

for a particular choice of

, and are closely related to the flag Dressian and the tropical complete flag variety. Our main result in this area shows that all such subdivisions are persistent.

Theorem 7.7
.

Let
\mathcal{S},\mathcal{S}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{S}_{r}\subset W
and
\mathbf{a}\in B
. Suppose
P^{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbf{a})
and
P^{\mathcal{S}_{i}}(\mathbf{a})
are

-matroid polytopes. Then
\Theta=\{\mathcal{S}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{p}
is a
\mathbf{a}
-subdivision of

if and only if it is a persistent subdivision.

Proof.

The only statement that needs proof is that an
\mathbf{a}
-subdivision will be an
\mathbf{a}^{\prime}
-subdivision for all
\mathbf{a}^{\prime}
. This is essentially identical to the proof of Theorem
5.1
, using Proposition
5.2
with the same family of maps
\varphi_{\tau}
. To see that

induces a poset isomorphism for all of the polytopes, use the argument in the third paragraph of that proof with the reference to Lemma
3.2
replaced by a reference to Lemma

.
∎
