Introduction to Mathematical Programming with Equilibrium Constraints (MPECs) and Bilevel Optimization
Louis Shuo Wang

Abstract
Our aim is to explain mathematical programs with equilibrium constraints (MPECs), motivate them through applications, present the main equivalent formulations of equilibrium constraints, and summarize the basic existence theory for optimal solutions. The central message is that an MPEC is an optimization problem whose feasible set is partly defined by another optimization, variational inequality, complementarity system, or equilibrium model.



1
Introduction and Big Picture

An MPEC is an optimization problem in which part of the feasible set is defined implicitly through an equilibrium problem. The upper level chooses variables
(x,y)
to optimize a global objective, while lower-level feasibility of
y
is determined by a variational inequality, complementarity problem, or optimization problem parameterized by
x
.

This structure arises naturally in economics, engineering design, transportation, mechanics, robotics, and game theory. It generalizes bilevel programming and complementarity-constrained optimization. It also introduces major analytical challenges: nonconvex feasible regions, possible loss of closedness, nonsmoothness, and multi-valued lower-level response maps.

Problem Formulation

3.1
The general MPEC model

Let

f:\mathbb{R}^{n+m}\to\mathbb{R},\qquad F:\mathbb{R}^{n+m}\to\mathbb{R}^{m},

let
Z\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
be nonempty and closed, and let

C:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}

be a set-valued map with nonempty closed convex values on the domain of interest. Define

X=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:(x,y)\in Z\text{ for some }y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\},

and assume
X\subset\operatorname{dom}(C)
.

For each
x\in X
, let
S(x)
denote the solution set of the variational inequality

y\in C(x),\qquad(v-y)^{T}F(x,y)\geq 0\quad\forall v\in C(x).

Then the MPEC is

\displaystyle\min_{x,y}

\displaystyle f(x,y)

(MPEC)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle(x,y)\in Z,

\displaystyle y\in S(x).

Its feasible region is

\mathcal{F}=Z\cap\operatorname{Gr}(S),

where

\operatorname{Gr}(S)=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+m}:y\in S(x)\}.

3.3
Bilevel programming as a special case

If there exists a continuously differentiable function
\theta(x,y)
such that

F(x,y)=\nabla_{y}\theta(x,y),

then the lower-level VI is the first-order optimality condition for

\min_{y}\;\theta(x,y)\quad\text{s.t. }y\in C(x).

If
\theta(x,\cdot)
is convex and an appropriate constraint qualification holds, the VI solution set coincides with the optimizer set of the lower-level problem.

Remark 3.1

(Optimistic versus pessimistic bilevel modeling)
.

If the lower-level problem has multiple optimizers, the condition
y\in S(x)
does not by itself specify how the lower-level solution is selected. The formulation above is the standard optimistic or strong version when the upper level is allowed to choose the most favorable
y\in S(x)
. A pessimistic or weak version instead evaluates the upper-level objective against the least favorable lower-level optimizer.

3.4
Complementarity systems as a special case

If
C(x)
is a convex cone, then the VI is equivalent to a generalized complementarity problem:

y\in C(x),\qquad F(x,y)\in C(x)^{*},\qquad y^{T}F(x,y)=0,

where the positive dual cone is

C(x)^{*}=\{z:z^{T}v\geq 0\ \forall v\in C(x)\}.

This sign convention matches the VI convention
(v-y)^{T}F(x,y)\geq 0
. Under a polar-cone convention, the same statement is written with the corresponding sign change.

When
C(x)=\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m_{2}}
, one obtains mixed complementarity systems. When
m_{1}=0
, one obtains standard nonlinear complementarity problems.

3.5
Why MPECs are difficult

The main difficulties are structural rather than cosmetic.

[label=()]

1.

Nonconvexity.
Even if each lower-level problem is convex, the graph of the solution map
S
can be highly nonconvex, so
\mathcal{F}
is typically nonconvex.

2.

Possible failure of closedness.
Although
Z
may be closed,
\operatorname{Gr}(S)
may fail to be closed unless the lower-level solution map has suitable closed-graph properties.

3.

Nonsmoothness.
Even when
S(x)
is single-valued, it may fail to be differentiable in
x
.

4.

Multivaluedness.
The lower-level solution map may be set-valued, which creates ambiguity in the upper-level model and motivates optimistic and pessimistic Stackelberg interpretations.

5.

Combinatorial structure.
Complementarity constraints encode disjunctions. For example,
0\leq a\perp b\geq 0
means either
a=0
or
b=0
or both. This creates a latent finite-union structure similar to disjunctive programming.

4
Representative Problems

This section motivates MPEC through representative applications. The common modeling template is

\text{design, policy, or strategy choice}\quad+\quad\text{equilibrium response}.

4.1
Bracken–McGill bilevel programs

These are early bilevel models from defense, production, and marketing. A firm or agency chooses upper-level decisions while accounting for adversarial or competitive lower-level choices. The lower-level variables represent the competitors’ or opponents’ optimizing reactions.

4.2
Stackelberg games

A leader chooses
x

y\in S(x)
. The leader solves

\min_{x,y}f(x,y)\quad\text{s.t. }x\in X,\ y\in S(x).

If

is multi-valued, two versions arise:

2.

4.3
Misclassification minimization

A minimum-misclassification hyperplane problem can be reformulated as a bilevel linear program by introducing lower-level variables that encode misclassification indicators through optimization.

4.4
Robotics and contact mechanics

In robotic motion with frictional contact, the dynamic constraints together with Coulomb friction and complementarity conditions lead to a complementarity system. If the original physical equations are infeasible or inconsistent, one may minimize a residual subject to the remaining equilibrium constraints. This produces an MPEC formulation.

4.5
Residual minimization of complementarity systems

Given an NCP,

x\geq 0,\qquad F(x)\geq 0,\qquad x^{T}F(x)=0,

one may minimize a residual such as

\theta(x)=\frac{1}{2}\|\min(x,F(x))\|^{2}.

This residual minimization problem can itself be written as an MPEC or as a closely related nonsmooth optimization problem.

4.6
Transportation and network design

The upper level selects capacities, tolls, demands, or service frequencies. The lower level is a traffic equilibrium, route-choice equilibrium, or shortest-path problem. Representative examples include continuous network design, origin–destination demand adjustment, and transit planning.

4.7
Facility location and production

A large firm chooses plant locations and production quantities while anticipating market-clearing prices produced by a spatial price equilibrium problem. The lower-level equilibrium is often expressible as a complementarity system; the upper level optimizes profit.

4.8
Structural mechanics and optimal design

Design variables such as bar volumes or prestress forces appear at the upper level, while displacements, contact forces, and frictional forces are governed by complementarity systems at the lower level. These models are common in contact mechanics and shape optimization.

5.2
KKT formulation

Assume now that

C(x)=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:g(x,y)\leq 0\},

where each
g_{i}(x,\cdot)
is convex and differentiable. Under a suitable multiplier constraint qualification, such as Slater’s condition for the lower-level convex feasible set or another condition guaranteeing KKT multipliers, the VI is equivalent to a KKT system: there exists
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
such that

\displaystyle F(x,y)+\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\lambda_{i}\nabla_{y}g_{i}(x,y)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}

\displaystyle\geq 0,

\displaystyle g_{i}(x,y)

\displaystyle\leq 0,

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}g_{i}(x,y)

\displaystyle=0\qquad(i=1,\dots,\ell).

Thus the MPEC becomes

\displaystyle\min_{x,y,\lambda}

\displaystyle f(x,y)

(KKT-MPEC)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle(x,y)\in Z,

\displaystyle F(x,y)+\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\lambda_{i}\nabla_{y}g_{i}(x,y)=0,

\displaystyle\lambda\geq 0,\quad g(x,y)\leq 0,\quad\lambda_{i}g_{i}(x,y)=0\quad i=1,\dots,\ell.

This is one of the most important equivalent forms in theory and computation.

5.3
SBCQ and closedness of the feasible region

A central issue is whether KKT multipliers can be chosen in a uniformly bounded way along feasible sequences. A sequentially bounded constraint qualification (SBCQ) says, roughly, that along any convergent feasible sequence, one can choose bounded multipliers.

Under continuity, convexity, and suitable multiplier-existence assumptions, SBCQ implies:

5.4
Piecewise structure of the feasible set

The complementarity conditions partition the feasible region into finitely many pieces according to which constraints are active and which multipliers are positive. Therefore, under suitable assumptions, the feasible set is a finite union of closed sets. This reveals the latent combinatorial structure of MPEC.

For each complementarity pair
0\leq\lambda_{i}\perp u_{i}\geq 0
, where
u_{i}=-g_{i}(x,y)
, the feasible alternatives are

\lambda_{i}=0,\ u_{i}\geq 0\qquad\text{or}\qquad\lambda_{i}\geq 0,\ u_{i}=0.

With
\ell
complementarity pairs, this creates up to
2^{\ell}
active-set regimes.

5.5
Affine variational inequalities and MPAECs

If

F(x,y)=q+Nx+My,\qquad g(x,y)=b+Ax+By,

then the lower-level VI is affine and the resulting problem is often called an MPEC with affine equilibrium constraints, or MPAEC. In this case, each active-set piece of the feasible set is polyhedral, so the global feasible set is a finite union of polyhedra.

This special case is much more structured and often serves as a first serious testbed for theory and algorithms.

5.6
Merit-function reformulations

Complementarity conditions can be converted into equations using an NCP function. A standard example is the Fischer–Burmeister function:

\phi(a,b)=\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}-(a+b).

It satisfies

\phi(a,b)=0\iff a\geq 0,\ b\geq 0,\ ab=0.

For the KKT inequalities
g_{i}(x,y)\leq 0
, define the nonnegative slack

u_{i}(x,y):=-g_{i}(x,y)\geq 0.

Then complementarity is equivalently written as

\phi(\lambda_{i},u_{i}(x,y))=0,\qquad i=1,\dots,\ell.

This yields an equation-based reformulation of MPEC and motivates smooth or semismooth numerical methods.

5.7
Implicit-program formulation

If, for each
x
, the lower-level VI has a unique solution
y(x)
, then the MPEC reduces to

\min_{x}f(x,y(x))\quad\text{s.t. }x\in X,\ (x,y(x))\in Z.

A common sufficient condition for uniqueness is strict or strong monotonicity of
F(x,\cdot)
on
C(x)
.

Definition 5.1

(Monotonicity)
.

A mapping
G:K\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is:

[label=()]

1.

monotone
if

for all

,

2.

3.

if there exists

such that

(u-v)^{T}(G(u)-G(v))\geq c\|u-v\|^{2}\quad\forall u,v\in K.

Proposition 5.2

(Existence and uniqueness for a VI)
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
be nonempty, closed, and convex, and let
G:K\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be continuous. If
K
is compact, then
\operatorname{VI}(G,K)
has a solution. If, in addition,
G
is strictly monotone, the solution is unique. If
K
is unbounded, existence requires an additional coercivity or growth condition; strong monotonicity is a common condition that yields both uniqueness and, under the standard VI hypotheses, existence.

For complementarity problems on orthants, a related uniqueness condition is the uniform
P
condition.

5.8
Constraint qualifications that imply SBCQ

SBCQ is implied by more familiar nonlinear programming conditions, including:

[label=()]

1.

Mangasarian–Fromovitz constraint qualification (MFCQ),

2.

constant-rank constraint qualification (CRCQ),

3.

linear independence constraint qualification (LICQ).

LICQ is strongest among these and yields uniqueness of the multiplier vector.

5.9
Teaching summary

The most important takeaway is:

The same equilibrium constraint can be expressed in projection form, KKT form, complementarity form, merit-function form, or implicit form. Different forms are useful for different proofs and algorithms.

6
Existence of Optimal Solutions

6.1
General principle

Existence of a minimizer usually follows from two ingredients:

[leftmargin=1.5em]

1.

the feasible set is closed,

2.

at least one lower level set of the objective is nonempty and bounded.

If these hold and the objective is continuous, a minimizer exists by the Weierstrass theorem.

6.2
Coercivity and bounded level sets

A function
\psi
on a set
S
is coercive if

\|z\|\to\infty,\ z\in S\implies\psi(z)\to\infty.

A coercive continuous objective on a closed feasible set attains its minimum. More generally, it is enough that one lower level set is nonempty and bounded.

6.3
Existence via the KKT formulation

For the KKT reformulation, if the feasible set in
(x,y,\lambda)
-space is closed and one lower level set is nonempty and bounded, then an optimal solution exists.

For the original MPEC, a clean sufficient condition is the following.

Theorem 6.1

(Basic existence result for MPEC)
.

Assume that:

[label=()]

1.

f
is continuous,

2.

Z
is closed,

3.

the lower-level data are continuous,

4.

each
g_{i}(x,\cdot)
is convex and differentiable,

5.

a multiplier constraint qualification and SBCQ hold so that
\operatorname{Gr}(S)
is closed,

6.

some lower level set
\{(x,y)\in\mathcal{F}:f(x,y)\leq\alpha\}
is nonempty and bounded.

Then the MPEC has a global optimal solution.

6.4
The affine/quadratic case

In the MPAEC setting with polyhedral
Z
and quadratic objective
f
, each active-set piece is polyhedral. An analog of the Frank–Wolfe theorem says that a quadratic objective over a finite union of polyhedra attains a global solution when it is bounded below on the feasible set, equivalently when it is bounded below on every feasible ray of every polyhedral piece.

6.5
Interpretation

The hard part of existence theory for MPEC is usually not continuity of the objective. The hard part is proving that the feasible region is closed despite being generated by equilibrium constraints. Once closedness and boundedness are available, existence follows from standard compactness arguments.

Exercises

7.1
True or False

[label=()]

1.

Topology of the Feasible Region:

For a general Mathematical Program with Equilibrium Constraints (MPEC), the feasible region is guaranteed to be a closed set, even though it is typically nonconvex and potentially disconnected.

2.

Complexity of Bilevel Programs:

A bilevel linear program—where the overall objective is linear, the joint constraints are polyhedral, and the inner problem is a linear program—belongs to the class of strongly NP-hard problems.

3.

Stackelberg Game Formulations

In a Stackelberg game where the followers’ rational reaction set
S(x)
is multi-valued, the ”strong” (optimistic) and ”weak” (pessimistic) formulations yield equivalent objective values for the leader.

4.

The Normal Map Reformulation:

Reformulating the inner variational inequality of an MPEC using the normal map equation (involving the projection operator
\Pi
) successfully transforms the problem into a standard, continuously differentiable equality-constrained optimization problem.

5.

Constraint Qualifications:

The Sequentially Bounded Constraint Qualification (SBCQ) is a critical assumption because it guarantees that the feasible region of the MPEC is closed, provided the lower-level constraints are continuous and convex.

6.

Constraint Qualification Hierarchy:

If the Mangasarian-Fromovitz Constraint Qualification (MFCQ) holds at all pairs in the joint feasible region, it implies that the SBCQ also holds.

7.

Monotonicity and Implicit Forms:

If the equilibrium function
F(x,\cdot)
is strongly monotone on a closed convex set
C(x)
for each
x
, the reaction map
S(x)
becomes single-valued, allowing the MPEC to be reformulated as an implicit optimization problem solely in terms of the upper-level variable
x
.

8.

Existence of Optimal Solutions:

The residual minimization problem of any Affine Variational Inequality (AVI) over a nonempty polyhedron is guaranteed to have an optimal solution.

Solutions for True or False

False. Example 1.1.2 explicitly demonstrates that the feasible region of an MPEC can be disconnected and not closed. The lack of closedness is a primary reason why MPECs are so intractable without assuming constraint qualifications like the SBCQ.

2.

True. As stated in Section 1.1, despite its apparent simplicity (linear objective, polyhedral constraints, linear inner problem), the bilevel linear program is strongly NP-hard, meaning no fully polynomial approximation scheme exists unless P = NP.

3.

False. When S(x) is a singleton, the two are identical. However, when S(x) is multi-valued, the strong formulation assumes followers choose the reaction most favorable to the leader (minimizing the leader’s cost over S(x)), while the weak formulation assumes they choose the least favorable reaction (taking the supremum over S(x)). These are generally not equivalent.

4.

False. While the normal map formulation does transform the VI into a single equation, the projection operator used in this formulation is generally not Fréchet or Gâteaux differentiable. Therefore, it results in a non-smooth/nondifferentiable optimization problem.

5.

True. Theorem 1.3.4 explicitly proves that under the SBCQ (and assuming convexity/continuity of the lower-level constraint functions), the feasible region of the MPEC is a closed set. SBCQ ensures that bounded KKT multipliers exist for convergent sequences.

6.

True. Proposition 1.3.6 establishes that MFCQ is a stronger condition that implies the SBCQ. Roughly speaking, MFCQ ensures that all multipliers are bounded on bounded sets, which easily satisfies the sequential boundedness required by SBCQ.

7.

True. Strong monotonicity guarantees that the inner variational inequality has exactly one solution (Proposition 1.3.10c). Because S(x) is a singleton, let’s call it y(x), we can substitute it into the upper-level objective to get f(x,y(x)), reducing the problem to an implicit program in x.

True. Corollary 1.4.4 proves this. By reformulating the AVI residual minimization into an MPAEC (Mathematical Program with Affine Equilibrium Constraints) with a convex quadratic objective, the problem is bounded below by zero. Extending the Frank-Wolfe theorem (Theorem 1.4.3), a quadratic function bounded below on the feasible rays of a polyhedral set attains its minimum.

Solutions for computational questions

Question 1: Topology of the Rational Set and SBCQ (25 points)

Part (a): Compute
S(x)
(8 points)

The inner problem is:
\min y
subject to
y\in[-1,1]
and
xy\leq 0
.

•

If
x\in(0,1]
,
xy\leq 0\implies y\leq 0
. The feasible set for
y
is
[-1,0]
. The minimum is at
y=-1
.

•

If
x\in[-1,0)
,
xy\leq 0\implies y\geq 0
. The feasible set for
y
is
[0,1]
. The minimum is at
y=0
.

•

If
x=0
,
xy\leq 0\implies 0\leq 0
(always true). The feasible set for
y
is
[-1,1]
. The minimum is at
y=-1
.

Therefore, the reaction map is:

S(x)=\begin{cases}\{-1\}&\text{if }x\in[0,1]\\
\{0\}&\text{if }x\in[-1,0)\end{cases}

Rubric: 4 pts for identifying the correct intervals. 4 pts for the correct
y
values.

Part (b): Feasible Region
\mathcal{F}
(8 points)

The joint feasible region is
\mathcal{F}=\{(x,-1)\mid x\in[0,1]\}\cup\{(x,0)\mid x\in[-1,0)\}
.

Closedness:

\mathcal{F}
is
not closed
. Take the sequence
x_{k}=-1/k
. As
k\to\infty
,
x_{k}\to 0
and
y_{k}\to 0
. The limit point is
(0,0)
. However,
S(0)=\{-1\}
, so
(0,0)\notin\mathcal{F}
.

Convexity:

\mathcal{F}
is
not convex
. It is the union of two disjoint, non-collinear line segments.

Rubric: 2 pts for the correct set definition. 3 pts for proving it is not closed (limit point argument). 3 pts for proving it is not convex.

Part (c): SBCQ Failure (9 points)

The Lagrangian for the inner problem is
L(y,\lambda,\mu)=y+\lambda(xy)+\mu_{1}(y-1)+\mu_{2}(-y-1)
.

The stationarity KKT condition is
\nabla_{y}L=1+\lambda x+\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}=0
.

At
x_{k}=-1/k
and
y_{k}=0
, the constraints
y\leq 1
and
y\geq-1
are strictly inactive, so
\mu_{1}=\mu_{2}=0
. The constraint
xy\leq 0
is active (
0\leq 0
).
Substitute into the KKT condition:

1+\lambda_{k}\left(-\frac{1}{k}\right)=0\implies\lambda_{k}=k

As
k\to\infty
,
\lambda_{k}\to\infty
. Thus, the multipliers are unbounded for a sequence converging to a feasible limit point, meaning
SBCQ fails
at
x=0
. This computational failure perfectly aligns with the topological failure in (b), as SBCQ is the sufficient condition for
\mathcal{F}
to be closed.

Rubric: 3 pts for correct KKT setup. 4 pts for calculating
\lambda_{k}=k
. 2 pts for linking the unbounded multiplier to the lack of closedness.

Question 2: The Normal Map Reformulation (25 points)

Part (a): Operator and Normal Map (8 points)

The projection operator onto the non-negative orthant is the component-wise maximum:
\Pi_{\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}}(z)=\max(z,0)=z^{+}
.

The normal map associated with the pair
(F,\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+})
is defined as
F_{\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}}(z)=F(z^{+})-z^{-}
, where
z^{-}=\max(-z,0)
.

Substituting
F(x,y)
:

F_{\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}}(x,z)=Mz^{+}+Nx+q-z^{-}

Rubric: 4 pts for the correct projection operator. 4 pts for the correct normal map expression.

Part (b): Reformulated MPEC (9 points)

Using the substitution
y=z^{+}
, the new optimization problem is over variables
(x,z)
:

minimize

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|x-z^{+}\|^{2}

subject to

\displaystyle x\geq 0,

and

\displaystyle Mz^{+}+Nx+q-z^{-}=0.

Rubric: 4 pts for substituting
y=z^{+}
in the objective. 5 pts for accurately replacing the complementarity constraints with the single normal map equation.

Part (c): Computational Hurdles (8 points)

The primary hurdle is that the operators
z^{+}
and
z^{-}
are
non-differentiable
at
z_{i}=0
. Consequently, the normal map equation is non-smooth, meaning standard gradient-based or Newton methods cannot be directly applied.

Overcoming this:
One can use
Semismooth Newton methods
(which rely on generalized Jacobians/subdifferentials), or apply a
smoothing function
(e.g., replacing
\max(0,z_{i})
with a smooth approximation like the Fischer-Burmeister function or a softplus function) to restore differentiability.

Rubric: 4 pts for identifying non-differentiability at
z=0
. 4 pts for a valid algorithmic solution (semismooth Newton, smoothing functions, etc.).

Question 3: Constraint Qualifications (25 points)

Part (a): Jacobians (6 points)

\nabla_{y}g_{1}(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
2y_{2}\end{pmatrix},\quad\nabla_{y}g_{2}(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}

Rubric: 3 pts for each correct gradient vector.

Part (b): Linear Independence at the Origin (6 points)

At
(x,y)=(0,0,0,0)
, we have:

\nabla_{y}g_{1}(0,0)=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix},\quad\nabla_{y}g_{2}(0,0)=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}

These two vectors are identical and therefore
linearly dependent
.

Rubric: 3 pts for evaluating the gradients at the origin. 3 pts for stating they are linearly dependent.

Part (c): MFCQ vs CRCQ (13 points)

MFCQ:
MFCQ holds if there exists a vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
v^{T}\nabla_{y}g_{1}<0
and
v^{T}\nabla_{y}g_{2}<0
.
Let
v=\begin{pmatrix}-1\\
0\end{pmatrix}
.

v^{T}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}=-1<0

Thus, MFCQ holds at the origin.

CRCQ:
CRCQ requires the rank of the set of active constraint gradients to remain constant in a neighborhood around the point.
The Jacobian matrix of the active constraints is
J=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
2y_{2}&0\end{pmatrix}
.
At the origin (
y_{2}=0
), the rank of
J
is 1. However, for any arbitrarily small neighborhood where
y_{2}\neq 0
, the determinant is
-2y_{2}\neq 0
, meaning the rank jumps to 2. Because the rank is not constant in the neighborhood,
CRCQ fails
.

Rubric: 6 pts for proving MFCQ (finding a valid vector
v
). 7 pts for proving CRCQ fails (showing the rank changes from 1 to 2 in the neighborhood).

Problem 4: Monotonicity and the Implicit Form

Part (a): Strong Monotonicity (8 points)

F(x,\cdot)
is strongly monotone on
C(x)
if there exists a constant
c>0
such that for all
u,v\in C(x)
:

(u-v)^{T}(F(x,u)-F(x,v))\geq c\|u-v\|^{2}

Since
F
is affine with respect to
y
,
F(x,u)-F(x,v)=M(u-v)
. The condition becomes
(u-v)^{T}M(u-v)\geq c\|u-v\|^{2}
. This holds if and only if
M
is a
positive definite matrix
.

Rubric: 4 pts for the mathematical definition of strong monotonicity. 4 pts for stating
M
must be positive definite.

Part (b): Topological Property of
S(x)
(8 points)

If strong monotonicity holds, the solution set
S(x)
consists of
exactly one element
(it is a singleton). The reaction map is single-valued.

Rubric: 8 pts for stating the solution is unique/a singleton.

Part (c): The Implicit Form (9 points)

Because
S(x)
is single-valued, let
y(x)
denote this unique solution vector. The implicit form of the MPEC is:

minimize

\displaystyle f(x,y(x))

subject to

\displaystyle x\in X

Advantage:
It reduces the MPEC to a standard nonlinear program (NLP) in the variable
x
alone, stripping away the combinatorial complexity of the complementarity constraints.

Limitation:
If there are joint constraints
(x,y)\in\mathcal{Z}
(rather than just
x\in X
), the implicit function
y(x)
might violate these constraints. It is extremely difficult to characterize the feasible region of
x
such that
(x,y(x))\in\mathcal{Z}
, which can lead to an empty feasible set or highly intractable constraints.

Rubric: 3 pts for the correct formulation. 3 pts for the advantage (reduces to NLP). 3 pts for the limitation (handling joint constraints).
