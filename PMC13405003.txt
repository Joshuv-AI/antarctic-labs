Level and predictors of micronutrient intake inadequacy among pregnant women attending public health facilities in Bahir Dar City, Northwest Ethiopia: a multicentre, cross-sectional study

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo determine the level and predictors of micronutrient intake inadequacy among pregnant women attending antenatal public health facilities in Bahir Dar City, Northwest Ethiopia.DesignA multicentre, cross-sectional study.SettingThe study was conducted in public health facilities in Bahir Dar City, Northwest Ethiopia from January to March 2024.ParticipantsAbout 420 pregnant women aged 18 years and older attending antenatal care at public health facilities in Bahir Dar. Pregnant women diagnosed with chronic diseases, sick and unable to communicate, and who had attended weddings, birthday celebrations or religious festivals within the past 24 hours were excluded.Primary outcome measuresMicronutrient intake inadequacy among pregnant women was measured using a single multiphasic 24-hour dietary recall. The Nutria-Survey Food Processor software, the Ethiopian food composition table and the World food composition table were used for the calculation of the nutrient value of micronutrients. The nutrient intakes were assessed by nutrient adequacy ratio and mean adequacy ratio. Data were analysed using SPSS V.26. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was done to identify the predictors of micronutrient intake inadequacy.ResultsA total of 411 pregnant women were recruited in the study, yielding a 97.8% response rate. The overall prevalence of micronutrient intake inadequacy was 76.4% (95% CI 72.0% to 80.3%). The proportions of zinc, vitamin A, iron and calcium intake inadequacies were 48.2%, 66.7%, 14.8% and 74%, respectively. Having nutrition counselling (adjusted ORs (AOR)=3.5; 95% CI 2.00 to 6.48), having <4 meal frequency (AOR=3.66, 95% CI 1.93 to 6.94), having <4 dietary diversity score (AOR=4.78; 95% CI 2.67 to 8.55) and being food insecure (AOR=3.04, 95% CI 1.68 to 6.23) were significant predictors of overall micronutrient inadequacy.ConclusionsMicronutrient intake among pregnant women was lower than the recommended levels. Therefore, strengthening community efforts to promote the consumption of a diversified diet for pregnant women, encourage nutrient-dense food production and enhance food security is essential.

Introduction

Nutritional adequacy refers to consuming enough essential nutrients to meet dietary requirements for optimal health.1 Nutritional assessment, conducted at both individual and population levels, evaluates whether food and nutrient intake meet these needs. Ideally, comparing intake with requirements helps determine if a diet is sufficient.2

Micronutrient deficiencies are among the most common nutritional problems affecting women during pregnancy worldwide.3 Pregnancy increases the demand for essential micronutrients required for maternal tissue expansion, placental development and fetal growth. Inadequate intake of micronutrients during this period has been associated with adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes, including anaemia, preeclampsia, preterm birth, low birth weight, impaired fetal growth and increased morbidity and mortality.4 5

The burden of micronutrient deficiencies is particularly high in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where poor dietary quality, food insecurity, recurrent infections, poverty and limited access to healthcare services are common.6–8 These factors are often compounded by broader socioeconomic challenges, including poverty, climate-related shocks, rising food prices and inadequate sanitation and healthcare infrastructure.9

Previous studies have documented substantial variation in the prevalence of micronutrient inadequacy among pregnant women across different settings. For example, zinc inadequacy has ranged from 63% in Japan10 to more than 90% in China,11 Vietnam12 and Indonesia.13 Similarly, calcium and vitamin A inadequacies have been reported at high levels in several countries, although the magnitude differs considerably across populations.14 These differences are likely related to variations in dietary patterns, food availability, socioeconomic conditions, cultural food practices, nutrition interventions and methods used to assess dietary intake. Such findings suggest that the burden of micronutrient inadequacy is context-specific and requires local evidence to inform effective interventions.

In Ethiopia, micronutrient deficiencies remain important public health concerns despite ongoing efforts to improve maternal nutrition through supplementation programmes, health extension services and national nutrition strategies.15 The predominantly cereal-based diet, limited dietary diversity, household food insecurity and cultural food restrictions during pregnancy may contribute to inadequate micronutrient intake among Ethiopian women.16

Most previous studies in Ethiopia have focused on specific nutrient deficiencies or nutritional status indicators, and evidence on overall micronutrient intake inadequacy and its determinants among pregnant women remains limited.17–19

Therefore, this study aimed to assess the level of micronutrient intake inadequacy and identify its predictors among pregnant women attending public health facilities in Bahir Dar City, Northwest Ethiopia. The findings of this study would be valuable for designing targeted nutrition interventions and informing policies that promote optimal dietary practice and enhance pregnancy outcomes.

Methods

Study setting

The study was conducted in public health facilities in Bahir Dar City, Northwest Ethiopia, from January to March 2024. Bahir Dar City is the capital city of the Amhara region and it is 565 km far from Addis Ababa, the capital city of Ethiopia. The total population of the city is estimated to be 390 429 comprising 191 970 males and 198 459 females. Approximately, 24% of the female residents are of reproductive age, with 3.4% of them being pregnant. Bahir Dar city has a total of three governmental hospitals and ten health centres.

Study design and population

A health-facility-based cross-sectional study was conducted among pregnant women aged 18 years and older attending antenatal care (ANC) at public health facilities in Bahir Dar City. A cross-sectional design was selected because it allows the estimation of the prevalence of micronutrient intake inadequacy and identify associated factors within the defined population at a specific point in time, which aligns with the study objectives. Pregnant women diagnosed with chronic diseases, sick and unable to communicate, and who had attended weddings, birthday celebrations or religious festivals within the past 24 hours were excluded.

Sample size determination

The sample size was calculated by using a single population proportion formula, with assumptions of a 95% CI, a 5% margin of error and a 55% calcium intake inadequacy proportion, which generated the largest required sample size among the micronutrients considered in the study.20 This yielded a sample size of 381, to which a 10% non-response rate was added, resulting in a final sample size of 420.

Sampling procedure

All of the 13 public health facilities (3 hospitals and 10 health centres) found in Bahir Dar City were included in the study to enhance the representativeness of the sample. The sample size was proportionally allocated based on the average monthly attendance of pregnant women at each facility’s ANC unit. Systematic sampling was used, with the first participant selected by lottery and subsequent participants determined by a sampling interval (K) of three. Systematic random sampling was chosen because it is feasible in high-flow ANC settings and provides even distribution of participants throughout the data collection period. To minimise selection bias, the sampling frame included all eligible pregnant women attending ANC services during the study period, and proportional allocation ensured adequate representation from all public health facilities. Random selection of the first participant further reduced the likelihood of selection bias.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

Data collection tool and measurements

Data were collected using a pretested, semistructured interviewer-administered questionnaire covering sociodemographic, reproductive health, household food security and dietary factors prepared after reading various literature.21–23 The full questionnaire is provided in online supplemental information file. Data were collected by six trained public health officers and two BSc nurses and supervised by two public health officers.

Household food insecurity was measured using the Household Food Insecurity Access Scale (HFIAS), a standardised and validated tool developed by the Food and Nutrition Technical Assistance Project and widely used in LMIC, including Ethiopia.24 The tool consists of nine questions that capture three domains of food insecurity: anxiety about food supply, sufficient food quality and insufficient food intake and its physical consequences. Each of the nine items was scored from 0 to 3 based on frequency of occurrence (0=never, 1=rarely, 2=sometimes, 3=often).

Dietary assessment was conducted using the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) an interactive multiple-pass single 24-hour recall method, a widely used and validated approach for assessing individual dietary intake in population-based studies.25 The multiple-pass approach consisted of obtaining a quick list of all foods and beverages consumed during the previous 24 hours; collecting detailed information on ingredients, preparation methods and quantities consumed; and reviewing the recalled foods to improve completeness and accuracy. All collected data are documented by the interviewer on a record sheet found in the food consumption record booklet. Respondents were asked to name all foods and beverages eaten during the previous day, including everything consumed outside the home as well as the cooking method.

To improve the validity of portion size estimation, a preliminary survey was conducted among 21 pregnant women and local markets to identify commonly consumed foods, household utensils and preparation methods. Photographs of locally used equipment and food portions usually eaten at one meal were taken during surveillance. These utensils were purchased at the market. After that, each utensil and portion were taken photograph and assigned a code. Those utensils used for food serving were standardised with food portions and water using a measuring cylinder and digital food portion weighing scale. The respondents were asked which utensil they used from the photographic atlas and the portion at the average by the equipment. Some food items like orange, mango, banana and lemon were recorded in number and size as large, medium and small. For mixed dish foods, the respondents were asked to list all the food types and the ingredients. For mixed dishes, the nutrient content was obtained from their recipes (figure 1).

In addition to assessing nutrient intake, the 24-hour recall data were used to determine the dietary diversity score (DDS) for the pregnant women. Dietary diversity was measured using the Minimum Dietary Diversity for Women tool developed and validated by FAO, which classifies foods into ten standard food groups.22 It included grains (white roots, tubers and plantains), pulses (beans, peas and lentils), nuts and seeds, dairy, meat (poultry and fish), eggs, dark green leafy vegetables, vitamin A-rich fruits and vegetables, other vegetables and fruits.

Operational definitions of terms

Nutrient inadequacy

An intake of essential nutrients below the recommended levels without necessarily leading to clinical symptoms or biological dysfunction, as defined by dietary assessments.1

Nutrient deficiency

A state of one or more nutrient shortages that impairs normal physiological functions or leads to specific health disorders, as defined by clinical or biochemical assessments.1

Micronutrient deficiency

A state of micronutrient shortage that impairs normal physiological functions or leads to specific health disorders, as defined by clinical or biochemical assessments.26

Micronutrient inadequacy

An intake of essential micronutrients below the recommended levels without necessarily leading to clinical symptoms or biological dysfunction, as defined by dietary assessments. In this study, a mean adequacy ratio (MAR) of less than 1 for the four key micronutrients namely vitamin A, calcium, iron and zinc among pregnant women was labelled as ‘inadequate’ micronutrient intake. Conversely, an MAR of equal to or greater than 1 for these micronutrients was labelled as ‘adequate’ micronutrient intake.27

Nutrient adequacy ratio

The actual micronutrient intake per day for a particular micronutrient divided by the estimated average requirement (EAR) of that micronutrient. In this study, nutrient adequacy ratio (NAR) was determined for vitamin A, calcium, iron and zinc. Then, an NAR of less than ‘1’ was labelled as ‘inadequate’, and an NAR of equal to or greater than ‘1’ was labelled as ‘adequate’ for each micronutrient.27

Mean adequacy ratio

The sum of NARs for micronutrients divided by the number of micronutrients evaluated in the study.27

Dietary diversity

The number of food groups ingested within the last 24 hours. A DDS of 10 was used to determine the food groups; low diversity score (<5 food groups) and high diversity score (>5 food groups).22

Portion size

The amount of a food item consumed at a time.28

Household food insecurity

Households that responded ‘no’ to all nine HFIAS questions, or responded ‘yes’ only to the first question and reported it occurred rarely, were classified as food secure. Households reporting any occurrence of reduced food quality or quantity, or ‘worry’ occurring more frequently were classified as food insecure.24

Meal frequency

The frequency of meals pregnant women consumed within the last 24 hours.29

Estimated average requirement

The average daily dietary intake level that is sufficient to meet the nutrient requirements of half of pregnant women.30

Data quality control

The questionnaire was prepared in English, translated into Amharic (local language), and back-translated for consistency. Training was provided to data collectors and supervisors. A pretest on 5% of the sample was done to assess the clarity of the questions and the applicability of the instrument. The reliability of the questionnaires was checked by reliability index measurement, which showed adequate internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha) of 0.862. The pretest results are not included in the final dataset and analysis. Data completeness was checked daily in the field, and incomplete questionnaires were reviewed and corrected immediately whenever possible. Consequently, no missing data were identified in the final dataset. The food weighing scale was calibrated to zero during the standardisation of the portion sizes of consumed food and photographs of household apparatuses were used for portion size estimation.

Data processing and analysis

The data were double-entered into the Epi-data and exported to SPSS V.26 for cleaning and statistical analysis. To characterise the study population, descriptive statistics including percentages, means, medians, SD and ranges were calculated.

The nutrient value of each food item per 100 g was primarily obtained from the Ethiopian food composition tables (EFCT).31 The World Food Composition Table was used to extract the micronutrient values that were not included in the EFCT.32 To calculate the actual micronutrient intake, each food item consumed by respondents, as well as the corresponding nutrient values of the studied micronutrients (calcium, zinc, iron and vitamin A), were entered into the nutria-survey 2007 Food Processor software. Finally, the nutrient values calculated for each respondent were transferred to Microsoft Excel and then to SPSS for analysis.

Inadequate intake of micronutrients (vitamin A, calcium, iron and zinc) was calculated by NAR and the overall prevalence of micronutrient intake inadequacy as calculated by MAR. The following formula was used to determine the NAR of the selected micronutrients and the MAR of all micronutrients included in this study. The EAR of nutrients was taken from the Institute of Medicine, the National Academies Press of America.30

Each NAR was truncated at 1, meaning that a nutrient with a high NAR cannot make up for a nutrient with a low NAR.

Both bivariable and multivariable binary logistic regression analyses were employed to identify the factors associated with overall micronutrient intake inadequacy. Initially, each independent variable was assessed using bivariable logistic regression. Variables with a p<0.25 in the bivariable analysis were subsequently included in the multivariable logistic regression model to avoid excluding potentially important predictors. The multivariable logistic regression analysis was then conducted to identify factors independently associated with micronutrient intake inadequacy while controlling for potential confounding effects. Model fitness was assessed using Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test (p>0.05). Adjusted ORs (AOR) with a 95% CI were computed, and statistical significance was declared at a p<0.05. Analysis comparing hospitals and health centres was performed using χ² test. No statistically significant difference in micronutrient inadequacy was observed between facility types (p>0.05), which is supported by the regression analysis presented in table 5.

Level of micronutrient inadequacy of pregnant women

Zinc, vitamin A, iron and calcium intake inadequacies were 48.2% (95% CI 44.7% to 52.2%), 66.7% (95% CI 63.3% to 70.7%), 14.8% (95% CI 11.8% to 17.2%) and 74% (95% CI 69.8% to 77.2%), respectively. The overall level of micronutrient intake inadequacy among respondents was 76.4% (95% CI 72.0% to 80.3%) (figure 2).

Predictors of overall micronutrient inadequacy

On multivariable binary logistic regression analysis, nutrition counselling, household food security status, meal frequency and DDS were significant predictors of the overall micronutrient inadequacy.

Pregnant women who did not receive nutrition counselling during ANC were 3.5 times more likely to have micronutrient intake inadequacy as compared with those who received nutrition counselling (AOR=3.6; 95% CI 2.00 to 6.48). Pregnant women who had less than four meal frequency were 3.66 times more likely to have overall micronutrient intake inadequacy as compared with those who had meal frequency greater than or equal to four (AOR=3.66, 95% CI 1.93 to 6.94). The odds of having overall micronutrient intake inadequacy were 4.78 times higher in pregnant women who had less than five DDS as compared with those who had greater than or equal to five (AOR=4.78; 95% CI 2.67 to 8.55). Being food insecure was three times more likely to have overall micronutrient intake inadequacy than those who are food secure (AOR=3.04, 95% CI 1.68 to 6.23) (table 5).

Discussion

This study examined the level and predictors of micronutrient intake inadequacy among pregnant women attending public health facilities in Bahir Dar City, Northwest Ethiopia. Based on the findings of this study, the overall micronutrient intake inadequacy among pregnant women was 76.4% (95% CI 72.0% to 80.3%).

The result of this study is in line with a study conducted in Mecha district, Northwest Ethiopia (72.3%)33 suggesting similar dietary and socioeconomic contexts. However, it is much higher than studies done in Indonesia (43%).34 Such differences may be attributed to variations in household food security, dietary diversity, nutrition education and access to nutrient-rich foods. In Ethiopia, diets are predominantly cereal-based and often lack adequate animal-source foods, fruits and vegetables, which may increase the risk of multiple micronutrient inadequacies. Furthermore, food insecurity remains common and may limit access to diverse and nutrient-dense foods during pregnancy.

In this study, the proportion of zinc intake inadequacy among pregnant women was 48%. This result is lower than the study done in Sidama, Ethiopia (99%),35 China (91%),36 Japan (63%),37 Indonesia (95.1%)38 and Vietnam (91.8%).39 However, it is higher than the results found in Brazil (15.3%)40 and USA (15.3%).41 These differences may reflect variations in dietary patterns and food sources of zinc. In many high-income settings, regular consumption of animal-source foods improves zinc intake, whereas Ethiopian diets are largely plant-based and depend heavily on cereals and legumes. Although legumes contribute zinc, the high phytate content of cereal-based diets can reduce zinc bioavailability.

According to this study, the proportion of iron intake inadequacy was 14.8%. This result is lower than the study done in Thailand (45.2%),20 China (64%),36 Indonesia (88.8%),38 Vietnam (96.9%,39 Brazil (97%),40 Boston USA (57%)41 and Portugal (88%).42 This difference might be due to the difference in consumption of ‘injera’ made up of ‘teff’ which is an iron-rich food commonly consumed in the study area, potentially contributing to better iron intake compared with populations where iron-rich foods are consumed less frequently.

This study revealed that the proportion of calcium intake inadequacy among pregnant women was 74%. This result is lower than the study done in Indonesia (97.9%),38 Vietnam (86.7%)39 and Brazil (82%).40 The difference may be due to differences in food availability and sociocultural factors that affect dietary habits among study participants. In the study area, Ethiopia, diets are mainly cereals and legumes which have low calcium content. Consumption of dairy products, rich sources of calcium, are limited in pregnant women due to low access and some cultural factors of dairy-food restriction during pregnancy. In addition, the predominance of cereal-based diets, which are generally poor sources of calcium, may further contribute to inadequate intake.

This study showed that the proportion of vitamin A intake inadequacy was 66.7%. This result is lower than the study done in Vietnam (81.6%)39 and Brazil (71%).40 However, it is higher than the study conducted in Japan (41.7%)37 and the USA (10.2%).41 Such differences may be associated with variations in the availability, accessibility and consumption of vitamin A-rich foods, including fruits, vegetables and animal-source products. Seasonal fluctuations in food availability, socioeconomic status and local dietary practices may also influence vitamin A intake. In Ethiopia, low consumption of fruits and vegetables, particularly among food-insecure households, may contribute to inadequate vitamin A intake during pregnancy.

Regarding predictors of overall micronutrient intake inadequacy, nutritional counselling, meal frequency, DDS and household food security status were significantly associated with overall micronutrient intake inadequacy. Pregnant women who did not receive nutrition counselling during ANC were 3.5 times more likely to have micronutrient intake inadequacy as compared with those who received nutrition counselling. This finding is supported by the studies conducted in East Wollega Zone, Ethiopia.43 This can be explained by the fact that nutritional guidance and information have a direct contribution in enhancing women’s nutritional knowledge and attitude which can enable them to choose diversified and nutritious foods rich in micronutrient content.

Likewise, pregnant women who did have less than four meal frequencies were 3.66 times more likely to have micronutrient intake inadequacy as compared with those who had meal frequencies greater than or equal to four. The possible explanation for this association is the fact that as meal frequency increases the intake of micronutrients in the diet will be increased and vice versa.

The odds of having overall micronutrient intake inadequacy were 4.78 times higher in pregnant women who had less than five DDS as compared with those who had greater than or equal to five. This finding is supported by studies conducted in South Africa.44 The likely reason could be the fact that as women’s DDSs increase the dietary quality improves, which in turn enhances the intake of different micronutrients.

Pregnant women who are food insecure were three times more likely to have overall micronutrient intake inadequacy than those who are food secure. It was supported by a study conducted in Indonesia.45 This could be because food insecurity can limit the availability and access of food, which in turn results in poor intake of micronutrients.

Our study has some strengths, including the application of the multiple-pass 24-hour dietary recall method and the quantitative estimation of micronutrient intakes. However, several limitations should be mentioned. First, the cross-sectional design precludes establishing temporal or causal relationships between predictor variables and micronutrient inadequacy. Second, dietary intake was assessed using a single 24-hour dietary recall, which may not accurately reflect usual dietary intake due to day-to-day variation in food consumption. Third, recall bias may have occurred because dietary assessment relied on participants’ memory. Fourth, social desirability bias may have influenced participants’ responses regarding dietary practices. Finally, because the study was conducted among ANC attendees in public health facilities, the findings may not be generalisable to pregnant women who do not attend ANC services or who receive care in private facilities.

Conclusions and recommendations

This study found that micronutrient intake inadequacy was high among pregnant women. Inadequacies of calcium, vitamin A, zinc and iron were also substantial, indicating poor dietary quality during pregnancy. Lack of nutrition counselling during ANC, low meal frequency, inadequate dietary diversity and household food insecurity were significant predictors of micronutrient intake inadequacy. These findings suggest that micronutrient inadequacy among pregnant women is influenced not only by individual dietary behaviours but also by broader food security and health service factors. Addressing these determinants is essential to improve maternal nutrition and reduce the risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes associated with inadequate micronutrient intake.

Therefore, strengthening integration of nutrition counselling into routine ANC services, promoting consumption of diversified diets through community-based behaviour change interventions, supporting nutrient-dense food production through home gardening and small-scale livestock production and enhancing social protection programmes to improve food security are essential. Moreover, further analytical studies are recommended to identify unexplored determinants of micronutrient inadequacy and assess seasonal variations in dietary intake.
