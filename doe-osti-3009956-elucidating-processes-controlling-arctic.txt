Final Technical Report, 09/01/2018 – 08/31/2025

Abstract/Executive Summary: Atmospheric aerosols play critical roles in the Earth’s
energy budget, directly by scattering or absorbing solar and terrestrial radiation and
indirectly by serving as seeds (nuclei) for cloud droplet and ice crystal formation and by
depositing on snow and ice surface, thereby changing the surface albedo. These effects are
dependent on aerosol particle size and chemical composition and impact the hydrological
cycle as well. This project provided single-particle size and chemical composition
measurements across the entire annual cycle in the high Arctic and in the Alaskan Arctic
during fall – winter, addressing the most signiﬁcant gaps in Arctic aerosol observational data.
These needs were based on recent rapid sea ice loss across the entire Arctic, as well as the
major annual delays in sea ice freeze-up during fall in the Chukchi Sea and increased
wintertime sea ice fracturing in the Beaufort Sea, both off the North Slope of Alaska.
 Two DOE Atmospheric Radiation Measurement (ARM) ﬁeld campaigns were
conducted for atmospheric aerosol sampling. The Aerosols during the Polar Utqiagvik Night
(APUN – ‘snow on ground’ in Iñupiaq) ARM ﬁeld campaign at Utqagivik, Alaska was
conducted from Oct. 28 – Dec. 22, 2018. Aerosol sizing instrumentation and a single-particle
mass spectrometer were successfully deployed for size-resolved number concentration
measurements and measurements of individual particle size and chemical composition,
respectively. These results show the inﬂuence of locally-produced sea spray aerosol, with
high cloud-forming potential, due to delayed sea ice freeze-up in the fall. During the 2019-
2020 international Multidisciplinary drifting Observatory for the Study of Arctic Climate
(MOSAiC) expedition, daily atmospheric aerosol particles were collected aboard the
German icebreaker Polarstern in the Central Arctic from Nov. 2019 – Oct. 2020. Sea salt
aerosol and marine organics were observed year-round during MOSAiC with varying
morphologies and sources. These ﬁndings are important because most Arctic models do not
include a sea spray aerosol source, despite this source increasing with declining sea ice
extent. In addition to collecting new samples and data, this project also conducted further
analysis of previously collected single-particle chemical composition measurements within
the North Slope of Alaska oil ﬁelds and at Utqiaġvik, AK, during Aug. – Sep. 2015 and 2016
ﬁeld campaigns. This work resulted in the discovery of chemical reactions of oil ﬁeld
combustion emissions occurring within fog droplets across the North Slope of Alaska and
forming secondary aerosol, showing the impact of Arctic oil ﬁeld emissions beyond black
carbon aerosol and greenhouse gases. In addition, the distribution of chemical species
across the aerosol population within the oil ﬁelds was quantiﬁed, using these data and a
previously development framework. We also presented the ﬁrst ambient evidence of the

 Overall, this project has elucidated connections between seawater biogeochemistry,

Scientiﬁc and Technical Information Summary: This project provided single-particle size
and chemical composition measurements across the entire annual cycle in the high Arctic
and in the Alaskan Arctic during fall – winter, addressing the most signiﬁcant gaps in Arctic
aerosol observational data. These needs were based on recent rapid sea ice loss across the
entire Arctic, as well as the major annual delays in sea ice freeze-up during fall in the Chukchi
Sea and increased wintertime sea ice fracturing in the Beaufort Sea, both off the North Slope
of Alaska. Two DOE Atmospheric Radiation Measurement (ARM) ﬁeld campaigns (2018-
2020) were conducted in the Arctic for atmospheric aerosol sampling and measurements.
Due to lab-based experimental delays due to COVID-19 and national lab instrument
downtime, analysis of the data resulting from these two campaigns were delayed, and
therefore, additional analyses of samples and data collected during prior Arctic ARM ﬁeld
campaigns (2015 and 2016) were conducted.
 Using atmospheric particle samples collected during our previous Aug. - Sep. 2015
ARM ﬁeld campaign at Utqiaġvik, AK, and with sample and data analysis enabled by this
grant, we published our manuscript "Solid organic-coated ammonium sulfate particles at
high relative humidity in the summertime Arctic atmosphere" in the Proceedings of the
National Academy of Sciences (Kirpes et al., 2022; doi:10.1073/pnas.2104496119). In this
work, we reported the observation of solid organic-coated ammonium sulfate particles
representing 30% of particles, by number, in a key size range (<0.2 µm) for cloud activation
within marine air masses from the Arctic Ocean. The composition and size of the observed
particles are consistent with recent Arctic modeling and observational results (in Svalbard
and the Canadian Archipelago) showing new particle formation and growth from
dimethylsulﬁde oxidation to form sulfuric acid, reaction with ammonia, and condensation
of marine biogenic sulfate and highly oxygenated organic molecules. The observed solid
phase is hypothesized to occur from contact efflorescence during collision of a newly
formed Aitken mode sulfate particle with an organic-coated ammonium sulfate particle, and
this work represents the ﬁrst ambient evidence supporting laboratory proposed contact
efflorescence. With declining sea ice in the warming Arctic, this particle source is expected
to increase with increasing open water and marine biogenic emissions. The results reported
in Kirpes et al. (2022) were highlighted in an article in the April 2022 DOE Atmospheric
System Research (ASR) newsletter. These results were also highlighted in press releases
from the University of Michigan News, Science Daily, EurekAlert! (AAAS), and Phys.org.

 Using data collected during our previous Aug. - Sep. 2016 ARM ﬁeld campaign at
Oliktok Point, AK, and with data analysis enabled by this grant, we published our manuscript
"Hydroxymethanesulfonate (HMS) Formation during Summertime Fog in an Arctic Oil Field"
in Environmental Science & Technology Letters (Liu et al., 2021;
doi:10.1021/acs.estlett.1c00357). This work shows that sulfur dioxide (SO2) and
formaldehyde (HCHO), emitted within the North Slope of Alaska oil ﬁelds, dissolve into fog
droplets and aqueous aerosols during frequent regional fog events and react to form HMS.
We estimated the production of HMS to be ~10 times higher in the oil ﬁelds compared to the
upwind Beaufort Sea, showing the importance of local Arctic emissions in secondary
aerosol production. HMS has gained recent attention due to observations in Chinese winter
haze, as well as global modeling. This work represents the ﬁrst observation of HMS in the
Arctic and points to far greater impacts of Arctic resource extractions emissions than simply
primary black carbon and greenhouse gases.
 Also using data collected during our previous Aug. - Sep. 2016 ARM ﬁeld campaign at
Oliktok Point, AK, and with sample and data analysis enabled by this grant, we published our
manuscript "Quantifying the Diversity of an Atmospheric Aerosol Population in an Arctic Oil
Field on a Single-Particle Level" in the Journal of Geophysical Research-Atmospheres (Wu et
al., 2024; doi:10.1029/2024JD041001). In this work, we quantiﬁed atmospheric aerosol
diversity using the framework of Riemer and West (2013) and online single-particle mass
spectrometry data (32,880 individual particles), offline single-particle microscopy data
(20,912 individual particles), and online bulk aerosol mass spectrometry and aethalometer
data. Individual atmospheric aerosol particle masses were dominated by only a few
chemical species of interest, with aged aerosol particles (those containing nitrate and/or
sulfate) exhibiting higher single-particle diversities compared to recently emitted (fresh)
aerosol particles. During oil ﬁeld plume periods, average single-particle diversities reﬂected
an abundance of diesel combustion particles, which were rich in sulfate, black carbon, and
organic aerosol. Overall, the submicron aerosol population within the Arctic oil ﬁelds were
found to be relatively externally mixed (mixing state index < 50%) due to the constant local
emissions within oil ﬁelds combining with background aerosol and locally emitted sea spray
aerosol at the coastal site. These insights improve assumptions for regional and global
atmospheric chemical transport modeling.
 Based on PI Pratt’s experience studying atmospheric aerosol composition at the ARM
Oliktok Point site, she was invited and contributed as a coauthor to the publication led by
Paciﬁc Northwest National Laboratory Environmental Molecular Sciences Laboratory (PNNL
EMSL) collaborators titled “Vertical gradient in atmospheric particle phase state: a case
study over the Alaskan Arctic oil ﬁelds” by Lata et al. (2025) in Environmental Science:
Atmospheres (doi:10.1039/d4ea00150h). This work reported on the single-particle chemical
composition and phase states of individual atmospheric particles collected via tethered
balloon sampling at Oliktok Point, AK, within the NSA oil ﬁelds. These results have
implications for cloud formation and processing of aerosols within clouds.
 We conducted the Aerosols during the Polar Utqiagvik Night (APUN – ‘snow on
ground’ in Iñupiaq) ARM ﬁeld campaign at Utqagivik, AK (North Slope of Alaska, NSA ARM

site), from Oct. 28 – Dec. 22, 2018, during Polar Night (24 h of darkness). We successfully
deployed our aerosol time-of-ﬂight mass spectrometer (ATOFMS) for measurements of
individual particle size and chemical composition in real time (>500,000 individual particles
from Nov. 8 – Dec. 18, 2018). In addition, online aerosol size distribution measurements were
conducted, and particles were collected on substrates for offline analysis by scanning
electron microscopy with energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM-EDX) at PNNL EMSL.
The APUN ﬁeld campaign resulted in an abundance of data that are contributing to ﬁve
manuscripts, under internal and coauthor review and revisions, for journal submission soon.
1) Our manuscript titled “Sea ice leads as a source of Arctic sea spray aerosol: A combined
 in-situ measurement and remote sensing study” (Wu et al.) has been drafted for
 submission to the Journal of Geophysical Research-Atmospheres. This work combines
 our in-situ APUN sea spray aerosol observations with a new NASA-funded remote
 sensing sea ice product for examination of sea spray aerosol production from leads
 (open fractures in the sea ice). We showed that even <1% open water over sea ice within
 a 24 h backward air mass trajectory can explain local sea spray aerosol observations.
 This is important as global models typically neglect sea spray aerosol emissions in the
 polar regions when open water is <50%.
2) Our manuscript titled “Delayed Arctic sea ice freeze-up results in late fall sea spray
 aerosol production” (Costa et al.) has been drafted for submission to ACS Earth & Space
 Chemistry. This work combines real-time, single-particle mass spectrometry and offline
 SEM-EDX and Raman microspectroscopy for a detailed chemical characterization of sea
 spray aerosol. Fresh (locally produced) sea salt aerosol were abundant and displayed
 coatings of marine carbohydrates. These results show that declining levels of Arctic sea
 ice and delayed sea ice freeze-up are resulting in the local production of sea spray
 aerosol, with high cloud-forming potential, later into the year.
3) Our manuscript titled “Quantifying Arctic aerosol diversity from aging processes during
 the fall-winter transition using in-situ and bulk measurements” (Wu et al.) has been
 drafted for submission to the Journal of Geophysical Research-Atmospheres. For this
 work, detailed laboratory experiments were conducted to quantify instrument
 sensitivities to transparent (sea salt) and absorbing (biomass burning) aerosols. Single-
 particle and bulk aerosol measurements were combined to chemically characterize and
 quantify the aerosol population, which was observed to be externally mixed, with aged
 particles from aerosol transport (i.e. Arctic haze) and constant local aerosol emissions
 (e.g., sea spray aerosol). Most particles were observed to undergo atmospheric
 reactions, with higher average sulfate mass fractions within carbon-rich, combustion-
 derived particles than inorganic-rich (dust and sea spray aerosol) particles. This work
 included aerosol diversity quantitation based on the framework of Riemer and West
 (2013).
4) Our manuscript titled “Natural and anthropogenic contributions to submicron Arctic
 haze” (Selimovic et al.) has been drafted for submission to Science Advances. In this
 work, we used single-particle mass spectrometry data to examine the chemical
 composition and sources of atmospheric aerosol particles contributing to Arctic haze.

 Aerosol mass, number, and light scattering are quantitatively assessed in the context of
 these single-particle data. Our ﬁndings challenge the prevailing reliance on using bulk
 aerosol tracer species for source apportionment and highlight the need for policies that
 address both anthropogenic (industrial and combustion) and wildﬁre emissions.
5) Our manuscript titled “Natural and anthropogenic sources of atmospheric iron” (Wu et
 al.) has been drafted for submission to Science Advances. Iron is a vital nutrient
 inﬂuencing phytoplankton productivity and global carbon recycling. A major iron source
 in oceans is atmospheric particle deposition, but iron aerosol source studies in remote
 marine regions, particularly the Arctic, are limited. This study investigates the chemical
 composition of individual iron-containing particles in the Alaskan Arctic winter for source
 and atmospheric processing information impacting iron solubility, deposition, and
 recycling. We evaluated our single-particle results using an Earth System model.
 We deployed our atmospheric particle sampler (3-stage DRUM impactor) for the
historic 2019 –2020 Multidisciplinary drifting Observatory for the Study of Arctic Climate
(MOSAiC) expedition aboard the German icebreaker Polarstern in the Central Arctic. PI Pratt
attended the March 2019 MOSAiC planning workshop in Germany and was an active
participant in virtual MOSAiC Atmosphere Team meetings. PI Pratt also contributed as a
coauthor to the publication titled “Overview of the MOSAiC expedition: Atmosphere” in
Elementa (Shupe et al., 2022, doi:10.1525/elementa.2021.00060). Installation and
operation of our guest instrument was achieved in collaboration with Los Alamos National
Laboratory, the ARM program, and Dr. Jessie Creamean (Colorado State University). Daily,
size-resolved atmospheric particle samples were collected from Nov. 2019 to Sep. 2020,
except for Feb. 2020 due to technician crew change delays. We developed a protocol for
separating the daily samples and analyzing them via SEM-EDX, as the long-term sampling
required the use of a unique particle substrate that needed additional characterization and
method development prior to sample analyses. Sample analyses were severely delayed due
to electron microscope instrument access restrictions due to COVID-19 and instrument
failures at PNNL EMSL. We contributed single-particle ﬂuorescence imaging to the
publication led by our Swiss collaborators titled “Characteristics and sources of ﬂuorescent
aerosols in the central Arctic Ocean” in Elementa (Beck et al., 2024;
doi:10.1525/elementa.2023.00125). We have two manuscripts in preparation. The ﬁrst
manuscript titled “Morphology and chemical composition of sea salt aerosols over the
Central Arctic Ocean during the MOSAiC expedition” (Zhu et al.) is in preparation for
submission to Science Advances. This work uses distinct observed single-particle
morphology to differentiate sea spray and blowing snow sources of individual sea salt
aerosol, with implications for modeling of the sea salt aerosol source of cloud condensation
nuclei. The second manuscript “Primary Marine Organics in Sea Spray Aerosols across the
Annual Cycle during the MOSAiC Expedition” (Zhu et al.) is in preparation for submission to
ACS Earth & Space Chemistry. This work examines the morphologies of marine microgels
emitted as sea spray with and without inorganic salts using SEM and the chemical
composition of these marine organics using Raman and optical photothermal infrared (O-
PTIR) microscopy.
