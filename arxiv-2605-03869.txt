On Adaptivity in Zeroth-Order Optimization
Hassan Dbouk, Nidham Gazagnadou, Matthias Reisser, Christos Louizos

Abstract
We investigate the effectiveness of adaptive zeroth-order (ZO) optimization for memory-constrained fine-tuning of large language models (LLMs). Contrary to prior claims, we show that adaptive ZO methods such as ZO-Adam offer no convergence advantage over well-tuned ZO-SGD, while incurring significant memory overhead. Our analysis reveals that in high dimensions, ZO gradients lack coordinate-wise heterogeneity, rendering adaptive mechanisms memory inefficient. Leveraging this insight, we propose MEAZO, a memory-efficient adaptive ZO optimizer that tracks only a single scalar for global step size adaptation. We support our method with theoretical convergence guarantees under standard assumptions. Experiments across multiple LLM families and tasks demonstrate that MEAZO matches ZO-Adam’s performance with the memory footprint of ZO-SGD. Additional experiments on synthetic quadratic problems and LLM fine‑tuning further demonstrate MEAZO’s enhanced robustness to step size choices, particularly in grouped or block‑structured optimization settings.

Introduction

Fine-tuning large language models (LLMs) can specialize their behavior to various use-cases: aligning to a specific domain, application, task
(
12
)
or integrating user preferences
(
8
)

Backpropagation is a standard but memory and computationally heavy fine-tuning algorithm, due to its need of activation caching and floating-point precision.

Zeroth-order (ZO) methods on the other hand have recently gained attention in the LLM literature as they allow model fine-tuning using only forward passes
(
18
)
, and are thus more memory and computationally efficient.
In a nutshell, ZO optimization approximates the gradient through the loss difference at randomly-perturbed trainable parameters.

Due to the inherent stochasticity of parameter perturbations, ZO gradients exhibit high-variance leading to slower convergence in terms of number of optimization steps compared to first-order (FO) methods.
However, each ZO step is typically less computationally intensive than the backpropagation step required by FO methods.
Therefore, ZO vs. FO poses a trade-off in speed/energy to convergence given specific hardware constraints and task-specific characteristics.

A substantial line of recent work aims to address this variance issue by introducing adaptive ZO algorithms, such as ZO‑Adam and its variants
(
4
;
20
;
14
;
30
)
. These methods mirror FO adaptive optimizers by tracking first and second moments of ZO gradients with the goal of improving convergence.

In this work, we revisit this assumption and show that, contrary to prior claims, FO‑style adaptivity provides limited benefits for ZO optimization in high‑dimensional transformer models. Recent studies on FO training dynamics
(
41
;
32
)
demonstrate that Adam’s advantage over SGD arises from gradient heterogeneity: a strong coordinate‑wise variation in gradient magnitudes. We provide evidence that ZO gradient estimates, due to the isotropic nature of random perturbations in high dimension, are essentially homogeneous. As a consequence, adaptive per‑coordinate normalization has little meaningful structure to exploit.

Adaptivity and Zeroth-order Optimization

Coordinate-wise adaptive optimizers, such as Adam
(
15
)
, have become the default choice for training and fine-tuning LLMs in first-order (FO) optimization settings. Their success stems from the ability to leverage per-coordinate gradient statistics, enabling effective step size adaptation across different dimensions. Specifically, Adam maintains bias-corrected exponential moving averages (EMAs) of the first moment,
\hat{\mathbf{m}}_{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, and second moment,
\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, of the gradient and updates parameters
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
as:

\mathbf{x}_{t+1}=\mathbf{x}_{t}-\eta\frac{\hat{\mathbf{m}}_{t}}{\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{t}}+\zeta},

(4)

where
\eta>0
is the step size and
\zeta>0
is a small quantity to improve numerical stability.
This formulation allows Adam to adaptively scale updates for each coordinate based on historical gradient information.

This advantage does not naturally extend to the ZO optimization context, where such per-coordinate structure is absent. Unlike FO gradients, the estimator in (
2
) is dominated by randomness for practical values of
q
rather than per-coordinate gradient information. The estimator averages over only
q
random directions, where typically
q\ll d
. Consequently, even when averaging, the signal remains highly isotropic and does not capture rich per-coordinate patterns. Consequently, applying coordinate-wise adaptive methods such as Adam in this setting introduces unnecessary complexity and computational overhead without providing meaningful benefits.

Proposition
3.1
formalizes this intuition by analyzing the expected squared gradient:

Proposition 3.1

(Gaussian)
.

Let
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be an
L
-smooth function, and let

\hat{\nabla}f_{\varepsilon}^{q}(\mathbf{x})
denote the
q
-sample ZO estimator in (
2
),
where
\mathbf{u}_{i}\sim{\cal N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}_{d})
for all
i\in[q]\vcentcolon=\{1,\ldots,q\}
. Then in the
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
limit we have:

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}}\left[{\hat{\nabla}f_{\varepsilon}^{q}(\mathbf{x})^{2}}\right]\approx\frac{1}{q}\left(\left\|{\nabla f(\mathbf{x})}\right\|^{2}\mathbf{1}_{d}+\nabla f(\mathbf{x})^{2}\right)+\nabla f(\mathbf{x})^{2},

(5)

where
\mathbf{(\cdot)}^{2}
stands for the element-wise power of 2 and
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}}
denotes the expectation over
\mathbf{u}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{q}
.

The proof of Proposition
3.1
appears in Appendix
H.1
, and the corresponding result for the uniform perturbation estimator is given in Appendix
H.2
.
Proposition
3.1
reveals that the use of ZO gradient estimators introduces a bias in the expected squared gradient. This bias term scales as
\frac{1}{q}\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\|^{2}
and vanishes as
q\to\infty
. However, in practical settings where
q\ll d
, and under the reasonable assumption that
\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\|^{2}
grows with dimension
d
faster than
1/q
, this bias becomes dominant. Importantly, the dominant component depends on the global gradient norm rather than individual coordinates, making it effectively coordinate-agnostic. The same observation also applies to Proposition
H.1
with uniform perturbations. Consequently, using coordinate-wise adaptive optimizers (
e.g.
, Adam) is misaligned with the underlying statistics, since the estimator tracks global rather than per-coordinate information in expectation.

MEAZO

Design Intuition.
MEAZO (Algorithm
1
) is inspired by adaptive methods like Adam but tailored for the ZO setting. In standard Adam, per-parameter first and second moments enable fine-grained adaptivity. However, as demonstrated in Sec.
3
, perturbations are isotropic and directions change every iteration, making per-parameter statistics ineffective and wasting memory. Instead, MEAZO maintains a
single global statistic
of the squared averaged projected gradient norm:

v_{t}\approx\text{EMA}\big(g_{t}^{2}\big),\quad g_{t}=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{i=1}^{q}\Delta f_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}_{t};\mathbf{u}_{i}).

This scalar tracks global curvature and enables adaptive step size scaling without sacrificing ZO’s simplicity.

Update Rule.
The MEAZO update at iteration
t
is:

\mathbf{x}_{t+1}=\mathbf{x}_{t}-\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{\hat{v}_{t}}+\zeta}\left(\frac{1}{q}\sum_{i=1}^{q}\Delta f_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}_{t};\mathbf{u}_{i})\mathbf{u}_{i}\right),

where
\hat{v}_{t}
is the bias-corrected EMA of
g_{t}^{2}
, and
\zeta
is a small constant for numerical stability. This preserves the benefits of adaptivity while keeping memory overhead comparable to ZO-SGD.

Remark 4.5
.

Affine-variance bounded adaptive SGD has been previously studied in
(
6
;
1
)
in the context of AdaGrad-Norm
(
34
)
. However, their motivation is fundamentally different from ours. These works employ the affine-variance assumption because they aim to derive guarantees for AdaGrad-Norm under the least restrictive possible conditions (without assuming bounded gradients or bounded variance). In contrast, our EMA-based method requires the affine-variance assumption solely because zero-order (ZO) gradient estimators inherently produce it: even under standard variance-bounded noise, ZO gradients introduce an extra gradient-dependent term, yielding an affine-variance structure. Thus, this assumption is not a modeling choice but an unavoidable consequence of using ZO gradients.

Corollary 4.9

(MEAZO Convergence Rate)
.

If the assumptions and conditions in Theorem
4.7
hold, then after
T
iterations of Algorithm
1
with
\mathbf{u}\sim\mathrm{Uniform}({\mathbb{S}})
, the iterates satisfy

O\!\left(\ \frac{F(\mathbf{x}_{0})-F^{*}}{\eta\,T}+\sigma^{2}+\frac{d}{q}\sigma^{2}+\frac{d^{2}}{q}\varepsilon^{2}\right),

(13)

where the
O(\cdot)
notation hides constants independent of
d,q,T,
and
\sigma^{2}
, and non-dominant terms in
\varepsilon^{2}
.

We observe that the first term correspond to the standard convergence rate of FO Adam to a small neighborhood of size
O\left(\sigma^{2}\right)
around a stationary point, proved in
(
37
)
, which is often sufficient for large-scale machine learning problems in line with the risk-computation tradeoff emphasized by
(
3
)
.

As defined in Assumption
4.2
, the constant
O\left(\sigma^{2}\right)
is inherent to the stochasticity of the gradients, which are themselves estimated with ZO gradients. Thus the second term implies a convergence of MEAZO to a small neighborhood of size
O\left(\frac{d}{q}\sigma^{2}\right)
around a stationary point.
Unlike the previous noise term, the latter can be reduced by increasing the number of samples
q
.

To get a convergence in
O\left(\frac{d}{T}\right)
for the third ZO gradient estimation term, we require setting the perturbation magnitude to a small value often proportional to
\varepsilon=O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{dT}}\right)
, as also observed for ZO-AdaMM under non-convex assumptions
(
4
)
.

4.2
Grouped MEAZO

MEAZO naturally extends to the grouped ZO estimator in (
3
). In block ZO, each block
j
produces its own projected gradient:

g_{j}=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{i=1}^{q}\Delta f_{\varepsilon}\big(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{m}_{j}\odot\mathbf{u}_{i}\big),

allowing us to maintain a scalar
v_{j}
per block. The grouped MEAZO update becomes:

\sum_{j=1}^{p}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\hat{v}_{j}}+\zeta}\left(\frac{1}{q}\sum_{i=1}^{q}\Delta f_{\varepsilon}\big(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{m}_{j}\odot\mathbf{u}_{i}\big)(\mathbf{m}_{j}\odot\mathbf{u}_{i})\right),

providing block-level adaptivity without incurring per-parameter memory costs.

Efficient Implementation
. Following the decomposition strategy in
(
40
)
, we treat each decoder block as a single optimization block. To exploit the sequential structure of the network, we apply perturbations beginning at the first block, ending at the last block. To avoid recomputing the base activation for block
j
during each perturbation, we cache the unperturbed activation and reuse it for all queries within that block. This reduces redundant computation, especially for
q>1
, while preserving correctness. See Appendix
F
for details.

Discussion and Future Work

Please find a discussion of relevant related work in Appendix
B
.
Our results show that well-tuned ZO-SGD can match or outperform adaptive methods like ZO-Adam, due to the lack of coordinate-wise structure in ZO gradients in high dimensions, which limits the utility of adaptivity. We also show that ZO-SGD is less robust to the choice of step size, where adaptive methods have an advantage. We believe this robustness is particularly valuable for edge‑device personalization, where memory is limited, ZO fine‑tuning can be executed using built‑in inference engines, and data heterogeneity across devices leads to differing optimal step sizes. Motivated by this, we introduce MEAZO, a scalar adaptive ZO method that achieves ZO-Adam-level performance with the memory efficiency of ZO-SGD and show its robustness across step sizes. Future directions include refining our convergence results to
0
rather than to a noise-dominated neighborhood of a stationary point. We also consider extending MEAZO to subspace ZO frameworks to leverage adaptation (sparsity, block-coordinate descent, low-rank perturbations)
(
25
)
, as well as full-model fine-tuning, where the low-memory property of MEAZO is most pronounced.

Impact Statement

This paper presents work whose goal is to advance the field of Machine
Learning. There are many potential societal consequences of our work, only one
of which bears relevance here: ZO optimization specifically targets low-memory training environments and therefore opens an avenue for more researchers and practitioners with access to limited resources to participate in fine-tuning models.

Appendix B
Related Work

B.1
Zeroth-Order Gradient Estimation

Theoretical foundations of ZO optimization were established in early works such as
(
10
)
and
(
21
)
, which analyzed convergence properties of ZO-SGD using Gaussian perturbations, or the convergence in the online setting with perturbations sampled uniformly from the unit sphere
(
7
)
.
Recently, MeZO
(
18
)
introduced a memory-efficient variant of ZO-SGD by storing scalar seeds instead of sampled perturbations, significantly reducing memory usage.

Comprehensive benchmarking of ZO methods has been conducted by
(
40
)
to evaluate their empirical performance across tasks, highlighting trade-offs between estimator variance, convergence speed, and memory efficiency.

B.2
Adaptive Zeroth-Order Methods

To enhance convergence and robustness, adaptive ZO algorithms have incorporated techniques inspired by first-order optimization. ZO-AdaMM
(
4
)
and ZEMA
(
20
)
introduced adaptive step sizes based on first and second moment estimates of the gradient approximation. However, these methods did not demonstrate theoretical convergence improvements over ZO-SGD in convex and non-convex constrained settings.

Recent advances have focused on refining the use of momentum and variance reduction. ZO-AdaMU
(
14
)
introduced adaptive momentum for perturbations, smoothing both gradient estimates and parameter updates.
FZOO
(
5
)
jointly improved ZO gradient estimation, by combining Rademacher perturbations, and step size adaptation, using the standard deviation of the batch losses.

\mathcal{R}
-AdaZO
(
30
)
leveraged variance-reduced gradient information embedded in the momentum to refine second moment estimates, leading to improved convergence on synthetic data.

D.2
Classification Training Loss

40

C

In our setup, we instead treat classification (SST-2) as straightforward causal language modeling (CLM). During training, we provide only the ground-truth continuation (the correct verbalized label) and maximize its next-token likelihood (no negative labels and no classification loss). However, at evaluation time, we adopt the same procedure: for each class, we compute the same average log-probability score for its verbalized label and select the label with the highest score. Despite the simpler training objective, we find minimal degradation in final model performance.

Coarse grid.

\eta\in\{{1}\mathrm{e}{-6},{5}\mathrm{e}{-6},\ldots,{1}\mathrm{e}{-1}\}

Let
\eta^{\star}

\eta^{-},\eta^{+}

(\eta^{-},\eta^{\star})
and
(\eta^{\star},\eta^{+})
, i.e., values of the form
\eta=m\times 10^{k}
with
m\in\{1,\dots,9\}
whose magnitude lies within the bracket. When a bracket crosses a decade boundary, we enumerate the remaining integer-mantissa values in the lower decade and the leading values in the upper decade.

Conclusion.

Overall, the conclusions drawn from our LLM experiments carry over to both the vision and multimodal settings. First, when properly tuned, ZO-SGD performs comparably to adaptive methods. Second, per-coordinate adaptivity offers no tangible benefit: scalar-based MEAZO closely tracks the performance of ZO-Adam. Finally, the primary advantage of adaptivity lies in improved robustness to step-size selection, rather than in per-coordinate scaling itself.

E.7
Impact of
\beta_{1}
in Adaptive ZO Optimization

In MEAZO (Alg.
1
) we employ second moment normalization of the projected gradients only. MEAZO can be viewed as a compressed variant of ZO-Adam with
\beta_{1}=0
, by design. The key quantity is the average projected gradient
g
(line 8), which captures directional information.

In the update step (line 12),

\mathbf{x}_{t}\leftarrow\mathbf{x}_{t-1}-\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{\hat{v}}+\zeta}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{q}\frac{\Delta f_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{t};\xi)}{q}\mathbf{u}_{i}\right),

each noise vector
\mathbf{u}_{i}
is weighted by its corresponding projected gradient. This ensures proper directional credit assignment. Replacing the sum with a single smoothed scalar would discard this structure and degrade the update quality.

Figure
18
further supports this design choice: disabling
\beta_{1}
in ZO-Adam has little to no impact on convergence when fine-tuning Llama 3.2 1B on XSum. This reinforces our decision to avoid smoothed first-moment updates in MEAZO.

Appendix G
General Definitions and Lemmas

Let us state first useful optimization definitions and lemmas needed for our convergence proofs.

Definition G.1

(
L
-smoothness)
.

A differentiable function
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is said to be
L
-smooth
if its gradient is
L
-Lipschitz continuous, that is,

\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x})-\nabla f(\mathbf{y})\|\leq L\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|,\quad\forall\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

Equivalently, for all
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

f(\mathbf{y})\leq f(\mathbf{x})+\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|^{2}.

(17)

Lemma G.2

(Lemma 1.2.3 of
(
22
)
)
.

If a differentiable function
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is
L
-smooth
, then for all
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

|f(\mathbf{y})-f(\mathbf{x})-\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\rangle|\leq\frac{L}{2}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|^{2}.

(18)

Lemma G.3

(ZO Projected Gradient upper bound)
.

Let
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
\varepsilon>0
.
If a differentiable function
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is
L
-smooth
, then

\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{u}\rangle-\frac{L\varepsilon}{2}\left\|{\mathbf{u}}\right\|^{2}\leq\frac{f(\mathbf{x}+\varepsilon\mathbf{u})-f(\mathbf{x}-\varepsilon\mathbf{u})}{2\varepsilon}\leq\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{u}\rangle+\frac{L\varepsilon}{2}\left\|{\mathbf{u}}\right\|^{2},

(19)

which implies that

\left|\frac{f(\mathbf{x}+\varepsilon\mathbf{u})-f(\mathbf{x}-\varepsilon\mathbf{u})}{2\varepsilon}\right|\leq\left|\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{u}\rangle\right|+\frac{L\varepsilon}{2}\left\|{\mathbf{u}}\right\|^{2}.

(20)

Proof.

Applying (
17
) with
\mathbf{y}\vcentcolon=\mathbf{x}+\varepsilon\mathbf{u}
leads to:

f(\mathbf{x}+\varepsilon\mathbf{u})\leq f(\mathbf{x})+\varepsilon\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{u}\rangle+\frac{L\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\left\|{\mathbf{u}}\right\|^{2}.

Similarly, applying (
18
) with
\mathbf{y}\vcentcolon=\mathbf{x}-\varepsilon\mathbf{u}
and multiplying the left-hand side by
-1
gives

-f(\mathbf{x}-\varepsilon\mathbf{u})\leq-f(\mathbf{x})+\varepsilon\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{u}\rangle+\frac{L\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\left\|{\mathbf{u}}\right\|^{2}.

Summing up the above two equations and dividing by
2\varepsilon
proves to (
19
):

\frac{f(\mathbf{x}+\varepsilon\mathbf{u})-f(\mathbf{x}-\varepsilon\mathbf{u})}{2\varepsilon}\leq\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{u}\rangle+\frac{L\varepsilon}{2}\left\|{\mathbf{u}}\right\|^{2}.

(21)

One can also apply the proved inequality to
\mathbf{u}\leftarrow-\mathbf{u}
and multiply it by
-1
, which proves the left-hand side inequality of:

\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{u}\rangle-\frac{L\varepsilon}{2}\left\|{\mathbf{u}}\right\|^{2}\leq\frac{f(\mathbf{x}+\varepsilon\mathbf{u})-f(\mathbf{x}-\varepsilon\mathbf{u})}{2\varepsilon}\leq\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{u}\rangle+\frac{L\varepsilon}{2}\left\|{\mathbf{u}}\right\|^{2}.

(22)

Applying the fact that for any
A,M\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
B\geq 0
,
A-B\leq M\leq A+B\implies\left|M\right|\leq\left|A\right|+B
to inequalities in (
22
) concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma G.4

(Smoothing -
G
-Lipschitzness Transfer)
.

Let
F:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a
G
-Lipschitz function. Then its smoothed version
F_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}\sim\mathcal{D}}\big[F(\mathbf{x}+\varepsilon\mathbf{u})\big]
is also
G
-Lipschitz.

Proof.

Since
F
is
G
-Lipschitz, for any
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
we have

\left|F(\mathbf{x})-F(\mathbf{y})\right|\leq G\left\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right\|.

For the smoothed function
F_{\varepsilon}
, consider

\left|F_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})-F_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{y})\right|=\left|\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[{F(\mathbf{x}+\varepsilon\mathbf{u})-F(\mathbf{y}+\varepsilon\mathbf{u})}\right]\right|.

\left|F_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})-F_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{y})\right|\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[{\left|F(\mathbf{x}+\varepsilon\mathbf{u})-F(\mathbf{y}+\varepsilon\mathbf{u})\right|}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[{G\left\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right\|}\right]=G\left\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right\|.

Hence

is
G
-Lipschitz.

(Smoothing -
L
-smoothness Transfer)
.

Let
F:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be an
L
-smooth function. Then its smoothed version
F_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}\sim\mathcal{D}}\big[F(\mathbf{x}+\varepsilon\mathbf{u})\big]
is also
L
-smooth.

Remark G.6
.

The above lemma can be found in eq (12) of
(
21
)
for normal perturbation, Lemma 4.1 (a) of
(
9
)
for uniform spherical perturbation.

Proof.

By Leibniz’s rule (differentiation under the expectation), we have

\nabla F_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}}\big[\nabla F(\mathbf{x}+\varepsilon\mathbf{u})\big].

For any
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

\|\nabla F_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})-\nabla F_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{y})\|=\Big\|\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}}\big[\nabla F(\mathbf{x}+\varepsilon\mathbf{u})-\nabla F(\mathbf{y}+\varepsilon\mathbf{u})\big]\Big\|.

Applying Jensen’s inequality (since
\|\cdot\|
is convex), we get

\|\nabla F_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})-\nabla F_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{y})\|\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}}\big[\|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}+\varepsilon\mathbf{u})-\nabla F(\mathbf{y}+\varepsilon\mathbf{u})\|\big].

Since
F
is
L
-smooth, its gradient is
L
-Lipschitz, so

\|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}+\varepsilon\mathbf{u})-\nabla F(\mathbf{y}+\varepsilon\mathbf{u})\|\leq L\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|.

Combining (a) and (b), we obtain

\|\nabla F_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})-\nabla F_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{y})\|\leq L\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|.

Hence,
F_{\varepsilon}
is also
L
-smooth.
∎

Lemma G.7

(Smoothing - Function Error)
.

We first present a useful lemma that provides an upper bound on the expected squared norm of the zeroth-order (ZO) gradient, where the expectation is taken over the perturbation randomness:

Lemma H.3

(ZO Estimator - Expected Squared Norm Bound)
.

Let
\hat{\nabla}f_{\varepsilon}^{q}(\mathbf{x})
be the zeroth-order (ZO) gradient estimator defined in (
32
).
If
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is differentiable and
L
-smooth, then:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\cal U}_{q}\sim({\mathbb{S}})^{q}}\left[{\left\|{\hat{\nabla}f_{\varepsilon}^{q}(\mathbf{x})}\right\|^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{q}\left(2d\left\|{\nabla f(\mathbf{x})}\right\|^{2}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}d^{2}}{2}L^{2}\right)+\left(1-\frac{1}{q}\right)\left\|{\nabla f_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})}\right\|^{2}

(54)

where
\varepsilon>0
,
f_{\varepsilon}
is defined in (
33
) and
{\cal U}_{q}\vcentcolon=\{\mathbf{u}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{u}_{q}\}
.

Proof.

First, note that
\hat{\nabla}f_{\varepsilon}^{q}(\mathbf{x})
is an average of
q
i.i.d. estimators:

\hat{\nabla}f_{\varepsilon}^{q}(\mathbf{x})\overset{\eqref{eq:zo-uniform}}{=}\frac{d}{q}\sum_{i=1}^{q}\frac{f(\mathbf{x}+\varepsilon\mathbf{u}_{i})-f(\mathbf{x}-\varepsilon\mathbf{u}_{i})}{2\varepsilon}\mathbf{u}_{i}=\vcentcolon\frac{1}{q}\sum_{i=1}^{q}\hat{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u}_{i}).

By independence of the perturbations, we get:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\cal U}_{q}\sim({\mathbb{S}})^{q}}\left[{\Big\|\frac{1}{q}\sum_{i=1}^{q}\hat{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u}_{i})\Big\|^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{{\cal U}_{q}}\left[{\sum_{i=1}^{q}\|\hat{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u}_{i})\|^{2}+\sum_{i\neq j}\langle\hat{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u}_{i}),\hat{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u}_{j})\rangle}\right]

\displaystyle{=}\frac{1}{q^{2}}\Bigg(q\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}\sim{\mathbb{S}}}\left[{\|\hat{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})\|^{2}}\right]+q(q-1)\Big\langle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}\sim{\mathbb{S}}}\left[{\hat{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})}\right],\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}\sim{\mathbb{S}}}\left[{\hat{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})}\right]\Big\rangle\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}}\left[{\|\hat{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})\|^{2}}\right]+\Big(1-\frac{1}{q}\Big)\big\|\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}}\left[{\hat{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})}\right]\big\|^{2}

\displaystyle{=}\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}}\left[{\|\hat{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})\|^{2}}\right]+\left(1-\frac{1}{q}\right)\left\|{\nabla f_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})}\right\|^{2}.

(55)

Taking the expected norm squared of a single estimator:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}\sim{\mathbb{S}}}\left[{\|\hat{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})\|^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}}\left[{\left\|{d\frac{f(\mathbf{x}+\varepsilon\mathbf{u})-f(\mathbf{x}-\varepsilon\mathbf{u})}{2\varepsilon}\mathbf{u}}\right\|^{2}}\right]

(56)

\displaystyle=d^{2}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}}\left[{\left|\frac{f(\mathbf{x}+\varepsilon\mathbf{u})-f(\mathbf{x}-\varepsilon\mathbf{u})}{2\varepsilon}\right|^{2}\left\|{\mathbf{u}}\right\|^{2}}\right]

(57)

\displaystyle{\leq}d^{2}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}}\left[{\left(\left|\left\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{u}\right\rangle\right|+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}L\left\|{\mathbf{u}}\right\|^{2}\right)^{2}\left\|{\mathbf{u}}\right\|^{2}}\right]

(58)

\displaystyle{=}d^{2}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}}\left[{\left(\left|\left\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{u}\right\rangle\right|+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}L\right)^{2}}\right]

(59)

\displaystyle{\leq}d^{2}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}}\left[{2\left\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{u}\right\rangle^{2}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}L^{2}}\right]

(60)

\displaystyle=2d^{2}{\nabla f(\mathbf{x})}^{\top}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}}\left[{\mathbf{u}{\mathbf{u}}^{\top}}\right]\nabla f(\mathbf{x})+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}d^{2}}{2}L^{2}

(61)

\displaystyle=2d\left\|{\nabla f(\mathbf{x})}\right\|^{2}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}d^{2}}{2}L^{2}.

(62)

Therefore, we get:

\boxed{\mathbb{E}_{{\cal U}_{q}\sim({\mathbb{S}})^{q}}\left[{\left\|{\hat{\nabla}f_{\varepsilon}^{q}(\mathbf{x})}\right\|^{2}}\right]\leq\frac{1}{q}\left(2d\left\|{\nabla f(\mathbf{x})}\right\|^{2}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}d^{2}}{2}L^{2}\right)+\left(1-\frac{1}{q}\right)\left\|{\nabla f_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})}\right\|^{2}.}

∎

We also state the following result on the total variance of the norm of the ZO estimator:

Lemma H.4

(Affine Variance Bound and Unbiasedness for ZO Gradient Estimator)
.

Let
F:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be the population loss defined as

F(\mathbf{x})=\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[{f(\mathbf{x};\xi)}\right]\quad\forall\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},

where each individual sample loss
f(.;\xi)
is
L
-smooth. Let
f_{\varepsilon}
and
F_{\varepsilon}
be their smoothed counterparts according to (
33
).
Assume that, there exists
\sigma>0
such that, for all
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, the variance of the sample loss gradient satisfies

\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[{\left\|{\nabla f(\mathbf{x};\xi)-\nabla F(\mathbf{x})}\right\|^{2}}\right]\leq\sigma^{2},

(63)

Let
q\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
,
\varepsilon>0
and let
\hat{\nabla}f_{\varepsilon}^{q}(\mathbf{x};\xi)
be the zeroth-order (ZO) gradient estimator defined in (
32
) for uniform perturbations.
Then, for any
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\xi,{\cal U}_{q}}\left[{\hat{\nabla}f_{\varepsilon}^{q}(\mathbf{x};\xi)}\right]

\displaystyle=\nabla F_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}),

(64)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\xi,\,{\cal U}_{q}}\left[{\left\|{\hat{\nabla}f_{\varepsilon}^{q}(\mathbf{x};\xi)-\nabla F_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})}\right\|^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{d\varepsilon^{2}L^{2}}{2q}(8+d)+\left(\frac{2d-1}{q}+1\right)\sigma^{2}+\frac{4d-1}{q}\left\|{\nabla F_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})}\right\|^{2}.

(65)

Proof.

The unbiasedness of (
32
) is already established due to Lemma
G.13
:

\mathbb{E}_{\xi,{\cal U}_{q}}\left[{\hat{\nabla}f_{\varepsilon}^{q}(\mathbf{x};\xi)}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[{\mathbb{E}_{{\cal U}_{q}}\left[{\hat{\nabla}f_{\varepsilon}^{q}(\mathbf{x};\xi)\mid\xi}\right]}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[{\nabla f_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x};\xi)}\right]=\nabla F_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}).

(66)

We recall that the variance of a random vector
X
is given by
\text{Var}\left({\left\|{X}\right\|}\right)=\mathbb{E}\left[{\left\|{X-\mathbb{E}\left[{X}\right]}\right\|^{2}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[{\left\|{X}\right\|^{2}}\right]-\left\|{\mathbb{E}\left[{X}\right]}\right\|^{2}
.
We aim at computing the variance of our ZO stochastic gradient estimator:

(67)

Then, we focus on upper-bounding the first term of the variance decomposition

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\xi,{\cal U}_{q}}\left[{\left\|{\hat{\nabla}f_{\varepsilon}^{q}(\mathbf{x};\xi)}\right\|^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle{\leq}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[{\frac{1}{q}\left(2d\left\|{\nabla f(\mathbf{x};\xi)}\right\|^{2}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}d^{2}}{2}L^{2}\right)+\left(1-\frac{1}{q}\right)\left\|{\nabla f_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x};\xi)}\right\|^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle{\leq}\frac{2d}{q}\left(\left\|{\nabla F(\mathbf{x})}\right\|^{2}+\sigma^{2}\right)+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2q}d^{2}L^{2}+\left(1-\frac{1}{q}\right)\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[{\left\|{\nabla f_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x};\xi)}\right\|^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle{\leq}\frac{2d+q-1}{q}\sigma^{2}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2q}d^{2}L^{2}+\frac{2d}{q}\left\|{\nabla F(\mathbf{x})}\right\|^{2}+\left(1-\frac{1}{q}\right)\left\|{\nabla F_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle{\leq}\left(\frac{2d-1}{q}+1\right)\sigma^{2}+\left(d+8\right)\frac{d}{2q}\varepsilon^{2}L^{2}+\left(\frac{4d-1}{q}+1\right)\left\|{\nabla F_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})}\right\|^{2}

(68)

Combining (
67
) and (
68

\boxed{\mathbb{E}_{\xi,{\cal U}_{q}}\left[{\left\|{\hat{\nabla}f_{\varepsilon}^{q}(\mathbf{x};\xi)-\nabla F_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})}\right\|^{2}}\right]\leq\left(\frac{2d-1}{q}+1\right)\sigma^{2}+\left(d+8\right)\frac{d}{2q}\varepsilon^{2}L^{2}+\frac{4d-1}{q}\left\|{\nabla F_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})}\right\|^{2}.}

∎

(SGD with Affine Variance Bound)
.

T

\mathbf{g}_{t}=g(\mathbf{x}_{t};\xi_{t})

\mathbf{x}_{t}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\xi_{t}}\left[{F(\mathbf{x}_{t+1})|\mathbf{x}_{t}}\right]

(122)

(123)

(ZO-SGD)
.

Let

be the population loss defined as
