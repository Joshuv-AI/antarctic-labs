GPU-Accelerated Inverse Structural Anastylosis from Block Collapse Dynamics
L.A. Muñoz

Abstract
The physical anastylosis of collapsed architectural monuments—the meticulous reassembly of fallen stone elements into their original structural configuration—represents one of the most intellectually demanding challenges in conservation science. Traditional approaches depend heavily on expert archaeologist judgement and manual block-by-block correspondence, a process that is both labour-intensive and inherently subjective. Inspired by the combinatorial complexity of this problem as manifested in the game of Jenga, we present Jenga Inverse Predictor v2 (this method), a GPU-accelerated deep learning framework that addresses structural anastylosis as an inverse prediction task. Given an image of a collapsed block assembly, this method reconstructs the most probable prior tower configuration by: (1) implementing a complete rigid-body physics engine with OBB/SAT collision detection and a Projected Gauss-Seidel (PGS) contact solver accelerated with Numba JIT and CuPy CUDA; (2) applying the analytical force thresholds of Ziglar (CMU, 2006) — F a ​ p ​ p = 3 ​ μ s ​ m ​ g F_{app}=3\mu_{s}mg (Y-axis, torque-free) and F a ​ p ​ p = 4 ​ μ s ​ m ​ g F_{app}=4\mu_{s}mg (X-axis, torque risk) — over three friction levels ( μ s ∈ { 0.25 , 0.40 , 0.60 } \mu_{s}\in\{0.25,0.40,0.60\} ) across 450 simulated episodes; (3) training a dual-stream ResNet-18 that injects a friction one-hot vector and jointly predicts block removal count, per-position removal probabilities, centre-of-mass imbalance, and Ziglar torque risk; and (4) generating a smooth 3-D video of the block-by-block reverse reconstruction. We discuss implications for computer-assisted anastylosis at the UNESCO Maya site of Uxmal, Yucatán, and provide a detailed technical description of the full pipeline, architecture, and loss formulation. Keywords: structural anastylosis, inverse kinematics, rigid-body simulation, deep learning, ResNet, PGS solver, Jenga, archaeological conservation, Uxmal, GPU acceleration.



1. Introduction

The restoration of collapsed archaeological monuments is a multidisciplinary
endeavour at the intersection of structural engineering, art history, computer
vision, and cultural heritage science.
Among the most technically demanding restoration techniques is

anastylosis
—the systematic reassembly of original architectural
components that have fallen or been displaced over centuries.
The challenge was codified in the Athens Charter (1931)
[
20
]
and
the Venice Charter (1964)
[
8
]
: reassemble original materials
wherever possible, document all interventions, and introduce new materials only
where strictly necessary.

The archaeological zone of
Uxmal
, Yucatán, México—one of the
finest examples of Puuc-style Maya architecture and a UNESCO World Heritage
Site—typifies the scale of this challenge.
As documented by Muñoz et al.
[
14
]
and supported by ongoing
fieldwork by INAH and the Universidad Autónoma de Yucatán (UADY), the site
presents hundreds of architectural mounds, each composed of stone blocks whose
original configuration must be inferred from the spatial distribution of fallen
debris.
Archaeologists excavate layer by layer, cataloguing each stone, sketching its
position, and hypothesising how it fits into the original façade.
This laborious process is well-documented as a principal bottleneck in the
conservation of Mesoamerican sites
[
1
]
.

Information technologies have been transforming archaeological documentation
since at least the 1990s
[
17
]
.
Three-dimensional photogrammetry, laser scanning, and database-driven systems
now record the position of every fragment with centimetre precision.
What remains partially unsolved is the
inverse problem
: given the
current scatter of stones, how can a computational system propose a plausible
reconstruction of the original structure?
This paper addresses exactly that question, using the game of Jenga as a
physically tractable and visually abundant proxy for collapsing block towers.

Jenga is a commercially available stacking game in which players remove blocks
one at a time from a stable tower until structural failure occurs.
The resulting collapse produces precisely the kind of scattered block
configuration that an anastylosis practitioner faces when approaching a fallen
wall section.
Critically, Jenga blocks observe fixed physical proportions
(length : width : thickness
\approx 3\!:\!1\!:\!0.6
, approximately

81\times 26\times 18
mm
[
19
]
), providing a ground-truth geometric
constraint against which detected blocks can be validated.

1.1. Motivation: Anastylosis as an Inverse Problem

The central intellectual contribution of this paper is the reframing of
anastylosis as an
inverse problem
amenable to machine learning.
In classical forward kinematics, one computes the final state of a system given
its initial configuration and applied dynamics.
Inverse kinematics reverses the question: given the final state, recover the
initial configuration.
Anastylosis is precisely the latter—given scattered stones (final state),
recover the original wall (initial configuration).

Prior computational approaches to block reassembly have largely relied on
geometric matching of fracture surfaces
[
12
]
, colour and texture
correspondence
[
18
]
, or constraint-satisfaction solvers over CAD
models
[
11
]
.
These methods work well when block surfaces are well-preserved and fragment
counts are small.
They scale poorly to large mound structures where hundreds of blocks must be
simultaneously placed, where surface weathering has erased original texture,
and where only partial information survives.

We propose instead to
learn a structural prior distribution
over valid
tower configurations from large collections of visual observations, using neural
networks that generalise across block positions, orientations, and levels.
The Jenga domain provides the training data; the archaeological domain provides
the application.

Our project addresses three concrete technical questions:

Q1.

How can a realistic and efficient rigid-body physics engine be
implemented for hardwood blocks, respecting the analytical equations
derived by Ziglar (2006)?

Q2.

What exactly does a neural network learn when it sees images of a
collapsed tower, and how does that learning relate to the underlying
physics?

Q3.

How can the inverse temporal sequence of a collapse be reconstructed
from a single static image?

Wu et al.
[

]
trained models to predict the stability of block towers

[

[

3. Problem Formulation

Let a tower configuration be described by a set of
N\leq 54
blocks, each
parameterised as a tuple

b_{i}=\bigl(\mathbf{p}_{i},\,\mathbf{s}_{i},\,\theta_{i}\bigr),

where
\mathbf{p}_{i}\in[0,1]^{2}
is the normalised centroid position in the
image plane,
\mathbf{s}_{i}\in(0,1]^{2}
encodes block dimensions (width and
height, normalised to image size), and
\theta_{i}\in[0^{\circ},180^{\circ})
is the
in-plane rotation angle.
A tower with
N_{0}
blocks at time
t=0
evolves under gravity and contact
forces to produce a scattered configuration at time
t=T
.

Task.
Given an image
\mathbf{I}_{T}
of the final scattered
configuration, recover the prior block set

\mathcal{S}_{0}=\bigl\{(\mathbf{p}_{i}^{0},\,\mathbf{s}_{i}^{0},\,\theta_{i}^{0})\bigr\}_{i=1}^{N_{0}}

together with per-block displacement vectors

\mathbf{d}_{i}=\mathbf{p}_{i}^{f}-\mathbf{p}_{i}^{0}
,
where
\mathbf{p}_{i}^{f}
is the observed final centroid.

3.1. Structural Constraints

Physical Jenga blocks satisfy fixed dimensional ratios:

L=3W,\qquad T=0.6W,

where
W
is the block width.
In a well-formed tower, blocks are arranged in alternating perpendicular layers,
3 blocks per layer, 18 layers total, yielding
N_{0}=54
blocks.
The network outputs a fixed-length tensor of dimension
54\times 6
, where the
six values per block are: presence probability
e_{i}\in[0,1]
, centroid

(cx_{i},cy_{i})
, width
w_{i}
, height
h_{i}
, and normalised angle

\alpha_{i}\in[-1,1]
(Tanh-encoded).

4. Rigid-Body Foundations

4.1. State of a Rigid Body

A rigid body in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is fully described by:

\mathcal{S}=\bigl(\mathbf{x},\,\mathbf{q},\,\dot{\mathbf{x}},\,\bm{\omega}\bigr)\;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{S}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3},

where
\mathbf{x}
is the centre-of-mass position,

\mathbf{q}=[w,x,y,z]^{\top}
is the unit orientation quaternion
(
\|\mathbf{q}\|=1
),
\dot{\mathbf{x}}
is the linear velocity, and

\bm{\omega}
is the angular velocity.

4.1.1. Quaternions and Rotations

A unit quaternion
\bm{q}
encodes a rotation of angle
\theta
about axis

\hat{\bm{n}}
as

\bm{q}=\Bigl[\cos\tfrac{\theta}{2},\;\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2}\hat{n}_{x},\;\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2}\hat{n}_{y},\;\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2}\hat{n}_{z}\Bigr]^{T}.

The associated rotation matrix is

\mathbf{R}(\bm{q})=\begin{bmatrix}1-2(y^{2}+z^{2})&2(xy-zw)&2(xz+yw)\\
2(xy+zw)&1-2(x^{2}+z^{2})&2(yz-xw)\\
2(xz-yw)&2(yz+xw)&1-2(x^{2}+y^{2})\end{bmatrix},

(1)

and quaternion time integration is performed via

\dot{\bm{q}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\bm{q}\otimes[0,\bm{\omega}]^{T},\qquad\bm{q}_{t+\Delta t}=\frac{\bm{q}_{t}+\dot{\bm{q}}\,\Delta t}{\left\|\bm{q}_{t}+\dot{\bm{q}}\,\Delta t\right\|},

(2)

where
\otimes
denotes the Hamilton product.

4.1.2. Inertia Tensor

For a uniform rectangular prism of mass
m
and dimensions

\ell\times w\times h
, the body-frame inertia tensor is diagonal:

\mathbf{I}_{\mathrm{body}}=\frac{m}{12}\begin{bmatrix}w^{2}+h^{2}&0&0\\
0&\ell^{2}+h^{2}&0\\
0&0&\ell^{2}+w^{2}\end{bmatrix}.

(3)

For Jenga blocks (
m=19.6
g,
\ell=8.1
cm,
w=2.6
cm,
h=1.8
cm):

I_{xx}\approx 1.18\times 10^{-5}\ \mathrm{kg\cdot m^{2}},\quad I_{yy}\approx 9.56\times 10^{-5}\ \mathrm{kg\cdot m^{2}},\quad I_{zz}\approx 1.04\times 10^{-4}\ \mathrm{kg\cdot m^{2}}.

In world coordinates:
\mathbf{I}_{\mathrm{world}}^{-1}=\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{I}_{\mathrm{body}}^{-1}\mathbf{R}^{T}
.

4.2. Equations of Motion

The Newton–Euler equations for a free body are

m\,\ddot{\bm{x}}=\bm{F}_{\mathrm{ext}}+\bm{F}_{\mathrm{contact}},\qquad\mathbf{I}_{\mathrm{world}}\dot{\bm{\omega}}+\bm{\omega}\times(\mathbf{I}_{\mathrm{world}}\bm{\omega})=\bm{\tau}_{\mathrm{ext}}+\bm{\tau}_{\mathrm{contact}}.

(4)

Semi-implicit Euler integration is applied at
\Delta t=1/240
s with

n_{\mathrm{sub}}=3
substeps:

\displaystyle\dot{\bm{x}}_{t+\Delta t}

\displaystyle=\dot{\bm{x}}_{t}+\Delta t\,\bm{g}+\Delta t\,\mathbf{I}_{m}^{-1}\bm{J}_{\mathrm{imp}},

(5)

\displaystyle\bm{x}_{t+\Delta t}

\displaystyle=\bm{x}_{t}+\Delta t\,\dot{\bm{x}}_{t+\Delta t},

(6)

where
\bm{g}=[0,0,-9.81]^{T}
m/s
2
and
\bm{J}_{\mathrm{imp}}
are the
contact impulses computed by the PGS solver (
Section

7
).

5. Ziglar’s Mechanical Analysis (2006)

5.1. Coulomb Friction Model

The maximum static friction force between two blocks in contact is

f_{s,\max}=\mu_{s}N=\mu_{s}mg,

(7)

with
\mu_{s}=0.4
for hardwood (Forest Products Laboratory, 2002
[
4
]
).
The
stick-slip
condition is enforced: as long as the tangential force
does not exceed
f_{s,\max}
, blocks remain adhered (
stick
); otherwise
they slide (
slip
).

5.2. Y-Axis Translation — Side Block (Equation 4)

Withdrawing a side block along the Y-axis (parallel to its length) yields the
free-body diagram in
Figure

2
a.
The upper layer is pulled by
f_{\mathrm{slide}}
but resisted by
f_{1}
(centre
block) and
f_{2}
(opposite block):

f_{\mathrm{slide},\max}=\mu_{s}mg<f_{1,\max}+f_{2,\max}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\text{upper layer: {does not move}.}

Ziglar — Equation 4 (Y-axis translation)

F_{app}=3\,\mu_{s}\,m\,g\;\xrightarrow{\;\mu_{s}=0.4,\;m=0.0196\,\mathrm{kg}\;}\;\boxed{F_{app}=0.23\ \mathrm{N}}

(8)

Result:
for
F_{app}>0.23
N the block slides
without
disturbing the upper or lower layers
.

Optimal and robust move.

5.3. X-Axis Translation — Side Block (Equation 8)

Withdrawing the block along the X-axis (perpendicular to its length) causes

f_{\mathrm{slide}}
to act off-centre with respect to the upper blocks,
generating a net torque:

\tau=f_{\mathrm{slide}}\cdot d,

with angular acceleration

\alpha=\tau/I\approx\tau/1.18\times 10^{-5}
rad/s
2
— very large
due to the small inertia of the blocks.

Ziglar — Equation 8 (X-axis, torque condition)

F_{app}<4\,\mu_{s}\,m\,g\;\xrightarrow{\;\mu_{s}=0.4\;}\;\boxed{F_{app}=0.307\ \mathrm{N}}

(9)

Result:
for
F_{app}>0.307
N a torque is applied to the upper layer.

Sub-optimal move
: safe only if the opposite side
block is still present (
f_{2}
counteracts the torque).

6. Physics Engine — Collision Detection

6.1. Broadphase: GPU AABB

Candidate pairs are detected using
Axis-Aligned Bounding Boxes (AABB)
.
Bodies
i
,
j
with centres
\bm{c}_{i}
,
\bm{c}_{j}
and
half-extents
\bm{e}_{i}
,
\bm{e}_{j}

overlap
if

\forall k\in\{x,y,z\}:\quad|c_{i,k}-c_{j,k}|\leq e_{i,k}+e_{j,k}+\varepsilon,

(10)

with
\varepsilon=0.002
m.

In the
main process
(GPU available), this is computed matricially with
CuPy:

\mathbf{M}_{ij}=\lnot\bigvee_{k}\!\left(\min_{i}^{(k)}>\max_{j}^{(k)}\;\cup\;\max_{i}^{(k)}<\min_{j}^{(k)}\right),

(11)

with
O(N)
GPU complexity via broadcasting.

Spawn workers
always use the CPU-NumPy version to avoid

cudaErrorInitializationError
on CUDA context inheritance via

fork()
.

6.2. Narrowphase: OBB SAT

For each overlapping AABB pair, the
Separating Axis Theorem (SAT)
is
applied over the 15 candidate axes of two Oriented Bounding Boxes:

\mathcal{A}=\{\hat{\bm{a}}_{i}\}_{i=0}^{2}\cup\{\hat{\bm{b}}_{j}\}_{j=0}^{2}\cup\{\hat{\bm{a}}_{i}\times\hat{\bm{b}}_{j}\}_{i,j=0}^{2}.

For each axis
\hat{\bm{u}}
:

c_{A}=\bm{x}_{A}\cdot\hat{\bm{u}},\quad r_{A}=\sum_{i}e_{A,i}\,|\hat{\bm{a}}_{i}\cdot\hat{\bm{u}}|.

Penetration
\delta=r_{A}+r_{B}-|c_{A}-c_{B}|
; if
\delta\leq 0
on any axis
there is
no collision
.
The axis with minimum
\delta
defines the contact normal.

6.2.1. Contact Manifold (Face Clipping)

Multiple contact points (necessary for stack stability) are obtained via
Sutherland–Hodgman clipping in the 2-D projection of the overlapping faces:

a)

Project the 8 corners of each OBB onto the plane perpendicular to
the contact normal.

b)

Compute the convex hull (QuickHull).

c)

Clip polygon A with polygon B
(Sutherland–Hodgman,
O(n^{2})
).

d)

Sample up to 4 points from the result polygon.

7. Contact Solver — Projected Gauss-Seidel (PGS)

7.1. Impulse Formulation

Rather than integrating contact forces continuously, the solver works with
discrete
impulses

\lambda
.
For a body pair
(A,B)
with contact point
\bm{p}
, normal
\hat{\bm{n}}
,
and tangent vectors
\hat{\bm{t}}_{1},\hat{\bm{t}}_{2}
, the constraint
Jacobian is

\mathbf{J}_{n}=\begin{bmatrix}-\hat{\bm{n}}^{T}&-(\bm{r}_{A}\times\hat{\bm{n}})^{T}&\hat{\bm{n}}^{T}&(\bm{r}_{B}\times\hat{\bm{n}})^{T}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times 12},

with
\bm{r}_{A}=\bm{p}-\bm{x}_{A}
,
\bm{r}_{B}=\bm{p}-\bm{x}_{B}
.
The non-penetration condition requires:

v_{n}^{\prime}=v_{n}+\frac{\lambda_{n}}{k_{n}}\geq 0,

(12)

where the effective mass is

k_{n}=m_{A}^{-1}+m_{B}^{-1}+\hat{\bm{n}}\cdot\bigl[(\mathbf{I}_{A}^{-1}(\bm{r}_{A}\times\hat{\bm{n}}))\times\bm{r}_{A}+(\mathbf{I}_{B}^{-1}(\bm{r}_{B}\times\hat{\bm{n}}))\times\bm{r}_{B}\bigr].

(13)

The normal impulse (with restitution
e
) is

\lambda_{n}=\frac{-(1+e)v_{n}}{k_{n}},\qquad\lambda_{n}^{\mathrm{acc}}=\max(0,\;\lambda_{n}^{\mathrm{acc}}+\lambda_{n}).

(14)

7.2. Discrete Coulomb Friction

The tangential impulse is projected into the Coulomb cone:

\lambda_{t,i}^{\mathrm{acc}}=\mathrm{clip}\!\left(\lambda_{t,i}^{\mathrm{acc}}-\tfrac{v_{t,i}}{k_{t,i}},\;-\mu_{s}\lambda_{n}^{\mathrm{acc}},\;+\mu_{s}\lambda_{n}^{\mathrm{acc}}\right),\quad i\in\{1,2\}.

(15)

8. Stability Detection and Collapse

8.1. Geometric Criterion — Support Polygon

The necessary condition for static equilibrium is that the projection of the
centre of mass (COM) onto the horizontal plane lies
inside
the convex
polygon formed by the ground contact points:

\mathrm{COM}_{xy}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{support}}=\mathrm{ConvexHull}\!\left(\bigcup_{b:\,z_{b}-e_{b,z}\leq\varepsilon}\mathrm{corners}(b)[:,\{x,y\}]\right).

(17)

The analytical margin
d
is the minimum signed distance from
\mathrm{COM}_{xy}

to each edge of the support polygon.

8.2. Composite Collapse Criterion

Collapse is declared when
all three
conditions hold simultaneously:

\text{collapsed}=\underbrace{\Delta x_{\max}>f_{d}\,\ell}_{C_{d}}\;\wedge\;\underbrace{E_{k}>\varepsilon_{k}}_{C_{k}}\;\wedge\;\underbrace{\theta_{\max}>\theta_{th}}_{C_{\theta}},

(18)

with
f_{d}=0.5
,
\varepsilon_{k}=5\times 10^{-5}
J,
\theta_{th}=30^{\circ}
.

8.3. Full Floor Contact Simulation

After collapse detection,
simulate_until_rest()
runs up to 400
additional steps until global kinetic energy falls below
10^{-7}
J:

E_{k}^{\mathrm{total}}=\sum_{b}\tfrac{1}{2}m_{b}\left\|\dot{\bm{x}}_{b}\right\|^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\bm{\omega}_{b}\cdot\mathbf{I}_{b}\bm{\omega}_{b}<10^{-7}\ \text{J}.

9. Neural Network Architecture

9.1. From Physics to Learning

Core intuition

Imagine viewing a photograph of a collapsed Jenga tower.
An experienced player can estimate:
“about six blocks were removed
before collapse, from the lower layers, and there was a dangerous move that
twisted the upper layer.”
The model learns exactly that —

to map visual patterns of the debris to the physical parameters that
caused them
— just as a forensic engineer reconstructs an accident from
the marks it left behind.

9.2. Backbone: Adapted ResNet-18

The
Residual Neural Network
(ResNet) was proposed by He et
al.
[
6
]
to overcome the vanishing-gradient problem in very deep
networks.
The key idea is
skip connections
that add the input directly to the
block output:

Definition 9.1

(Residual Block)
.

\bm{y}=\sigma\!\bigl(\mathbf{F}(\bm{x},\{W_{i}\})+W_{s}\bm{x}\bigr),

where
\mathbf{F}(\bm{x})
is the learned transformation (two Conv
3\times 3
+
BN layers),
W_{s}\bm{x}
is the shortcut projection (identity if dimensions
match), and
\sigma
is ReLU.

The block learns the
residual

\mathbf{F}(\bm{x})=\bm{y}-\bm{x}

(the “correction” relative to identity), which is easier to learn than the
full mapping.
During backpropagation, the gradient flows directly through the shortcut:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\bm{x}}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\bm{y}}\underbrace{\left(1+\frac{\partial\mathbf{F}}{\partial\bm{x}}\right)}_{\geq 1},

guaranteeing that the gradient is never smaller than at the output layer.

Figure 4
:
Residual Block detail.
The main path (blue) learns
\mathbf{F}(\bm{x})
; the skip connection
(orange) adds
\bm{x}
directly.
The green node “
+
” performs element-wise summation.

10. What Does the Model Learn?

Section for non-technical readers

This section uses accessible metaphors to explain exactly what the neural
network “sees” at each stage of processing.

10.1. Layer 1: Edges and Textures (“detects the blocks”)

The first convolutional layers learn
edge detectors at different angles
and scales
.
In the Jenga context: the network learns that blocks are brown rectangles.
Many short, dispersed edges in unusual positions signals a fallen tower.

10.2. Intermediate Layers: Spatial Configuration (“understands the mess”)

ResBlock groups 2 and 3 (128–256 channels) combine low-level detections into

high-level structures
: the relative arrangement of pieces, whether
there are stacks or isolated blocks on the floor, which layers are empty.

10.3. Final Layer (128-dim Embedding): Semantic Representation

The 128-dimensional vector is a
numerical fingerprint of the game
:
two similarly collapsed Jengas will have similar fingerprints.
Visually, these vectors form clusters separated by friction level
(
Figure

6
b): the network learns that
\mu=0.60
produces more
compact collapses than
\mu=0.25
.

10.4. Head 1: How Many Blocks Were Removed?

Counts the “amount of disorder”: few blocks removed
\to
nearly intact tower;
many
\to
scattered fragments.
The model correlates block density and dispersal with number of rounds played.

10.5. Head 2: Where Were They Removed From? (Heat Map)

The richest prediction: for each of the
N
positions (layer
\times
slot),
the model outputs the
probability that that block was removed
.
The network learns that lower and side blocks are chosen first
(more accessible, less load above).

10.6. Head 3: COM Imbalance

The COM imbalance (
-
analytical margin) indicates how “tilted” the tower
was before falling.
The network infers it from the asymmetric position of the debris.

10.7. Head 4: Accumulated Torque Risk (Ziglar)

The most innovative head: predicts how many of the moves performed were of
type
side_xaxis
(those that generate torque per Ziglar Eq. 8).
The network learns that certain collapse patterns (blocks tilted toward a
specific side) are a signature of prior torque-generating moves.

11. Inverse Reconstruction System

11.1. Motivation

The model predicts
global parameters
of the game but has no access to
the temporal sequence of moves.
Inverse reconstruction uses the
most similar simulated episode
as a
temporal proxy: if the prediction says “6 blocks removed from layers 1–3”,
we search
all_results
for an episode with those characteristics and
play its frames in reverse.

11.2. Reference Episode Search Algorithm

Step 1.

If the image filename is
nominal_exp_0042_final.png
,
retrieve

.

Step 2.

For external images, search by similarity:

e^{*}=\argmin_{e\in\mathcal{E}}\bigl|n_{\mathrm{removed}}(e)-\hat{n}_{\mathrm{removed}}\bigr|\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\mathrm{collapsed}(e)=\mathrm{True}.

Step 3.

If no collapsed episode is found, select the one with the
highest round count.

14. Discussion and Implications for Anastylosis

The transfer of this method’s principles to real-world anastylosis at sites such as
Uxmal rests on two analogies.
First, fallen stone blocks, like fallen Jenga blocks, are governed by rigid-body
mechanics: their current positions encode their dynamic history.
Second, the structural prior learned by the network—which configurations of
blocks are physically stable, which layers tend to be cleared first—is directly
analogous to the architectural grammar of a Puuc-style Maya facade, where block
courses alternate between fill and facing in predictable patterns.

Practical deployment would require: (i) replacing Jenga’s fixed-proportion
blocks with a learned 3-D block detector calibrated from photogrammetric scans;
(ii) enriching the training data with synthetic collapse simulations of
specific wall typologies documented at Uxmal; and
(iii) incorporating expert archaeological constraints as additional loss terms.

15. Conclusion

This paper demonstrates that:

1.

A complete rigid-body physics engine (OBB SAT + PGS +
semi-implicit Euler) can be implemented respecting Ziglar’s analytical
equations to within
<1\%
numerical error.

2.

A multi-task ResNet-18, trained over 450 episodes at three friction
levels, learns to
deduce the physical conditions of a game

(move count, positions, torque risk) from a single image of the fallen
pieces.

3.

The
friction injection architecture
(
\mu
one-hot) allows
a single model to generalise across different materials without
retraining.

4.

The inverse reconstruction system closes the loop:
static image
\to
physical prediction
\to
temporal video showing
how the collapse unfolded and how the tower can be rebuilt.

Closing statement

“The model learned to see the past of a collapse

by looking only at the rubble of the present.”
