EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Riparian wetlands are dynamic interfaces that exert strong control over water quality,
contaminant mobility, and greenhouse gas emissions. These systems are characterized by
hyporheic exchange between oxic surface water and anoxic groundwater, which generates steep
redox gradients and promotes spatially and temporally variable microbial activity. Despite
growing recognition of tightly coupled iron (Fe), sulfur (S), and carbon (C) cycling in these
environments, the mechanisms governing these interactions—particularly under low-sulfate
freshwater conditions—remain poorly constrained.

This project developed a mechanistic understanding of how hydrologic variability and microbial
processes interact to control Fe–S–C cycling in iron-rich riparian wetlands. Using a multi-scale
and multi-method approach integrating field observations, geochemical and spectroscopic
analyses, metagenomics, and reactive transport modeling, we demonstrate that “cryptic” sulfur
cycling—rapid sulfur transformations involving intermediate-valence species—plays a dominant
and previously underrecognized role in freshwater wetlands. These processes persist despite low
sulfate concentrations and significantly influence iron reduction, carbon mineralization, and
methane dynamics.

The results show that cryptic sulfur cycling enhances dissolved Fe²⁺ production, regulates
methane concentrations, and is strongly controlled by climate-driven hydrologic fluxes. By
linking hydroclimate, subsurface flow, and biogeochemical reactions, this work provides a
predictive framework for understanding how wetland systems respond to environmental change,
with direct implications for water quality and carbon cycling.

Objectives:

a. What were the major goals and objectives of the project?

The overall goal of this project was to develop a mechanistic understanding of how hyporheic
exchange influences coupled abiotic and biotic Fe–S–C cycling in riparian wetland
environments. This work specifically targeted the role of cryptic sulfur cycling in regulating iron
and carbon transformations under hydrologically dynamic conditions.

The first objective was to characterize the occurrence, pathways, and environmental controls of
sulfur cycling and its coupling to iron and carbon transformations using field-based geochemical,
spectroscopic, and molecular measurements. The second objective was to quantify how
hydrologic exchange between surface water and groundwater regulates redox conditions and
microbial activity across spatial and temporal scales. The third objective was to develop and
apply a reactive transport modeling framework that integrates hydrologic dynamics with
microbially mediated reactions to predict Fe–S–C cycling under variable environmental
conditions.

b. What was accomplished under these goals?

This project produced a comprehensive, integrated dataset and modeling framework that
collectively advance understanding of coupled Fe–S–C cycling in freshwater wetlands. Field
investigations at the Tims Branch riparian wetland system (Savannah River Site) captured
hydrologic variability across diurnal to interannual timescales and linked these dynamics to
geochemical and microbial processes. Continuous monitoring of groundwater–surface water
exchange, combined with high-resolution porewater sampling and sediment analyses, revealed
strong spatial and temporal variability in redox conditions.

Geochemical analyses demonstrated elevated dissolved Fe²⁺ concentrations in sediment
porewaters and persistent gradients indicative of active iron reduction under anoxic conditions.
Notably, Fe(III)-bearing phases were detected throughout the sediment profile, even under
reducing conditions, suggesting a dynamic reservoir of electron acceptors that can sustain
ongoing redox cycling. Sulfur K-edge X-ray absorption spectroscopy further revealed the
widespread presence of intermediate-valence sulfur species, including elemental sulfur and
organosulfur compounds, which are indicative of rapid and continuous sulfur transformations.

Metagenomic analyses provided independent evidence for active and diverse microbial pathways
involved in sulfur cycling. Functional gene distributions indicated the co-occurrence of both
oxidative and reductive sulfur metabolisms, including pathways associated with thiosulfate
oxidation and sulfide transformation. These findings support the existence of a complete and
dynamic sulfur cycle in these sediments and demonstrate that microbial communities are well
adapted to rapidly shifting redox conditions.

A major outcome of this work is the development of a reactive transport model that integrates
hydrologic fluxes with microbially mediated Fe–S–C reactions. The modeling effort, led by a

graduate researcher, incorporated reaction pathways constrained by geochemical observations
and metagenomic functional potential. Model simulations demonstrated that cryptic sulfur
cycling significantly enhances Fe²⁺ production, increasing concentrations by up to threefold
relative to scenarios without sulfur coupling. The model further showed that sulfur cycling
moderates methane concentrations by suppressing methanogenesis and supporting anaerobic
methane oxidation pathways.

Importantly, the results reveal that hydrologic variability is a dominant control on these
processes. Alternating periods of upwelling and downwelling create shifting redox environments
that generate biogeochemical “hot moments” and amplify the effects of coupled Fe–S–C cycling.
Climate-driven factors, including precipitation and evapotranspiration, were shown to regulate
these hydrologic fluxes, establishing a direct mechanistic link between hydroclimate and
subsurface biogeochemistry.

RESULTS AND OUTCOMES

This project provides a significant conceptual advance by demonstrating that cryptic sulfur
cycling is a central control on iron and carbon dynamics in freshwater wetlands. Contrary to
long-standing assumptions that low sulfate concentrations limit sulfur cycling, our results show
that rapid recycling of sulfur intermediates sustains an active and complete sulfur cycle that
strongly influences redox processes. These findings fundamentally revise current understanding
of freshwater wetland biogeochemistry.

The coupling of sulfur cycling to iron and carbon transformations was shown to have system-
scale impacts. Sulfur-mediated reactions substantially enhance iron reduction, increasing Fe²⁺
availability and promoting conditions favorable for the formation of iron-rich flocs that can
sequester contaminants. At the same time, interactions between sulfur cycling and carbon
metabolism regulate methane production and attenuation, linking subsurface processes to
greenhouse gas emissions.

The integration of field observations with reactive transport modeling represents a major
methodological advance. By explicitly incorporating microbially mediated sulfur reactions
informed by metagenomic data, the model provides a mechanistic and predictive framework for
evaluating how hydrologic variability influences biogeochemical cycling. This approach bridges
the gap between molecular-scale microbial processes and ecosystem-scale outcomes.

Opportunities for training and professional development the project provided
This project supported the training and professional development of a postdoctoral researcher
and multiple graduate students, providing interdisciplinary experience at the interface of
hydrology, geochemistry, microbiology, and modeling. The postdoctoral researcher led the
metagenomic analyses and contributed to the interpretation of microbial functional potential in
relation to biogeochemical processes. Graduate students played key roles in both field data
collection and the development of the reactive transport modeling framework, gaining expertise
in integrating observational and computational approaches. These activities contributed to the

development of a highly skilled workforce capable of addressing complex environmental
challenges.

Dissemination of results to communities of interest?

Results from this project have been disseminated through conference presentations and peer-
reviewed publications. A manuscript led by a graduate researcher presents the integrated field
and modeling results, demonstrating the role of hydroclimate in driving cryptic sulfur cycling
and its impacts on iron and carbon dynamics. This manuscript is currently under review.

In addition, a complementary manuscript led by the postdoctoral researcher and PI focuses on
microbial community composition and functional gene diversity. This work provides genome-
informed evidence of diverse sulfur metabolic pathways and further supports the prevalence and
importance of cryptic sulfur cycling in riparian wetlands. This manuscript will be submitted once
the first manuscript is accepted for publication. Additionally, one M.S. thesis was published
with results from this research. Together, these publications establish a strong and cohesive
body of work linking microbial processes to system-scale biogeochemical dynamics.

CONCLUSIONS

This project establishes cryptic sulfur cycling as a critical and previously underrecognized
regulator of iron and carbon cycling in freshwater wetlands. By integrating field observations,
advanced analytical techniques, and reactive transport modeling, the work provides a new
mechanistic framework linking hydroclimate, subsurface hydrology, and microbial processes to
water quality and carbon cycling outcomes. These findings have broad implications for
predicting environmental responses to climate variability and for understanding the processes
that control contaminant fate and greenhouse gas emissions in dynamic wetland systems.
