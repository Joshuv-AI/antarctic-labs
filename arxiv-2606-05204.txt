xCPS : an xAct package for covariant phase space, Noether charges, and entropy
Juan Margalef–Bentabol

Abstract
xCPS is an xAct tensor algebra package for symbolic computations within the covariant phase space formalism of field theories. From a generic Lagrangian, xCPS automates the derivation of equations of motion and symplectic currents. It systematically determines whether an infinitesimal transformation in the space of fields is a Noether symmetry and computes the associated Noether charge. Additionally, xCPS can in many cases determine whether a tensorial expression is a divergence and, if so, find its divergence potential. By implementing vertical exterior calculus through a graded, supercommutative wedge product and vertical operators, the package enables efficient computations in gauge theories and higher-derivative models of gravity, including the derivation of thermodynamic quantities like Wald’s entropy. xCPS is open-source under the GPL license and available at https://github.com/juanmargalef .

Introduction

To extract physical information from a local field theory, one can adopt either the canonical or the covariant framework. Although closely related, they are conceptually different. The former splits geometric objects into tangent (spatial) and normal (temporal) parts, specifies an initial state, and evolves it forward in time
20
. Its main advantages are the dynamical perspective and the existence of universal structures like the symplectic form of a cotangent bundle, which are a natural starting point for numerical methods and quantization. However, this approach breaks certain symmetries like covariance, and complicates the study of asymptotic regions.

The covariant phase space (CPS) approach, by contrast, treats fields over the entire spacetime and, under mild conditions, is fully equivalent to the canonical one
19
. Symmetries remain manifest, null infinity is accessible, conserved Noether quantities can be computed systematically, and higher-derivative theories are on equal footing with first-order ones. The main difficulty is the absence of canonical structures in the spaces involved. To obtain a symplectic structure (essential, among other things, for black hole thermodynamics and quantization), one must fix an action and rely on the CPS formalism
11
;
27
, which has been extended to include boundaries
18
and successfully applied to several theories of gravity
3
;
4
;
5
.

The CPS formalism has proven especially powerful for black hole thermodynamics
14
;
16
;
25
;
23
and the study of asymptotic symmetries
7
;
22
. Interest has been renewed recently by gravitational wave astronomy and the discovery of infinitely many conserved charges associated with asymptotic symmetry groups
9
;
10
;
6
;
12
. Despite its conceptual elegance, manual calculations quickly become intractable for theories involving higher-derivative terms or complex gauge structures. However, no general computational implementation of the CPS formalism exists.

To fill this gap, we introduce
xCPS
, a Mathematica package designed to automate these calculations efficiently while preserving the geometric nature of the formalism. Built on the
xAct
tensor algebra bundle
21
,
xCPS
provides automated tools for variational calculus (equations of motion and symplectic currents), Noether symmetry testing, and the computation of Noether charges. It also includes algorithms for divergence detection and heuristic potential-finding. The package allows users to work with concrete Lagrangians (e.g., gravity or electromagnetism) or with
unspecified
Lagrangians depending on tensorial fields
\{\Phi^{i}\}_{i}
and finitely many of their derivatives. By managing the intricate index manipulations and super-commutativity inherent in the CPS formalism,
xCPS
allows researchers to focus on physics rather than algebraic bookkeeping. The following representative outputs illustrate the package’s core capabilities (see the companion executable

Mathematical background

This section summarizes the mathematical foundation underlying
xCPS
. We consider an
n
-manifold without boundary
\mathcal{M}
and a space of tensorial fields
\mathcal{F}
over
\mathcal{M}
. Both spaces are naturally endowed with an exterior derivative:
\mathrm{d}
for
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathbbm{d}
for
\mathcal{F}

1

2.1
First variation

Consider a local Lagrangian
L:\mathcal{F}\to\Omega^{n}(\mathcal{M})
, i.e., a top-form depending on the fields of
\mathcal{F}
and finitely many of their derivatives. Equivalently,
L\in\Omega^{(n,0)}(\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{F})
is a bi-graded
(n,0)
-form over
\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{F}
(see
1
;
18
). The fundamental CPS identity is the canonical decomposition of the first variation:

\mathbbm{d}L=\mathrm{EOM}_{i}\mathbbm{d}\Phi^{i}+\mathrm{d}\Theta

(1)

where
\mathrm{EOM}_{i}\in\Omega^{(n,0)}(\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{F})
is the Euler-Lagrange (EL) form whose kernel provides the solution to the equations of motion
1
1

1

To avoid cluttering the notation, we assume that
\Phi^{i}
has horizontal degree
k=0
, though it can have any index structure. If
\Phi^{i}
is a
k
-form with
k\neq 0
, we have
\mathrm{EOM}_{i}\in\Omega^{(n-k,0)}(\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{F})
and
\mathbbm{d}L=\mathrm{EOM}_{i}\wedge\mathbbm{d}\Phi^{i}+\mathrm{d}\Theta
, with the remaining arguments unchanged.

\mathrm{EOM}_{i}=0
associated with the dynamical field
\Phi^{i}\in\mathcal{F}
;
\Theta\in\Omega^{(n-1,1)}(\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{F})
is a symplectic potential; and
\mathbbm{d}\Phi^{i}
acts as an infinitesimal variation of the field
\Phi^{i}
, analogous to
\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y,\mathrm{d}z
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. The global decomposition (
1

;
1
and is achieved in practice by repeatedly applying the Leibniz rule to remove derivatives from
\mathbbm{d}\Phi^{i}
.

Remark 2.1
.

In the physics literature, the equations of motion are often denoted as
\mathrm{EOM}_{i}=:\frac{\delta L}{\delta\Phi^{i}}
.

Example 1:

Given a fixed metric
g
, consider the Lagrangian of a scalar field theory given by

L_{\mathrm{scl}}(\phi)=\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{a}\phi\nabla^{a}\phi\,\mathrm{vol}_{g}

(2)

where
\mathrm{vol}_{g}
is the metric volume form. Its vertical exterior derivative is

\mathbbm{d}L_{\mathrm{scl}}=\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{a}\mathbbm{d}\phi\nabla^{a}\phi\,\mathrm{vol}_{g}+\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{a}\phi\nabla^{a}\mathbbm{d}\phi\,\mathrm{vol}_{g}=\nabla_{a}\mathbbm{d}\phi\nabla^{a}\phi\,\mathrm{vol}_{g}

(3)

where we have used that
\mathbbm{d}g_{ab}=0
. Now, using
\nabla g_{ab}=0
and the Leibniz rule leads to:

\mathbbm{d}L_{\mathrm{scl}}=\nabla_{a}(\mathbbm{d}\phi\nabla^{a}\phi)\mathrm{vol}_{g}-\mathbbm{d}\phi\nabla_{a}\nabla^{a}\phi\,\mathrm{vol}_{g}=\mathrm{EOM}_{\mathrm{scl}}\,\mathbbm{d}\phi+\mathrm{div}(\vec{U}_{\mathrm{scl}})\mathrm{vol}_{g}

(4)

where
U_{\mathrm{scl}}^{a}=\mathbbm{d}\phi\nabla^{a}\phi
. The last term of (

\mathcal{L}_{\vec{U}}\mathrm{vol}_{g}=\mathrm{div}(\vec{U})\mathrm{vol}_{g}
,
\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{g}=0
, and
\mathcal{L}_{\vec{U}}=\mathcal{\iota}_{\vec{U}}\mathrm{d}+\mathrm{d}\mathcal{\iota}_{\vec{U}}
(
\mathcal{\iota}:\Omega^{k}(M)\to\Omega^{k-1}(M)
is the interior product). Thus, the first variation takes the form of
equation

1
with
\mathrm{EOM}_{\mathrm{scl}}=-\nabla_{a}\nabla^{a}\phi\mathrm{vol}_{g}\in\Omega^{(n,0)}(\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{F})
the EL form of
\phi
(whose kernel is the solutions to the wave equation
\nabla_{a}\nabla^{a}\phi=0
) and
\Theta_{\mathrm{scl}}=\iota_{\vec{U}_{\mathrm{scl}}}\mathrm{vol}_{g}\in\Omega^{(n-1,1)}(\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{F})
a symplectic potential.

2.2
Symplectic form

For a given symplectic potential
\Theta
, we define its symplectic current as

\omega=\mathbbm{d}\Theta\in\Omega^{(n-1,2)}(\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{F})

(5)

Integrating the horizontal part of
\omega
over an
(n-1)
-dimensional submanifold
\Sigma\subset\mathcal{M}
yields a
2
-form on
\mathcal{F}
. The natural choice is to take
\Sigma
a Cauchy surface. We then define the (pre)symplectic form

\Omega_{\Sigma}=\int_{\Sigma}\imath^{*}\omega\in\Omega^{2}(\mathcal{F})

(6)

where
\imath:\Sigma\hookrightarrow\mathcal{M}
is the inclusion (though it is customary to omit this pullback, we retain it to avoid common misunderstandings). Clearly
\Omega_{\Sigma}
is a closed
2
-form (
\mathbbm{d}^{2}=0
), though we cannot generally guarantee its non-degeneracy.

Example 2:

From the Lagrangian (
2
), we obtained
\Theta_{\mathrm{scl}}=\iota_{\vec{U}_{\mathrm{scl}}}\mathrm{vol}_{g}
with
U_{\mathrm{scl}}^{a}=\mathbbm{d}\phi\nabla^{a}\phi
. This leads to

\omega_{\mathrm{scl}}=\mathbbm{d}\Theta_{\mathrm{scl}}=\iota_{\mathbbm{d}\vec{U}_{\mathrm{scl}}}\mathrm{vol}_{g}

where
\mathbbm{d}U_{\mathrm{scl}}^{a}=\mathbbm{d}^{2}\phi\nabla^{a}\phi-\mathbbm{d}\phi\mathrel{{\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox to6.66669pt{\hss\,$\wedge$\hss}\hss}\hbox{$\mathrel{\wedge}$}}}\nabla^{a}\mathbbm{d}\phi=-\mathbbm{d}\phi\mathrel{{\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox to6.66669pt{\hss\,$\wedge$\hss}\hss}\hbox{$\mathrel{\wedge}$}}}\nabla^{a}\mathbbm{d}\phi
. Here we use the natural wedge product
\mathrel{{\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox to6.66669pt{\hss\,$\wedge$\hss}\hss}\hbox{$\mathrel{\wedge}$}}}
in the space of forms over
\mathcal{F}
,
\mathbbm{d}^{2}=0
, and
\mathbbm{d}g_{ab}=0
since

is fixed.

Integrating this current to obtain the symplectic form requires the future-directed, unit normal vector field
\vec{n}
to the chosen Cauchy surface
\Sigma
(in abstract-index notation:
n^{a}
). Over
\Sigma
, and with a slight abuse of notation, we have
\mathrm{vol}_{g}=-n\wedge\mathrm{vol}_{\gamma}

1

(with indices:

(\Omega_{\mathrm{scl}})_{\Sigma}=\int_{\Sigma}\imath^{*}\iota_{\mathbbm{d}\vec{U}_{\mathrm{scl}}}\mathrm{vol}_{g}=\int_{\Sigma}(-n_{a}\mathbbm{d}\vec{U}_{\mathrm{scl}}^{a})\mathrm{vol}_{\gamma}=\int_{\Sigma}\mathbbm{d}(\imath^{*}\phi)\mathrel{{\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox to6.66669pt{\hss\,$\wedge$\hss}\hss}\hbox{$\mathrel{\wedge}$}}}\mathbbm{d}\imath^{*}(\mathcal{L}_{\vec{n}}\phi)\mathrm{vol}_{\gamma}

Roughly speaking,
\imath^{*}\phi

\imath^{*}(\mathcal{L}_{\vec{n}}\phi)
correspond to the position and momenta in the canonical formalism

(\Omega_{\mathrm{scl}})_{\Sigma}
corresponds to the canonical symplectic form.

2.3
Ambiguities

The preceding constructions involve several choices. We now verify that the physically relevant objects are independent of them. First, “the” potential
\Theta
is not well-defined: any potential
\Theta^{\prime}=\Theta+\alpha
(for a horizontally-closed
\alpha\in\Omega^{(n-1,1)}(\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{F})
) is equally valid. Because the vertical degree is non-zero, the generalized Poincaré lemma implies
\alpha
is exact (
\alpha=\mathrm{d}Z
)
24
. Thus, while
\Theta
is ambiguous, its cohomology class
[\Theta]
is not.

A second source of ambiguity arises from the Lagrangian. Since two Lagrangians equal up to a total derivative define the same action,
\mathbb{S}_{L}=\int_{\mathcal{M}}L
, it is natural to regard
\mathbb{S}_{L}
as the physically relevant object. The converse, “
\mathbb{S}_{L}=\mathbb{S}_{L^{\prime}}
implies
L^{\prime}=L+\mathrm{d}Y
”, holds under suitable topological conditions on
\mathcal{F}

18
that we assume hereafter. Once again, the Lagrangian is ambiguous, but its cohomology class is not. In particular, any physically meaningful derived quantity must vanish for the trivial Lagrangian
L=\mathrm{d}Y
, since
\mathbb{S}_{\mathrm{d}Y}=0
. For instance,
equation

1
with
L=\mathrm{d}Y
reduces to

\mathbbm{d}L=\mathbbm{d}(\mathrm{d}Y)=\mathrm{d}\mathbbm{d}Y

(7)

so
\mathrm{EOM}=0
(also obvious using
\mathbb{S}_{\mathrm{d}Y}=0
),
\Theta=\mathbbm{d}Y+\mathrm{d}Z
(two sources of ambiguity), and
[\Theta]=[\mathbbm{d}Y]
(one source of ambiguity). Thus, EOM is a physical quantity but
\Theta
and
[\Theta]
are not. The symplectic current is also non-physical since for
L=\mathrm{d}Y
we have

\omega=\mathbbm{d}(\mathbbm{d}Y+\mathrm{d}Z)=\mathbbm{d}^{2}Y+\mathrm{d}\mathbbm{d}Z=\mathrm{d}(\mathbbm{d}Z)

However, the (pre)symplectic form is independent of
(Y,Z)
as, upon integration via Stokes’ theorem, it yields the trivial presymplectic form
\Omega_{\Sigma}=0
.

Finally, the choice of Cauchy surface
\Sigma
affects the presymplectic form over
\mathcal{F}
. Indeed, given two disjoint Cauchy surfaces
\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2}
bounding a region
\mathcal{M}_{12}
, using Stokes’ theorem, we have

\Omega_{\Sigma_{2}}-\Omega_{\Sigma_{1}}=\int_{\mathcal{M}_{12}}\mathrm{d}\omega=\int_{\mathcal{M}_{12}}\mathbbm{d}\mathrm{d}\Theta=-\int_{\mathcal{M}_{12}}\mathbbm{d}\mathrm{EOM}_{i}\mathrel{{\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox to6.66669pt{\hss\,$\wedge$\hss}\hss}\hbox{$\mathrel{\wedge}$}}}\mathbbm{d}\Phi^{i}

Although
\Omega
depends on the chosen Cauchy surface, its pull-back to the space of solutions (called on-shell) does not. Indeed, if
\mathbbm{j}:\mathrm{Sol}(\mathbb{S})\hookrightarrow\mathcal{F}
is the inclusion into the solution space, then
\mathbbm{j}^{*}\Omega_{\Sigma_{2}}=\mathbbm{j}^{*}\Omega_{\Sigma_{1}}
since
\mathbbm{j}^{*}\mathrm{EOM}\equiv 0
. This (pre)symplectic form, denoted
\Omega_{\mathbb{S}}
, is canonically associated with
\mathbb{S}
.

2.5
Variational vector fields

In classical mechanics, transformations like rotations or translations take place in the space of coordinates. Rather than working with finite transformations, which form a group, we usually work with infinitesimal transformations, which form an algebra, i.e., vector fields over the space of coordinates. In field theories, the role of coordinates is played (loosely speaking) by tensor fields, so an infinitesimal transformation is a vector field
\mathbb{X}\in\mathfrak{X}(\mathcal{F})
, called a variational vector field (VVF).

For a tensorial field
\Phi^{i}
, we define
\mathbb{X}_{\Phi^{i}}:=\Psi^{i}\in T_{\Phi}\mathcal{F}
. Here,
\Psi^{i}
satisfies the linearized conditions of
\Phi^{i}
so, in particular, shares its index character. Setting aside infinite-dimensional subtleties
18
,
\mathbb{X}
can be equivalently understood as a derivation via the Lie derivative of
\mathcal{F}
:

\mathbb{X}_{\Phi^{i}}:=\Psi^{i}\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003pt\equiv\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003pt\mathbbm{L}_{\mathbb{X}}\Phi^{i}:=\Psi^{i}\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003pt\equiv\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003pt\mathbbm{i}_{\mathbb{X}}\mathbbm{d}\Phi^{i}:=\Psi^{i}

(9)

Remark 2.4
.

The last two formulas are related via Cartan’s magic formula over
\mathcal{F}
(which holds for a broad class of VVFs but not all, see
section

4.3
):
\mathbbm{L}_{\mathbb{X}}=\mathbbm{i}_{\mathbb{X}}\mathbbm{d}+\mathbbm{d}\mathbbm{i}_{\mathbb{X}}
.

The last expression of
equation

9
serves as an implicit definition of
\mathbb{X}
as a map acting on
1
-forms via the interior product. This enables a formal expression analogous to the finite-dimensional case:

\xi=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\iota_{\xi}(\mathrm{d}x_{j})\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}\in\mathfrak{X}(\mathcal{M})\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003pt\mathbb{X}=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\mathbbm{i}_{\mathbb{X}}(\mathbbm{d}\Phi^{i})\frac{\delta}{\delta\Phi^{i}}\in\mathfrak{X}(\mathcal{F})

(10)

These expressions conveniently package their respective components,
\{\iota_{\xi}(\mathrm{d}x_{j})\}_{j}
and
\{\mathbbm{i}_{\mathbb{X}}(\mathbbm{d}\Phi^{i})\}_{i}
, into a single geometric object. While
equation

10
is purely formal, it is highly practical for implementing VVFs in
xCPS
, as demonstrated in
section

4.2
.

Example 3:

Given
\xi\in\mathfrak{X}(\mathcal{M})

\mathbb{X}_{\xi}\in\mathfrak{X}(\mathcal{F})
as the infinitesimal action of

\mathbbm{i}_{\mathbb{X}_{\xi}}\mathbbm{d}\Phi^{i}:=\mathcal{L}_{\xi}\Phi^{i}\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\equiv\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\mathbbm{L}_{\mathbb{X}_{\xi}}\Phi^{i}:=\mathcal{L}_{\xi}\Phi^{i}\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\equiv\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\mathbb{X}_{\xi}=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{L}_{\xi}\Phi^{i}\frac{\delta}{\delta\Phi^{i}}

The action of

generates, loosely speaking, the transformation
\Phi^{i}\mapsto\Phi^{i}+\varepsilon\mathcal{L}_{\xi}\Phi^{i}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})

\delta_{\xi}\Phi=\mathcal{L}_{\xi}\Phi^{i}
.

2.6
Noether symmetries and charges

Definition 2.5
.

Given a local action
\mathbb{S}
, we say that a VVF,
\mathbb{X}
, is a symmetry of
\mathbb{S}
if
\mathbbm{L}_{\mathbb{X}}\mathbb{S}=0
.

Such a symmetry leaves the action
\mathbb{S}
(and therefore the physics) invariant, which subsequently preserves the set of critical points
\mathrm{Sol}(\mathbb{S})
. Thus, its restriction
\overline{\mathbb{X}}:=\mathbb{X}|_{\mathrm{Sol}}(\mathbb{S})
is a vector field of
\mathrm{Sol}(\mathbb{S})
. A natural question is whether
\overline{\mathbb{X}}
is a Hamiltonian vector field with respect to
\Omega_{\mathbb{S}}
. Among all symmetries, the
\mathrm{d}
-symmetries defined below admit a systematic treatment via Noether’s theorem.

Definition 2.6
.

A VVF,
\mathbb{X}
, is a
\mathrm{d}
-symmetry (or variational symmetry) of
L
if

\mathbbm{L}_{\mathbb{X}}L=\mathrm{d}S_{\mathbb{X}}

(12)

for some
S_{\mathbb{X}}\in\Omega^{(n-1,0)}(\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{F})
. In this case,
S_{\mathbb{X}}
is called a symmetry potential of
\mathbb{X}
.

Remarks.

2.7.

If
\mathbb{X}
is a
\mathrm{d}
-symmetry of
L
, then it is also a
\mathrm{d}
-symmetry of any
L^{\prime}=L+\mathrm{d}Y
.

2.8.

If
\mathbb{X}
is a
\mathrm{d}
-symmetry of
L
, then
\mathbb{X}
is a symmetry of
\mathbb{S}_{L}
. The converse is not generally true.

2.9.

S_{\mathbb{X}}
is defined up to a closed form, which might not be exact. For simplicity, we assume that every closed

-form is exact (see

Applying Cartan’s magic formula and

Definition 2.10
.

Given a
\mathrm{d}
-symmetry
\mathbb{X}
of
\mathbb{S}_{L}
, we define its
\mathbb{X}
-current (or Noether current) as

J_{\mathbb{X}}=S_{\mathbb{X}}-\mathbbm{i}_{\mathbb{X}}\Theta\in\Omega^{(n-1,0)}(\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{F})

Integrating over a Cauchy surface
\Sigma
yields the
\mathbb{X}
-charge (or Noether charge):

\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{X}}:=\int_{\Sigma}\imath^{*}J_{\mathbb{X}}\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003pt\longrightarrow\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003pt\mathbb{H}_{\overline{\mathbb{X}}}:=\mathbbm{j}^{*}\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{X}}

(13)

Tracking the ambiguities outlined in
section

2.3
, one finds that
\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{X}}
depends only on
\mathbb{S}
,
\mathbb{X}
, and
\imath:\Sigma\hookrightarrow\mathcal{M}
, while its pull-back to the space of solutions
\mathbb{H}_{\overline{\mathbb{X}}}
(on-shell), depends exclusively on
\mathbb{S}
and
\overline{\mathbb{X}}
. This latter quantity represents the physically meaningful conserved Noether charge, taking the same value on any Cauchy surface
\Sigma
. Furthermore,
\mathbb{H}_{\overline{\mathbb{X}}}
is the Hamiltonian of
\overline{\mathbb{X}}
, i.e.,
\mathbbm{i}_{\overline{\mathbb{X}}}\Omega_{\mathbb{S}}=\mathbbm{d}\mathbb{H}_{\overline{\mathbb{X}}}
. Finally, we define a gauge transformation as a VVF over the space of solutions
\overline{\mathbb{X}}
such that:

\mathbbm{i}_{\overline{\mathbb{X}}}\Omega_{\mathbb{S}}=0\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 10.00002pt\equiv\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 20.00003pt\mathbb{H}_{\overline{\mathbb{X}}}=0\text{ (up to a constant)}\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 10.00002pt\equiv\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 10.00002ptJ_{\overline{\mathbb{X}}}=\mathrm{d}Q_{\overline{\mathbb{X}}}

(14)

Example 4:

Let us consider the electromagnetic (EM) Lagrangian given by

L_{\mathrm{EM}}(A)=\frac{1}{4}g^{ac}g^{bd}(\mathrm{d}A)_{ab}(\mathrm{d}A)_{cd}\mathrm{vol}_{g}

where
A\in\Omega^{1}(\mathcal{M})
and
g
is some fixed metric. A straightforward computation shows that

\mathrm{EOM}_{\mathrm{EM}}^{b}=-\nabla_{a}(\mathrm{d}A)^{ab}\mathrm{vol}_{g}\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003pt\Theta_{\mathrm{EM}}=\iota_{\vec{U}}\mathrm{vol}_{g}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{with}\hskip 10.00002ptU^{a}=(\mathrm{d}A)^{ab}(\mathbbm{d}A)_{b}

Letting
\lambda\in\Omega^{0}(\mathcal{M})
, we define
\mathbb{X}_{\lambda}
by:

\mathbbm{i}_{\mathbb{X}_{\lambda}}\mathbbm{d}A:=\mathrm{d}\lambda\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 20.00003pt\equiv\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 20.00003pt\mathbbm{L}_{\mathbb{X}_{\lambda}}A:=\mathrm{d}\lambda\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 10.00002pt\equiv\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 20.00003pt\mathbb{X}_{\lambda}=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\mathrm{d}\lambda\frac{\delta}{\delta A}

(15)

Since
\mathrm{d}^{2}=0
, it is easy to check that

\mathbbm{L}_{\mathbb{X}_{\lambda}}L_{\mathrm{EM}}=0\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 20.00003pt\longrightarrow\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 20.00003ptJ_{\mathbb{X}_{\lambda}}=-\mathbbm{i}_{\mathbb{X}_{\lambda}}\Theta=-\iota(\mathbbm{i}_{\mathbb{X}_{\lambda}}\vec{U})\mathrm{vol}_{g}=-\iota((\mathrm{d}A)^{ab}(\mathrm{d}\lambda)_{b})\mathrm{vol}_{g}

Integrating by parts produces the Noether charge:

\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{X}_{\lambda}}=\int_{\Sigma}\lambda n_{a}\mathrm{EOM}_{\mathrm{EM}}^{a}\mathrm{vol}_{\gamma}\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003pt\longrightarrow\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003pt\mathbb{H}_{\overline{\mathbb{X}}_{\lambda}}=0

Thus,
\mathbb{X}_{\lambda}
is a
\mathrm{d}
-symmetry and
\overline{\mathbb{X}}_{\lambda}
is, according to
equation

14
, a gauge transformation.

2.7
Diff-invariance and non-Noether charges

An action
\mathbb{S}
is Diff-invariant if
\mathbb{X}_{\xi}
, as defined in
equation

11
, is a symmetry of
\mathbb{S}
for all
\xi\in\mathfrak{X}(\mathcal{M})
. A Lagrangian
L
is Diff-invariant if
\mathbbm{L}_{\mathbb{X}_{\xi}}L=\mathcal{L}_{\xi}L
for all
\xi\in\mathfrak{X}(\mathcal{M})
. If
L
is Diff-invariant, then
\mathbb{X}_{\xi}
is a
\mathrm{d}
-symmetry of
L
(though the converse might not hold) with

S_{\mathbb{X}_{\xi}}=\iota_{\xi}L\hskip 10.00002pt\longrightarrow\hskip 10.00002ptJ_{\mathbb{X}_{\xi}}=\iota_{\xi}L-\mathbbm{i}_{\mathbb{X}_{\xi}}\Theta\hskip 10.00002pt\longrightarrow\hskip 10.00002pt\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{X}_{\xi}}:=\int_{\Sigma}\imath^{*}J_{\mathbb{X}_{\xi}}\hskip 10.00002pt\longrightarrow\hskip 10.00002pt\mathbb{H}_{\overline{\mathbb{X}}_{\xi}}:=\mathbbm{j}^{*}\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{X}_{\xi}}

(16)

Notably, these quantities can be defined even when
\mathbb{X}_{\xi}
is not a
\mathrm{d}
-symmetry:

S_{\xi}:=\iota_{\xi}L\hskip 10.00002pt\longrightarrow\hskip 10.00002ptJ_{\xi}:=\iota_{\xi}L-\mathbbm{i}_{\mathbb{X}_{\xi}}\Theta\hskip 10.00002pt\longrightarrow\hskip 10.00002pt\mathbbm{Q}^{\Sigma}_{\xi}:=\int_{\Sigma}\imath^{*}J_{\xi}\hskip 10.00002pt\longrightarrow\hskip 10.00002pt\overline{\mathbbm{Q}}^{\Sigma}_{\xi}:=\mathbbm{j}^{*}\mathbbm{Q}_{\xi}^{\Sigma}

(17)

that we call
\xi
-potential,
\xi
-current, off-shell
\xi
-charge, and on-shell
\xi
-charge. If
\mathbb{X}_{\xi}
is not a symmetry,
\overline{\mathbbm{Q}}^{\Sigma}_{\xi}
is a non-Noether charge. This generally depends on the chosen Lagrangian representative and the Cauchy surface
\Sigma
, leading to a flux law relating charges on different Cauchy surfaces.

Example 5:

The
\xi
-charge of the Lagrangian
L_{\mathrm{scl}}
given by
equation

2
is:

\mathbbm{Q}_{\xi}^{\Sigma}=\int_{\Sigma}n_{a}\xi_{b}T^{ab}_{\mathrm{scl}}\mathrm{vol}_{\gamma}

Here,
T^{ab}_{\mathrm{scl}}=\nabla^{a}\phi\nabla^{b}\phi-g^{ab}L_{\mathrm{scl}}(\phi)
is the energy-momentum tensor.

2.8
Top-forms vs densities

Because
xAct
operates on tensorial expressions rather than geometric integrals,
xCPS
works with densitized currents instead of top-forms and their integrated counterparts. These approaches are equivalent once a fiducial volume form
\mathrm{vol}_{0}
on
\mathcal{M}
is fixed, which we assume henceforth. We now explicitly define
\widetilde{\Lambda}
, the density-level analogue to
\Lambda
from
equation

8
.

First consider a Lagrangian function
\mathcal{L}\in\Omega^{(0,0)}(\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{F})
. Clearly
L:=\mathcal{L}\mathrm{vol}_{0}\in\Omega^{(n,0)}(\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{F})
is a Lagrangian with the usual decomposition
\mathbbm{d}L=\mathrm{EOM}\mathbbm{d}\Phi^{i}+\mathrm{d}\Theta
given by
equation

1
. Since
\Theta
has horizontal-co-degree
1
, there exists a symplectic potential vector
\vec{U}
such that
\Theta=\iota_{\vec{U}}\mathrm{vol}_{0}
. Therefore, the same computation used after
equation

4
shows that
\mathrm{d}\Theta=\mathrm{div}(\vec{U})\mathrm{vol}_{0}
. Thus, using the fact that
\mathrm{vol}_{0}
is fixed (
\mathbbm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{0}=0)
, we obtain the density-equivalent decomposition to
equation

1
:

\mathbbm{d}\mathcal{L}=\frac{\mathbbm{d}L}{\mathrm{vol}_{0}}=\frac{\mathrm{EOM}_{i}}{\mathrm{vol}_{0}}\mathbbm{d}\Phi^{i}+\frac{\mathrm{d}\Theta}{\mathrm{vol}_{0}}=\frac{\mathrm{EOM}_{i}}{\mathrm{vol}_{0}}\mathbbm{d}\Phi^{i}+\frac{\mathrm{div}(\vec{U})\mathrm{vol}_{0}}{\mathrm{vol}_{0}}=\mathrm{eom}_{i}\mathbbm{d}\Phi^{i}+\mathrm{div}(\vec{U})

(18)

Here,
\mathrm{eom}_{i}
is the densitized equations of motion (i.e.
\mathrm{EOM}_{i}=\mathrm{eom}_{i}\mathrm{vol}_{0}
), whose kernel is the same as
\mathrm{EOM}_{i}
. Consequently, we define the canonical decomposition map
\widetilde{\Lambda}
as:

\begin{array}[]{cccc}\widetilde{\Lambda}:&\Omega^{(0,0)}(\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{F})&\longrightarrow&\Omega^{(0,0)}(\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{F})\times\mathcal{T}^{(1,1)}(\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{F})\\
&\mathcal{L}&\longmapsto&(\ \mathrm{eom}_{i}\ ,\ \vec{U}\ )\end{array}

Notice that
\vec{U}
is a vector in
\mathcal{M}
and a
1
-form in
\mathcal{F}
(see
sections

2.1
and
2.6
).

4.1
Vertical forms and the field-space wedge product

DefTensor
has been modified in three key ways:

1.

It accepts the additional option
VertDeg
, a non-negative integer (0 by default) indicating that the defined tensor of
M
is also a form on the space of fields
\mathcal{F}
of that degree. The term “vertical” refers to the fiber direction in the variational bicomplex
18
;
1
as opposed to the “horizontal” degree on
M
.

2.

For any tensor
T
, an additional tensor
dlT
is defined: the vertical exterior derivative of
T
, printed as
\mathbbm{d}\texttt{{T}}
, with the same characteristics as
T
except that
VertDeg[
\mathbbm{d}
T]=VertDeg[T]+1
.
2
2

2

The prepend “
dl
” and the print symbol
\mathbbm{d}
can be modified via
$NameVerticalExteriorDerivative
and
$SymbolVerticalExteriorDerivative
.

3.

For any tensor
T
, an additional tensor
VariationalVectorT
is defined: the dual to the first-order variation of
T
, printed as
\frac{\delta}{\delta\texttt{{T}}}
. It has “opposite” characteristics to
T
: opposite variance of indices (but identical symmetries), opposite vertical degree,
VertDeg[
\frac{\delta}{\delta\texttt{{T}}}
]=-VertDeg[T]
, and opposite density weight,
WeightOfTensor[
\frac{\delta}{\delta\texttt{{T}}}
]=-WeightOfTensor[T]
.

Remark 4.1
.

\mathbbm{d}\texttt{{T}}
and
\frac{\delta}{\delta\texttt{{T}}}
are dual: their contraction yields a scalar (see details on
section

4.6
).

The vertical degree enables a new bilinear supergraded product called
WWedge
, corresponding to the natural wedge of
k
-forms on the space of fields:

\alpha\mathrel{{\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox to6.66669pt{\hss\,$\wedge$\hss}\hss}\hbox{$\mathrel{\wedge}$}}}\beta=(-1)^{\texttt{{VertDeg[$\alpha$]}}\texttt{{VertDeg[$\beta$]}}}\beta\mathrel{{\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox to6.66669pt{\hss\,$\wedge$\hss}\hss}\hbox{$\mathrel{\wedge}$}}}\alpha

(22)

Remarks.

4.2.

The
WWedge
product is associative and reduces to the standard
Times
product if at least one of the two terms has a
VertDeg
of zero.

4.3.

Several
xAct
commands have been modified to account for the
WWedge
product. For instance,
LieD
and
Bracket
must satisfy the Leibniz rule with respect to the
WWedge
.

4.4.

Part of the setup so far is the vertical analog of the
xTerior

13
(which implements the horizontal

product of horizontal forms). A key difference is that horizontal
k
-forms vanish when
k

M

\mathcal{F}
is infinite-dimensional).

4.2
Variational vectors and variational vector fields

As mentioned before, each tensor defined via

\left(\frac{\delta}{\delta g}\right)^{ab}

(23)

Remarks.

4.5.

While physics literature frequently uses the notation
\frac{\delta}{\delta g_{ab}}

equation

4.6.

In certain physical contexts, it is preferable to consider variations of the inverse metric

xCPS

$UseInverseMetric=True

4.3.5
SortVertOperators
,
VertCartanMagicFormula
, and
VertBracketToVertLie

These three commands rewrite expressions containing nested vertical operators in terms of the others. To understand how, first notice that there are six possible super-commutators between
\mathbbm{L}
,
\mathbbm{i}
, and
\mathbbm{d}
, defined as:

[P,Q]=PQ-(-1)^{\texttt{{VertDeg[$P$]}}\texttt{{VertDeg[$Q$]}}}QP

(27)

Under appropriate conditions on the vector fields (they are prolongations of evolutionary vector fields, see for instance
(
1
, 1.16,1.17,3.17,3.26)
), their result can be written in terms of
\{\mathbbm{L},\mathbbm{i},\mathbbm{d}\}

\begin{array}[]{lll}[\mathbbm{L}_{\mathtt{vvf1}},\mathbbm{L}_{\mathtt{vvf2}}]=\mathbbm{L}_{\llbracket\mathtt{vvf1},\mathtt{vvf2}\rrbracket}&[\mathbbm{L}_{\mathtt{vvf1}},\mathbbm{i}_{\mathtt{vvf2}}]=\mathbbm{i}_{\llbracket\mathtt{vvf1},\mathtt{vvf2}\rrbracket}&[\mathbbm{L}_{\mathtt{vvf}},\mathbbm{d}]=0\\
&[\mathbbm{i}_{\mathtt{vvf1}},\mathbbm{i}_{\mathtt{vvf2}}]=0&[\mathbbm{i}_{\mathtt{vvf}},\mathbbm{d}]=\mathbbm{L}_{\mathtt{vvf}}\\
&&[\mathbbm{d},\mathbbm{d}]=0\end{array}

The last one is
\mathbbm{d}^{2}=0
, which is implemented automatically. Whenever other operators are nested, we can sort them out using
SortVertOperators
according to a specified sorting order
\{P,Q,R\}
(which can be parsed as an argument or defined globally via
$SortVertOperatorsOrder
). The command

(analogous to

of

to choose the order

VVF1
and
VVF2

Conversely, the LHS can be recovered from the RHS by applying
VertCartanMagicFormula
and
VertBracketToVertLie
. The former substitutes
\mathbbm{L}
by applying Cartan’s magic formula. Meanwhile, the latter rewrites

in terms of

as follows (omiting indices for simplicity):

\llbracket T\mathrel{{\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox to6.66669pt{\hss\,$\wedge$\hss}\hss}\hbox{$\mathrel{\wedge}$}}}\left(\frac{\delta}{\delta R}\right),S\mathrel{{\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox to6.66669pt{\hss\,$\wedge$\hss}\hss}\hbox{$\mathrel{\wedge}$}}}\left(\frac{\delta}{\delta Q}\right)\rrbracket=\left(\mathbbm{L}_{T\left(\frac{\delta}{\delta R}\right)}S\right)\mathrel{{\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox to6.66669pt{\hss\,$\wedge$\hss}\hss}\hbox{$\mathrel{\wedge}$}}}\left(\frac{\delta}{\delta Q}\right)-(-1)^{\|\texttt{{vvf1}}\|\|\texttt{{vvf2}}\|}\left(\mathbbm{L}_{S\left(\frac{\delta}{\delta Q}\right)}T\right)\mathrel{{\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox to6.66669pt{\hss\,$\wedge$\hss}\hss}\hbox{$\mathrel{\wedge}$}}}\left(\frac{\delta}{\delta R}\right)

where

[V^{i}\partial_{i},W^{j}\partial_{j}]^{k}\partial_{k}=(V^{m}\partial_{m}W^{k}-W^{m}\partial_{m}V^{k})\partial_{k}=(\mathcal{L}_{V}W^{k})\partial_{k}-(\mathcal{L}_{W}V^{k})\partial_{k}

(29)

4.7.2

FindPotentialGradient[der][expr]
attempts to find a tensorial expression

such that

. This is the “inverse” of the Leibniz rule. This is a genuinely difficult problem in general. Even in the well-studied case of differential forms, knowing whether a

\beta
should be a
(k-1)
-form (a totally antisymmetric tensor). That forces

\mathrm{d}\alpha=0
since
\mathrm{d}^{2}=0
. For general tensors we do not have that constraint but there is no known algorithm to obtain such potential. The procedure is heuristic: it either terminates with a potential or fails to converge (because it gets into a loop, which is automatically detected by the algorithm, or because it does not exist). Remark 5.1
.

xAct
implements the Leibniz rule (variational derivative) with
VarD
in a straightforward but not always efficient way. Roughly speaking, given a monomial,
VarD
acts on each factor by removing all derivatives in front of it via the Leibniz rule, discarding the total derivative:

(DDT^{ab})(DS_{ab})\hskip 10.00002pt\overset{\texttt{{VarD}}}{\longrightarrow}\hskip 10.00002pt-(DT^{ab})(DDS_{ab})\hskip 10.00002pt\overset{\texttt{{VarD}}}{\longrightarrow}\hskip 10.00002ptT^{ab}(DDDS_{ab})

The base case is reached when no derivatives remain:
VarD
checks whether the bare tensor is the one being varied, returning the rest of the expression if so, and zero otherwise. The same procedure is then applied to each remaining factor of the monomial:

(DDT^{ab})(DS_{ab})\hskip 10.00002pt\overset{\texttt{{VarD}}}{\longrightarrow}\hskip 10.00002pt-(DDDT^{ab})(S_{ab})

This approach has obvious disadvantages:

1.

VarD
cannot know if it is removing the derivatives from the correct tensor until it finishes. If the wrong tensor is reached, the entire branch is discarded. This is particularly inefficient for higher order theories as we will see at the end of this subsection.

2.

There is no easy way to keep track of the total derivative terms that appear. For each step, we have one total derivative term but it might be discarded at the end.

3.

VarD
does not take into account variational relations. For instance,

In[45]:=

0

with respect to the first order perturbation of the tensor. This procedure is non-trivial for new users and additional variational relations cannot be incorporated. Moreover, the first two aforementioned problems still remain.

7
Conclusions

We have introduced
xCPS
, a Mathematica package built upon the
xAct
tensor algebra bundle, designed to automate symbolic computations within the covariant phase space formalism. Starting from a specified Lagrangian density,
xCPS
systematically derives equations of motion, symplectic potentials, and both Noether and non-Noether currents. As a highly useful byproduct, it also introduces an exact divergence-detection algorithm and a heuristic potential-finding algorithm, both of which hold independent computational value beyond the phase space framework.

The package introduces several novel features that, to the best of our knowledge, remain unavailable in any prior tensor algebra implementation. Chief among these is the accommodation of generic Lagrangians via
PartialPartial
tensors, which encode functional derivatives of unspecified scalar functions with respect to a tensor and its derivatives. This innovation allows
xCPS
to seamlessly manipulate generalized frameworks such as
f(\mathrm{Riem})
gravity and
L(\mathrm{Riem},\nabla\mathrm{Riem})
theories without demanding a fixed functional form. Furthermore, the variational graph and its recursive constantness-propagation algorithm provide a systematic, extensible mechanism for tracking tensor dependencies, resolving the structural limitations of the
VarD
approach. Finally, a native implementation of vertical exterior calculus—featuring the supercommutative
WWedge
product and the four foundational vertical operators—makes the symplectic current computable in absolute generality, a milestone unachievable via perturbation-based tools such as
xPert
.

The applications presented in
section

6
demonstrate the reliability and expansive reach of the package. For standard frameworks like the Klein–Gordon scalar field, electromagnetism, and General Relativity,
xCPS
reproduces known expressions. For higher-derivative gravity, the package effortlessly generates highly complex outputs that would be exceptionally tedious, if not impossible, to derive by hand or with pre-existing software.

Several development ideas remain open:

•

Algorithmic Enhancements:
The divergence-detection algorithm,
DivergenceQ
, currently requires a scalar input; extending it to expressions with free indices would significantly broaden its applicability.

•

Ecosystem Integration:
A basic compatibility with
xCoba
is already in place but further integration and testing is required. A combination with
xTerior
is also desirable. Together, the two packages would provide a complete implementation of the variational bicomplex, handling both horizontal and vertical differential forms within a unified framework. However, this relies on a double-graded wedge product, requiring a non-trivial addition to
xAct
.

•

Physical Extensions:
Finally, extending
xCPS
to accommodate manifolds with boundaries
18
will unlock the automated computation of boundary charges and corner terms, which are increasingly vital in the modern study of asymptotic symmetries and edge modes.

By automating the tedious and error-prone derivations inherent to the CPS formalism, we hope
xCPS
will serve as a powerful and reliable asset for the theoretical physics community.
