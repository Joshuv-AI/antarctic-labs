Rare Pyloric Stenosis in Mitochondrial Disease Treated with Hand-Assisted Laparoscopic Pyloromyotomy
Authors: Francis S. Lee, Christina W. Lee, Clare H. Lee, Vincent Hunley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mitochondrial diseases are rare genetic disorders that affect the body’s ability to produce energy at the cellular level. Because many organs rely heavily on mitochondrial function, these diseases can cause a wide range of symptoms involving the nervous system, muscles, heart, and gastrointestinal tract. Gastrointestinal complications are common and may include difficulty swallowing, gastroparesis, and intestinal motility disorders. Although pyloric stenosis has rarely been reported, mitochondrial dysfunction affecting gastrointestinal smooth muscle and enteric neuronal function provides a plausible mechanism for the development of gastric outlet obstruction and pyloric dysfunction, leading to severely limited nutritional intake, weight loss, and malnutrition. We present the case of a young woman with a mitochondrial encephalomyopathy, lactic acidosis, and stroke-like episodes (MELAS) variant who developed severe pyloric stenosis resulting in chronic gastric outlet obstruction and profound malnutrition. Her condition progressed to complete dependence on total parenteral nutrition (TPN) and was complicated by recurrent septicemia related to central line-associated bloodstream infections (CLABSI). After failing conservative treatment, she underwent laparoscopic hand-assisted pyloromyotomy with intraoperative esophagogastroduodenoscopy. Following surgery, her gastric emptying improved significantly, allowing her to regain weight and discontinue TPN. With her nutritional status and strength restored, she was able to resume normal activities and successfully complete her college education. This case demonstrates the significant gastrointestinal complications that can occur in mitochondrial disease and highlights the potential benefit of surgical intervention in selected patients with severe pyloric dysfunction.

INTRODUCTION

Mitochondrial diseases are a rare group of inherited disorders that impair the body’s ability to produce energy at the cellular level. Mitochondria are responsible for converting nutrients into adenosine triphosphate (ATP), the primary source of energy for nearly all cells.1,4 Because organs with high energy demands rely heavily on mitochondrial function, these diseases most commonly affect the brain, skeletal muscles, heart, and gastrointestinal tract.3–5 Although mitochondrial diseases are individually rare and often difficult to diagnose due to their wide range of genetic mutations and clinical presentations, they collectively represent the most common inherited metabolic disorders, affecting approximately 1 in 5,000 individuals.2

Gastrointestinal symptoms are common in patients with some mitochondrial diseases and can significantly impact nutrition and quality of life. Manifestations may include dysphagia, nausea and vomiting, gastroparesis, abnormal intestinal motility, abdominal pain, gastroesophageal reflux, and chronic intestinal pseudo-obstruction.1 As a result, many patients experience undernutrition, failure to thrive, and in severe cases, dependence on enteral or parenteral nutritional support. These symptoms are believed to result from mitochondrial dysfunction affecting both the smooth muscle of the gastrointestinal tract and the enteric nervous system.1

While gastroparesis and delayed gastric emptying are well-recognized manifestations of multiple types of mitochondrial disease, severe pyloric dysfunction and pyloric stenosis are rarely described. We present the case of a young woman with mitochondrial encephalomyopathy, lactic acidosis, and stroke-like episodes (MELAS). This is a multisystem mitochondrial disorder most commonly caused by the m.3243A>G mitochondrial DNA mutation.6 The m.3243A>G mtDNA mutation is often associated with gastrointestinal manifestations, including dysmotility and nutritional complications.6,7 This patient’s gastrointestinal symptoms had progressed to severe gastroparesis and pyloric stenosis, resulting in life-threatening malnutrition, complete dependence on total parenteral nutrition (TPN), and recurrent central line-associated bloodstream infection (CLABSI). Following treatment with a laparoscopic hand-assisted pyloromyotomy, she experienced significant improvement in gastric emptying, regained her nutritional status, discontinued TPN, and was ultimately able to resume normal activities and successfully complete her college education.

CASE PRESENTATION

The patient is a 23-year-old Caucasian female that carried a diagnosis of a MELAS variant which manifested in her gastrointestinal tract causing nearly complete pyloric stenosis. She was completely dependent on TPN and a G-tube. Her first symptoms appeared at 8 years old with complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS). At the age of 15 she had frequent, intractable pain and migraine headaches. When she turned 16 years old, she had a T1-S1 spinal fusion due to her scoliosis. Subsequently, she slowly developed severe gastroparesis and required a G-tube for feeding. At the age of 19 years, she started TPN since gastroparesis and pyloric stenosis made her nausea and vomiting unbearable and led her to become severely malnourished. During the subsequent 5 years on TPN dependency, the CLABSI’s caused multiple septicemias and several hospitalizations leading to intensive care and at one time intubation for respiratory support. Given the patient’s prolonged symptoms, severe nutritional compromise, and failure of conservative management, definitive surgical pyloromyotomy was selected. Alternative approaches such as endoscopic dilation or botulinum toxin injection were considered less durable, whereas pyloromyotomy offered the potential for long-term restoration of gastric emptying and enteral nutrition.

The patient underwent a laparoscopic hand-assisted pyloromyotomy with intraoperative esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD). The EGD findings revealed a severely narrowed pyloric channel that could not be traversed by the endoscope (Figure 1). Next, the goal was to identify and mark the gastric side of the pylorus, allowing precise localization during the operation (Figures 1 and 2). After standard preparation and draping, a 5-mm peri-umbilical port was placed for a 30-degree laparoscope, followed by 2 additional 5-mm working ports in the right upper quadrant and left upper abdomen. A 12-mm port was then positioned directly over the marked pylorus. Dense adhesions related to the patient’s preexisting gastrostomy tube (Figure 3) were carefully lysed. The 12-mm port was subsequently replaced with a ring forceps to facilitate exposure and stabilization of the pylorus.

Hook cautery introduced through the left upper quadrant port was used to create a 3-cm longitudinal seromuscular incision over the pylorus. The myotomy was then progressively deepened using electrocautery and blunt dissection until the Endomark-stained submucosa was clearly identified. The pyloromyotomy was completed by extending the seromuscular split approximately 1 cm beyond both the proximal and distal margins of the pylorus, resulting in a total myotomy length of approximately 5 cm (Figures 4 and 5). Repeat intraoperative EGD demonstrated a widely patent pylorus with easy passage of the endoscope into the duodenum. An air insufflation leak test was negative, confirming an intact mucosa and an excellent operative result.

The patient’s recovery was rapid and remarkable. Within 24 hours she was tolerating a liquid diet and progressively advanced to solid foods without nausea or vomiting. Over the following months, she steadily gained weight and achieved a body mass index greater than 20. Six months after surgery, she was successfully weaned from TPN, and her tunneled central venous catheter was removed. Following catheter removal, she experienced no further episodes of septicemia, line infection, or hospitalization. Her nutritional status continued to improve, allowing her to return to normal daily activities and regain a quality of life that had been severely limited by years of gastroparesis, malnutrition, and recurrent catheter-related infections.

DISCUSSION

Mitochondrial diseases are inherited disorders caused by mutations in mitochondrial or nuclear DNA that impair cellular energy production. Because tissues with high energy demands rely heavily on mitochondrial function, these disorders can affect multiple organ systems, particularly the nervous system, muscles, heart, and gastrointestinal tract. Gastrointestinal involvement is common and may present as dysphagia, gastroparesis, chronic intestinal pseudo-obstruction, abnormal intestinal motility, and malnutrition. The m.3243A>G mutation, commonly associated with mitochondrial encephalopathy, lactic acidosis, and stroke-like episodes (MELAS) and related mitochondrial syndromes, has been linked to severe gastrointestinal dysmotility and nutritional impairment.6

While gastrointestinal symptoms are frequently encountered in mitochondrial disease, severe pyloric stenosis is rarely reported. Most patients develop progressive gastroparesis or generalized gastrointestinal dysmotility rather than a fixed gastric outlet obstruction. The exact mechanism remains unclear, but mitochondrial dysfunction affecting gastrointestinal smooth muscle and the enteric nervous system likely contributes to impaired gastric emptying and progressive pyloric dysfunction. In this patient, longstanding gastroparesis eventually progressed to near-complete pyloric obstruction, resulting in profound malnutrition, complete dependence on TPN, and recurrent catheter-related bloodstream infections.

This case highlights the significant morbidity that can result from advanced gastrointestinal involvement in mitochondrial disease. Despite long-term nutritional support through a gastrostomy tube and TPN, the patient experienced recurrent CLABSI-related septicemias requiring multiple hospitalizations, intensive care admissions, and even mechanical ventilation. Over time, these complications became the greatest threat to both her health and quality of life.

Because conservative management had failed and the complications of long-term TPN were becoming increasingly severe, a laparoscopic hand-assisted pyloromyotomy was performed. Intraoperative EGD allowed accurate localization of the pylorus and safe completion of the myotomy. The patient’s response was immediate. She progressed from clear liquids to solid foods without nausea or vomiting, regained her baseline weight, and was successfully weaned from TPN. Within months, her central venous catheter was removed, and she experienced no further episodes of catheter-related sepsis or hospitalization.

The impact on her quality of life was profound. Nutritional recovery allowed her to regain independence and return to normal daily activities. She resumed her college education with the goal of helping patients facing challenges similar to her own. She described the operation as life-changing, allowing her to share a meal with her family for the first time in 6 years and to live without the constant cycle of hospitalization and recovery that had previously defined her life.

This case demonstrates that laparoscopic pyloromyotomy may be a safe and effective treatment option for patients with mitochondrial disease who develop severe pyloric stenosis, allowing restoration of enteral nutrition, elimination of TPN dependence, and substantial improvement in quality of life.

The impact on her life was transformative. In her own words:

“The emphasis I wish to place on the benefits of my surgery (pyloromyotomy) is that though my family and I did our best to make the most of my life, I had very little else, outside of maintaining my catheters and trying to stay alive. I was in the hospital with a severe infection every couple of months for nearly 6 years. What time I spent at home was spent recovering from hospital trips and then whatever activity I could manage was promptly interrupted by another life-threatening infection requiring hospitalization. After the surgery, I shared my first meal in 6 years with my sister and was able to eat again without vomiting.”

CONCLUSION

To our knowledge, severe pyloric stenosis has been rarely reported in association with mitochondrial disease. In this patient, progressive gastric outlet obstruction resulted in profound malnutrition, long-term TPN dependence, and recurrent life-threatening catheter-related infections. Laparoscopic pyloromyotomy successfully restored enteral nutrition, eliminated the need for TPN, and resulted in a remarkable improvement in both functional status and quality of life. Recognition of this uncommon presentation may help clinicians identify other patients with mitochondrial disease who could benefit from surgical intervention when severe pyloric dysfunction becomes the dominant feature of their illness.
