Introduction

It is by now well known
[
1
,
4
]
that for the Korteweg-de Vries (KdV) equation in the form

\partial_{t}u-3\partial_{x}u^{2}+\partial_{x}^{3}u=0

(1)

with Schwartz-class initial data
u_{0}
, the long time behavior of the solution consists mainly of a dispersive region where
x/t<-\delta
(the Zakharov-Manakov or similarity region) and a soliton region where
x/t>\delta
for some
\delta>0
.

According to
[
1
,
4
]
, the behavior of the KdV solution for large
t
when
x/t
is small is rather complicated, and part of the story involves self-similar solutions of (
1
). As was first worked out carefully in
[
12
]
, these are based on the ansatz
u(t,x)=bt^{p}U(\xi)
, with a similarity variable
\xi=x/(at^{q})
. Substitution into (
1
) results in

bpt^{p-1}U(\xi)-bqt^{p-1}\xi U^{\prime}(\xi)-6\frac{b^{2}}{a}t^{2p-q}U(\xi)U^{\prime}(\xi)+\frac{b}{a^{3}}t^{p-3q}U^{\prime\prime\prime}(\xi)=0.

(2)

Balancing the powers of
t
gives
p=-\frac{2}{3}
and
q=\frac{1}{3}
. Furthermore choosing
a=3^{1/3}
and
b=3^{-2/3}
for convenience yields the profile equation

\xi U^{\prime}(\xi)+2U(\xi)+6U(\xi)U^{\prime}(\xi)-U^{\prime\prime\prime}(\xi)=0.

(3)

To solve this equation, one can introduce the Miura transformation
U(\xi)=V(\xi)^{2}+V^{\prime}(\xi)
, leading to

\left(2V(\xi)+\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\xi}\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\xi}\left(V^{\prime\prime}(\xi)-2V(\xi)^{3}-\xi V(\xi)\right)=0.

(4)

Thus, whenever
V(\xi)
is a solution of the homogeneous Painlevé-II equation in the form

V^{\prime\prime}(\xi)-2V(\xi)^{3}-\xi V(\xi)=0,

(5)

the profile
U(\xi)=V(\xi)^{2}+V^{\prime}(\xi)
satisfies (
3
) and hence determines a self-similar solution
u(t,x)=(3t)^{-2/3}U(x/(3t)^{1/3})
of (
1
).

In the asymptotic regime
t\to+\infty
with
\xi=x/(3t)^{1/3}
bounded (so
x/t=O(t^{-2/3})
), it is known that the solution
u(t,x)
of (
1
) is accurately approximated by a self-similar solution based on different solutions of the Painlevé-II equation (
5
) depending on the value at
\lambda=0
of the reflection coefficient
r(\lambda)
from the direct scattering problem (stationary Schrödinger equation) with potential
u_{0}
. In the generic case one has
r(0)=-1
, and the relevant solution of (
5
) is universally given by the
Hastings-McLeod
solution
V=V_{\mathrm{HM}}(\xi)
. This solution is distinguished by its asymptotic behavior as
\xi\to+\infty
, wherein
V_{\mathrm{HM}}(\xi)\sim-\mathrm{Ai}(\xi)
. In the opposite limit one has instead that
V_{\mathrm{HM}}(\xi)
grows proportional to
\sqrt{-\xi}
as
\xi\to-\infty
. In the non-generic case one has instead a strict inequality
|r(0)|<1
, and the relevant solution of (
5
) is one of the
Ablowitz-Segur
solutions
V=V_{\mathrm{AS}}(\xi)
determined by the asymptotic
V_{\mathrm{AS}}(\xi)\sim r(0)\mathrm{Ai}(\xi)
as
\xi\to+\infty
. By contrast with the Hastings-McLeod solution,
V_{\mathrm{AS}}(\xi)
exhibits oscillatory decay as
\xi\to-\infty
qualitatively similar to that of a multiple of
\mathrm{Ai}(\xi)
.

In the generic case
r(0)=-1
, there is also a somewhat larger region called the
collisionless shock region
, wherein
u(t,x)
is described by a modulated nonlinear wavetrain of diminishing amplitude proportional to
(\ln(t)/t)^{2/3}
. This region can be found between the dispersive region
x<-\delta t
and the self-similar region
|x|=O(t^{-1/3})
, and it occupies scales where
x
is negative and proportional to
t^{1/3}\ln(t)^{2/3}
. In the non-generic case
|r(0)|<1
there is no need for the collisionless shock region and the self-similar and dispersive approximations formally match. The known asymptotic results omit certain thin transitional layers between the four regions, and there is particular interest in resolving the solution of (
1
) between the collisionless shock region and the self-similar region in the generic case.

We now turn to the large-time limit for the Benjamin-Ono (BO) equation instead:

\partial_{t}u+\partial_{x}(u^{2})=\partial_{x}|D|u,\quad\widehat{|D|u}(\diamond)=|\!\diamond\!|\cdot\widehat{u}(\diamond).

(6)

The asymptotic behavior of solutions of this equation in regions corresponding to the dispersive and soliton regions has only recently been settled
[
7
]
. The behavior of the solution in these two regions, again given by
x<-\delta t
and
x>\delta t
for some arbitrarily small fixed
\delta>0
, is sufficient to globally characterize the solution as
t\to+\infty
in the
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
sense. However, the question of universality of asymptotic behavior in the regime
t\to+\infty
with
x/t
small has not been addressed. Based on the KdV problem and other integrable equations, one might expect self-similar solutions to play an important role. To characterize them for BO, we substitute the ansatz
u(t,x)=bt^{p}U(\xi)
with
\xi:=x/(at^{q})
into (
6
) and accounting for the scale invariance of the Hilbert transform one obtains

bpt^{p-1}U(\xi)-bqt^{p-1}\xi U^{\prime}(\xi)+\frac{2b^{2}}{a}t^{2p-q}U(\xi)U^{\prime}(\xi)=\frac{b}{a^{2}}t^{p-2q}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\xi}|D|U(\xi),

(7)

where
|D|
now denotes a Fourier multiplier with respect to the
\xi
-variable.
Matching the powers of
t
now requires
p=-\frac{1}{2}
and
q=\frac{1}{2}
. Then taking
a=2
and
b=1
for convenience, one arrives at the profile equation

-2U(\xi)-2\xi U^{\prime}(\xi)+4U(\xi)U^{\prime}(\xi)=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\xi}|D|U(\xi).

(8)

For profiles
U(\xi)
that tend to zero sufficiently rapidly in one or the other limit
\xi\to\pm\infty
we may integrate with zero integration constant to obtain

-2\xi U(\xi)+2U(\xi)^{2}=|D|U(\xi).

(9)

Therefore, one might expect that in the limit
t\to+\infty
with
x=O(t^{1/2})
, the solution
u(t,x)
of (
6
) for a large class of initial data would be accurately approximated by a self-similar solution
u=t^{-1/2}U(x/(2t^{1/2}))
with
U(\xi)
satisfying (
9
).

As in the case of the Korteweg-de Vries equation, there is a dichotomy in the inverse-spectral theory of the Benjamin-Ono equation: a given initial datum
u_{0}
in a weighted
L^{2}
space is either generic or non-generic. The dichotomy again involves the reflection coefficient
\beta(\lambda)
defined for
\lambda>0
now from a different (nonlocal) Lax equation. In particular the asymptotic behavior of
\beta(\lambda)
as
\lambda\downarrow 0
is of crucial importance. The two alternatives are:

•

u_{0}
is generic if and only if

\beta(\lambda)=\frac{2\pi\mathrm{i}}{\ln(\lambda)}(1+o(1)),\quad\lambda\downarrow 0.

(10)

•

u_{0}
is nongeneric if and only if

\beta(\lambda)=o\left(\frac{1}{\ln(\lambda)}\right),\quad\lambda\downarrow 0.

(11)

The rate of decay is given as
O(\lambda)
in
[
10
]
and
O(\lambda^{\epsilon}\ln(\lambda))
for some
\epsilon\in(0,1)
in
[
14
]
.

In particular, all multisoliton solutions are nongeneric since they are reflectionless potentials for which
\beta(\lambda)
vanishes identically for
\lambda>0
.

1.1
Results

Fix distinct points
p_{1},\dots,p_{N}
in the open upper half-plane and complex constants
c_{1},\dots,c_{N}
with the property that

\mathrm{Re}(c_{1}+\cdots+c_{N})=0.

(12)

In this paper we consider the Benjamin-Ono equation (
6
) with rational initial data
u_{0}(x)=u(0,x)
of the form

u_{0}(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left[\frac{c_{n}}{x-p_{n}}+\frac{c_{n}^{*}}{x-p_{n}^{*}}\right],\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

(13)

The condition (
12
) guarantees that
u_{0}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. Equivalently, the sum of the residues at all of the poles vanishes, so as a function of the complex variable
z
,
u_{0}
has a single-valued antiderivative
L(z)
defined in the neighborhood of
z=\infty
normalized so that
L(\infty)=0
.

We define a quantity
\Delta
that depends on the pole and residue data as follows. First, we say that an index
n=1,\dots,N
is
exceptional
if
\mathrm{i}c_{n}
is a negative integer:
\mathrm{i}c_{n}=-1,-2,-3,\dots
. Otherwise
n
is
non-exceptional
. We will assume without loss of generality that if there exists at least one non-exceptional index, then the poles are ordered so that
N
is non-exceptional. If
n
is a non-exceptional index, we let
C_{n}
denote a closed oriented Jordan curve on the Riemann sphere punctured at
p_{1},\dots,p_{N},p_{1}^{*},\dots,p_{N}^{*}
passing through
z=\infty
and
z=0
(interior/exterior on the left/right) such that
p_{1},\dots,p_{n}
lie in the interior while all other poles of
u_{0}
are in the exterior. Then we write
C_{n}=C_{n}^{-}\sqcup C_{n}^{+}\sqcup\{0,\infty\}
where
C_{n}^{-}
denotes the arc of
C_{n}
originating at
z=\infty
and terminating at
z=0
. By the residue theorem, analytic continuation of
L(z)
along
C_{n}
from
z=\infty
with initial value
L(\infty-)=0
back to
z=\infty
results in a limiting value of
L(\infty+)=2\pi\mathrm{i}(c_{1}+\cdots+c_{n})
. Setting

E_{n}:=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(\infty+)}=\mathrm{e}^{2\pi(c_{1}+\cdots+c_{n})},\quad n=1,\dots,N,\quad\text{for $n$ non-exceptional},

(14)

we see that
\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}
varies from
1
to
E_{n}
as
z
traverses
C_{n}
from
z=\infty
and back again. If instead
n
is exceptional, we let
C_{n}
be an oriented Jordan arc on the punctured Riemann sphere from
z=\infty
to
z=p_{n}
passing through
z=0
, and we again decompose
C_{n}
by cutting it at
z=0
into
C_{n}^{-}
followed by
C_{n}^{+}
. In the exceptional case we require that
C_{n}^{-}\cup\mathbb{R}_{+}
with orientation on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
continued from
C_{n}^{-}
is a contour with
p_{1},\dots,p_{N}
on its left and
p_{1}^{*},\dots,p_{N}^{*}
on its right, and that
C_{n}^{+}
lies in
\mathbb{C}_{+}
after its initial point
z=0
. We set

E_{n}:=0,\quad n=1,\dots,N,\quad\text{for $n$ exceptional}.

(15)

See Figure
3
(center and right panels) and Figure
6
for illustrations of these contours.

With this setup, we define
\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}
differently on each contour
C_{n}
by analytic continuation from
z=\infty
with initial value
\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(\infty)}=1
. Then a matrix
\mathbf{J}
of dimension
N\times N
is defined by

J_{mn}:=\int_{C_{m}^{-}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}-1}{z-p_{n}}\,\mathrm{d}z+\int_{C_{m}^{+}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}-E_{m}}{z-p_{n}}\,\mathrm{d}z+2\pi\mathrm{i}E_{m}\delta_{m\geq n},\quad m,n=1,\dots,N.

(16)

Note that if
m
is exceptional, then the second integral is on a path from
z=0
to
z=p_{m}
and the integrand is
\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}/(z-p_{n})
which is integrable at the terminal endpoint regardless of whether or not
m=n
because
\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}
vanishes to at least first order there. Finally, we define
\Delta
as

\Delta:=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\det(\mathbf{J}\mathop{\longleftarrow}^{n}\mathbf{d}),\quad\mathbf{d}:=(1-E_{1},\dots,1-E_{N})^{\top}

(17)

where the notation
\mathbf{J}\displaystyle\mathop{\longleftarrow}^{n}\mathbf{d}
means the matrix obtained from
\mathbf{J}
by replacing its
n
th column with
\mathbf{d}
. We remark that the choice of
z=0
as the break point for the contours
C_{m}
is quite arbitrary in that different choices of break point amount to adding to each column of
\mathbf{J}
a multiple of the vector
\mathbf{d}
; hence
\Delta
is invariant.

It is known
[
13
]
that the multisoliton solutions of the Benjamin-Ono equation (
6
) are exactly those rational functions of the form (
13
) for which
\mathrm{i}c_{1},\dots,\mathrm{i}c_{N}
are all
positive
integers. In this situation none of the indices are exceptional, and it is straightforward to confirm from (
14
) that
E_{n}=1
for all
n=1,\dots,N
. Therefore
\mathbf{d}=\mathbf{0}
and consequently
\Delta=0
for all such reflectionless and hence nongeneric solutions. On the other hand, in
[
2
]
the solution of (
6
) for
u_{0}(x)=-2/(1+x^{2})
was analyzed for large
t
. In the same work, the reflection coefficient was computed for this datum as

u_{0}(x)=\frac{-2}{1+x^{2}}\implies\beta(\lambda)=\frac{2\pi\mathrm{i}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda}}{\mathrm{Ei}(2\lambda)-\mathrm{i}\pi},\quad\lambda>0.

(18)

Using the series expansion of
\mathrm{Ei}(2\lambda)
for small
\lambda
(see
[
5
, Equation 6.6.1]
) one easily confirms that (
18
) satisfies (
10
), so this is a generic potential. The potential
u_{0}(x)=-2/(1+x^{2})
is also of the form (
13
) with
N=1
,
p_{1}=\mathrm{i}
, and
c_{1}=\mathrm{i}
, so the only index
n=1
is exceptional. Using (
15
) gives
E_{1}=0
and hence the vector
\mathbf{d}
amounts to the scalar
1
. It follows from (
17
) that for this generic potential,
\Delta=1\neq 0
.

More generally we have the following.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose that
u_{0}
is given by (
13
) subject to (
12
). Then
\beta(\lambda)
has the form

\beta(\lambda)=\frac{2\pi\mathrm{i}\varphi_{1}(\lambda)+\lambda\varphi_{2}(\lambda)}{\varphi_{1}(\lambda)\ln(\lambda)+\varphi_{3}(\lambda)},\quad\lambda>0,

(19)

where
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2},\varphi_{3}
are functions analytic at
\lambda=0
and
\varphi_{1}(0)=\Delta
. If
\Delta\neq 0
, then
u_{0}
is a generic initial datum for the Benjamin-Ono equation (
6
).

We give the proof in the Appendix. We do not know whether
\Delta\neq 0
is a necessary condition for genericity of a rational potential of the form (
13
). As a simple generalization of the two cases considered above, we note that if

u_{0}
is a rational potential of the form (
13
) with
N=1
and
c_{1}\in\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
, then
\Delta\neq 0
and hence
u_{0}
is generic
unless

c_{1}=-\mathrm{i}n
for
n=1,2,3,\dots
(in which case
u_{0}
is a
n
-soliton solution with
\beta(\lambda)\equiv 0
). Indeed, either
c_{1}=\mathrm{i}n
for
n=1,2,3,\dots
giving the exceptional case with
E_{1}=0
and hence
\Delta=1-E_{1}=1
, or
c_{1}\in\mathrm{i}(\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Z})
giving the non-exceptional case with
E_{1}=\mathrm{e}^{2\pi c_{1}}\neq 1
, so
\Delta=1-E_{1}\neq 0
.

When one considers rational approximation of initial data, a natural question is whether anything is lost by assuming the condition
\Delta\neq 0
sufficient for genericity. To partially address this question, we have the following result.

Theorem 2
.

Potentials
u_{0}
of the form (
13
) satisfying (
12
) and for which
\Delta\neq 0
are dense in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R})
.

Proof.

Let
v\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R})
and
\epsilon>0
be given. We will find a potential
u_{0}
with the desired properties so that
\|u_{0}-v\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}<\epsilon
.

First note that simple-pole rational functions
u_{0}
of the form (
13
) satisfying the condition (
12
) and having no exceptional indices are dense in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R})
. Therefore, there exists such a function
u_{0}^{\epsilon}
for which
\|u^{\epsilon}_{0}-v\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}<\frac{1}{2}\epsilon
. If
\Delta\neq 0
for
u_{0}^{\epsilon}
, then we are done because
\|u_{0}^{\epsilon}-v\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}<\frac{1}{2}\epsilon<\epsilon
.

Otherwise, we will find another simple-pole rational function
\widetilde{u}_{0}^{\epsilon}
of the same type but for which
\Delta\neq 0
and for which
\|\widetilde{u}_{0}^{\epsilon}-u_{0}^{\epsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}<\frac{1}{2}\epsilon
so that
\|\widetilde{u}_{0}^{\epsilon}-v\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\leq\|\widetilde{u}_{0}^{\epsilon}-u_{0}^{\epsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}+\|u_{0}^{\epsilon}-v\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}<\frac{1}{2}\epsilon+\frac{1}{2}\epsilon=\epsilon
. Fix
N=N(\epsilon)
as the number of poles in the upper half-plane for
u_{0}^{\epsilon}
, and fix also the poles
p_{n}=p_{n}^{\epsilon}
,
n=1,\dots,N
. We will construct
\widetilde{u}_{0}^{\epsilon}
by perturbing the coefficients
c_{n}=c_{n}^{\epsilon}
of
u_{0}^{\epsilon}
consistent with the condition (
12
). Let
\mathbb{H}
denote the complex hyperplane in
\mathbb{C}^{2N}
consisting of vectors
\mathbf{v}
with components satisfying
v_{1}+v_{3}+\cdots+v_{2N-1}=0
. The identification
c_{n}=v_{2n-1}+\mathrm{i}v_{2n}
and
c_{n}^{*}=v_{2n-1}-\mathrm{i}v_{2n}
shows that if
\mathbf{v}
is real, then
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{H}
is the same as the condition (
12
). Now allowing
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{H}
to be complex, we define an entire function
\widetilde{\Delta}:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{C}
by applying the same procedure used to define
\Delta
except that we define the contours
C_{1},\dots,C_{N}
and the quantities
E_{1},\dots,E_{N}
as if all indices
n=1,\dots,N
were non-exceptional. Clearly, if
\mathbf{v}
is real, then
\widetilde{\Delta}=\Delta
for fixed
p_{1},\dots,p_{N}
, unless there is an exceptional index. Since no indices are exceptional for
u_{0}^{\epsilon}
, which is an open condition on the coefficients
c_{1},\dots,c_{N}
, there is a neighborhood
U
of the real part
\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}
of
\mathbb{H}
containing
\mathbf{v}_{0}:=(\mathrm{Re}(c_{1}^{\epsilon}),\mathrm{Im}(c_{1}^{\epsilon}),\dots,\mathrm{Re}(c_{N}^{\epsilon}),\mathrm{Im}(c_{N}^{\epsilon}))^{\top}
on which
\Delta=\widetilde{\Delta}
is real-analytic.

We claim that
\widetilde{\Delta}
does not vanish identically on
\mathbb{H}
. To see this, we choose a point of the form
\mathbf{v}=(0,v_{2},0,\dots,0)^{\top}\in\mathbb{H}
(or
c_{2}=\cdots=c_{N}=0
while
c_{1}=\mathrm{i}v_{2}
and
c_{1}^{*}=-\mathrm{i}v_{2}
), so that
\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}
is the branch of
\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}=[(z-p_{1})/(z-p_{1}^{*})]^{v_{2}}
that tends to
1
as
z\to\infty
backward along each contour
C_{1},\dots,C_{N}
. Then,
E_{1}=E_{2}=\cdots=E_{N}=\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}v_{2}}
is the (common) corresponding limiting value along each contour in the forward direction, so all elements of the vector
\mathbf{d}
are the same:
d_{m}=1-E_{m}=1-\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}v_{2}}
,
m=1,\dots,N
. In the matrix elements of
\mathbf{J}
, the initial contour arcs
C_{m}^{-}
may all be taken to be the same (denoted
C^{-}
), while a residue computation shows that

\int_{C_{m}^{+}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}-\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}v_{2}}}{z-p_{n}}\,\mathrm{d}z=\int_{C_{1}^{+}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}-\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}v_{2}}}{z-p_{n}}\,\mathrm{d}z+2\pi\mathrm{i}(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(p_{n})}-\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}v_{2}})\delta_{1<n\leq m},\quad m\geq 2.

(20)

we see that the first row elements of

\det(\mathbf{J}\mathop{\longleftarrow}^{n}\mathbf{d})\\
=\det\left(\begin{bmatrix}f_{1}+2\pi\mathrm{i}\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}v_{2}}&f_{2}&f_{3}&\cdots&f_{N}\\

\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&0\\

0\\
0\\
\vdots\\

(22)

This determinant obviously vanishes unless

, in which case it is easily evaluated. We deduce that

\widetilde{\Delta}(0,v_{2},0,\dots,0)=\det(\mathbf{J}\displaystyle\mathop{\longleftarrow}^{1}\mathbf{d})=(1-\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}v_{2}})(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{N-1}\prod_{n=2}^{N}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(p_{n})},

(23)

which only vanishes if

.

Returning to the neighborhood

, the real and imaginary parts of

are both real-analytic functions on

is a real-analytic function on

that does not vanish identically, although

for
\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{v}_{0}\in U
. We introduce explicit coordinates
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{2N-1})^{\top}
on
U
with
\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{v}_{0}
corresponding to
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}
by

\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x}):=\mathbf{v}_{0}+\left(-\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}x_{2j},x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{2N-1}\right)^{\top}

(24)

and we write
m=m(\mathbf{x})
. Let
k\geq 1
be the smallest integer such that
\partial_{\mathbf{x}}^{\alpha}m(\mathbf{0})=0
for all multi-indices
\alpha
with
|\alpha|<k
. Note that
k<\infty
because
m(\mathbf{x})
does not vanish identically. Let
P_{k}(\mathbf{x})
denote the (nonvanishing) homogeneous polynomial consisting of the terms of degree
k
in the Taylor expansion of
m(\mathbf{x})
about
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}
. Then there is a unit vector
\mathbf{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N-1}
such that
P_{k}(\mathbf{e})\neq 0
. For
\rho>0
, we then have
m(\rho\mathbf{e})=P_{k}(\rho\mathbf{e})+O(\rho^{k+1})=\rho^{k}P_{k}(\mathbf{e})+O(\rho^{k+1})
as
\rho\downarrow 0
. In particular,
m(\rho\mathbf{e})\neq 0
for
\rho>0
sufficiently small. Since for fixed
N
and
p_{1},\dots,p_{N}
, the rational form (
13
) defines a continuous map from the coefficients
c_{1},\dots,c_{N}
into
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, choosing
\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}=\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{v}(\rho\mathbf{e})
for
\rho>0
sufficiently small gives the coefficients
\widetilde{c}_{n}=\widetilde{v}_{2n-1}+\mathrm{i}\widetilde{v}_{2n}
of a rational function
\widetilde{u}_{0}^{\epsilon}
with the desired properties, and the proof is complete.

The main result of this paper is the following theorem, which (i) shows that for a wide class of rational initial data, the self-similar approximation is not quite correct in that the rate of decay has to be changed from
t^{-1/2}
to
t^{-1/2}/\ln(t)
and (ii) yields a completely explicit expression for the limiting profile
U(\xi)
.

Theorem 3
.

Fix distinct points
p_{1},\dots,p_{N}
in the open upper half-plane and complex constants
c_{1},\dots,c_{N}
satisfying (
12
). Let
u(t,x)
be the solution of the Benjamin-Ono equation (
6
) with rational initial data
u_{0}(x)=u(0,x)
of the form (
13
).
If also
\Delta\neq 0
, then

\lim_{t\to+\infty}t^{1/2}\ln(t)u(t,2t^{1/2}\xi)=U(\xi)

(25)

in the
L^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
sense with respect to
\xi
, where

U(\xi):=F(\xi)+F(\xi)^{*}=2\mathrm{Re}(F(\xi)),

(26)

and, with
L_{+}
denoting a simple contour from
\infty\mathrm{e}^{3\pi\mathrm{i}/4}
to
\infty\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\pi/4}
that passes above the origin,

F(\xi):=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\pi/4}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{L_{+}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(w-\xi)^{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}w}{w}.

(27)

Although it decays with a well-defined universal profile
U(\xi)
and
\xi=x/(2t^{1/2})
is indeed the similarity variable for the Benjamin-Ono equation, the leading term of
u(t,x)
is evidently
not
a self-similar solution of the Benjamin-Ono equation, because it comes with a faster rate of decay,
t^{-1/2}/\ln(t)
rather than the self-similar rate of
t^{-1/2}
.

The proof of Theorem
27
is based on an explicit formula for the solution of the Cauchy problem found by one of the authors in
[
9
]
, combined with an implied expression valid for rational initial data that was obtained in
[
2
]
.

1.2
Properties of
F(\xi)
and
U(\xi)

It is clear from the formula (
27
) that
\xi\mapsto F(\xi)
is an entire function. Its asymptotic behavior as
\xi\to\infty
in
\mathbb{C}
can be analyzed by the method of steepest descent.
One can therefore see that

F(\xi)=\frac{1}{\mathrm{i}\xi}+O(\xi^{-3}),\quad|\xi|\to\infty,\quad 0\leq\arg(\xi)<\pi,

(28)

and accounting for a residue at the origin,

F(\xi)=2\sqrt{\pi}\mathrm{e}^{-3\pi\mathrm{i}/4}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\xi^{2}}+\frac{1}{\mathrm{i}\xi}+O(\xi^{-3}),\quad\xi\to-\infty.

(29)

In particular,
F
is bounded in the closed upper half-plane, and thus is an element of the Hardy-type space
L_{+}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
.
Also, observe that
F
satisfies a first-order linear differential equation:

\begin{split}F^{\prime}(\xi)&=-2\mathrm{i}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\pi/4}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{L_{+}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(w-\xi)^{2}}\frac{\xi-w}{w}\,\mathrm{d}w\\
&=-2\mathrm{i}\xi F(\xi)+\frac{2\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\pi/4}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{L_{+}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(w-\xi)^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}w\\
&=-2\mathrm{i}\xi F(\xi)+2.\end{split}

(30)

The unique solution vanishing in the limit
\xi\to+\infty
(according to (
28
)) is
given explicitly in terms of a convergent improper Fresnel-type integral:

F(\xi)=-2\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\xi^{2}}\int_{\xi}^{+\infty}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}w^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}w\xRightarrow[\eqref{eq:U-F-intro}]{}U(\xi)=-4\int_{\xi}^{+\infty}\cos(w^{2}-\xi^{2})\,\mathrm{d}w.

(31)

Using (
26
), the asymptotics
(
28
)–(
29
) imply that

U(\xi)=\begin{cases}O(\xi^{-3}),&\xi\to+\infty\\
4\sqrt{\pi}\cos(\xi^{2}+3\pi/4)+O(\xi^{-3}),&\xi\to-\infty,\end{cases}

(32)

because the terms proportional to
\xi^{-1}
do not contribute to
U(\xi)
in either limit.
See Figure
1
for a plot of
U(\xi)
.

:
The universal profile function
U(\xi)
.

For a function
f\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, the nonlocal operator
|D|
in the Benjamin-Ono equation is defined by

|D|f(\xi):=-\mathrm{i}\partial_{\xi}(\Pi_{+}f(\xi)-\Pi_{-}f(\xi))=-\mathrm{i}(\Pi_{+}f^{\prime}(\xi)-\Pi_{-}f^{\prime}(\xi)),

(33)

where
\Pi_{\pm}
are the complementary and orthogonal Cauchy-Szegő projectors from
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
onto the Hardy spaces
L^{2}_{\pm}(\mathbb{R})
. We sometimes use the shorthand
\Pi:=\Pi_{+}
. The function
f
can itself be written as a sum of the projections:
f(\xi)=\Pi_{+}f(\xi)+\Pi_{-}f(\xi)
. Now suppose that
g\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})\cap C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and that it is known that there exists a function
g_{+}\in L^{\infty}_{+}(\mathbb{R})\cap C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
such that
g(\xi)=g_{+}(\xi)+g_{+}(\xi)^{*}
. We may define
|D|g
as follows. First, we show that
g_{+}
is uniquely determined from
g
up to a constant. Indeed, if
g_{+}(\xi)+g_{+}(\xi)^{*}=\tilde{g}_{+}(\xi)+\tilde{g}_{+}(\xi)^{*}
with
g_{+},\tilde{g}_{+}\in L^{\infty}_{+}(\mathbb{R})\cap C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, then
\tilde{g}_{+}-g_{+}\in L^{\infty}_{+}(\mathbb{R})\cap C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
is continuous and purely imaginary, and hence by Schwarz reflection extends to an entire function that is bounded. By Liouville’s theorem,
\tilde{g}_{+}-g_{+}
is a purely imaginary constant function. Let
g_{+}
denote any element of the corresponding equivalence class.
Next, we introduce a cutoff function by

\chi_{\epsilon}(\xi):=\frac{1}{1+\mathrm{i}\epsilon\xi},\quad\epsilon>0.

(34)

Note that for each
\epsilon>0
,
\chi_{\epsilon}\in L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{R})
, and that
\chi_{\epsilon}\to 1
in
L^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
as
\epsilon\downarrow 0
. Therefore, for
\epsilon>0
, we have
\chi_{\epsilon}g_{+}\in L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{\infty}_{+}(\mathbb{R})
, and
\chi_{\epsilon}g_{+}\to g_{+}
in
L^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
.
Then, we consider, for each
\epsilon>0
,
g_{\epsilon}
defined by

g_{\epsilon}:=\chi_{\epsilon}g_{+}+\chi_{\epsilon}^{*}g_{+}^{*},

(35)

for which
\Pi_{+}g_{\epsilon}=\chi_{\epsilon}g_{+}
and
\Pi_{-}g_{\epsilon}=\chi_{\epsilon}^{*}g_{+}^{*}
.
Then, for each
\epsilon>0
the action
|D|g_{\epsilon}
makes sense and is given by

\displaystyle|D|g_{\epsilon}(\xi)

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{i}\partial_{\xi}(\chi_{\epsilon}(\xi)g_{+}(\xi)-\chi_{\epsilon}(\xi)^{*}g_{+}(\xi)^{*})

(36)

\displaystyle=-\epsilon\chi_{\epsilon}(\xi)^{2}g_{+}(\xi)-\epsilon\chi_{\epsilon}(\xi)^{*2}g_{+}(\xi)^{*}-\mathrm{i}(\chi_{\epsilon}(\xi)g_{+}^{\prime}(\xi)-\chi_{\epsilon}(\xi)^{*}g_{+}^{\prime}(\xi)^{*}).

Under the conditions in force the right-hand side has a limit in
L^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
as
\epsilon\downarrow 0
, and we define

|D|g(\xi):=\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}|D|g_{\epsilon}(\xi)=-\mathrm{i}(g_{+}^{\prime}(\xi)-g_{+}^{\prime}(\xi)^{*}).

(37)

Note that the definition is independent of the choice of equivalence class representative for
g_{+}
.

Theorem 4
.

The function
U(\xi):=F(\xi)+F(\xi)^{*}
with
F\in L^{\infty}_{+}(\mathbb{R})\cap C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
defined by (
27
) satisfies

-\xi U(\xi)=\frac{1}{2}|D|U(\xi).

(38)

Proof.

Applying (
37
) with
g=U
and
g_{+}=F
gives

\frac{1}{2}|D|U(\xi)=-\mathrm{i}\left(\frac{1}{2}F^{\prime}(\xi)-\frac{1}{2}F^{\prime}(\xi)^{*}\right).

(39)

Then, using the differential equation (
30
),

\frac{1}{2}|D|U(\xi)=-\mathrm{i}\left([-\mathrm{i}\xi F(\xi)+1]-[\mathrm{i}\xi F^{\prime}(\xi)^{*}+1]\right)=-\xi(F(\xi)+F(\xi)^{*})=-\xi U(\xi),

(40)

so the proof is finished.

Theorem
38
shows that the profile
U(\xi)
is a solution of (
38
) rather than the self-similar profile equation (
9
). Noting that the former can be obtained from the latter by omitting the nonlinear term
2U(\xi)^{2}
, we have the heuristic interpretation that the linearization arises because, according to Theorem
27
,
t^{1/2}u(t,2t^{1/2}\xi)
is an increasingly small function of
\xi
(decaying in
L^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}
as
1/\ln(t)
), so
U(\xi)
should be an infinitesimal solution of the nonlinear profile equation (
9
).

Without the hypotheses of Theorem
27
, the asymptotic behavior of
u(t,2t^{1/2}\xi)
can be quite different. Indeed, if
u(t,x)
is a
n
-soliton solution, then
\Delta=0
and since the soliton with speed
v>0
decays as
O((x-vt)^{-2})
we find that
u(t,2t^{1/2}\xi)=O(t^{-2})
as
t\to+\infty
uniformly for bounded
\xi
. This is a substantially faster rate of decay. On the other hand, there are examples showing that if the condition (
12
) is violated, then there can be infinitely many Lax eigenvalues
\lambda_{n}<0
corresponding to infinitely many solitons with speeds
v_{n}>0
accumulating at zero (see, for instance,
[
8
]
). One therefore might expect that the solution should be larger than predicted by Theorem
27
and indeed one can prove that in some examples
t^{1/2}u(t,2t^{1/2}\xi)
converges as
t\to+\infty
to a non-universal nonzero limiting profile
[
6
]
. The decay rate of such a solution is slower by a logarithmic factor than that predicted by Theorem
27
.

That said, a reasonable question is whether an analogue of Theorem
27
holds if only the assumption of rationality is dropped. A generalization of the condition (
12
) would be an assumption that
u_{0}(\diamond)
and
\diamond u_{0}(\diamond)
lie in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, and a natural analogue of the condition
\Delta\neq 0
would simply be the assumption that
u_{0}
is generic. While a proof might still be based on the explicit formula of
[
9
]
, it would necessarily be quite different without the rational assumption.

1.3
Further remarks

The universal profile function
U(\xi)
may be viewed as an analogue for the BO equation of the Miura transform
U_{\mathrm{HM}}(\xi):=V_{\mathrm{HM}}(\xi)^{2}+V_{\mathrm{HM}}^{\prime}(\xi)
of the Hastings-McLeod solution
V_{\mathrm{HM}}(\xi)
of the Painlevé-II equation for the KdV equation. Both are specific functions that yield the asymptotic behavior for large
t
and
x/t
sufficiently small for a wide class of initial data. However, unlike
U_{\mathrm{HM}}(\xi)
,
U(\xi)
is easily expressed in terms of elementary functions. This observation continues a theme that was also observed recently in connection with universality in the small-dispersion limit
[
3
]
.

In the large-time behavior of the KdV equation, Hastings-McLeod universality for
x/t=O(t^{-1/3})
is always accompanied by a collisionless shock region to the left of the central self-similar region. This was originally suggested
[
1
]
by a mismatch of the self-similar asymptotic formula with the leading behavior in the dispersive decay region
x/t<-\delta
. Similar reasoning suggests that there is no analogue of the collisionless shock region for the BO equation.
Indeed, the large-
t
formula for
u
given in Theorem
27
agrees asymptotically with the large-
t
formula given in
[
2
]
in an overlap domain corresponding to large negative
\xi
in (
57
) and small negative
y
in
[
2
, Equation (1.19)]
. Of course this observation does not amount to a proof because the two regions of validity do not obviously overlap.

Proof of Theorem
27
: elementary example

Let us first give a quick proof of Theorem
27
in a special case.
We analyze the solution
u(t,x)
of (
6
) with initial data
u_{0}(x)=-2/(1+x^{2})
(the negative of a soliton profile), which following
[
2
, Section 6]
can be written in the form
u(t,x)=\Pi u(t,x)+\Pi u(t,x)^{*}
with

\Pi u(t,x)=\frac{N(t,x)}{D(t,x)},

(41)

where

N(t,x)=-2\begin{vmatrix}J_{0}(t,x)&I_{0}(t,x)\\
J_{1}(t,x)&I_{1}(t,x)\end{vmatrix},\quad D(t,x)=\begin{vmatrix}K_{0}(t,x)&I_{0}(t,x)\\
K_{1}(t,x)&I_{1}(t,x)\end{vmatrix}

(42)

and for
p=0,1
,

\begin{gathered}I_{p}(t,x):=\int_{C_{p}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(z-x)^{2}/(4t)}\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{z+\mathrm{i}},\quad J_{p}(t,x):=\int_{C_{p}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(z-x)^{2}/(4t)}\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{(z+\mathrm{i})^{2}},\\
K_{p}(t,x):=\int_{C_{p}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(z-x)^{2}/(4t)}\,\mathrm{d}z.\end{gathered}

(43)

Here
C_{0}
and
C_{1}
are contours that can be parametrized by
z=\mathrm{i}+\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\pi/4}\zeta
with
-\infty<\zeta<\infty
and
-\infty<\zeta<0
respectively.

We now study
u(t,x)
with
x=2t^{1/2}\xi
for bounded
\xi
in the limit
t\to+\infty
by studying the integrals
I_{p}
,
J_{p}
, and
K_{p}
for
p=0,1
. As pointed out in
[
2
, Section 6]
,
K_{0}
is a Gaussian integral independent of
x
given by

K_{0}(t,x)=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\pi/4}2\sqrt{\pi t},\quad t>0.

(44)

To find the asymptotic behavior of the other five integrals when
x=2t^{1/2}\xi
for
\xi=O(1)
, we make
the corresponding scaling
z=2t^{1/2}w
,
and hence obtain

\begin{split}I_{p}(t,2t^{1/2}\xi)&=\int_{C_{p}/(2t^{1/2})}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(w-\xi)^{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}w}{w+\mathrm{i}/(2t^{1/2})},\\
J_{p}(t,2t^{1/2}\xi)&=\frac{1}{2t^{1/2}}\int_{C_{p}/(2t^{1/2})}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(w-\xi)^{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}w}{(w+\mathrm{i}/(2t^{1/2}))^{2}},\\
K_{p}(t,2t^{1/2}\xi)&=2t^{1/2}\int_{C_{p}/(2t^{1/2})}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(w-\xi)^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}w.\end{split}

(45)

In the case of
I_{0}(t,2t^{1/2}\xi)
and
J_{0}(t,2t^{1/2}\xi)
, we can use Cauchy’s theorem to replace the integration contour with one that is independent of
t>0
; any simple contour
L_{+}
from
\infty\mathrm{e}^{3\pi\mathrm{i}/4}
to
\infty\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\pi/4}
that passes above the origin will do. Hence one sees that

I_{0}(t,2t^{1/2}\xi)=\int_{L_{+}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(w-\xi)^{2}}{\frac{\mathrm{d}w}{w}}+O(t^{-1/2}),\quad J_{0}(t,2t^{1/2}\xi)=\frac{1}{2t^{1/2}}\int_{L_{+}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(w-\xi)^{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}w}{w^{2}}+O(t^{-1}),

(46)

in the limit
t\to+\infty
, uniformly for
\xi=O(1)
. For the integrals over
C_{1}
, we see that the rescaled contour
C_{1}/(2t^{1/2})
terminates at

. Thus
K_{1}(t,2t^{1/2}\xi)
can be expanded as

K_{1}(t,2t^{1/2}\xi)=2t^{1/2}\int_{\mathrm{e}^{3\pi\mathrm{i}/4}\infty}^{0}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(w-\xi)^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}w+O(1),

(47)

as
t\to+\infty
with
\xi=O(1)
. For
I_{1}(t,2t^{1/2}\xi)
and
J_{1}(t,2t^{1/2}\xi)

L_{1}
be the diagonal half-line terminating at
w=\mathrm{i}
with
\arg(w-\mathrm{i})=3\pi/4
. Then by Cauchy’s theorem we can replace
C_{1}/(2t^{1/2})
with the contour

and therefore

I_{1}(t,2t^{1/2}\xi)=\int_{L_{1}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(w-\xi)^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}w}{w+\mathrm{i}/(2t^{1/2})}+\int_{\mathrm{i}}^{\mathrm{i}/(2t^{1/2})}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(w-\xi)^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}w}{w+\mathrm{i}/(2t^{1/2})}.

(48)

The first term has a limit as
t\to+\infty

O(1)
. The second term can be written as

\int_{\mathrm{i}}^{\mathrm{i}/(2t^{1/2})}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(w-\xi)^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}w}{w+\mathrm{i}/(2t^{1/2})}=\int_{\mathrm{i}}^{\mathrm{i}/(2t^{1/2})}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(w-\xi)^{2}}-\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(-\mathrm{i}/(2t^{1/2})-\xi)^{2}}}{w+\mathrm{i}/(2t^{1/2})}\,\mathrm{d}w\\

I_{1}(t,2t^{1/2}\xi)=-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\xi^{2}}\ln(t)+O(1)

(50)

as

for
\xi=O(1)

J_{1}(t,2t^{1/2}\xi)

2t^{1/2}J_{1}(t,2t^{1/2}\xi)=\int_{L_{1}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(w-\xi)^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}w}{(w+\mathrm{i}/(2t^{1/2}))^{2}}+\int_{\mathrm{i}}^{\mathrm{i}/(2t^{1/2})}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(w-\xi)^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}w}{(w+\mathrm{i}/(2t^{1/2}))^{2}},

(51)

. By the same grouping of the exponential terms in the numerator as in (

\displaystyle\int_{\mathrm{i}}^{\mathrm{i}/(2t^{1/2})}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(w-\xi)^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}w}{(w+\mathrm{i}/(2t^{1/2}))^{2}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\xi^{2}}\int_{\mathrm{i}}^{\mathrm{i}/(2t^{1/2})}\frac{\mathrm{d}w}{(w+\mathrm{i}/(2t^{1/2}))^{2}}+O(\ln(t))

\displaystyle=t^{1/2}\mathrm{i}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\xi^{2}}+O(\ln(t))

\displaystyle N(t,2t^{1/2}\xi)

\displaystyle=-2\left[J_{0}(t,2t^{1/2}\xi)I_{1}(t,2t^{1/2}\xi)-J_{1}(t,2t^{1/2}\xi)I_{0}(t,2t^{1/2}\xi)\right]

\Pi u(t,2t^{1/2}\xi)=\frac{F(\xi)}{t^{1/2}\ln(t)}+O\left(\frac{1}{t^{1/2}\ln(t)^{2}}\right),

3.2.1
First column of

According to (

A_{j1}(t,2\xi\sqrt{t})=\int_{C_{j-1}}u_{0}(z)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}h(z;t,2\xi\sqrt{t})}\,\mathrm{d}z=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\xi^{2}}\int_{C_{j-1}}u_{0}(z)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}z^{2}/(4t)}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\xi z/\sqrt{t}}\,\mathrm{d}z.

Since

and is independent of

and for each

z\mapsto\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}z^{2}/(4t)}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\xi z/\sqrt{t}}

A_{j1}(t,2\xi\sqrt{t})=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\xi^{2}}\int_{C_{j-1}}u_{0}(z)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}\,\mathrm{d}z+o(1),\quad t\to+\infty.

(64)

We can improve the estimate by using the fact that on
C_{j-1}
we have both
\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}z^{2}/(4t)}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\xi z/\sqrt{t}}-1=O(1)
and
\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}z^{2}/(4t)}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\xi z/\sqrt{t}}-1=O(z/\sqrt{t})
, both holding uniformly for bounded
\xi
. Since also
u_{0}(z)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}=O(1/(1+|z|^{2}))
on
C_{j-1}
,

\left|\int_{C_{j-1}}u_{0}(z)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}z^{2}/(4t)}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\xi z/\sqrt{t}}-1\right]\,\mathrm{d}z\right|\\
\begin{aligned} &\lesssim\int_{C_{j-1}}\frac{\min\{1,|z|/\sqrt{t}\}}{1+|z|^{2}}\,|\mathrm{d}z|\\
&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}\int_{C_{j-1}\cap\{|z|<\sqrt{t}\}}\frac{|z|\,|\mathrm{d}z|}{1+|z|^{2}}+\int_{C_{j-1}\cap\{|z|\geq\sqrt{t}\}}\frac{|\mathrm{d}z|}{1+|z|^{2}}.\end{aligned}

(65)

Clearly, the second term is
O(1/\sqrt{t})
while the first term is
O(\ln(t)/\sqrt{t})
. Therefore the error term
o(1)
in (
64
) can be replaced with the sharper
O(\ln(t)/\sqrt{t})
as
t\to+\infty
, which is uniform for bounded
\xi
.

Finally, we use

to explicitly evaluate the leading term. If
j=1
, or if

and

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\xi^{2}}\left(E_{j-1}-1\right)+O\left(\frac{\ln(t)}{\sqrt{t}}\right),\quad t\to+\infty,\quad j=1,\dots,N+1.

) is an entire function of
\lambda
, with power series expansion

F_{mn}(\lambda):=\int_{C_{m}\cap\{|z|\leq R\}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\lambda z}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}}{z-p_{n}}\,\mathrm{d}z=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\mathrm{i}\lambda)^{k}}{k!}\int_{C_{m}\cap\{|z|\leq R\}}\frac{z^{k}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}}{z-p_{n}}\,\mathrm{d}z.

(108)

For the second term, since
|z|\geq R
, we may use the convergent Laurent series of
\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}/(z-p_{n})
in the integrand:

\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}}{z-p_{n}}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu_{k}^{(n)}}{z^{k}},\quad|z|\geq R,\quad\mu_{1}^{(n)}=1.

(109)

In particular, this implies that the function
g_{n}(\lambda)
defined by

g_{n}(\lambda):=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{|z|=R_{1}>R}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\lambda z}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}}{z-p_{n}}\,\mathrm{d}z=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu_{k}^{(n)}}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{|z|=R_{1}>R}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\lambda z}}{z^{k}}\,\mathrm{d}z=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu_{k}^{(n)}}{(k-1)!}(-\mathrm{i}\lambda)^{k-1}

(110)

is an entire function with
g_{n}(0)=1
. Using (
109
) in the integrand,

\int_{-\mathrm{i}R}^{-\mathrm{i}\infty}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\lambda z}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}}{z-p_{n}}\,\mathrm{d}z=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mu_{k}^{(n)}\int_{-\mathrm{i}R}^{-\mathrm{i}\infty}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\lambda z}\,\mathrm{d}z}{z^{k}}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mu_{k}^{(n)}(\mathrm{i}\lambda)^{k-1}\int_{R\lambda}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-w}\,\mathrm{d}w}{w^{k}}.

(111)

The integral over
w
can be expanded as follows:

\displaystyle\int_{R\lambda}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-w}\,\mathrm{d}w}{w^{k}}

\displaystyle=\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-w}\,\mathrm{d}w}{w^{k}}+\int_{R\lambda}^{1}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-w}\,\mathrm{d}w}{w^{k}}

(112)

\displaystyle=\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-w}\,\mathrm{d}w}{w^{k}}+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{j!}\int_{R\lambda}^{1}w^{j-k}\,\mathrm{d}w

\displaystyle=\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-w}\,\mathrm{d}w}{w^{k}}+\mathop{\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}}_{j\neq k-1}\frac{(-1)^{j}(1-(R\lambda)^{j-k+1})}{j!(j-k+1)}-\frac{(-1)^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}\ln(R\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{k}}{(k-1)!}\ln(\lambda)+c_{k}(R)-\frac{1}{(R\lambda)^{k-1}}\mathop{\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}}_{j\neq k-1}\frac{(-R\lambda)^{j}}{j!(j-k+1)},

where

c_{k}(R):=\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-w}\,\mathrm{d}w}{w^{k}}+\mathop{\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}}_{j\neq k-1}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{j!(j-k+1)}+\frac{(-1)^{k}\ln(R)}{(k-1)!}.

(113)

We note in passing that

c_{1}(R)=-\ln(R)-\gamma

(114)

where
\gamma
is the Euler constant.
Consequently,

\int_{-\mathrm{i}R}^{-\mathrm{i}\infty}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\lambda z}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}}{z-p_{n}}\,\mathrm{d}z=-\ln(\lambda)g_{n}(\lambda)\\
+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mu_{k}^{(n)}\left[c_{k}(R)(\mathrm{i}\lambda)^{k-1}-\left(\frac{\mathrm{i}}{R}\right)^{k-1}\mathop{\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}}_{j\neq k-1}\frac{(-R\lambda)^{j}}{j!(j-k+1)}\right],

(115)

where
g_{n}(\lambda)
is the entire function defined by (
110
). The sum on the second line can be shown to be analytic for
|\lambda|<R^{-1}
, so combining with the entire function
F_{mn}(\lambda)
defined by (
108
) and using (
107
) gives

M_{mn}(\lambda)=H_{mn}(\lambda)-(E_{m}-1)g_{n}(\lambda)\ln(\lambda),

(116)

where
H_{mn}(\lambda)
is analytic for
|\lambda|<1/R
.

Since the second term in (
116
) is an element of a rank-
1
matrix, in particular we have deduced the analytic structure of
\det(\mathbf{M}(\lambda))
near
\lambda=0
:

\det(\mathbf{M}(\lambda))=\ln(\lambda)\sum_{n=1}^{N}g_{n}(\lambda)\det(\mathbf{H}(\lambda)\mathop{\longleftarrow}^{n}\mathbf{d})+\det(\mathbf{H}(\lambda)),

(117)

where
\mathbf{d}:=(1-E_{1},\dots,1-E_{N})^{\top}
as defined in (
17
) and
\displaystyle\mathbf{H}(\lambda)\mathop{\longleftarrow}^{n}\mathbf{d}
is the matrix
\mathbf{H}(\lambda)
with its
n
th column replaced by
\mathbf{d}
.

Now we turn our attention to the integrals in the first row and column of the numerator determinant
\mathcal{N}(\lambda)
defined in (
106
). Recall that if there exists at least one non-exceptional index, we have chosen to order the poles so that
N
is non-exceptional. In this case, we can relate the integrals in the first row of
\mathcal{N}(\lambda)
, columns
2,\dots,N+1
, to those in the last row by

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\lambda z}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}}{z-p_{n}}\,\mathrm{d}z-\int_{C_{N}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\lambda z}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}}{z-p_{n}}\,\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=-E_{N}\oint_{|z|=R_{1}>R}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\lambda z}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}}{z-p_{n}}\,\mathrm{d}z

(118)

\displaystyle=-2\pi\mathrm{i}E_{N}g_{n}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=-2\pi\mathrm{i}\mathrm{e}^{2\pi(c_{1}+\cdots+c_{N})}g_{n}(\lambda),\quad\text{for $N$ non-exceptional},

for
n=1,\dots,N
, where
g_{n}(\lambda)
is the entire function defined by (
110
) and we used (
14
) to evaluate
E_{N}
. On the other hand, if all indices
m=1,\dots,N
are exceptional, there is no need to subtract the last row, because the path can be closed in the lower half-plane and there are no singularities in the upper half-plane. Therefore, for
n=1,\dots,N
,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\lambda z}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}}{z-p_{n}}\,\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=-2\pi\mathrm{i}g_{n}(\lambda)

(119)

\displaystyle=-2\pi\mathrm{i}\mathrm{e}^{2\pi(c_{1}+\cdots+c_{N})}g_{n}(\lambda),\quad\text{for all indices exceptional},

where the last line follows by the definition of an exceptional index.

Now we work on the integrals in the first column of the numerator determinant
\mathcal{N}(\lambda)
in (
106
), rows
2,\dots,N+1
. Proceeding as above, for
m=1,\dots,N
we have

\int_{C_{m}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\lambda z}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}u_{0}(z)\,\mathrm{d}z=f_{m}(\lambda)+(E_{m}-1)\int_{-\mathrm{i}R}^{-\mathrm{i}\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\lambda z}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}u_{0}(z)\,\mathrm{d}z

(120)

where
f_{m}(\lambda)
is the entire function

f_{m}(\lambda):=\int_{C_{m}\cap\{|z|\leq R\}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\lambda z}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}u_{0}(z)\,\mathrm{d}z=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\mathrm{i}\lambda)^{k}}{k!}\int_{C_{m}\cap\{|z|\leq R\}}z^{k}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}u_{0}(z)\,\mathrm{d}z.

(121)

Since
L^{\prime}(z)=u_{0}(z)
,
f_{m}(0)=\mathrm{i}(E_{m}-1)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(-\mathrm{i}R)}
. This holds whether or not
m
is an exceptional index; in the exceptional case that
C_{m}
terminates at
z=p_{m}
, we recall that
E_{m}=0
and observe that
\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}
vanishes to at least first order at
z=p_{m}
. For the second term on the right-hand side in (
120
), we use the Laurent expansion of
\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}u_{0}(z)
convergent for
|z|\geq R
:

\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}u_{0}(z)=\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\frac{\nu_{k}}{z^{k}},\quad|z|\geq R.

(122)

Note that since the left-hand side is the derivative of
\mathrm{i}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}
and
L(z)
is single valued in the domain
|z|\geq R
with
L(z)\to 0
as
z\to\infty
,

\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}=1+\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{i}\nu_{k}}{(k-1)z^{k-1}},\quad|z|\geq R.

(123)

Then
g(\lambda)
defined by

\displaystyle g(\lambda):=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{|z|=R_{1}>R}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\lambda z}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}u_{0}(z)\,\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\frac{\nu_{k}}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{|z|=R_{1}>R}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\lambda z}}{z^{k}}\,\mathrm{d}z

(124)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\frac{\nu_{k}}{(k-1)!}(-\mathrm{i}\lambda)^{k-1}

is entire, and
g(0)=0
.
Furthermore, integrating the Laurent series (
122
) term-by-term as was done in (
111
) we get

\int_{-\mathrm{i}R}^{-\mathrm{i}\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\lambda z}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}u_{0}(z)\,\mathrm{d}z\\
\begin{aligned} &=\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\nu_{k}(\mathrm{i}\lambda)^{k-1}\int_{R\lambda}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-w}\,\mathrm{d}w}{w^{k}}\\
&=-\ln(\lambda)g(\lambda)+\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\nu_{k}\left[c_{k}(R)(\mathrm{i}\lambda)^{k-1}-\left(\frac{\mathrm{i}}{R}\right)^{k-1}\mathop{\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}}_{j\neq k-1}\frac{(-R\lambda)^{j}}{j!(j-k+1)}\right],\end{aligned}

(125)

where again the sum on the last line is analytic for
|\lambda|<R^{-1}
, taking the value

\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\frac{\nu_{k}}{k-1}\left(\frac{\mathrm{i}}{R}\right)^{k-1}=-\mathrm{i}(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(-\mathrm{i}R)}-1)

(126)

at
\lambda=0
, where we used (
123
). Combining the results shows that

\int_{C_{m}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\lambda z}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}u_{0}(z)\,\mathrm{d}z=h_{m}(z)-(E_{m}-1)\ln(\lambda)g(\lambda),\quad m=1,\dots,N,

(127)

where
h_{m}(\lambda)
is analytic for
|\lambda|<R^{-1}
with

h_{m}(0)=\mathrm{i}(E_{m}-1)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(-\mathrm{i}R)}-\mathrm{i}(E_{m}-1)(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(-\mathrm{i}R)}-1)=\mathrm{i}(E_{m}-1).

(128)

In other words, if
\mathbf{h}(\lambda):=(h_{1}(\lambda),\dots,h_{N}(\lambda))^{\top}
, then
\mathbf{h}(0)=-\mathrm{i}\cdot\mathbf{d}
, where
\mathbf{d}:=(1-E_{1},\dots,1-E_{N})^{\top}
as defined in the introduction.

It remains to study the integral in the upper left-hand corner of the numerator determinant. If there exists a non-exceptional index, so by our convention
N
is non-exceptional, then

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\lambda z}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}u_{0}(z)\,\mathrm{d}z-\int_{C_{N}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\lambda z}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}u_{0}(z)\,\mathrm{d}z\\
\begin{aligned} &=-E_{N}\oint_{|z|=R_{1}>R}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\lambda z}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}u_{0}(z)\,\mathrm{d}z\\
&=-2\pi\mathrm{i}E_{N}g(\lambda)\\
&=-2\pi\mathrm{i}\mathrm{e}^{2\pi(c_{1}+\cdots+c_{N})}g(\lambda),\quad\text{for $N$ non-exceptional}.\end{aligned}

(129)

Otherwise, without subtracting the lower-left-hand corner element, we close the contour in the lower half-plane and get

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\lambda z}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}u_{0}(z)\,\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=-2\pi\mathrm{i}g(\lambda)

(130)

\displaystyle=-2\pi\mathrm{i}\mathrm{e}^{2\pi(c_{1}+\cdots+c_{N})}g(\lambda),\quad\text{for all indices exceptional}.

Now we use these results to analyze the numerator determinant
\mathcal{N}(\lambda)
defined in (
106
).
If
N
is non-exceptional, we subtract the last row from the first using (
118
) and (
129
); otherwise all indices are exceptional and we simply use (
119
) and (
130
). Either way, the result is the same:

\mathcal{N}(\lambda)=-2\pi\mathrm{i}\mathrm{e}^{2\pi(c_{1}+\cdots+c_{N})}\begin{vmatrix}g(\lambda)&g_{1}(\lambda)&\cdots&g_{N}(\lambda)\\
\int_{C_{1}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\lambda z}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}u_{0}(z)\,\mathrm{d}z&&&&\\
\vdots&&\mathbf{M}(\lambda)&&\\
\int_{C_{N}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\lambda z}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}u_{0}(z)\,\mathrm{d}z&&&&\end{vmatrix}.

(131)

Then, using (
116
) and (
127
) we express
\mathcal{N}(\lambda)
in block form using the column vectors
\mathbf{h}(\lambda)
,
\mathbf{g}(\lambda):=(g_{1}(\lambda),\dots,g_{N}(\lambda))^{\top}
,
\mathbf{d}
, and the matrix
\mathbf{H}(\lambda)
:

\mathcal{N}(\lambda)=-2\pi\mathrm{i}\mathrm{e}^{2\pi(c_{1}+\cdots+c_{N})}\begin{vmatrix}g(\lambda)&\mathbf{g}(\lambda)^{\top}\\
\mathbf{h}(\lambda)+\ln(\lambda)g(\lambda)\mathbf{d}&\mathbf{H}(\lambda)+\ln(\lambda)\mathbf{d}\mathbf{g}(\lambda)^{\top}\end{vmatrix}.

(132)

Adding
(E_{m-1}-1)\ln(\lambda)=-d_{m-1}\ln(\lambda)
times the first row to row
m
,
m=2,\dots,N+1
removes all logarithms, yielding

\mathcal{N}(\lambda)=-2\pi\mathrm{i}\mathrm{e}^{2\pi(c_{1}+\cdots+c_{N})}\begin{vmatrix}g(\lambda)&\mathbf{g}(\lambda)^{\top}\\
\mathbf{h}(\lambda)&\mathbf{H}(\lambda)\end{vmatrix}.

(133)

Therefore,
\mathcal{N}(\lambda)
is obviously an analytic function of
\lambda
at
\lambda=0
, and expanding along the first row gives

\mathcal{N}(\lambda)=-2\pi\mathrm{i}\mathrm{e}^{2\pi(c_{1}+\cdots+c_{N})}\left(g(\lambda)\det(\mathbf{H}(\lambda))-\sum_{n=1}^{N}g_{n}(\lambda)\det(\mathbf{H}(\lambda)\mathop{\longleftarrow}^{n}\mathbf{h}(\lambda))\right).

(134)

Using (
117
) and (
134
) in (
105
) gives

\beta(\lambda)=2\pi\frac{\displaystyle g(\lambda)\det(\mathbf{H}(\lambda))-\sum_{n=1}^{N}g_{n}(\lambda)\det(\mathbf{H}(\lambda)\mathop{\longleftarrow}^{n}\mathbf{h}(\lambda))}{\displaystyle\ln(\lambda)\sum_{n=1}^{N}g_{n}(\lambda)\det(\mathbf{H}(\lambda)\mathop{\longleftarrow}^{n}\mathbf{d})+\det(\mathbf{H}(\lambda))}.

(135)

Using
\mathbf{h}(0)=-\mathrm{i}\cdot\mathbf{d}
, this can be written in the claimed form (
19
) where
\varphi_{1}(\lambda)
,
\varphi_{2}(\lambda)
, and
\varphi_{3}(\lambda)
given by

\varphi_{1}(\lambda):=\sum_{n=1}^{N}g_{n}(\lambda)\det(\mathbf{H}(\lambda)\mathop{\longleftarrow}^{n}\mathbf{d}),

(136)

\varphi_{2}(\lambda):=2\pi\frac{g(\lambda)}{\lambda}\det(\mathbf{H}(\lambda))-2\pi\sum_{n=1}^{N}g_{n}(\lambda)\det\left(\mathbf{H}(\lambda)\mathop{\longleftarrow}^{n}\left[\frac{\mathbf{h}(\lambda)-\mathbf{h}(0)}{\lambda}\right]\right)

(137)

and

\varphi_{3}(\lambda):=\det(\mathbf{H}(\lambda)).

(138)

are all analytic at
\lambda=0
, recalling that the entire function
g(\lambda)
satisfies
g(0)=0
.

Next we show that
\varphi_{1}(0)=\Delta
. Since each function
g_{n}(\lambda)
satisfies
g_{n}(0)=1
, we have

\varphi_{1}(0)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\det(\mathbf{H}(0)\mathop{\longleftarrow}^{n}\mathbf{d}).

(139)

We simplify
H_{mn}(0)
as follows:

H_{mn}(0)=F_{mn}(0)+(E_{m}-1)\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mu_{k}^{(n)}\left.\left[c_{k}(R)(\mathrm{i}\lambda)^{k-1}-\left(\frac{\mathrm{i}}{R}\right)^{k-1}\mathop{\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}}_{j\neq k-1}\frac{(-R\lambda)^{j}}{j!(j-k+1)}\right]\right|_{\lambda=0}.

(140)

Using (
109
) with

and (

\displaystyle H_{mn}(0)

\displaystyle=\int_{C_{m}\cap\{|z|\leq R\}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}\,\mathrm{d}z}{z-p_{n}}+(E_{m}-1)\left(\mu_{1}^{(n)}c_{1}(R)+\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\frac{\mu_{k}^{(n)}}{k-1}\left(\frac{\mathrm{i}}{R}\right)^{k-1}\right)

(141)

\displaystyle=\int_{C_{m}\cap\{|z|\leq R\}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}\,\mathrm{d}z}{z-p_{n}}+(E_{m}-1)\left(\int^{-\mathrm{i}\infty}_{-\mathrm{i}R}\left[\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}}{z-p_{n}}-\frac{1}{z}\right]\,\mathrm{d}z-\ln(R)-\gamma\right)

In the second integral on the right-hand side,
\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}
refers to the branch that is single-valued for
|z|>R
and that tends to
1
as
z\to\infty
, while in the first integral
\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}
refers to the analytic continuation of the same function into
|z|\leq R
along
C_{m}
. Now

\int_{-\mathrm{i}R}^{-\mathrm{i}\infty}\left[\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}}{z-p_{n}}-\frac{1}{z}\right]\,\mathrm{d}z=\int_{-\mathrm{i}R}^{-\mathrm{i}\infty}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}-1}{z-p_{n}}\,\mathrm{d}z+\log\left(\frac{-\mathrm{i}R}{-\mathrm{i}R-p_{n}}\right).

(142)

We recall from the introduction the splitting at
z=0
of the contour
C_{m}
into an an initial arc
C_{m}^{-}
from
z=\infty
to
z=0
along which
\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}\to 1
as
z\to\infty
and a terminal arc
C_{m}^{+}
. If the index
m
is non-exceptional, then
C_{m}^{+}
goes from
z=0
to

along which
\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}\to E_{m}
as

and

. In both cases the results can therefore be combined as

\displaystyle H_{mn}(0)

\displaystyle=\int_{C_{m}^{-}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}-1}{z-p_{n}}\,\mathrm{d}z+\int_{C_{m}^{+}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}-E_{m}}{z-p_{n}}\,\mathrm{d}z

(143)

\displaystyle+\int_{C_{m}^{-}\cap\{|z|\leq R\}}\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{z-p_{n}}+E_{m}\int_{C_{m}^{+}\cap\{|z|\leq R\}}\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{z-p_{n}}

\displaystyle+(E_{m}-1)\left(\log\left(\frac{-\mathrm{i}R}{-\mathrm{i}R-p_{n}}\right)-\ln(R)-\gamma\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{C_{m}^{-}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}-1}{z-p_{n}}\,\mathrm{d}z+\int_{C_{m}^{+}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}-E_{m}}{z-p_{n}}\,\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle+2\pi\mathrm{i}E_{m}\delta_{m\geq n}

\displaystyle+(E_{m}-1)\left(\log\left(\frac{-\mathrm{i}R}{-\mathrm{i}R-p_{n}}\right)-\ln(R)-\gamma-\int_{C_{m}^{-}\cap\{|z|\leq R\}}\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{z-p_{n}}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{C_{m}^{-}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}-1}{z-p_{n}}\,\mathrm{d}z+\int_{C_{m}^{+}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}L(z)}-E_{m}}{z-p_{n}}\,\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle+2\pi\mathrm{i}E_{m}\delta_{m\geq n}+(E_{m}-1)\left(-\frac{\mathrm{i}\pi}{2}-\gamma-\log(-p_{n})\right).

Since the last term is a contribution to each column of
\mathbf{H}(0)
that is a multiple of
\mathbf{d}
, it makes no contribution to
\displaystyle\det(\mathbf{H}(0)\mathop{\longleftarrow}^{n}\mathbf{d})
for any
n

\varphi_{1}(0)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\det(\mathbf{J}\mathop{\longleftarrow}^{n}\mathbf{d}),

(144)

where
\mathbf{J}
is the
N\times N
matrix whose elements are defined by (
16

17

\varphi_{1}(0)=\Delta
as desired.

Finally, we observe that according to the representation (
19

\Delta=\varphi_{1}(0)\neq 0\implies\beta(\lambda)=\frac{2\pi\mathrm{i}}{\ln(\lambda)}+O\left(\frac{1}{\ln(\lambda)^{2}}\right),\quad\lambda\downarrow 0.

(145)

This proves the genericity of the initial datum
u_{0}
and completes the proof of Theorem
