Executive Summary of CRADA Work:
 The research conducted under this CRADA contributed to understanding and advancement of
 hybrid electro-bioreactors. Specifically, this research advanced power-to-gas electro-
 bioreactors, the goal of which is to convert electricity and CO2 into methane, which is the
 major chemical component of natural gas. This can be viewed as an energy storage
 technology—electrical energy is converted into chemical energy, which can be stored,
 distributed, and utilized using existing infrastructure. The research specifically involved
 understanding how the performance of the electro-bioreactor was affected by reactor
 engineering and design considerations, with a focus on understanding behavior as the reactor
 size is increased. Based on the performance metrics achieved under this CRADA and analysis
 performed, the cost of methane produced this way using manure digester biogas is
 comparable to methane produced by capturing CO2 from the atmosphere and converting it
 using conventional thermochemical technologies. Directions for future research include:
 improving fluid flow distribution and engineering in electrochemical stacks, improving energy
 efficiency of the reactor, and reducing the capital cost of electro-bioreactor components.

Summary of Research Results:
 Technical Objectives:
 The technical objectives of this project were to (1) develop a small flow electrobioreactor with
 high energy efficiency, (2) optimize cathode geometry at the small scale, (3) increase the
 electrobioreactor scale while maintaining energy efficiency, (4) develop strategies for
 operation with intermittent electricity, (5) evaluate the reactor and microbial tolerance to
 biogas contaminants, (6) develop thermal management strategies, (7) optimize cathode
 geometries for module-scale, (8) develop a stack of electrobioreactor cells, and (9) perform
 technoeconomic analysis and examine the sensitivities to key inputs.

 Actual Accomplishments:
 Task 1: Develop flow electrobioreactor with high energy efficiency
 An integral component of the flow electrobioreactor is the cathode electrode that, in-situ,
 generates H2 for the microbial methanogenesis reaction. LLNL successfully designed, 3D
 printed, and post-processed structures for use as electrodes in the flow electrobioreactor.
 These electrodes were 3D printed out of a commercial plastic resin that was subjected to heat
 treatment to convert to conductive carbon, Figure 1, or directly 3D printed using selective
 laser sintering of stainless-steel particles, Figure 2. While the plastic resin can create
 electrodes with very fine features, shrinkage and warping during the carbonization process
 present challenges for achieving good contact between the electrode and the current
 collector, and the fragility of the carbon resulted in carbon particles being released during
 testing. Therefore, for the rest of the project, we used stainless-steel 3D electrodes.

 printed with either 500 µm (top) or 1000 µm (bottom) wall thicknesses and the specific surface
 areas labeled. b) A plastic (left) and carbon (right) electrode, after heat treatment, highlighting
 the change in size induced during the carbonization process. These electrodes are fabricated
 with a disposable tab to make an electrical connection during the electroplating process. c)
 Cathode flow plate machined using graphite for holding 3D electrodes.

 Figure 2. (a) 3D-printed stainless-steel electrodes with varying geometries, unit cell sizes, and
 total areas from a single print at LLNL. (b) SEM image of the surface of the electrode.

 The 3D electrodes were electroplated with a nickel-molybdenum (NiMo) catalyst layer and
 tested within a flow electrobioreactor (Figure 3) for their electrochemical performance under
 abiotic conditions (0.3 M sodium sulfate solution). In general, electrodes with higher surface
 area per electrode volume (as determined by the CAD file) perform better, measured by a
 higher current at constant cell voltage. These electrodes also have lower electrode-surface-
 area-normalized current density, which would result in a smaller pH gradient at the electrode
 surface; we have previously demonstrated that this is beneficial for the metabolic activity of
 the biocatalyst. In addition, we measured the fluid flow pressure drop through the electrodes
 as a proxy for pumping power requirement, and measured the impact of fluid flow rate on
 electrochemical performance. At low flow rate, the cell voltage is higher, indicating that mass
 transfer or bubble formation limit performance, whereas higher flow rate facilitates bubble
 dissipation.

 Figure 3. Assemblage of the electrobioreactor. (a) Iridium anode side, (b) membrane, (c)
 carbonized 3D-printed gyroid cathode, and (d) assembled cell connected to electrolyte tanks
 and circulation system. The carbon cathode (black) is situated in the graphite cathode flow
 plate with Teflon gaskets and tape (white) to hold it in position during testing. The anode and
 cathode chambers are connected to independent electrolyte reservoirs on the left side and
 the flow is controlled via peristaltic pump.

 Electro-biomethanogensis experiments were performed at Electrochaea to assess the
 conversion efficiency of CO2 and electrons into CH4. These experiments were typically
 operated in galvanostatic mode, measuring the cell voltage, to control the ratio of H 2 to CO2
 available to the biocatalyst and ensure that there was a 4:1 molar ratio to match the reaction
 stoichiometry. In these experiments, the system operated stably around 3 V (Figure 4).

 The measured conversion of CO2 and electrons to hydrogen and methane is shown in Table 1.
 The outgas from the catholyte was collected in gas bags for prolonged periods (1–2 h) and then

 Task 2: Optimize cathode geometry
 Based on experiments performed with the electrodes developed in Task 1, primarily in an
 electrobioreactor with a membrane surface area of 5 cm 2, we down selected a range of
 electrode porosities/unit cell sizes and geometries for evaluation at the 25-cm 2 scale.
 Electrodes with diamond, gyroid, and octet truss geometries with varying unit cell size and
 electrode thickness/height were printed at the 25-cm2 scale in a single print run at LLNL, with
 duplicates to ensure that we had electrodes that could be tested at both LLNL and
 Electrochaea (Figure 5). This resulted in 27 unique designs and 78 electrodes in total.

 Figure 5. Photograph of 25 cm2 3D-printed electrodes.

 Task 3: Increase flow reactor scale while maintaining energy efficiency
 Due to the large surface area of the 25 cm2 [membrane surface area] electrodes, we needed to
 develop a method for electroplating the electrodes with NiMo that was suitable at the bench-
 scale with available lab equipment. We examined three methods: electroplating in a flow cell
 using a circulating solution of the NiMo precursor, electroplating in a stirred bath, and
 electroplating in a sonicated bath. While the flow cell method resulted in a visually uniform
 coating and slightly better abiotic electrochemical performance, to reduce operational
 complexity we opted to use the sonication deposition method, which had sufficient
 performance and reduced the need for consumables like membranes in the flow cell and time
 for assembling and leak testing.

 At this scale, we conducted studies into the impact of temperature, electrolyte flow rate, NiMo

 catalyst layer thickness, and anode material, summarized as follows:

 Temperature: as expected from theory and prior literature, higher temperatures were found to
 enhance the reaction kinetics of both hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) and oxygen evolution
 reaction (OER), ion transport kinetics, and electrical conductivity, resulting in improved overall
 cell current at constant cell voltage. We observed a roughly linear correlation between
 temperature and current, with an approximate increase of 105 mA for every 10 °C rise in
 temperature. The electrobioreactor operates around 60 °C, which is optimal for
 Electrochaea’s biocatalyst.

 Flow rate: for the 25-cm2 electrode and electrobioreactor flow cell, only marginal increase in
 current was observed when increasing the electrolyte flow rate from 200–500 mL/min. This
 suggests that within this range, the reactor is not mass-transport limited.

 NiMo catalyst thickness: our current procedure theoretically electrodeposits approximately
 10–20 mg NiMo/cm2. We also tripled the amount of charge passed during electrodeposition,
 theoretically resulting in more catalyst being deposited. Though the initial performance
 increased, after 6 hours of operation, there was no observable increase in performance for the
 thicker NiMo layer, suggesting that the stainless-steel electrode surface is sufficiently covered
 by catalyst using our typical procedure.

 Anode material: our baseline anode material is an Ir-sputtered Ti foam electrode. To
 demonstrate that the overall performance of the reactor is not limited by the performance of
 the anode, we used a highly active, but expensive, Ir-based cathode paired with a NiMo-
 deposited carbon felt. This combination of cathode materials had better conductivity
 compared to our best-performing 3D cathode and achieved higher current at both room
 temperature and elevated temperature. Additionally, similar tests performed in the presence
 of the biocatalyst did not show any improvement, suggesting that the anode did not limit the
 overall performance of the reactor.

 Task 4: Develop strategies for stabilizing performance with intermittent electricity
 To simulate variable electricity generation and intermittency, such as a typical solar electricity
 generation profile, Electrochaea developed a startup/shutdown procedure suitable for
 protecting microbial health. In these experiments, the CO2 flow rate was held constant and
 the conversion of H2 to CH4 was used to track performance.

 The cell was operated galvanostatically at 1.0 A to reach approximately 70% conversion. The
 current was then reduced to either 0.75 A or 0.50 A, held for some time, and then returned to
 1.0 A, and the change in performance was noted (Figure 6). At the reduced current, the H2
 conversion increased slightly, only to return to initial values after the current was increased
 again. The increase in conversion can be ascribed to the lower amount of hydrogen available
 to the biocatalyst, resulting in a larger fraction of the hydrogen being converted. Importantly,
 the performance of the biocatalyst is not compromised by variable electricity.

  A shut-down procedure was developed based on Electrochaea’s shut-down procedure for a
 standalone bioreactor, first by initiating cooling and then stopping CO 2 flow and electrical
 current (Figure 7). After an overnight 15-hour period of non-operation, the procedure was
 reversed, operating the cell potentiostatically while the electrolyte was still cool and then
 switching to galvanostatic operation above 55 °C. After a few hours of operation, the microbial
 electrolysis cell regained >80% of the initial conversion, which is significantly faster than initial
 startup involving inoculation of the cell (around 100 hours). This suggests that if renewable
 electricity is not available for some time, the system can be safely shut down and operation
 resumed later.

 Task 5: Evaluate reactor and microbial tolerance to biogas contaminants
 The impact of sulfide contaminant on the abiotic reactor was evaluated through introduction
 of Na2S into the electrolyte. Aqueous Na2S exists as an equilibrium mixture of S2–, HS–, and
 dissolved H2S and adequately simulates the relevant species that may interfere with the
 biocatalyst and electrocatalyst. The addition of Na 2S resulted in a decrease in current at 2.5 V
 by approximately 550 mA, likely due to the simultaneous increase in pH.

 The performance of the biocatalyst and full electrobioreactor were evaluated in the presence
 of potential contaminants present in biogenic CO₂ sources, including ethanol originating from
 fermentation, silicone, ammonium, and sulfide from biogas that can be used as CO 2 source.
 Ethanol (4 mL / Lbiocat) and silicone (1 mL / Lbiocat) contaminants did not have a long-term effect
 on the conversion performance of the biocatalyst, and it remained around 97%. Similarly, high
 concentration of ammonia (335 mM) did not reduce performance. Electrochaea previously
 demonstrated no adverse effect of the hydrogen sulfide on the biocatalyst.

 Task 6: Develop thermal management strategy
 A strategy to remove heat from the reactor was not needed for our experimental setup; heating
 was provided at the electrolyte reservoir to maintain 65 °C operation in the reactor. We expect
 that, if necessary, a similar strategy would be appropriate to remove heat (e.g. via a cooling
 loop and/or heat fins) at larger scale, as integrating thermal management into an external
 reservoir is significantly easier and more cost-effective than integrating into the reactor.

 Based on an average cell performance of 2.5 V and 1 A and a thermoneutral voltage for H 2
 production of 1.48 V, the excess electrical power applied is 1.02 W, equivalent to ~49 kJ/g CH 4.
 The enthalpy of reaction for CO2 methanation at 65 °C (producing liquid water) is ~16 kJ/g CH 4.
 Therefore, approximately 65 kJ/g CH4 needs to be removed from the reactor.

 For our current setup with ~300 mL catholyte volume, circulating through the reactor roughly
 once per minute, adiabatic operation would result in an average temperature rise of
 approximately 0.001 °C/s, which is very likely far outweighed by heat lost to the lab
 environment. For a hypothetical scaled up 1000 L electrobioreactor at 1 MW input, assuming
 similar voltaic efficiency, adiabatic operation would result in an average temperature rise of
 approximately 0.13 °C/s; isothermal operation leads to the generation of 0.54 MW of heat that
 needs to be removed from the system and that could be for other purposes, e.g. building
 heating.

 The two-cell stack was tested abiotically using 3D printed cathodes and Ir-Ti anodes, with a Ti

 electrode to examine the impact of the stack on energy efficiency (Figure 13). At the same

 CH4eq/kWh), whereas the stack reactor had a per-cell voltage of 2.58 V (28.9 g CH 4eq/kWh).

 The stack was operated with the biocatalyst inside the catholyte, Figure 14. The voltage shows
 sharp increase at the beginning of the operation, but afterwards it stabilizes and shows a slight
 decrease. After only 10 h, the CO2-to-CH4 conversion reaches about 60%, but afterwards it

 conversion of CO2 to CH4 at an energy efficiency of approximately 30 g CH 4eq/kWh. As high
 conversion has already been demonstrated for a single-cell reactor, we believe that high CO 2
 conversion could be achieved with proper stack engineering.

 Finally, the pump direction was reversed at various points during the experiment, as indicated

 Task 9: Refine technoeconomic and sensitivity analysis
 Process flow modeling and technoeconomic analysis was performed to calculate the cost of
 producing pipeline-quality CH4 from biogas (60% CH4, 40% CO2) from a covered lagoon
 digester. The process flow diagram is shown in Figure 15. Methane production costs were
 evaluated for a baseline case scenario: capital costs were estimated from the literature and
 by Electrochaea and experimentally achieved performance parameters from this project were
 used. The initial techno-economic analysis (TEA) yields a methane production cost of
 $50/MMBtu, which is on the high end of zero-emissions methane production costs and is
 comparable to the cost of capturing CO2 from the atmosphere and converting it to methane
 via conventional thermochemical processes or using it to offset the emissions from
 conventional natural gas production (see Aui et al., Cell Reports Sustainability 2025). This cost
 is significantly higher than the typical Henry Hub natural gas spot price in the United States
 (roughly $3/MMBtu) and is still three times the typical European Union natural gas prices
 (roughly $16/MMBtu). The primary operational expenditure (OPEX) components include: (1)
 electricity consumption for system operation, compression, and dewatering, (2) nutrient
 supply, (3) CO₂ feedstock from biogas, and (4) electrolyzer stack replacement at end of life.
