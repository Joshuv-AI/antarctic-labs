A. Public Executive Summary
 Agricultural croplands are the largest anthropogenic source of nitrous oxide (N2O), the
third most important greenhouse gas. Emissions are characterized by “hot spots” and “hot
moments”, meaning emissions are highly heterogeneous in space and time. Emissions are driven
by nitrification and denitrification processes which depend upon complex factors such as soil
characteristics (type, compaction, pH, moisture, topography), management practices (fertilizer
type and application, tillage, crop type, field history, irrigation), biogeochemistry (organic carbon,
microbial composition), and meteorology (precipitation, temperature, and wind). For these
reasons, quantifying cropland emissions by either measurements or modeling is extremely
challenging with large uncertainties. While the bioeconomy has much promise for replacing
conventional fuel sources (e.g. sustainable aviation fuel) with large benefits to rural economies
and domestic fuel supplies, assessing the full climatic impact of these changes is poorly known
due to the challenges of measuring cropland emissions of N2O.
 In this project, we developed and deployed a new optical imaging technique to quantify
the spatiotemporal patterns of N2O emissions in real-time in the field. In essence, we developed a
“cat scan” imaging system of an agricultural field through casting an “optical web” of eye-safe
laser beams across a field. The “NitroNet” system is composed of two “towers” where mid-infrared
lasers scan across a field. The laser beams are reflected back to the towers through inexpensive
mid-infrared retroreflectors that line the perimeter of a field on simple stakes, fence posts, or utility
poles. Mid-infrared spectroscopy allows for the highest sensitivity and selectivity for N2O
detection compared to other spectral regions because the fundamental (strongest) absorption bands
are in this spectral window. Absorption spectroscopy is used to determine path-integrated
concentrations. The path-integrated measurements from the reflected laser scans of each reflector
results in a complex pattern of overlapping path-integrated concentrations, allowing for a
tomographic reconstruction of the concentrations across the field. When coupled to meteorological
data, spatially-resolved maps of emissions from the field are obtained at a spatial resolution only
limited by the spacing of the retroreflectors on the field’s perimeter.
 The NitroNet system was deployed in a soybean field in eastern Nebraska in summer 2024,
and a controlled release of N2O was conducted within the 1.2 acre field demonstration with a
modest number of (16) reflector positions (~ 4 per side on average). NitroNet scanned across the
field continuously in an automated manner every 37 minutes with no re-alignment needed after
the initial setup. NitroNet located the emission source ±2 m of the actual release location (confined
to within 0.26% of the measured area) and measured emissions that were ±15% of the actual
release rate. Because NitroNet’s spatial resolution scales only by the number of reflectors and
returning light on the detector (demonstrated to at least 400 m), the specific results are equivalent
to an 80 acre field (570 x 570 m) with a spatial resolution of 0.20 acre (±16 m). These metrics are
much improved over DOE SMARTFARM goals of a spatial resolution of 1 acre (±36 m) in an 80
acre field and 20% accuracy. More retroreflectors aligning the perimeter would allow for even
higher spatial resolution, and higher power lasers are now commercially available could extend
the distances to 1 km for even larger fields. Hotspot locations of N2O in the field were also
confirmed with the development and deployment of a drone-based N2O sensor. Flux detection
limits were below to well below the SMARTFARM target of 10 g N2O-N ha-1 day.

 The technologies developed in this grant include the following: 1) NitroNet system for
spatiotemporal quantification of N2O in croplands; 2) inexpensive retroreflectors for mid-infrared
sensing; 3) open-path, drone-based N2O sensor for mapping N2O in croplands, other agricultural
(feedlot), and industrial (e.g. wastewater) sectors; and 4) center pivot irrigation methods for
quantifying N2O emissions. Patent applications, and associated invention disclosures, have been
filed on these intellectual properties. Finally, a startup company, EcoFluxNet™ Inc.
(www.ecofluxnet.com), has been formed from the NitroNet program to quantify N2O and CH4
emission reductions that farmers implement. EcoFluxNet has exclusive license agreements to the
IP from Princeton University from this award. EcoFluxNet Inc. is a Delaware C-Corporation.

 EcoFluxNet Inc. is also a joint partner in the non-profit venture VeriAg™
(https://veriag.com) with Global Sustainability Developers™ (GSD)
(https://www.gsdpartnership.com). VeriAg™ will pay farmers to make sustainable choices
through emissions reduction technologies (verified through EcoFluxNet Inc.) and soil
health/carbon/water improvements (verified through GSD Inc.). VeriAg will use the VeriFood™
(https://dev.ethosconnected.com/solutions/verifood) platform to trace these sustainable agriculture
products from farmers to end users. VeriAg, with traceability and verifiable carbon credits, will
sell carbon credits at a premium to third parties with proceeds distributed to the farmer,
EcoFluxNet and GSD. Recent “greenwashing” lawsuits cast doubt on carbon neutrality pledges of
large companies. The market is seeking defensible carbon credits that are traceable and verifiable
with rigorous data. Currently, very few farmers will risk the higher costs associated with
implementing sustainable practices with only the “hope” that higher commodity prices will cover
these costs. In contrast, VeriAg, using the EFN (air) and GSD (soil) measurements, will pay
farmers to implement sustainable practices, improve rural economies through new income streams
to farmers, enable the bioeconomy, and add robustness to the floundering carbon credit markets.
Overall, this is a win for rural America through new injection of capital, the bioeconomy through
data-verified practices, and the environment through lower emissions of greenhouse gases.

D. Project activities
D1. Summary
 The NitroNet optical web system provides both spatial and temporal emissions resolution
and is nominally only limited by the spacing and number of the reflectors. The system provides a
“gold standard” for measuring emissions of interest, particularly N2O in agricultural settings.
Agricultural N2O emissions contribute to 56% of anthropogenic budget of N2O, the third most
important anthropogenic greenhouse gas with rapidly increasing emissions and a long atmospheric
lifetime of ~ 110 years (Tian et al., 2024). Hot spots and hot moments can be effectively measured
by NitroNet unlike any other technique, providing a single platform with which to optimize
agriculture practices for lowering emissions and verifying emission compliance. We developed
and deployed the mid-infrared, optical tomographic imaging system to quantify the locations and
magnitudes of N2O emissions in an agricultural field. A controlled release field test in a 1.2 acre
soybean field yielded a location accuracy of ±2 m and an emission rate ±15% of the release rate
using 16 reflector positions. By scaling the result to an 80 acre field, this is comparable to 0.2 acre
resolution of N2O emissions, significantly better than the program-specific goals of 1 acre
resolution at 80 acres at 20% uncertainty in emissions.

D2. Overall description
 The NitroNet system consists of low cost plastic mid-IR retroreflectors setup along the
perimeter of the field and two tower based optical subsystems each consisting of a quantum
cascade laser at 4.57 µm (QCL), mercury cadmium telluride (MCT) detectors, various mirrors,
and associated electronics, among other components. Each subsystem is gimbal mounted can
rotate horizontally and vertically. The QCL aims at any one retroreflector briefly before going to
the next reflector. By quickly measuring at every single retroreflector, a single “scan” is completed.
Both towers do this independently and simultaneously. After setup, scans can be done
autonomously and repeatedly. All the data from any single scan are then combined and use a
newly-developed algorithm to produce a tomographic image of the field. The data itself—
independent of any model—is able to identify a hotspot and measure concentrations over the field.
This computed output is then combined with Monte Carlo-based inversion models and
meteorological data to estimate to identify hotspot precise locations and hotspot emissions.
Furthermore, the integrated flux over the whole field can also determined.
 Besides the QCL, a visible laser is used to help with the alignment. During initial system
deployment and setup, the position of each retroreflector is saved and recorded by a joystick-
controlled gimbal, which moves the output beam to different retroreflectors. During
measurements, the system automatically returns to these locations while completing scans. The
resolution of the “pixels” measured on the tomographic reconstruction is determined by the density
of the perimeter retroreflectors. More retroreflectors allow for more individual path-integrated
measurements, and consequently when combined together, a finer tomographic reconstruction at
higher spatial resolution. Ultimately, the maximum resolution is limited by the diffraction limit of
the laser, which sets the maximum density at which retroreflectors can be deployed (~1 m at 500
m).

D3. Field Deployment
 The system was deployed on a 1.2 acre soybean field operated by the University of
Nebraska, Lincoln. The soybeans were treated with a combination of biochar and biosolids with
rain fed irrigation. 32 retroreflectors were setup on tripods at 16 locations (two at each location,
one retroreflector pointing in the direction of tower one, and another pointing towards the direction
of tower two) (Figure 1). The reflectors were setup evenly spaced, approximately 14 m apart. The
two towers were placed at opposite ends of the field (approximately 100 meters apart). A two-
minute averaging time was used at each retroreflector (the amount of time the tower pointed at the
retroreflector before moving on to the next one). With the gimbal travel and reset times, this
yielded approximately 37 minutes per complete field scan.

Fig. 1: Deployment configuration of the NitroNet system showing the 16 retroreflector positions
(two units at each position, 32 total) and the two towers in the soybean field (~ 1.2 acre plot).
The reflectors were evenly spaced (~14 m apart), and the two towers with rotating gimbals were
at the two opposite ends of the field (~100 m apart).

 For testing purposes, a controlled release experiment was performed. A tank of N2O was
placed in the field at a known location at a fixed flow rate (10.6 L min-1 flow rate, ~ 0.3 g N2O s-1).
Because the testing was being done in real world conditions in an agricultural field, a large hotspot
in a known location had to be artificially created to demonstrate the functionality of the system.
Otherwise, any hotspots that appeared could not be distinguished between real emissions features

or mathematical artifacts, nor would they be repeatable or verifiable. The flow rate was selected

D4. Tomographic Reconstruction and Inversion Modeling
 The positions of the retroreflectors and the towers, as well as the path-integrated
measurements, were used to compute a tomographic map (Fig. 3). This tomographic map does not
require wind information or any meteorological parameters, however, for context, wind direction
is shown in Fig. 3. The locations of the retroreflectors (shown as blue squares) and the locations
of the two towers (shown as red circles) were input as latitude and longitude coordinates (as
recorded by a global positioning system (GPS)). A clear enhancement in measured concentrations
was visible near the center on the right side of the field. This enhancement is exactly what would
be expected from a large point source with the associated wind direction (i.e. downwind of the
tank, shown by the black star). The GPS coordinates of the tank were not used in any way for the
tomographic map and were only plotted afterwards for visualization purposes. While the
tomographic reconstruction provides inferred concentrations for each pixel on the field (based
upon the combined path-integrated measurements), it does not provide a flux rate or exact locations
for the emission sources. Background concentrations of N2O were around 342 ppbv at the field
site, which is consistent with the derived tomographic reconstruction.

 An algorithm consisting of two separate iterative Monte Carlo simulations and an image
pattern matching comparison was developed and used to identify hot spot location first and then
calculate the emission rate. As with the tomographic reconstruction, GPS coordinates of the
retroreflectors, towers, and path-integrated measurements were used as input. In addition, the wind
 14

speed and direction during the scan time and a qualitative assessment of atmospheric stability
(which can be inferred from the time of day and how cloudy it is) also were used as algorithm
inputs. The first round of Monte Carlo simulated the most likely positions of the hotspot, producing
simulated path-integrated measurements, and then generated a tomographic reconstruction from
the synthetic data. Each reconstruction was compared to the original tomographic reconstruction
(Fig. 3) from measured data. This process was repeated many times until a “most-likely” location
was found after 1000 iterations. If, based off the tomographic reconstruction, multiple hotspots
were predicted, then the Monte Carlo simulation iteratively added multiple hotspot locations from
which to produce total emissions.
 Once the likely location of the hotspot(s) was identified, a second Monte Carlo simulation
was conducted where the location from the first simulation was used and different emission rates
were assessed. A tomographic reconstruction with each emission rate was compared to the
tomographic reconstruction from the measurement. A Gaussian plume model was used to simulate
the emission at any particular hotspot. This final inversion model (shown in Fig. 4) was used to
identify the hot spot location and the emission (flux) rate at that hot spot. The total simulated fluxes
over the field were compared to measurement data for added verification. Running over n=1000
Monte Carlo iterations (for location and emission), the code identified the hotspot location to
within 2 meters, the emission rate to within 15%, and 97% of the total N2O integrated flux from
the measurement space was accounted for.
 For reference, an emission rate of 0.3 g N2O s-1 distributed uniformally over the ~ 100x50
m measurement area (3.0 ha) yields an integrated flux of 8640 g N2O ha-1 day-1. Based upon past
field deployments and tests, a precision of at least 3000:1 at 1 Hz (0.1 ppbv) is obtained for a 1 s
integration time in the field. The tower scanning stopped at each reflector position for two minutes,
suggesting the likely precision was even better with some improvement due to averaging time (if
at least some of the noise is white in character as expected). Furthermore, because integrated
pathlengths are reconstructed from 32 measurements, this is another source of improved detection
(as opposed to a single pathlength measurement). In short, it is reasonable to assume the 3000:1
precision in measurement translates directly into a detection limit for the retrieved flux. By using
these assumptions, the nominal flux detection limit would be 2.9 g N2O ha-1 day-1 which is better
than the SMARTFARM performance metrics of 10 g N2O-N ha-1 day-1. Alternatively, if we had
taken the measurement over an 80 acre grid with the same setup, the equivalent flux over this area
would have been 131 g N2O ha-1 day-1. Given the similar performance of our measurement at these
distances, one would only need a 13:1 signal-to-noise ratio in the tomographic reconstruction to
achieve these goals which is well within the capabilities demonstrated.
 Ultimately, more field studies will have to be done under a wide range of conditions to
determine the factors that quantify the detection limit. We suspect time of measurement around a
field and measurement precision to be the controlling factors. At long distances (somewhere over
500-100 m), we expect the measurement precision to degrade simply due to less light reflecting
back to the detector. Then again, the white noise aspects would be ameliorated at least in part by
the dwell time spent on each reflector. It is unclear how sources of noise on a specific pathlengths
would translate into the full tomographic reconstruction (e.g. if one reflector was not tilted in the
right direction so less light is returned), and weighted reconstruction algorithms would need to be
tested. Practically speaking, this may make the case for using more reflectors than necessary in the
 15

field (again, in this case, we only used 4 reflector positions each “side” of the 50x100 m test plot).
The inexpensive costs of the reflectors and ease of setup would allow for some “noisy” or blocked
signals (e.g. bird droppings on reflectors, winds moving them, etc.).

D5. Outlook
 Currently available measuring tools are insufficient for identifying hot spots and hot
moments. The NitroNet platform produced tomographic maps of the agricultural field. These
tomographic maps provide hotspot detection, as directly observed through concentration
enhancements, through long range open-path mid-IR laser spectroscopy. The optical web system
is a paradigm shift for the measurement community by providing continuous measurements which
can autonomously detect different local emission sources. The new approach provides the temporal
benefits of approaches such as eddy covariance, while addressing micrometeorological and
geographic constraints, and the spatial benefits of localized measurement approaches such as soil
chambers and UAVs, all while covering the whole field simultaneously as opposed to a small
region.

 While the full system has been tested on acre-sized plot here with 100-meter path lengths,
the laser measurements and retroreflectors have also been successfully tested at 400 path length
differences in prior milestone work in this award. In past work, QCLs have been used with corner
cube arrays at kilometer scale distances (Michel et al., 2015). Indeed, as shown in Fig. 5, the
inexpensive (~ $10), 4 mm thick retroreflectors developed in this project were compared to
commercial-off-the-shelf (COTS) corner cube array reflectors ($1000s). Our retroreflectors
showed up to 60% higher reflectivities and a broader acceptance angle than COTS corner cube
reflectors (likely due to less beam shift, no gaps between corner cubes as is the case in conventional
devices). Therefore, the NitroNet system is capable of measuring over 100s of acres and spatial
resolution is really limited to the number and mounting of reflectors on fence posts, stakes, or
utility poles.

Retroreflector alignment currently is done manually during setup, which is time-consuming.
Algorithms can be implemented without the use of any new hardware for autonomous alignment
in the future. These could be run for micro-adjustments during measurement to also ensure that if
 18

any reflector moves due to be struck or shifting from wind, etc., alignment could again be rapidly
restored. Furthermore, any motor backlash from the gimbal could be more easily accounted for.

 Implementation of the optical web approach at scale will provide insights into how farming
processes can be optimized and constrain models. New, unknown biological processes can be
discovered to further refine our understanding of nitrogen cycling. Even without deploying the
optical web at every single farm, the optical web can determine constraints and corrects to existing
models and more common measurement approaches or models. The retroreflectors used here are
broadband and can work at many wavelengths. By changing the wavelength of the laser, the optical
web can be readily adapted to other gases of interest, such as ammonia or methane, and deployed
in locations like wastewater treatment plants and industrial facilities. Finally, we note that the
retroreflectors are robust in that they survived several months in field conditions (wind, rain, hail,
dust, insects) without apparent loss of signal. We also tested several retroreflectors on a
stratospheric NASA research aircraft at airspeeds of mach 0.8, altitudes of 17 km, temperatures to
-70ºC, and impaction by hydrometeors and dust at aircraft speeds. No apparent loss in reflectivity
was also observed after 40 flight hours, showing the robustness of the new retroreflectors.

 In summary, the entire NitroNet system – from laser scanning to inexpensive reflectors in
the mid-infrared – is ready for further commercialization efforts. Ongoing efforts beyond
SMARTFARM include additional deployments in corn, soybean, and lettuce fields and improving
optomechanical aspects for ease of field use. One could envision NitroNet as simply a rotating
mid-IR laser beam that picks up “reflections” whenever the backscattered signal reaches some
threshold where no real alignment or setup is required and reflectors are placed randomly
throughout a field. An algorithm could “remember” the altitude and azimuth of consistently high
signals and automatically scan those positions as needed. Such improvements would lower labor
costs in deployment and drive the cost structure for integration down even more. EcoFluxNet Inc.
is actively pursuing such improvements in the next generations of NitroNet.
