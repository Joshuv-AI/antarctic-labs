HyperLens: Quantifying Cognitive Effort in LLMs with Fine-grained Confidence Trajectory
Chengda Lu, Xiaoyu Fan, Wei Xu

Abstract
While Large Language Models (LLMs) achieve strong performance across diverse tasks, their inference dynamics remain poorly understood because of the limited resolution of existing analysis tools. In this work, we identify an intrinsic magnification mechanism in transformer architectures: deeper layers inherently magnify the small changes of layer-wise confidence, providing a fine-grained confidence trajectory. Building on this insight, we introduce HyperLens , a high-resolution probe designed to trace confidence trajectories and quantify the cognitive effort during inference. Across 8 8 LLMs and 8 8 datasets, HyperLens reveals a consistent divergence in confidence trajectories that separates complex from simple tasks. We abstract this pattern into a quantitative cognitive effort metric. Our analysis reveals a fundamental principle: complex tasks consistently require higher cognitive effort. Finally, we provide a mechanistic diagnosis of a common side effect of standard Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT): it can reduce cognitive effort and consequently degrade performance on in-domain tasks.

Introduction

While Large Language Models (LLMs) exhibit remarkable capabilities, understanding their
inference dynamics
, i.e., how internal states evolve, is crucial for real-world robustness. A prevailing paradigm interpreting the dynamics involves monitoring the
evolution of confidence
via layer-wise projections
(
25
;
30
;
38
;
3
)
. This signal is proven effective for various tasks like hallucination and jailbreak detection
(
7
;
23
)
, and early-exit strategies
(
41
;
5
)
.

Figure 1
:
The confidence trajectory of Qwen2.5-7B with different focal depths on easy (CoNaLa
(
45
)
) versus hard (APPS
(
17
)
) coding tasks. The confidence of three figures is derived with (1) zero focal depth, which is equivalent to Logit Lens; (2) shallow focal depth with one single layer; and (3) sufficient focal depth with five layers.

Intuitively, the trajectory of confidence should reflect the model’s
cognitive effort
. Specifically, for trivial tasks (e.g., knowledge retrieval) where the answer is readily apparent, the internal confidence should converge swiftly, whereas complex tasks (e.g., hard math problems) necessitate a longer low-confidence “struggle” phase to reach the solution.
However, observations using current probing methods fail to validate this intuition, frequently producing indistinguishable trajectories regardless of task difficulty.

We attribute this failure to the limited
resolution
of existing probes, which limits the utility to only the most obvious anomalies (e.g., sudden spikes / collapses or stable changes).
Existing probes primarily rely on static projections
(
1
;
16
;
27
)
, most notably the Logit Lens
(
1
)
, which decodes hidden states directly through the last unembedding layer.
These methods rely on a strong assumption that intermediate representations are linearly aligned with the output space. Unfortunately, because of the
anisotropy

(
14
;
34
)
and
representation drift

(
15
;
36
)
, these methods function as a
myopic observer
: without proper focal correction, they fail to resolve latent signals effectively.
To address this limitation, another line of work, like Tuned Lens
(
3
)
and others
(
11
;
28
;
16
)
introduces an external
learned adapter
to correct the projection misalignment. However, they inevitably act as a
biased distortion filter
: the external training introduces systematic distortions to the original signal
(
19
;
37
)
.

Unlike the current myopic or biased tools, we identify an inherent self-magnifying mechanism within transformer layers (Section
3
). We prove that instead of directly decoding the hidden state, propagating it through a sequence of future layers before projection magnifies the derived confidence. We term the number of the future layers as
focal depth
.

Based on this foundation, we propose a high-resolution probe,
HyperLens
, which reveals a critical phenomenon aligning with our intuition:
deepening the focal depth magnifies confidence, with the magnitude constrained by the intrinsic difficulty of the task
.
As Figure
1
illustrates, under zero focal depth (i.e., equivalent to Logit Lens), the confidence trajectories for easy and hard tasks are indistinguishable across all layers. Increasing the focal depth by 1 introduces a global magnification, while the trajectories remain entangled.
Notably, when we increase the focal depth to a sufficient level, the confidence trajectories for easy and hard tasks are clearly separated. The easy task exhibits a more rapid increase, whereas the hard task remains at a low-confidence state for much longer.
This divergence indicates that sufficient magnification makes the model’s internal cognitive effort observable.
To quantify this, we introduce the
refinement area (
\Omega
)
, defined as the area
above
the magnified confidence trajectory, serving as a precise measure of cognitive effort required to generate the final output. Intuitively, harder tasks require more cognitive effort to generate the final output, therefore have a higher
\Omega
.
For instance, the
\Omega
of Qwen2.5-7B increases significantly from
1.78
(Easy) to
3.05
(Hard) in code tasks (Figure
1
(3)).

Comprehensive evaluations across
8
models and
8
datasets (see Section
5
) confirm that task difficulty consistently correlates with higher
\Omega
.
Beyond robust measurement,
HyperLens
provides novel insights into the side effects of
Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)
(Section
6
). We uncover a “blind confidence” phenomenon: simple SFT can cause abnormal decreases in
\Omega
on in-domain tasks, thereby harming the performance. This suggests that the model learns to shortcut the low-confidence “struggle” phase, jumping to obtaining the output without sufficient consideration.

In summary, the contributions include:

(1) We propose
HyperLens
, a high-resolution probe that visualizes and quantifies the model’s cognitive effort. Utilizing
HyperLens
, we clearly separate the confidence trajectories of easy and hard tasks, revealing that complex tasks necessitate a longer low-confidence phase.

(2) We formally characterize the transformer’s inherent self-magnifying mechanism, proving that increasing focal depth strictly magnifies confidence, thereby ensuring a high-resolution view.

(3) By applying
HyperLens
, we uncover that simple SFT causes a “blind confidence” problem by cutting the cognitive effort when facing in-domain tasks, providing an interpretability explanation for performance degradation often observed in SFT models.

Related Works

Inference dynamics interpretation
focuses on theoretically understanding the evolution of internal states. Intuitively, the inference includes two phases: (1) transform the input into a high-dimensional latent space; and (2) map the latent state to the vocabulary space to generate the next-token
(
26
;
35
;
13
)

Moving beyond this high-level decomposition, a growing body of research focuses on a more thorough analysis. For instance,
31
prove that the residual stream effectively performs Bayesian updates across layers from the geometric perspective.
33
model the inference process as a kernel renormalization flow, formalizing the evolution of predictor statistics in a thermodynamic perspective.

Probing methods.
To decode these internal dynamics, researchers propose a set of
probing methods
to observe the internal state. According to the dependency on extra parameters, these probing methods can be classified into static and parametric probing.
Static probing methods include the widely adopted Logit Lens
(
25
)
and its variants like
(
44
)
, which uses cross-query semantic patching and a cosine-based representational lens to interpret the implicit reasoning phenomena. In this work, we focus on the static probing methods and mainly compare our method with the widely-adopted Logit Lens.

Parametric probing methods can be classified into two categories: one class, like Linear Probing
(
1
)
uses extra training to explore concept-specific features
(
11
;
28
)
. This class is typically task-driven, identifying specific features in the latent space that correspond to human-interpretable concepts.
Another class like Tuned Lens
(
3
)
and Patchscopes
(
16
)
, on the other hand, utilizes extra models as an adaptor to correct the inherent flaws of static probings. However, these methods inevitably introduce systematic distortions as stated in
(
19
;
37
)
.

Applications.
Based on the above interpretation of inference dynamics, researchers propose diverse real-world applications that generally fall into two categories: (1) detecting abnormal behaviors and (2) improving the models’ performance.
First, by monitoring the internal states, researchers can effectively detect safety risks such as hallucinations and jailbreak attempts
(
23
;
6
;
4
)
. For instance,
43
propose a component-level decoding, i.e., knowledge circuits, and use it to analyze and interpret language model behaviors such as hallucinations and in-context learning.
Also, the insights obtained through interpretation help to optimize model efficiency and performance. For example, inference-Time Intervention (ITI)
(
21
)
identifies “truthfulness” directions in the activation space and shifts the inference dynamics along these vectors to enhance the generation of truthful answers. Similarly,
7
leverage the contrast between early and late layer distributions to amplify factual signals and suppress hallucinations during decoding.

3
Intrinsic Magnification Mechanism

3.1
Formulations

Inference Dynamics.
Consider an LLM with
N
transformer layers
\{f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{N}\}
, and a vocabulary space
\mathcal{V}
. The inference dynamics is defined as an autoregressive process that transforms the given input token into a probability distribution over the vocabulary.
Let
X_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the input embedding of the
t
th token. The internal state evolves layer-by-layer through a residual mapping
\mathcal{F}
(containing Attention and FFN blocks). The internal state
H_{i,t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
at layer
i
is defined recursively:

H_{i,t}=f_{i}(H_{i-1,t})=H_{i-1,t}+\mathcal{F}_{i}(H_{i-1,t}),i\in\{1,\dots,N\},

(1)

where
H_{0,t}=X_{t}
is the initial input embedding.

To generate the final output, the last internal state
H_{N,t}
is projected back into the vocabulary space via a linear unembedding matrix
W_{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}|\times d}
. The final probability distribution
p_{t}\in\mathcal{P}(|\mathcal{V}|)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}|}\mid x\geq 0,\mathbf{1}^{\top}x=1\}
is obtained by:

p_{t}=\text{Softmax}(W_{U}H_{N,t}).

(2)

For simplicity, we omit the subscripts
t
and
i
when no confusion arises, referring
H_{i,t}
as
H_{i}
or
H
.

Confidence Trajectory
is one of the most widely used signals to interpret the inference dynamics of LLMs. For a generated sequence of length
T
, we define the layer-wise confidence
\mathcal{C}_{i}
as the expectation of the Top-K probability of the internal predicted token. Formally:

\mathcal{C}_{i}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\text{Top-K}(p_{i,t}).

(3)

p_{i,t}
is the probability of the
t
th token predicted by the
i
th layer, which can be obtained through a decoding function
\mathcal{D}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{V})
, i.e.,
p_{i,t}=\mathcal{D}(H_{i,t})
.
Existing methods instantiate
\mathcal{D}
differently.
The static Logit Lens employs the last unembedding matrix with softmax, i.e.,
\mathcal{D}(H)=\text{Softmax}(W_{U}H)
, whereas in other tunable methods like Tuned Lens,
\mathcal{D}
is a learned affine transformation. The confidence trajectory is then the expected confidence of the predicted token across all the
N
layers.

Decoding function with varied focal depth
m
.
Unlike the prior static probing methods, we generalize the decoding function
\mathcal{D}
from the last unembedding matrix to a composition of the final
m
layers of the model (and the unembedding projection). Formally:

\mathcal{D}_{m}(H)=\text{Softmax}(D_{m}(H)),

(4)

where
D_{m}
is the composition of the final
m
layers of the model and the unembedding projection. Formally:

D_{m}(H)=W_{U}(f_{N}\circ\cdots\circ f_{N-m+1}(H)).

(5)

For simplicity, we define
D_{m}
as the
decoding function (DF)
with focal depth
m
in the following analysis.

Refinement layers.
Prior research suggests that the computation of the transformer exhibits two stages, separated by a layer
i_{0}
(see Section
2
). The initial layers (
i<i_{0}
) map the inputs to the latent space, and the subsequent layers (
i\geq i_{0}
) decode the latent space to the output. In this work, we term the layers in the latter stage as
refinement layers
.

3.2
Assumptions

Confidence in the Logit Space.
To facilitate convex analysis, we analyze the confidence in the logit space rather than the probability simplex. Let
S_{t}
be the Top-K set. We define the
Logit Margin
function
g_{m}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
as:

g_{m}(H):=\log\sum_{v\in S_{t}}e^{\mathcal{D}_{m}(H)_{v}}-\log\sum_{v\notin S_{t}}e^{\mathcal{D}_{m}(H)_{v}}.

(6)

Since the Softmax function is monotonic w.r.t the margin, proving that the sequence-averaged margin
\bar{\mathcal{C}}^{(m)}_{i}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t}g_{m}(H_{i,t})
increases is equivalent to proving that the observed confidence
\mathcal{C}_{i}
increases. In our analysis, we use
\bar{\mathcal{C}}^{(m)}_{i}
as the approximation of the confidence
\mathcal{C}_{i}
.

Before obtaining the theoretical results, we make the following assumptions:

Assumption 3.1

(Smoothness of the Margin Function)
.

We assume the margin function
g_{m}(H)
has a
\beta
-Lipschitz continuous gradient over the domain of hidden states. Formally, for any two hidden states
H_{i},H_{k},k\geq i
, there exists a constant
\beta>0
such that:

\|\nabla g_{m}(H_{i})-\nabla g_{m}(H_{k})\|\leq\beta\|H_{i}-H_{k}\|.

(7)

The rationale behind this assumption is that the hidden states during the forward pass are normalized by LayerNorm (restricting the domain), and the Softmax-Log functions comprising
g_{m}
are smooth
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
functions. Consequently, the Hessian of
g_{m}
is bounded, preventing the gradient from changing arbitrarily fast.

Proposition 3.2

(Quadratic Smoothness Bound)
.

Under Assumption
3.1
, for any current hidden state
H
and a residual update vector
\Delta
, the margin function satisfies:

g_{m}(H+\Delta)\geq g_{m}(H)+\langle\nabla g_{m}(H),\Delta\rangle-\frac{\beta}{2}\|\Delta\|^{2}.

(8)

While Proposition
3.2
is a classic result (for proof, see Appendix
A.1
), it holds a specific meaning in our inference dynamics. Here,
\Delta
represents the residual update provided by a residual function on
any
prior hidden state
H_{i}
, i.e.,
\Delta=\mathcal{F}_{k}(H_{i}),k\geq i
.

Assumption 3.3

(Gradient-aligned Updates)
.

For any layer index
k\geq i_{0}
(where
i_{0}
denotes the start of the refinement layers (see Section
3.1
)), the residual update
\Delta
aligns positively with the gradient of the margin function
g_{m}(H)
in expectation. Specifically, there exists a constant
\mu>0
such that:

\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\nabla g_{m}(H),\Delta\rangle\mid H\right]\geq\mu.

(9)

This assumption formalizes the intuition that well-trained Transformers function as implicit optimizers. In the late refinement stages (
k\geq i_{0}
), the residual function
\mathcal{F}_{k}
provides updates that push the hidden state
H
closer to the decision boundary of the target token.

Assumption 3.4

(Boundedness)
.

The residual updates are bounded by a constant
R
, i.e.,
\|\Delta\|\leq R
. Furthermore, the fluctuations of the single-step margin gain around its mean are bounded by a constant
b
:

|g_{m}(H_{i,t})-\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[g_{m}(H_{i,t})\right]|\leq b.

(10)

Assumption
3.4
aligns with the use of normalization and residual connections, which constrain latent increments to prevent representation divergence.

3.3
Main Results

Based on the above assumptions, we obtain two fundamental properties of the confidence trajectory: the monotonic growth across layers (Result 1) and the magnification effect of larger focal depth
m
(Result 2). For the formal proof, please refer to Appendix
A.2
and Appendix
3.6
.

Result 1: The confidence of the refinement layers increases monotonically.

The first result quantifies the trajectory of the confidence (where focal depth
m
is fixed). We show that in the refinement phase, the layer-wise confidence statistically increases layer by layer.

Theorem 3.5

(Monotonicity of Confidence)
.

Under Assumptions
3.1
-
3.4
, for layers
i\geq i_{0}
and sequence length
T
, the probability that the sequence-averaged confidence
\bar{\mathcal{C}}^{(m)}_{i}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t}g_{m}(H_{i,t})
decreases in the next layer decays exponentially:

\mathbb{P}\left(\bar{\mathcal{C}}^{(m)}_{i+1}\leq\bar{\mathcal{C}}^{(m)}_{i}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{T\gamma^{2}}{2b^{2}}\right),

(11)

where
\gamma=\mu-\frac{\beta}{2}R^{2}
.

Theorem
3.5
illustrates that while individual tokens may exhibit noisy confidence fluctuations, the
averaged
confidence over a sequence statistically rises in refinement layers.

Result 2: Self-magnifying effect of the focal depth
m
.

We now analyze the impact of the focal depth
m
. We compare the confidence observed through the decoding function with focal depth
m+1

\mathcal{D}_{m+1}
versus
\mathcal{D}_{m}
with focal depth
m
at the
same
hidden state
H_{i}
.

Theorem 3.6

(Magnification of Focal Depth)
.

Let
D_{m+1}
be a decoding function with one additional layer compared to
D_{m}
. Based on Assumption
3.3
, the confidence trajectory observed through
D_{m+1}
statistically dominates
D_{m}
with a high probability:

\mathbb{P}\left(\bar{\mathcal{C}}^{(m+1)}_{i}>\bar{\mathcal{C}}^{(m)}_{i}\right)\geq 1-\exp\left(-\frac{T\gamma^{2}}{2b^{2}}\right).

(12)

Theorem
3.6
proves that increasing
m
introduces a monotonic upward shift of the confidence trajectory. Intuitively,
D_{m+1}
incorporates an additional “refining” transformation towards the direction of generating the output token, thereby effectively increasing the confidence compared to
D_{m}
.

4
High-resolution Probing and Measurement

Based on the theoretical foundations in Section
3
, we design
HyperLens
, a high-resolution probe to observe the confidence trajectory in a fine-grained manner. Furthermore, we formalize a quantitative metric from the trajectory to measure the cognitive effort of LLMs.

High-resolution probing with a proper focal depth.
Theorem
3.5
and
3.6
indicate that increasing focal depth
m
magnifies the confidence in the refinement layers. Therefore, unlike Logit Lens that fixes
m=0
,
HyperLens
utilizes the decoding function
D_{m}
(see Equation
5
) with focal depth
m\geq 1
to observe the confidence. Specifically, for each hidden state
H_{i}
, we first propagate it through
m
subsequent layers, i.e.,
\{f_{N-m+1},\dots,f_{N}\}
, and then project it to the logit space through the last unembedding layer to derive the magnified confidence
\mathcal{C}^{(m)}_{i}
.
Note that a proper
m
is crucial, because an excessively large
m
would over-magnify all signals to near-certainty, making the trajectories once again indistinguishable. At a proper
m
, magnification is constrained by the tasks’ inherent difficulty, making the trajectories for hard and easy tasks clearly separated.

Quantitative metric of cognitive effort.
Beyond visually observing the difference between easy and hard tasks, we formalize the cognitive effort into a quantitative metric. We define
refinement area
(
\Omega
) as the area above the magnified confidence curve of the refinement layers with a fixed focal depth
m
, formally:

\Omega=\sum_{k\geq i_{0}}(1-\mathcal{C}^{(m)}_{k}).

(13)

Intuitively,
\Omega
captures the cumulative uncertainty the model must resolve before converging on a final output, which is positively correlated with the cognitive effort: easy tasks exhibit a rapid confidence magnification, maintaining higher confidence across the refinement layers and yielding a lower
\Omega
; conversely, complex tasks necessitate a longer low-confidence struggle stage, resulting in a higher
\Omega
.

Automatic computation of refinement area.
To facilitate the computation of
\Omega
, we need to determine the beginning point of the refinement layers, i.e.,
i_{0}
, automatically. According to Theorem
3.5
, which establishes that the confidence demonstrates a statistical monotonicity in the refinement layers, we formulate the automatic determination algorithm through a
reverse-scanning
strategy.
Briefly, for a fixed model and focal depth
m
, we scan the confidence trajectory backward from the final layer, and identify
i_{0}
as the first layer where the monotonic decreasing trend is violated. The detailed algorithm is in Appendix
B
.

Empirical Validation

Validating Result1: the confidence trajectory increases monotonically in the refinement layers.

Figure
2
plots the confidence trajectory (as defined in Equation
3
, Section
3.1
) of four models with varied
m
on two datasets. We leave the full results in Appendix
D
for succinctness. We observe a robust monotonic growth in confidence throughout the deeper refinement layers across all cases.
The universality of this trend across diverse architectures and domains empirically validates Theorem
3.5
, demonstrating that the monotonically increasing trajectory is an intrinsic characteristic of the models’ inference dynamics.

Validating Result2: larger focal depth
m
magnifies the observed confidence.

From Figure
2
, we can see that increasing
m
strictly magnifies the confidence in the deeper refinement layers. Specifically, the trajectories exhibit a strict ordering in the deeper layers. The trajectory for
m=5
(red) lies above that for
m=3
(yellow), which in turn surpasses
m=1
(green) across all the models and datasets. The strict ordering phenomenon validates Theorem
3.6
. By incorporating a few transformer layers into the decoding function, we can effectively magnify the confidence signals and therefore provide a fine-grained view.

Further analysis.
Beyond the properties stated in Theorem
3.5
and Theorem
3.6
, we also observe several interesting properties in Figure
2
:

(1) Model-specific sensitivity of the magnification mechanism.
The magnitude of magnification with larger
m
varies significantly across models. For instance, Qwen2.5-3B exhibits a substantial gap between the trajectories of
m=1
and
m=3~\text{or}~5
, indicating a high sensitivity to small
m
. In contrast, Deepseek-7B shows a much narrower divergence of
m=1
and
3
. The trajectory becomes significantly lifted until
m=5
, suggesting that the detailed magnification mechanism varies across models.

(2) Intrinsic beginning points of the refinement layers.
The beginning points of the refinement layers, i.e., the layer where the confidence begins to climb, appear to be an intrinsic characteristic of the models with varied focal depth
m
, which remains remarkably consistent across diverse datasets for any fixed model and
m
.
For instance, when
m=1
, we can see that the beginning point is around
10
,
30
,
20
and
10
for the four respective models across datasets. This consistency indicates that the location of refinement layers is an intrinsic property of the model, independent of the specific datasets being processed.

Conclusion

By leveraging the transformer’s inherent self-magnifying mechanism, we develop
HyperLens
to precisely quantify a model’s cognitive effort, overcoming the limitations of current probes.
The effectiveness of
HyperLens
actually provides a novel insight for the interpretability community, i.e., the most effective probes through which to view a model’s internal mysteries may be the model itself. Our findings suggest that leveraging the model’s own features, such as the future layers, can resolve highly meaningful signals that are otherwise lost in the high-dimensional latent space.
For future work, we will explore the utility of
HyperLens
in other applications, including performance improvement, harmful behavior detection, and the interpretability of abnormal phenomena. More importantly, we plan to uncover additional intrinsic properties within the transformer architecture that can be leveraged for self-interpretation. By pursuing these paths, we aim to provide fresh insights and inspire future breakthroughs in AI interpretability.

Impact Statement

This paper presents work whose goal is to enhance the transparency and interpretability of Large Language Models (LLMs). By introducing
HyperLens
to quantify the internal cognitive effort of models, our work contributes to the development of more reliable and robust AI systems.

Appendix A
Proofs of results

A.1
Proof of Assumption
3.1

Proof.

From the fundamental theorem of calculus, we can express the difference as an integral along the path
H(t)=H+t\Delta
for
t\in[0,1]
:

g_{m}(H+\Delta)-g_{m}(H)=\int_{0}^{1}\langle\nabla g_{m}(H+t\Delta),\Delta\rangle\,dt.

(14)

Subtracting the first-order approximation
\langle\nabla g_{m}(H),\Delta\rangle
from both sides, we analyze the remainder:

\displaystyle\left|g_{m}(H+\Delta)-g_{m}(H)-\langle\nabla g_{m}(H),\Delta\rangle\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{0}^{1}\langle\nabla g_{m}(H+t\Delta)-\nabla g_{m}(H),\Delta\rangle\,dt\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\|\nabla g_{m}(H+t\Delta)-\nabla g_{m}(H)\|\cdot\|\Delta\|\,dt\quad(\text{Cauchy-Schwarz})

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\beta\|t\Delta\|\cdot\|\Delta\|\,dt\quad(\text{by Assumption~\ref{assump-lipschitz}})

\displaystyle=\beta\|\Delta\|^{2}\int_{0}^{1}t\,dt=\frac{\beta}{2}\|\Delta\|^{2}.

(15)

Removing the absolute value gives the lower bound:
g_{m}(H+\Delta)-g_{m}(H)\geq\langle\nabla g_{m}(H),\Delta\rangle-\frac{\beta}{2}\|\Delta\|^{2}
.
∎

A.2
Proof of Theorem
3.5
(Monotonicity of Confidence)

To make the notation simpler and easier to follow, we assign a specific subscript to the residual update in this and the next subsection. It represents the update contributed by the
k
-th residual block on the hidden state at layer
i
:

\Delta_{i,k}(H_{i,t})=\mathcal{F}_{k}(H_{i,t}),~k>i
.

In this section, we provide the derivation for the probabilistic bound on the confidence growth across layers. We establish that with sufficient alignment drift
\mu
and sequence length
T
, the average margin
\bar{\mathcal{C}}^{(m)}_{i}
increases monotonically with high probability.

A.2.1
Step 1: Local Gain Analysis

Consider the transition from layer
i
to
i+1
. For a specific token at position
t
, the hidden state updates as
H_{i+1,t}=H_{i,t}+\Delta_{i+1,t}
. We define the single-step margin gain random variable
X_{i,t}
as:

X_{i,t}:=g_{m}(H_{i+1,t})-g_{m}(H_{i,t}).

(16)

Invoking the
Quadratic Smoothness Bound
(Proposition
3.2
), which relates the function change to the gradient and the norm of the update, we have:

X_{i,t}\geq\langle\nabla g_{m}(H_{i,t}),\Delta_{i+1,t}\rangle-\frac{\beta}{2}\|\Delta_{i+1,t}\|^{2}.

(17)

Taking the conditional expectation with respect to the current state (filtration
\mathcal{F}_{t}
), and applying Assumption
3.3
(Positive Alignment Drift) and Assumption
3.4
(Boundedness
\|\Delta\|\leq R
):

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{i,t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\nabla g_{m}(H_{i,t}),\Delta_{i+1,t}\rangle\right]-\frac{\beta}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\Delta_{i+1,t}\|^{2}\right]

(18)

\displaystyle\geq\mu-\frac{\beta}{2}R^{2}.

We define the
effective drift

\gamma:=\mu-\frac{\beta}{2}R^{2}
. Assuming the alignment component dominates the curvature penalty, we have
\gamma>0
. Thus, the expected gain for any single token is strictly positive:

\mathbb{E}[X_{i,t}]\geq\gamma.

(19)

A.2.2
Step 2: Sequence Aggregation and Martingale Construction

We aim to bound the behavior of the average margin over the prompt of length
T
. The change in the average margin is:

\Delta\bar{\mathcal{C}}=\bar{\mathcal{C}}^{(m)}_{i+1}-\bar{\mathcal{C}}^{(m)}_{i}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}X_{i,t}.

(20)

Let
S_{T}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}X_{i,t}
be the total gain. We construct a Martingale Difference Sequence (MDS) to analyze the fluctuations of
S_{T}
around its expectation. Let
Y_{t}
be the centered variable:

Y_{t}=X_{i,t}-\mathbb{E}[X_{i,t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}].

(21)

The sequence
\{Z_{k}\}_{k=1}^{T}
defined by
Z_{k}=\sum_{t=1}^{k}Y_{t}
is a martingale. According to Assumption
3.4
, the fluctuations of the margin are bounded by
b
. Specifically, we assume the centered random variable is bounded such that
|Y_{t}|\leq b
almost surely.

A.2.3
Step 3: Concentration via Azuma-Hoeffding

We are interested in the failure probability, defined as the event where the average margin does not increase (i.e.,
\Delta\bar{\mathcal{C}}\leq 0
). This is equivalent to sum
S_{T}\leq 0
.

Decomposing the sum:

S_{T}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}[X_{i,t}]+\sum_{t=1}^{T}Y_{t}\geq T\gamma+Z_{T}.

(22)

Thus, the event
S_{T}\leq 0
implies
T\gamma+Z_{T}\leq 0
, or
Z_{T}\leq-T\gamma
. We apply the
Azuma-Hoeffding Inequality
for bounded martingale differences. For any
\epsilon>0
:

\mathbb{P}(Z_{T}\leq-\epsilon)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2\sum_{t=1}^{T}c_{t}^{2}}\right),

(23)

where
c_{t}
is the bound on
Y_{t}
. Since
|Y_{t}|\leq b
, the range of
Y_{t}
is at most
2b
, relying on the standard form for bounded random variables centered at 0, strictly
|Y_{t}|\leq b
implies the step squared in the denominator is bounded.
Substituting
\epsilon=T\gamma
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\Delta\bar{\mathcal{C}}\leq 0\right)=\mathbb{P}(S_{T}\leq 0)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(Z_{T}\leq-T\gamma)

(24)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{(T\gamma)^{2}}{2\sum_{t=1}^{T}b^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\frac{T^{2}\gamma^{2}}{2Tb^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\frac{T\gamma^{2}}{2b^{2}}\right).

This concludes the proof. The average confidence increases monotonically with a probability that approaches 1 exponentially fast as the sequence length
T
or the effective alignment strength
\gamma
increases.
∎

A.3
Proof of Theorem
3.6
(Magnification of Focal Depth)

To prove Theorem
3.6
, we treat the increase in decoding head depth effectively as a “virtual” forward pass through an additional Transformer layer which is similar to
A.2
.

A.3.1
Step 1: Local Function Analysis

Let
D_{m}(\cdot)
denote the function represented by the decoding head consisting of the final
m
layers of the model plus the unembedding projection. Let
\mathcal{F}_{k}(\cdot)
denote the transformation of the
k
-th Transformer block (where
k=N-m
is the specific layer added when moving from depth
m
to
m+1
).

By definition, applying the deeper head
D_{m+1}
to a hidden state
H_{i,t}
is mathematically equivalent to passing
H_{i,t}
through the block
\mathcal{F}_{N-m}
first, and then applying the shallower head
D_{m}
to the result. Formally:

D_{m+1}(H_{i,t})\equiv D_{m}\left(\mathcal{F}_{N-m}(H_{i,t})\right).

(25)

Consequently, the logit margin calculated by the deeper head, denoted as
\mathcal{C}^{(m+1)}_{i,t}
, can be expressed in terms of the margin function
g_{m}
evaluated at a perturbed state:

\mathcal{C}^{(m+1)}_{i,t}=g_{m}\left(\mathcal{F}_{N-m}(H_{i,t})\right)=g_{m}(H_{i,t}+\Delta_{head}),

(26)

where
\Delta_{head}=\mathcal{F}_{N-m}(H_{i,t})-H_{i,t}
represents the residual update contributed by the specific layer
N-m
.

A.3.2
Step 2: Sequence Aggregation and Martingale Construction

We are interested in the probability that the average margin increases, i.e.,
\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t}(\mathcal{C}^{(m+1)}_{i,t}-\mathcal{C}^{(m)}_{i,t})>0
. Let
X_{t}=\mathcal{C}^{(m+1)}_{i,t}-\mathcal{C}^{(m)}_{i,t}
be the instantaneous gain at token
t
.

Using the Quadratic Smoothness Bound (Proposition
3.2
), we expand the gain:

X_{t}=g_{m}(H_{i,t}+\Delta_{head})-g_{m}(H_{i,t})\geq\langle\nabla g_{m}(H_{i,t}),\Delta_{head}\rangle-\frac{\beta}{2}\|\Delta_{head}\|^{2}.

(27)

By Assumption
3.4
, the residual norm is bounded by
R
. By Assumption
3.3
, provided the layer index
N-m
falls within the refinement phase (
N-m\geq i_{0}
), the alignment condition holds:
\mathbb{E}[\langle\nabla g_{m},\Delta_{head}\rangle]\geq\mu
. Incorporating these into the expectation of
X_{t}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\nabla g_{m}(H_{i,t}),\Delta_{head}\rangle\right]-\frac{\beta}{2}R^{2}

(28)

\displaystyle\geq\mu-\frac{\beta}{2}R^{2}:=\gamma.

(29)

Here,
\gamma
represents the strictly positive effective drift (under the condition that alignment strength
\mu
dominates the curvature penalty).

A.3.3
Step 3: Concentration via Azuma-Hoeffding

We now bound the probability that the empirical average
\bar{X}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}X_{t}
is non-positive. Let
S_{T}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}X_{t}
. We construct a martingale difference sequence to control the deviation from the expected growth.

Since the single-step fluctuations are bounded by
b
(Assumption
3.4
), we apply the Azuma-Hoeffding inequality. We analyze the event where the sum is negative:

\mathbb{P}(S_{T}\leq 0)=\mathbb{P}(S_{T}-\mathbb{E}[S_{T}]\leq-\mathbb{E}[S_{T}]).

(30)

Since
\mathbb{E}[S_{T}]\geq T\gamma
, this implies:

\mathbb{P}(S_{T}\leq 0)\leq\mathbb{P}(S_{T}-\mathbb{E}[S_{T}]\leq-T\gamma).

(31)

Applying the inequality for bounded random variables:

\mathbb{P}\left(\bar{\mathcal{C}}^{(m+1)}_{i}-\bar{\mathcal{C}}^{(m)}_{i}\leq 0\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{(T\gamma)^{2}}{2\sum_{t=1}^{T}b^{2}}\right)=\exp\left(-\frac{T^{2}\gamma^{2}}{2Tb^{2}}\right)=\exp\left(-\frac{T\gamma^{2}}{2b^{2}}\right).

(32)

Conversely, the probability that the deeper head yields a strictly higher confidence is:

\mathbb{P}\left(\bar{\mathcal{C}}^{(m+1)}_{i}>\bar{\mathcal{C}}^{(m)}_{i}\right)\geq 1-\exp\left(-\frac{T\gamma^{2}}{2b^{2}}\right).

(33)

This completes the proof.
\square
