Determinants of levofloxacin prophylaxis failure in high-risk haematology patients: a prospective study integrating resistant colonization and pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic target attainment
Authors: Cemre Boşnak, Gökhan Metan, Pınar Zarakolu, Elifcan Aladağ Karakulak, Aslı Pınar, Hakan Göker, Jason A Roberts, Maria Patricia Hernandez-Mitre, Murat Akova
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo evaluate the clinical and microbiological factors associated with the failure of levofloxacin prophylaxis in high-risk haematology patients, with a particular focus on the attainment of the pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) target.MethodsThis prospective case–control study evaluated adult high-risk haematology patients receiving levofloxacin prophylaxis (750 mg/day) during neutropenia. Analyses were performed at the episode level. Individual levofloxacin exposure was described using a population PK model, with the PK/PD target defined as fAUC0–24/MIC ≥80. Surveillance cultures were used to characterize Gram-negative bacterial (GNB) colonization and resistance profiles.ResultsA total of 120 neutropenic episodes were analysed. Profound neutropenia was independently associated with prophylaxis failure (OR: 4.17; 95% CI: 1.70–10.2; P = 0.002], whereas achievement of fAUC/MIC ≥80 was independently associated with a reduced risk of failure (OR: 0.12; 95% CI: 0.02–0.62; P = 0.012). Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis identified fAUC/MIC ≥48.46 as the Youden-optimal cutoff (sensitivity 86.0%, specificity 38.3%; Youden J = 0.243). Achievement of this cutoff was also independently associated with a reduced risk of prophylaxis failure (OR: 0.26; 95% CI: 0.083–0.803; P = 0.019). Target non-attainment was strongly associated with levofloxacin-resistant GNB colonization, indicating that PK/PD failure was predominantly MIC driven.ConclusionsLevofloxacin prophylaxis failure appears to reflect an interplay between host factors and inadequate PK/PD target attainment, which was largely influenced by levofloxacin-resistant GNB colonization. The standard 750 mg dose was insufficient in a significant proportion of patients, supporting the need for prophylaxis strategies that integrate PK/PD targets and local resistance patterns.

Introduction

Fluoroquinolone (FQ) prophylaxis, particularly with levofloxacin, is the standard approach for managing high-risk patients with prolonged neutropenia. However, increasing Gram-negative bacterial (GNB) resistance has raised concerns regarding its effectiveness.1,2 Although prophylaxis efficacy declines when the prevalence of FQ-resistant GNB exceeds 20%, resistance alone does not fully explain prophylaxis failure.3 In a cohort of HSCT recipients receiving FQ prophylaxis, 16.8% of the 49 Enterobacterales isolates recovered from blood cultures were FQ susceptible. These infections would be expected to have been suppressed by prophylaxis; this suggests a possible role for inadequate FQ exposure in prophylaxis failure.4 In this context, we identified only one prior study evaluating the pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics (PK/PD) of levofloxacin in neutropenic patients. Although it found no neutropenia-related PK changes, it did not assess PK/PD target attainment in patients with prophylaxis failure.5

In addition, host factors and inter-patient PK variability may influence infection risk and antibiotic exposure.1,2 Although levofloxacin prophylaxis is widely used in patients with haematological malignancies and HSCT recipients, the optimal PK/PD target for prophylaxis remains unknown. Accordingly, we investigated determinants of levofloxacin prophylaxis success, focusing on PK/PD target attainment and identification of an optimal prophylactic PK/PD threshold.

Material and methods

Study design and population

This prospective, single-centre, case–control study was conducted at Hacettepe University Hospitals, Ankara, Türkiye. Adults with acute leukaemia or undergoing allogeneic-HSCT were enrolled if they had an absolute neutrophil count (ANC) <500 cells/mm3 for ≥1 week and received oral levofloxacin 750 mg/day as febrile neutropenia (FN) prophylaxis for ≥7 days. Patients with prior FQ-resistant colonization or infection within three months were excluded. A single patient could contribute multiple episodes if separated by ≥1 week. Patients with prophylaxis failure were matched to successful controls by duration of neutropenia and duration of prophylaxis.

Clinical definitions

FN was defined as a tympanic temperature ≥38.3°C on a single measurement or ≥38.0°C on two occasions within 1 h with ANC <500/mm3. Patients developing FN while on levofloxacin were classified as prophylaxis failures; those remaining neutropenic without fever or need for antibiotic initiation were classified as prophylaxis successes. Profound neutropenia was defined as an ANC <100 cells/mm3.

Microbiological evaluation

For patients developing FN, blood cultures and relevant specimens were collected per protocol. Surveillance swabs from the oropharynx, axillae, inguinal regions, and rectum were obtained at the same time with PK sampling. Swabs were placed in Mueller-Hinton broth containing levofloxacin (5 µg) and vancomycin (10 µg) antibiotic disks (Bioanalyse, Türkiye) and incubated at 37°C for 24 h.

After incubation, aliquots were subcultured onto MacConkey, blood, and eosin methylene blue agars (CondaLab, Spain) at 37°C for 24 h. Isolates were identified using API systems (BioMérieux, France) and confirmed via APIWeb (BioMerieux, France) software. Levofloxacin MICs were determined by gradient test (Etest, Oxoid, UK) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Meropenem MICs were determined by broth microdilution using meropenem trihydrate (PHR1772, Sigma-Aldrich, Germany), and all antimicrobial susceptibility results were interpreted according to EUCAST criteria.6 ESBL production was determined by the double-disk synergy test using ceftazidime and cefotaxime disks, as described previously.7 Quality control was performed using Escherichia coli ATCC 25922 and Klebsiella pneumoniae ATCC 700603.

Blood and urine culture isolates were identified by MALDI-TOF MS (Microflex LT, Bruker Daltonik, Germany) and antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed using BD Phoenix (Becton, Dickinson, USA).

PK sampling and analysis

Sample collection and processing

In patients with prophylaxis failure, PK sampling was performed on the day following FN onset. On that study day, a single 750 mg oral dose of levofloxacin was administered at the time of the patient's scheduled prophylaxis dose, and blood samples were collected at 2, 4, 8, and 24 h after dosing. In patients with successful prophylaxis, plasma sampling was performed using the same sampling schedule following a scheduled 750 mg prophylactic dose. All samples (3–5 mL) were collected in EDTA tubes, transported to the laboratory on ice within 30 min, and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 min. Plasma was stored at −80°C until analysis.

Levofloxacin plasma concentrations were measured by validated LC-MS/MS (Shimadzu LCMS-8040) using commercial kits (Obikrom, Türkiye), with calibration curves and quality controls included in each run. Detailed analytical methods and assay validation are provided in the Supplementary Methods (available as Supplementary data at JAC Online) (Section S1 and Table S1).

Population pharmacokinetic modeling

Population PK analysis was performed using nonlinear mixed-effects modeling with Monolix 2024R1 (Lixoft SAS, Antony, France) employing the stochastic approximation expectation maximization algorithm. One- and two-compartment models with first-order absorption and elimination were evaluated. Inter-individual variability (IIV) was assumed to follow a log-normal distribution and was estimated using an exponential model.

Residual unexplained variability was tested using constant, proportional, and combined error models. Model selection was based on the Akaike information criterion (AIC), objective function value (OFV), corrected Bayesian information criterion (BICc), and visual inspection of goodness-of-fit (GOF) plots.

Potential covariates were screened using a stepwise forward addition (P < 0.05, reduction in OFV ≥ 3.84) and backward elimination (P < 0.001, increase in OFV ≥10.83) procedure. Candidate covariates, including biologically related variables, were evaluated individually within the stepwise covariate modelling procedure, and only those meeting the predefined OFV criteria were retained in the final model. The following covariates were evaluated: age, sex, body weight, body mass index, body surface area, serum creatinine, renal function estimates (Cockcroft–Gault creatinine clearance (CRCL), modification of diet in renal disease, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), and Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) eGFR), serum total protein, ALT, AST, gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), and SOFA and APACHE II scores.

Model evaluation was based on GOF plots of observed versus population-predicted (PRED) and individual-predicted (IPRED) concentrations, individual weighted residuals (IWRES) versus time and IPRED, and normalized prediction distribution errors (NPDE) versus time and PRED. Visual predictive check (VPC) was performed using 1000 simulations of the final model. A non-parametric bootstrap with 1000 resampled datasets was used to obtain median parameter estimates and 95% confidence intervals.

PK/PD target attainment

Individual Bayesian posterior PK parameters were used to simulate concentration–time profiles (Simulx 2024R1). The primary PK/PD target was the ratio of the free 24-h area under the curve to the MIC (fAUC/MIC) ≥ 80. Protein binding was assumed 30% for levofloxacin.8

PTA was calculated for each patient based on the MIC of the bacteria isolated from clinical cultures. When no clinical isolate was available, the MIC of GNB from colonizing cultures was used instead. For patients without a positive clinical or colonization culture, the epidemiological cutoff (ECOFF) value of 2 mg/L for Pseudomonas aeruginosa reported by EUCAST was used to represent the possible worst-case scenario for a levofloxacin-susceptible isolate.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (Version 27.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normality of continuous variables was assessed with the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± SD; non-normally distributed as median (IQR; 25–75th percentile). Continuous variables were compared using Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test; categorical variables were analysed with the χ2 or Fisher’s exact test. A P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors associated with prophylaxis failure. Variables significant in univariate analyses and considered clinically relevant were entered into the multivariable model. The final model was selected based on GOF and clinical interpretability.

ROC curve analysis to define the PK/PD target optimal cutoff for prophylaxis was performed using SPSS. The area under the ROC curve (AUROC) was estimated using the trapezoidal method. The 95% CI for the AUROC was computed by non-parametric bootstrap resampling (2000 iterations, percentile method). The optimal cutpoint was determined by maximizing the Youden index (J = Sensitivity + Specificity − 1). Positive and negative likelihood ratios were calculated as LR + = Sensitivity/(1 − Specificity) and LR− = (1 − Sensitivity)/Specificity at each cutpoint.

Results

A total of 108 patients receiving levofloxacin prophylaxis during 120 neutropenia cycles were included. Neutropenia cycles were classified as prophylaxis success (n = 60) or failure (n = 60) based on FN occurrence during prophylaxis. PK/PD analyses were conducted in 103 neutropenia cycles for which all planned plasma samples for PK analysis were successfully collected (60 success, 43 failure).

Factors associated with prophylaxis success

Clinical, demographic, and laboratory characteristics

Patients with successful prophylaxis were significantly older and cardiovascular comorbidity was more frequent while diarrhoea was significantly more frequent in the prophylaxis failure group (Table 1). Profound neutropenia was significantly more common in the failure group (73.3% versus 38.3%, P < 0.001), with correspondingly lower median ANC (20 versus 190 cells/mm3, P < 0.001). Allogeneic-HSCT conditioning regimens were administered more frequently in the failure group (28.3% versus 8.2%, P = 0.005).

Population PK model

A one-compartment model with first-order absorption and elimination adequately described levofloxacin PK. Table S2 shows the demographic and clinical characteristics of the patients included in the PK analysis.

Levofloxacin plasma concentrations were highest at the early sampling time points (2–4 h) and declined by 24 h (Figure S1). Body weight significantly influenced the apparent volume of distribution (V/F), and CRCL significantly influenced apparent clearance (CL/F). GOF plots and the VPC demonstrated adequate model fit and predictive performance (Figures S2 and S3). The final model parameters and bootstrap results are provided in Table 3, with bootstrap analysis supporting the stability of the final model. Because sampling commenced after the expected Tmax (approximately 1.5 h), Ka was poorly identifiable and was therefore fixed to the preliminary estimate (1.12 h−1) obtained during model development. The complete Monolix model code is provided in the Supplementary Methods (Section S2) to facilitate reproducibility.

PK/PD target attainment

Among 103 patients with PK sampling (Table S2), 12/103 (11.7%) achieved the PK/PD target, fAUC0–24/MIC ≥80 (Table 4). The distribution of MIC values used for PK/PD analyses and the source of MIC assignment are summarized in Table S3. Of all clinical, demographic, laboratory, and treatment characteristics evaluated, levofloxacin-resistant GNB colonization was more frequent in patients who did not achieve the PK/PD target than in those who did (78% [71/91] versus 0% [0/12], P < 0.001)) (Table S4, S5).

ROC curve analysis identified an exploratory prophylaxis-associated fAUC0–24/MIC ≥48.46 as the optimal PK/PD cutoff for prophylaxis success, with sensitivity 86.0%, specificity 38.3%, and Youden J = 0.243 with best overall discrimination, although specificity was limited (Figure 1). This PK/PD target was achieved in 29 (28.2%) of 103 patients. Among the 74 patients who did not attain this threshold, 70/74 (94.6%) were colonized with levofloxacin-resistant GNB, compared with 0/29 (0%) among those who achieved the target (P < 0.001).

Multivariate analysis of prophylaxis failure

In multivariable analysis, profound neutropenia was independently associated with prophylaxis failure (OR: 4.17; 95% CI: 1.70–10.2; P = 0.002), whereas achievement of fAUC0–24/MIC ≥80 was associated with a reduced risk of prophylaxis failure (OR: 0.12; 95% CI: 0.02–0.62; P = 0.012). Similarly, attainment of fAUC0−24/MIC ≥48.46 was associated with a reduced risk of prophylaxis failure (OR: 0.26; 95% CI: 0.083–0.803; P = 0.019).

Discussion

In this prospective observational study of high-risk haematology patients receiving levofloxacin prophylaxis, we identified three interrelated determinants of prophylaxis failure: profound neutropenia, colonization with levofloxacin-resistant GNB, and inadequate PK/PD target attainment relative to organism susceptibility. We also identified an exploratory PK/PD threshold that may be relevant for levofloxacin prophylaxis in this patient population.

A key finding is the association between colonization with levofloxacin-resistant GNB, prophylaxis failure, and failure to achieve PK/PD targets. This observation supports the concept that the effectiveness of FQ prophylaxis is increasingly affected by local resistance ecology. In a study of HSCT recipients, 23% were colonized with FQ-resistant Enterobacterales pre-transplantation. Despite prophylaxis, 31% of colonized patients developed GNB bloodstream infections versus 1.1% of non-colonized, typically caused by the same colonizing strain.9 Notably, the relationship between resistance and PK/PD target attainment in our cohort reflects not only drug exposure but also the MIC distribution of colonizing organisms. In many patients, apparent PK/PD ‘failure’ was driven by elevated MICs rather than low systemic exposure. Consistent with this, no significant differences were observed in Cmax, Cmin, or AUC between prophylaxis success and failure groups (Table 4). These findings suggest that dose escalation or therapeutic drug monitoring alone may be insufficient to overcome resistance-driven loss of efficacy; however, the contribution of PK exposure warrants further evaluation in larger datasets.

Our findings also demonstrate that, although higher than the commonly used 500 mg/day, 750 mg/day levofloxacin prophylaxis, administered once daily frequently fails to achieve conventional PK/PD targets when assessed against current susceptibility patterns. Although this could be interpreted as underdosing, a more appropriate explanation is a mismatch between fixed dosing strategies and the evolving susceptibility of bacteria. While increasing the dose may improve exposure, it is unlikely to restore target attainment in patients colonized with highly resistant organisms with MIC > 2 mg/L. Therefore, unless combined with microbiologically informed patient selection, the clinical utility of PK/PD-guided dose optimization in prophylaxis may be limited.

Profound neutropenia was also independently associated with prophylaxis failure. This likely reflects greater mucosal barrier injury, and greater cumulative healthcare exposure. These findings are consistent with prior studies demonstrating that host vulnerability remains a major determinant of infection risk despite antimicrobial prophylaxis.10–13 Beyond serving as a marker of treatment intensity, profound neutropenia may also influence the clinical relevance of PK/PD target attainment. Under conditions of severely impaired host defence, equivalent antimicrobial exposure may not necessarily translate into equivalent prophylactic efficacy. This offers a biologically plausible explanation for the imperfect concordance between PK/PD target attainment and clinical outcome. Therefore, in the interpretation of prophylactic efficacy, antimicrobial exposure should be considered alongside host immune competence and microbiological susceptibility, although this interaction remains insufficiently characterized in neutropenic populations.14

Another key finding of this study is the critical role of PK/PD target attainment in prophylaxis efficacy. Patients achieving the established treatment target (fAUC0–24/MIC ≥80) had significantly lower failure risk, aligning with FQ PK/PD principles where AUC/MIC best correlates with antibacterial efficacy and with studies linking this target to improved outcomes.15–17 However, ROC-derived AUC/MIC threshold (fAUC0–24/MIC ≥48.46) was substantially lower than the infection treatment-based target (≥80), suggesting that standard PK/PD benchmarks may not be directly applicable to prophylactic settings. This is a logical and important finding that a lower PK/PD target is more relevant for suppressing growth of a smaller concentration of bacteria than for killing a larger concentration of pathogens. Further, this discrepancy likely reflects the dominant influence of resistance-driven MIC variability rather than true PK insufficiency. However, this ROC-derived threshold should be interpreted cautiously. Its relatively low Youden index (0.243) and specificity (38.3%) indicate limited discriminatory performance, and therefore this threshold should be considered exploratory and requires external validation before being applied as a clinical PK/PD target. A recent population PK simulation study demonstrated that fAUC/MIC ≥ 80 was rarely achieved with levofloxacin 750 mg/day in patients with higher eGFR (≥ 80 mL/min/1.73 m2) and infections with an MIC ≥ 1 mg/L.18

The impact on multidrug resistant organisms warrants careful consideration. In our cohort, 67.5% were colonized with levofloxacin-resistant GNB; among colonizing isolates, ESBL production was 20% and carbapenem resistance 10%, with no significant difference between prophylaxis failure and success groups (Table 2). Prior studies have shown that FQ exposure independently predicts ESBL-producing Klebsiella spp.19 and increases the risk of carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae bloodstream infection (BSI) in AML patients.20 Plasmid-mediated quinolone resistance genes are frequently co-carried with carbapenemase genes,21,22 and FQ use is a risk factor for carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales colonization/infection.23 Centers that discontinued FQ prophylaxis reported increased FN and bacteremia rates but fewer MDR infections, with no significant mortality impact.24–27 These observations underscore the need for a nuanced, risk-stratified approach rather than either universal prophylaxis or complete withdrawal.

Several limitations should be noted. The single-centre design and small subgroup sample sizes limit generalizability for assessment of clinical outcomes. Some patients contributed multiple neutropenic episodes, which may have influenced effect estimates. MIC values were derived from available colonizing or infecting isolates, introducing heterogeneity into target attainment calculations. Because only oral data were available, bioavailability variability could not be estimated separately. Additionally, the relationship between colonization, antimicrobial exposure, and infection could not be fully characterized. Finally, as an observational study, causality cannot be established and residual confounding cannot be excluded.

In conclusion, levofloxacin prophylaxis success is shaped by host, microbiological, and pharmacological factors. The 750 mg daily dose achieves the optimal PK/PD target in only one-third of patients when the target is defined as the ROC-derived cutoff of fAUC/MIC ≥ 48.46, and in approximately 10% of patients when the target is defined as fAUC/MIC ≥ 80, derived from levofloxacin treatment studies. Levofloxacin-resistant GNB colonization was strongly associated with target non-attainment in both scenarios. These findings suggest that the effectiveness of levofloxacin prophylaxis is limited not only by drug exposure, but also by underlying resistance patterns and patient-specific risk factors.
