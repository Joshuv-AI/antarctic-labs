 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The overall purpose of the Grand Challenge Integrated Research Project (IRP), Accelerating the
Qualification of Materials to Enable Rapid Deployment of Advanced Reactors, is to fulfill several
objectives. The first objective is to complete and demonstrate/establish the process for predicting the
microstructure and properties of structural materials in reactor and at high doses using ion irradiation as
an accurate predictive tool for assessing behavior under reactor irradiation. The second objective is for
this process to be adopted as part of an ASTM standard, and to work with the US Nuclear Regulatory
Commission to utilize this standard in licensing decisions on advanced reactor designs for which such
data are nonexistent or impractical to achieve. The need for standardized procedures is apparent based on
the outcomes of a recent round-robin experiment from laboratories using ion beams for radiation damage
studies [1] and the roadmap for ion beam technologies to address challenges for the advancement of
nuclear energy technologies [2].

Although there are many standards development organizations, one standard practice from ASTM
International most aligns with the goal of this program: ASTM E521, “Standard Practice for Investigating
the Effects of Neutron Radiation Damage Using Charged-Particle Irradiation” [3]. As a standard practice,
the document provides recommended approaches, procedures, and guidelines for performing various
tasks. The standard discusses correlation of microstructure evolution caused by both ions and neutrons,
including factors such as displacement gradients and free surfaces. The content has been revised regularly
(major revisions were published in 1996 [4] and 2016 [5]).

With the support of the Grand Challenge IRP, Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) led the revision of
the ASTM E521 standard practice. A formal task group was formed following the procedures of the
ASTM E10.05 subcommittee, which holds jurisdiction over the E521 standard. The group consisted of
experts from ORNL; the University of Michigan; The Pennsylvania State University; the Massachusetts
Institute of Technology; China Institute of Atomic Energy; GE Aerospace; the Australian Nuclear
Science and Technology Organisation; the University of California, Santa Barbara; Oxford Sigma; the
United Kingdom Atomic Energy Authority; the University of Wisconsin–Madison; Texas A&M
University; and the University of Tennessee, Knoxville. The group of materials scientists, radiation
effects scientists, and technical experts of other specialties met in full several times over the course of the
project with subgroups dedicated to specific sections of the standard.

A staggered approach was pursued to achieve three primary revisions: incorporation of best practices for
performing ion irradiation experiments, revisions of techniques and reporting requirements for pre- and
post-experiment characterization, and considerations of the use of charged particles to act as surrogates
for neutron irradiations with an emphasis on guidelines for the correlation of two irradiation
environments. Revisions were submitted to the relevant subcommittee and main committee following
ASTM procedures for ballots and votes. The first two primary revisions were formally adopted and added
to the E521 standard practice in 2025, and additional comments related to the incorporation of helium
were incorporated into ASTM E942, “Standard Guide for Investigating the Effects of Helium in
Irradiated Metals” [6]. The last set of primary revisions is currently under discussion within the task
group, and these revisions are anticipated to be submitted to the governing subcommittee in spring 2026.

The knowledge incorporated into the ASTM E521 standard practice led by this project built upon several
decades of research by the radiation damage effects community. Several operational factors included in
the practice are the awareness of the level vacuum cleanliness, temperature monitoring, and development
of control systems. These developments had only become feasible at the university laboratory scale about
20–25 years ago as the relevant technologies progressed. The Nuclear Energy Research Initiative (NERI)
project, A Novel Approach to Materials Development for Advanced Reactor Systems [7], used proton
irradiation to compare light-water reactor (LWR)-relevant alloys with those alloys created through similar

 1

radiation environments. The ion beam irradiation experiments on stainless steel involved microstructure
characterization as well as measurements of hardening and irradiation-assisted stress corrosion cracking
(IASCC) initiation. The results were combined with an analysis from the Electric Power Research
Institute (EPRI) Cooperative IASCC Research program and compared with microstructures from the
same alloy and heat irradiated in an LWR. The agreement among microstructure features, irradiation
hardening, and IASCC response validated the use of proton irradiation to emulate neutron irradiation
effects in stainless steel [8].

The pursuit of higher damage levels for advanced non-LWR facilities drove many research programs
toward self-ion irradiations where high damage rates are readily achievable with minimal impact to the
composition of the alloy under investigation. Upgraded measurement techniques were adopted, such as
2D thermal imaging of the entire irradiated surface [9] with calibration from nearby thermocouples for
high-fidelity spatial and temporal temperature data accumulation. The 2013 IRP on High Fidelity Ion
Beam Simulation of High Dose Neutron Irradiation [10] and the follow-on US Department of Energy
Office of Nuclear Energy (DOE-NE) Simulating Neutrons using Accelerated Particles (SNAP) program
[11], as well as concurrent additional DOE-NE sponsored programs, overcame numerous technical and
scientific barriers to bring generalized methods and procedures in use today to the ion irradiation
community. The IRP and SNAP programs tackled the difficult task of comparing self-ion irradiation with
radiation damage from fast test reactor experiments in which irradiations were conducted on the exact
same heats of a range of alloys at the same levels of damage and temperatures. These programs formed
the foundation on which the Grand Challenge IRP was built.

Technologically, the programs produced the ability to conduct dual ion irradiations [12, 13] with fixed
He/dpa ratios that fully account for the beam energy degradation through degrader foils with the spread
and loss of beam intensity as a function of foil thickness [14]. They also addressed the universal problem
of carbon contamination during irradiation [15]. The outcomes of these technological improvements led
to new understanding in how to use charged particles to emulate fast reactor irradiated microstructures
[16], predict microstructure evolution using advanced modeling and simulation [17], and assess
mechanical performance from the small volumes of ion irradiated surfaces [18-21]. Future work to be
considered for incorporation into ASTM standards are the significant efforts made to improve mechanical
performance from small volumes of irradiated materials, methods to calculate the amount of helium
generated from various neutron spectra, and continual updates to the E521 standard practice in pursuit of
a full ASTM test method. With the methodologies leading to these insights codified into the ASTM E521
standard practice, there is now an updated standard to guide the use of charged particles to accelerate
materials qualification efforts and aid in licensing decisions on advanced reactor designs.
