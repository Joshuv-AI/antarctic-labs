Soft‐Tissue Augmentation During Implant Placement in the Aesthetic Zone: Clinical, Radiographic, Histological and Patient‐Reported Outcomes From a Randomised Controlled Trial Using Connective Tissue Graft and a Volume‐Stable Collagen Matrix
Authors: Louiza Papantonatou, Leonidas Batas, Antigoni Delantoni, Eleutherios Anagnostou, Ioannis Vouros
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo evaluate the clinical, radiographic, histological and patient‐reported outcome measures (PROMs) of soft‐tissue augmentation (STA) during implant placement in the aesthetic zone using a connective tissue graft (CTG) or a volume‐stable collagen matrix (VCMX).Materials and MethodsIn this three‐arm randomised controlled trial, 25 patients were allocated to VCMX, SCTG, or no augmentation (control). Soft‐tissue thickness (STT) was assessed by ultrasonography, and buccal bone thickness (BBT) by CBCT, at baseline and 6 months. Clinical parameters, PROMs and histological findings were recorded. Statistical analysis was performed at the patient level using repeated‐measures general linear models, accounting for within‐subject variability.ResultsBoth VCMX and SCTG resulted in significant increases in STT compared with control (p < 0.001). Numerically greater STT gains were observed in the VCMX group. BBT decreased significantly over time in all groups, with no significant differences between treatments. PROMs were comparable, although CTG was associated with slightly higher morbidity. Histological analysis showed favourable integration of both grafting materials.ConclusionSTA significantly increased STT but did not prevent buccal bone remodelling. Within the study limitations, both augmentation approaches were associated with increased STT compared with no augmentation. Further well‐designed studies are required to confirm these findings.Trial RegistrationThis study is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT06622759; URL: https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06622759)

Introduction

Following tooth extraction, several biological processes lead to significant dimensional changes of the alveolar ridge, which is critical for stability and aesthetics (Cardaropoli et al. 2003; Schropp et al. 2003; Araujo and Lindhe 2005, 2009; Berglundh et al. 1991, 2007; Zheng et al. 2021; Tomasi et al. 2014). Soft‐tissue phenotype, particularly mucosal thickness and keratinised tissue, contributes to maintaining this seal and limiting recession and bone loss (Chappuis et al. 2017; Fu and Wang 2020; Tavelli et al. 2021), with thin phenotypes often requiring augmentation.

Soft tissue augmentation (STA), including sub‐epithelial connective tissue grafts (SCTGs) and free gingival grafts, has been shown to enhance peri‐implant stability (Thoma et al. 2009, 2021; Chackartchi et al. 2019). SCTG remains the gold standard but involves donor‐site morbidity and technical demands (Schmitt et al. 2016; Chackartchi et al. 2019). On the other hand, xenogeneic collagen matrices reduce morbidity and surgical time, achieving comparable volume gains. Nevertheless, they present limitations in terms of keratinised tissue formation and long‐term stability (Thoma et al. 2011, 2016; Schmitt et al. 2016, 2019; Zeltner et al. 2017).

Soft‐tissue thickness (STT) can be assessed using invasive or non‐invasive methods (Ronay et al. 2011). Ultrasonography provides reliable, radiation‐free measurements (Tsiolis et al. 2003; Puzio et al. 2018), while CBCT enables simultaneous hard‐ and soft‐tissue evaluation, and digital profilometry offers volumetric assessment (Ronay et al. 2011; Chappuis et al. 2015).

Mucosal thickness has been associated with buccal bone plate (BBP) stability, with thicker tissues linked to reduced marginal bone loss (Thoma et al. 2017; Tavelli et al. 2020). In contrast, a thin BBP (< 1.5 mm) is more prone to resorption (Spray et al. 2000; Kaminaka et al. 2015; Farronato et al. 2020; Monje et al. 2019, 2023; Imber et al. 2025). Although STA may mitigate these effects, the long‐term stability of xenogeneic matrices remains uncertain (Zuiderveld et al. 2018; Valles et al. 2022; Manfredini et al. 2023).

Given the limitations of current techniques and the morbidity associated with SCTG harvesting, further evidence is needed to validate xenogeneic substitutes as reliable alternatives. Therefore, the aim of this randomised controlled clinical trial was to evaluate the clinical, radiographic, histological and patient‐reported outcomes of soft‐tissue augmentation (STA) during implant placement in the aesthetic zone using either a connective tissue graft (SCTG) or a volume‐stable collagen matrix (VCMX).

Materials and Methods

Participant Recruitment and Sample Size Calculation

This study was designed as a three‐arm randomised controlled clinical trial with a superiority framework and was conducted at the Postgraduate Clinic of the Department of Periodontology and Implant Biology, Aristotle University of Thessaloniki. Patients in need of implant rehabilitation in the anterior maxilla, with clinically healthy gingiva on either an intact or reduced periodontium (Chapple et al. 2018), were screened. Patients with a history of periodontitis were included only if the disease had been successfully treated and it was stable at baseline; most of the participants presented with a history of stage I–II periodontitis. The protocol was approved by the local Ethics Committee (No. 20/29‐06‐2023) and was conducted in accordance with ethical principles, including the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Randomisation was performed electronically (www.randomizer.org), and allocation was supervised by an independent operator (I.V.) and revealed shortly prior to the therapeutic intervention. The null hypothesis was that STA at implant placement does not improve clinical, radiographic or patient‐reported outcomes (PROMs) compared with no augmentation and that no differences exist between a CTG and a VCMX. Sample size calculation was performed on a patient‐based analysis to detect a clinically significant difference in STT of 1 ± 0.5 mm SD between a test group and the control group at 6 months. Confidence interval was set at 95% (a = 0.05) and the power of a two‐tailed t‐test at 0.90. The minimum required sample size was calculated to be seven patients per group. To account for potential patient dropouts, finally, 25 patients were enrolled in this study. Specifically, the control group included eight patients, the CTG group eight patients and the VCMX group nine patients.

According to earlier studies, a difference of 1 mm between the test group and the control group was considered clinically significant (Thoma et al. 2016, 2020). Standard deviation (SD) estimation for between‐group comparisons was also based on previous investigations (Thoma et al. 2016; Zeltner et al. 2017). Power analysis was accomplished by the G*Power v.3 software (Faul et al. 2007, 2009).

Eligibility Criteria

Inclusion criteria were as follows: systemically healthy adults ≥ 18 years of age, patients requiring implant placement in the maxillary anterior region (second premolar to second premolar), ridge width ≤ 6 mm, and keratinised oral mucosa ≥ 2 mm. Only cases without severe soft‐tissue deficiencies were included, in which STA was not considered mandatory but could be performed to potentially improve the peri‐implant soft‐tissue phenotype and aesthetic outcomes. Exclusion criteria included poor oral hygiene (FMPS > 20%, FMBS > 15%), smoking > 10 cigarettes/day, untreated periodontitis, need for bone augmentation, systemic contraindications to surgery, recent malignancy, pregnancy or lactation, medications or systemic diseases affecting healing and allergy to collagen.

Study Design

Participants were randomly allocated to one of three groups: Group VCMX: implant placement with simultaneous STA using a porcine‐derived collagen matrix (VCMX; Geistlich Fibro‐Gide);Group SCTG: implant placement with simultaneous STA using an autologous sub‐epithelial connective tissue graft (SCTG); andGroup Control: implant placement without augmentation.

Treatment Timeline

Baseline examination included periodontal charting, periapical radiographs, intraoral photography and STT measurement using ultrasonography with customised stents. Oral hygiene was reinforced as needed. Surgery was performed at the second visit, and CBCT was obtained immediately after implant placement to assess the BBT.

At 2 weeks, sutures were removed and healing was clinically evaluated; patients also completed a questionnaire on their postoperative experience (PROMs). At 3 months, a second‐stage procedure was performed with soft‐tissue biopsy harvesting. At 6 months, clinical and radiographic assessment (CBCT) was repeated, including STT, BBT as well as plaque and bleeding scores.

Surgical Procedures and Post‐Surgical Care

All patients had completed the initial periodontal therapy at least 3 months prior to inclusion. Following a pre‐operative chlorhexidine rinse for 2 min and local anaesthesia, an incision was performed on the edentulous ridge, slightly palatal, extending to the mid‐buccal surface of the adjacent teeth. After full‐thickness flap elevation, prosthetically guided implant placement was performed. Implants were positioned at the bone level. The buccal flap was then modified to become split‐thickness to allow for coronal advancement.

In group A, the VCMX (size: 15 × 20 × 6 mm) was shaped to fit in the surgical site and was stabilised with horizontal mattress sutures beneath a coronally advanced flap. The thickness of the soft‐tissue xenograft was kept intact. Final suturing included two vertical external mattress sutures (PGA 5/0) at the mesial and distal aspect of the flap and single interrupted sutures (Figure 2).

Patients received amoxicillin (1 g for 7 days) and paracetamol (1 g for 3 days). Removal of sutures was performed 10 days after implant placement, and at that time point patients filled in a questionnaire about the overall surgical experience (PROMs).

Statistical Analysis

Data for clinical parameters (STT, BBT, FMPS, FMBS) were analysed using ANOVA within a hierarchical mixed linear model (West et al. 2015), including the main effects of ‘group’ (Control, CTG, VCMX), ‘time’ (baseline, 6 months) and their two‐way interaction. The patient was considered the primary unit of analysis. ‘Group’ was treated as a between‐patient factor and ‘time’ as a within‐patient repeated factor, both as fixed effects. Due to variability in the number of implants per patient, a hierarchical structure was applied, with implants nested within patients and patients within groups; both were included as random effects to account for clustering of observations arising from multiple implants within the same patient. The model was used to estimate standard errors for multiple comparisons, performed using the least significant difference (LSD) criterion (Toothaker 1993). Baseline variability was incorporated within the model, allowing adjustment for initial differences between groups. As the data were unbalanced, estimated (predicted) means with standard errors (SE), rather than observed means, are reported. Model assumptions were evaluated using residuals. Normality was assessed by histograms, boxplots, residual medians, skewness, kurtosis and the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, while homoscedasticity was examined using residuals versus predicted values and Spearman's‐ρ correlations. No relevant violations were detected. Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05 (p ≤ 0.05). Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics v.27.0 (IBM Corp., USA).

Results

A total of 27 patients were recruited initially. Two patients declined to sign for two CBCT examinations. One patient did not attend the 6‐month follow‐up. Data up to 3 months were included, while 6‐month outcomes were not available. Finally, 25 patients with 32 implants were included in the analysis. The median age of the participants was 58 years, and 68% were male. Twenty‐four percent of the patients were smokers. Regarding implant distribution, 12 were positioned in the control group, 10 in the CTG group and 10 in the VCMX group (see also Table S1). No relevant differences regarding baseline periodontal parameters, FMPS or FMBS were observed between the groups. Also, bone width of the alveolar ridge was measured by a bone calliper prior to surgery to include implants in the study (Table 1). The control and VCMX groups had similar mean values (5.33 and 5.30 mm, respectively), while the CTG group had a slightly lower mean (4.75 mm). Primary Outcome

Soft‐Tissue Thickness

At baseline, no statistically significant differences in STT were observed among the three groups (all pairwise comparisons, p > 0.05). Within‐group analysis showed a significant increase in STT from baseline to 6 months in the VCMX group (mean change: 1.635 mm; p < 0.001) and the CTG group (mean change: 0.904 mm; p < 0.001), whereas no significant change was observed in the Control group (mean change: 0.274 mm; p = 0.133). At 6 months, the STT values were significantly higher in the VCMX group compared with the CTG group (p = 0.026) and Control group (p < 0.001), while the CTG group also showed significantly higher STT values compared with Control group (p = 0.023). Secondary Outcomes

Buccal Bone Thickness

At all measured levels (BBT0, BBT2 and BBT4), statistically significant inter‐group differences were observed at baseline (p = 0.009, p = 0.012 and p = 0.003, respectively). From baseline to 6 months, BBT decreased in all groups. At the BBT0 level, the reduction was statistically significant in the VCMX group (mean change: −0.790 mm; p = 0.006) and the control group (mean change: −0.642 mm; p = 0.012), but not in the CTG group (mean change: −0.525 mm; p = 0.084). At the BBT2 level, a significant decrease was observed in all groups: VCMX (−0.730 mm; p < 0.001); CTG (−0.600 mm; p = 0.006); control (−0.633 mm; p = 0.001). At the BBT4 level, BBT also decreased significantly in all groups, with mean changes of −0.420 mm (VCMX; p = 0.005), −0.488 mm (CTG; p = 0.004) and −0.575 mm (control; p < 0.001). Notably, although the VCMX group presented higher baseline BBT0 values, it showed the greatest reduction over time, whereas the CTG group, despite having the lowest baseline BBT values, showed the smallest reduction.

FMPS, FMBS and Complications

At 6 months, FMPS and FMBS were significantly lower compared to baseline: 10.5% and 8.5%, respectively (both p‐values < 0.001). Regarding post‐operative complications in the control group, there were no complications. In the CTG group, impaired healing of the donor site was observed in 25% of patients, while in the VCMX group 40% of patients experienced secondary healing.

PROMs

At 10 days after treatment (Q1), pain levels were comparable between groups (p = 0.471), with median scores of 4/10 (Control), 5/10 (CTG) and 3/10 (VCMX) (Figure 4). Analgesic consumption (Q2) also showed no significant differences (p = 0.724), with median values of 2, 3 and 4, respectively. All patients (100%) reported overall satisfaction (Q3), and no social impact was noted (Q4). Willingness to repeat treatment (Q5) was lower in CTG (66.7%) compared to Control (88.9%) and VCMX (87.5%).

Histological Analysis

Thirty biopsy samples were harvested (VCMX = 10; SCTG = 10; Control = 10), of which 28 were processed for histological analysis. In the VCMX group, fibrous connective tissue with low cellularity and mature fibroblasts was observed, along with stratified squamous epithelium showing mild hyperplasia and no dysplasia. Mild perivascular inflammatory infiltrates were present, and graft remnants with clear vascularisation were identifiable. In the CTG group, low‐cellularity connective tissue with a loose collagen network and mature fibroblasts was noted, covered by stratified epithelium with mild to moderate hyperplasia and no dysplasia. Both grafts showed good integration within the augmented area. In the control group, fibrous connective tissue of low cellularity with mature fibroblasts and small thin‐walled vessels was observed (Figure 5).

Discussion

In this randomised clinical trial, we evaluated the effect of STA with VCMX and CTG on peri‐implant STT and BBT, including histological assessment. Both interventions significantly increased STT compared to control, while no significant inter‐group differences were observed for BBT.

In this study, STT was assessed using ultrasonography, a non‐invasive and accurate method for soft‐tissue evaluation (De Rouck et al. 2009; Figueredo et al. 2024). Both VCMX and CTG resulted in significantly greater STT gains compared to the non‐augmentation group, in agreement with previous studies (Cosyn et al. 2022; Stefanini et al. 2023). In the present study, VCMX showed the greatest increase in STT. This finding differs from that of the multi‐centre RCT by Cosyn et al. (2022), where CTG resulted in greater soft‐tissue thickening. Such discrepancies may be related to methodological and biological factors, including the difficulty in achieving standardised graft thickness with CTG and potential variability associated with graft composition, such as the presence of submucosal tissue. In contrast, VCMX provides a standardised and volume‐stable structure, which could partly explain the findings observed in the present study. Previous studies using transgingival probing have reported lower STT gains (Thoma et al. 2016), likely due to methodological limitations (Ronay et al. 2011) and shorter follow‐up. Longer term data indicate stable augmentation for both materials, with slightly greater long‐term stability for CTG (Thoma et al. 2020, 2023). Other measurement approaches, including injection needles (Cairo et al. 2017) and 3D digital modelling (Schmitt et al. 2016; Zeltner et al. 2017; Cosyn et al. 2022), have generally reported smaller increases, likely reflecting differences in measurement techniques.

A thin buccal bone phenotype has limited capacity to withstand remodelling after implant placement, partly due to the absence of the periodontal ligament and reduced vascularisation (Araujo et al. 2005). Establishment of the peri‐implant biological width may further compromise BBT stability in thin plates, particularly with insufficient mucosal thickness (Tavelli et al. 2020). In addition, thin soft tissues increase susceptibility to bone loss due to inflammation and mechanical stress (Thoma et al. 2017).

In the present study, BBT decreased at all implant levels (0, 2 and 4 mm) across groups at 6 months, confirming that bone remodelling is inherent. The magnitude of reduction varied, with VCMX showing the greatest decrease (up to 0.79 mm, p < 0.001) and SCTG smaller changes. Notably, despite higher baseline BBT in VCMX, it showed the greatest reduction, whereas SCTG, with lower baseline values, showed the smallest decrease. At 0 mm, bone loss in SCTG was limited and not significant (0.52 mm, p = 0.128), with similarly reduced changes at deeper levels, while the control group showed significant reductions throughout. However, significant baseline differences in BBT were present between groups, limiting the interpretation of inter‐group comparisons regarding buccal bone preservation. These findings suggest that STA does not prevent remodelling. The observed differences between groups should be interpreted cautiously. These results are consistent with evidence linking increased STT to improved peri‐implant stability (Chackartchi et al. 2019; Jensen et al. 2023). Similarly, Eeckhout et al. (2023) reported BBT reduction irrespective of the grafting approach. However, the absence of a non‐augmentation control group in that study limits interpretation, whereas the present study design allows assessment of remodelling in both augmented and non‐augmented sites. These findings should be interpreted with caution because of the limited sample size, short follow‐up period and baseline BBT differences between groups.

Histological analysis showed favourable integration of VCMX and SCTG, with minimal inflammation and adequate vascularisation. VCMX showed remodelling at 3 months with mild inflammatory infiltrates consistent with a physiological response, whereas SCTG showed a well‐organised connective tissue network. These findings align with evidence of early vascularisation, progressive remodelling and reduced morbidity for collagen matrices (Thoma et al. 2010, 2014, 2016; Thoma, Sancho‐Puchades, et al. 2012; Thoma, Villar, et al. 2012; Mathes et al. 2010).

Timing of STA may influence BBT stability. Immediate augmentation may support early wound stability and reduce buccal resorption (Zuiderveld et al. 2018, 2021), whereas delayed procedures may increase mucosal thickness but not compensate for early remodelling (Montero et al. 2022). In this study, immediate STA was performed to minimise early bone changes.

Complications were limited and treatment‐specific: CTG was associated with donor site morbidity, whereas VCMX showed mild inflammation and secondary healing; no complications occurred in the control group. These findings align with reports of higher morbidity for CTG and fewer complications with collagen matrices (Cairo et al. 2017; Thoma et al. 2016; Dadlani et al. 2024; Gargallo‐Albiol et al. 2019). PROMs showed comparable pain and analgesic intake (McGrath et al. 2012), with high overall satisfaction but lower willingness to repeat treatment in the CTG group, consistent with previous studies (Stefanini et al. 2021; Cairo et al. 2017).

Strengths of the present study include the combined clinical, radiographic and histological assessment, along with the use of non‐invasive and reproducible methods for STT and BBT evaluation (Puzio et al. 2018), with PROMs enhancing clinical relevance. In addition, the statistical approach accounted for patient‐level clustering and baseline variability. Limitations include the short follow‐up, limited sample size and uneven implant distribution, which may affect the detection of inter‐group differences and generalisability. Αlso, while not specifically designed according to the ID‐COSM framework (Tonetti et al. 2023), the selected outcomes broadly align with its core domains.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this randomised controlled clinical trial, the following conclusions could be made: Both VCMX and SCTG seem to lead to an increase in STT compared to no augmentation.
