Scope and Objective
Background
The drone-based Non-Intrusive Optical (NIO) Technology has been developed at NREL to allow
for efficient and automated optical characterization of heliostats in Concentrating Solar Power
(CSP) plants. For this project, the technology will be developed into a commercial tool package,
including software and user-interface (UI), operations manual, and training and support services.
The project team will partner with Tietronix to perform market assessment and stakeholder
engagement, develop the tool package and business model, and perform data collection and
analysis to demonstrate and refine the capabilities for use at a commercial plant. The team will
collaborate with a commercial plant to conduct the data collection operations and provide optical
error deliverables. The goal of the project is to advance the commercialization of the technology
to a stage where a beta version can be demonstrated at additional commercial plants and
developed into a licensable product.

Objectives
The goal of the project was to provide a beta version of a commercial Non-Intrusive Optical
(NIO) tool, that with further commercial demonstration, could be licensed by CSP plant
operators to conduct efficient measurements and monitoring of the solar field, which can be
incorporated into operation practices to improve power output. The accomplish this, the
following objectives were planned:

 • Obtain stakeholder data to determine the industry needs that the NIO metrology
 capabilities must meet.
 • Develop a commercial NIO package with a user-interface and documentation and
 training, and capability services to meet stakeholder needs.
 • Perform a commercial demonstration of the NIO tool by performing data collection
 activities and providing optical error data deliverables to a plant partner.
 • Develop a business strategy to market and launch a beta version of the commercial tool
 package at the conclusion of the project.

Path Forward
The main lessons learned from this project were:

 • Foreign drone laws and regulations can create barriers. We had originally planned to
 perform our own drone operations at Cerro Dominador but learned shortly before our
 planned trip that Chilean flight laws made this impossible. As a result, we identified and
 hired Aerodyne, a drone service provider, to complete the planned data collection tasks.
 If NREL hires a drone pilot directly, it is subject to DOE approval, but we were able to
 avoid a lengthy approval process by specifying the data collection operations as an end
 use agreement and directing Tietronix, our project partner, to contract the drone service
 to fulfill the data collection requirements. We learned from this experience that hiring a
 local drone service provider/pilot is the best option for foreign drone operations and
 offers the following advantages:
 o Hiring a local drone service/pilot ensures that the aircraft and pilot will already be
 registered in the country in which operations are to be performed and will be
 familiar with the local laws and regulations, which significantly reduces the time
 and complexity of the permitting and approval process for flights and reduces risk
 of inadvertently violating any local flight laws.
 o The NREL team does not need to transport drone equipment to the country where
 operations are being performed. Traveling with a drone poses risk for issues with
 export control or customs. Shipping drone parts, including batteries, must be done
 a month in advance. The process is much simpler if the drone already resides in
 the country where operations will take place.
 o Third-party drone service providers have access to DJI drones. As a national lab,
 NREL cannot purchase or use any Chinese-based aircraft which excludes most
 viable drone products. Third-party foreign services are not subject to such
 requirements and for future trips we recommend using a service that can provide a
 Matrice, which was used for this task, and is the best off-the-shelf option for NIO
 measurements. As long as data collection activities are specified as an end use
 agreement and an external project partner on the team contracts the pilot, the US
 federal regulations regarding the use of DJI drones do not apply.
 o The drone pilot is likely to be fluent in the native language, and can also support
 the team as a translator, and can communicate quickly and easily with plant
 operations staff and the control room. This helped operations in the field run
 smoothly and safely.
 • Additional development is needed to the automation to produce reliable results for
 differing plant conditions. Progress was slowed due to unforeseen challenges with
 specific data conditions at a new field. Work needs to be done to make the heliostat
 feature detection more general, requiring more training data. The main issues that need to
 be resolved are:
 o Reliable heliostat corner detection. The algorithm calculates the precise camera
 position using photogrammetry and for that it needs at least 3 reference points in
 the image, for which we use the outer 4 corners of the heliostat. Our corner

 9

 detection algorithm worked reliability for heliostats at Crescent Dunes, but did not
 operate with a sufficient success rate to produce viable data for Cerro Dominador
 heliostats. The coloring of the sand and tower at Cerro Dominador is very
 different from that of previous fields and this restricted the automated processing.
 The algorithm needs to be adapted using additional machine learning techniques
 with a larger quantity and variety of heliostat training data to allow the software to
 be generalizable to more solar fields.
 o The Cerro Dominador plant is unique in that the centrally located tower has a
 large hole in the middle. The algorithm our team developed expects a solid tower
 to appear in the reflection with having sky on either side, but also having sky
 appear in the middle of the tower confused the edge detection algorithm for a
 subset of frames and degraded the quality of the results. The NIO team needs to
 characterize the tower geometry conditions that allow for effective data
 processing.
 • Another obstacle to data processing was mirror quality. The team does not have a
 sufficient understanding of how lighting and soiling conditions impact the viability of
 optical error results. More work is required to characterize conditions to allow for
 successful data collection and processing before the tool can be marketed as a service.
 Reflectivity measurements should be collected on NIO measured heliostats whenever
 possible to correlate reflectivity with the quality of the results.
Based on these lessons learned, the team has identified the following best practices for future
NIO tests. These are the items the team would have done differently if they could repeat the
project again.

 • Verify understanding of drone flight laws with an employee in the local flight agency 6
 months in advance of the planned trip and direct a project partner to hire a local drone
 service provider that can supply a DJI Matrice drone. The pilot must be bilingual in the
 native language and English.
 • Prioritize collection of high-quality data on a small number of mirrors instead of data of
 questionable quality on many mirrors. Ensure that mirrors being measured have been
 recently cleaned and have a specular reflectance of at least 90%. Collect at least 10
 repeated measurements on all mirrors for uncertainty analysis. If possible collect
 reflectance measurements and BCS measurements on the NIO measured mirrors when in
 the field. Ideally the BCS measurements occur at the same time and with the heliostat in
 the same position as when it is being drone scanned for NIO measurements.
 • Test processing capabilities prior to trip if possible by instructing plant operators to do
 either ground based or drone measurements on a small group of mirrors. Test the
 effectiveness of the algorithm, identify and implement updates if necessary, and devise
 test plan based on processing capability.
Based on these lessons learned, the main tasks of FY25 will be to improve the reliability and
precisely define the necessary data collection procedures to achieve accuracy targets for
deployment on a single heliostat:

 • Through a recently funded lab call, NREL will deploy optical metrology capabilities at
 NREL’s Flatirons campus to be offered as a service to heliostat technology developers,
