Pregnancy in a young woman with bipolar disorder
Authors: Shira Gertsman, Deniz Bengi, Crystal T. Clark, Benicio N. Frey
Published: 2026

A 30-year-old woman presents to her family physician at 8 weeks’ gestation. She received a diagnosis of bipolar disorder I at age 25 years when she was admitted to hospital for a mixed episode. Initial treatment with quetiapine was changed to lithium at age 27 years, after another mood episode. Her mood has been stable for 3 years; she takes lithium 600 mg every 12 hours. Her most recent serum lithium level is 0.9 mmol/L at 12 hours postdose (therapeutic range 0.6 to 1.2 mmol/L).

What are the risks associated with bipolar disorder in the perinatal period?

Pregnancies in patients with bipolar disorder are at a higher risk of obstetric and fetal complications than unaffected pregnancies; complications include preterm birth, gestational hypertension, microcephaly, low birth weight, and congenital malformations.1 Risk mechanisms are multifactorial, including modifiable risk factors (e.g., smoking, cannabis use, obesity) and illness-associated behaviours (e.g., inconsistent prenatal care due to low energy and motivation, or uncontrollable impulsive behaviours).1 Individuals who abruptly stop medication during pregnancy are at a 50% risk of mood disorder relapse within 2 weeks of discontinuation.2 Postpartum, the risk of a mood episode is 23% with treatment and 66% without.3

How can these risks be managed during pregnancy and lactation?

Family doctors, nurse practitioners, or general obstetricians can provide perinatal care for patients with bipolar disorder whose pregnancies are otherwise uncomplicated. General or reproductive psychiatry services may be consulted in high-risk cases in which close monitoring is required or if psychiatric complications arise.

Risks for perinatal mood episodes and postpartum psychosis can be mitigated by pharmacotherapy and sleep protection.4 Mood stabilizers may be continued during pregnancy and lactation, except for carbamazepine during the first trimester of pregnancy and valproate (Table 1).4 For patients on lithium, holding lithium treatment for 24 to 48 hours before delivery and restarting the prepregnancy dose immediately postpartum prevents transient neonatal adverse effects (e.g., low muscle tone, sedation, breathing or feeding difficulties, or jaundice) and maternal toxicity postpartum.6

Patients should be screened at their regular prenatal and postpartum appointments for sleep disruption and manic or depressive symptoms, using the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale.7 Postpartum, clinicians should inquire about social supports and encourage a partner or support person to take turns responding to the baby overnight to protect the patient’s sleep. Pharmacotherapy should be considered for patients with ongoing insomnia despite behavioural and psychoeducational interventions. Although evidence is limited, trazodone may be used as needed during pregnancy and lactation, with a careful risk–benefit discussion with the patient.8 If ineffective, lorazepam or zopiclone may be used for short durations with monitoring for excessive sedation.8 If a chest-feeding parent uses sedatives or mood stabilizers, a family physician or pediatrician should inquire about behavioural changes or poor feeding in the infant.8,9

What monitoring is required for a pregnant person taking mood stabilizers?

Serum lithium levels should be measured at the first visit, at least once per trimester, and before delivery.4 Given lithium’s narrow therapeutic window and exclusive renal elimination, the dose required to maintain a patient’s prepregnancy therapeutic serum level during pregnancy may need to be increased owing to increasing renal clearance. The monitoring frequency of lithium should be increased if the patient exhibits mood symptoms (which may indicate a low level), signs and symptoms of toxicity (e.g., gastrointestinal distress, tremors, polydipsia and polyuria), or pregnancy complications that affect lithium excretion (e.g., hypertensive disorders), and the dose adjusted as required to maintain prepregnancy levels. Lamotrigine requires similar monitoring and dose adjustment to maintain serum levels, but most other mood-stabilizing medications do not (Table 1).4

Case revisited

The patient’s family physician and psychiatrist monitored her throughout pregnancy. Given her risk for relapse into a mood episode (history of previous relapse, hospital admission, and a mixed episode), she was counselled to continue lithium. When she was at about 14 weeks’ gestation, her partner noted increased irritability; her lithium levels were subtherapeutic, at 0.4 mmol/L. Her physician uptitrated lithium to 750 mg every 12 hours to return to her prepregnancy therapeutic level of 0.9 mmol/L. Lithium was held at spontaneous labour onset, then resumed at her prepregnancy dose immediately postpartum.

The patient’s partner noted mildly elevated mood and energy and sleep disruption 2 weeks postpartum. The patient began pumping breast milk and her partner assumed overnight feedings. Her family physician prescribed trazodone 25 to 50 mg as needed for sleep and confirmed therapeutic serum lithium levels. The patient’s mood symptoms stabilized as her sleep improved. Her infant met normal growth and developmental milestones at their 6-month checkup.
