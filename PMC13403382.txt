Fecal Microbiota Transplantation and Microbiota‐Based Therapeutics in Allogeneic Hematopoietic Stem Cell Transplantation: Current Evidence and Future Directions
Authors: Bálint Gergely Szabó, Dorina Korózs, Michal Bator, Krisztina Jeszenszky, Viktor Lakatos, László Gopcsa, Péter Reményi, János Sinkó
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The intestinal microbiome is a key regulator of immune homeostasis, metabolism, and epithelial barrier integrity. In patients with malignant hematological diseases, particularly those undergoing hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, microbiome perturbations by reduced diversity, pathobiont expansion, and loss of beneficial metabolites are common as a consequence of exposure to cytotoxic therapy and broad‐spectrum antimicrobials. Accordingly, enteral microbiome manipulation has emerged as a promising strategy. We performed a narrative review of the literature in PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, and the Web of Science from inception to October 2025. We focused on adult hematology and HSCT populations and synthesized evidence across microbiome‐directed interventions, including fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) and emerging standardized microbiota products, as well as adjunctive strategies such as pre‐, pro‐, and postbiotics, dietary modulation, and microbiome‐sparing antimicrobial practices. Available clinical evidence, predominantly from case series, small cohorts and a limited number of randomized trials, suggests that FMT is feasible in selected immunocompromised patients and may be beneficial for recurrent Clostridioides difficile infection, multidrug‐resistant organism decolonization and steroid‐refractory gastrointestinal GvHD. Mechanistic data support pleiotropic effects of microbiome restoration, including replenishment of immunoregulatory metabolites, improved colonization resistance and reinforcement of mucosal function. While most reported adverse events are mild, rare transmission events and product variability necessitate for rigorous donor screening, standardized manufacturing and regulatory oversight. Key knowledge gaps include patient selection, optimal timing, dosing strategies, durability of benefit and integration with concurrent medications. In conclusion, microbiome‐based interventions may transition from rescue therapy toward a structured component of supportive care in hematologic malignancy management.

According to multiple analyses, the human intestine harbors more than 3 million distinct microbial genes. Moreover, the total number of bacteria colonizing the human body, predominantly residing within the gastrointestinal tract, is estimated to range from approximately 1013 to 1014 cells, meaning that bacterial cells outnumber the total number of somatic and germ cells in a healthy adult human by at least an order of magnitude. In addition, it has been estimated that 1 g of fecal matter contains approximately 1012 bacterial cells, and studies suggest that the human gut microbiota comprises more than 1000 distinct bacterial species. Despite this large‐scale diversity, data derived from analyses of the human microbiota indicate that the gut microbiota is only composed of 12 bacterial phyla, of which approximately 93.5% are represented by the phyla Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, Proteobacteria, and Actinobacteria, and among dominant ones, Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes constitute the predominant components in healthy individuals. 1

It is also established that the human gut microbiome is essential to immune regulation, host metabolism and intestinal homeostasis. In patients with malignant hematological diseases, gut microbiome perturbations are common, driven by disease pathology, chemotherapy, irradiation and frequent exposure to systemic antimicrobials. 2 , 3 These factors may culminate in dysbiosis, characterized by reduced microbial diversity, the expansion of opportunistic pathogens and diminished production of beneficial metabolites such as short‐chain fatty acids (SCFA). Subsequently, dysbiosis is increasingly recognized as a prognostic marker and potential mediator of adverse clinical outcomes in hematologic malignancies and the setting of allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT). 4 , 5 For example, in a single‐center observational cohort study of allogeneic HSCT recipients, Taur et al. reported an association between reduced intestinal bacterial diversity and increased mortality after transplantation, while a later large multicenter observational study by Peled et al. similarly associated lower intestinal diversity at the time of neutrophil engraftment with inferior overall survival and transplant‐related outcomes. 6 , 7

In recent years, fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT), the transfer of stool‐derived microbial communities from a healthy donor to a recipient, has emerged as one of the strategies for microbiome restoration in hematology. Initially established for recurrent Clostridioides (formerly Clostridium) difficile infection (CDI) on the basis of randomized trials and large clinical studies conducted predominantly in non‐hematology recurrent‐CDI populations, FMT is now being explored for additional indications relevant to immunocompromised hosts, including steroid‐refractory acute gastrointestinal GvHD, multidrug‐resistant organism (MDRO) decolonization and post‐HSCT microbiome reconstitution. In hematology‐specific settings, available evidence is derived mainly from case series, single‐center cohorts, non‐randomized comparative studies, early phase trials and one randomized phase II trial, suggesting feasibility and possible biological activity rather than definitive efficacy across all indications (Table 1 ). However, high‐quality evidence remains limited, particularly due to the small number of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) within the field of hematology.

In this narrative review, we aimed to summarize current concepts, key limitations, and future directions in enteral microbiome manipulation among adults treated for malignant hematological diseases (Figure 1 ). Rather than considering FMT and related interventions solely as supportive care measures, we frame microbiome manipulation as an emerging therapeutic modality. Particular emphasis is placed on FMT and standardized microbiota‐based products among HSCT recipients, including their potential role in recurrent CDI, MDRO decolonization, microbiome reconstitution, and acute or chronic gastrointestinal GvHD.

LITERATURE SEARCH STRATEGY

A targeted literature search was performed in PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library from inception to 31 October 2025, supplemented by manual screening of reference lists of key publications. Search terms combined free‐text keywords relating to hematologic malignancy and cellular therapies and enteral microbiome manipulation. The full search strategy is provided in the Supplementary File . No language restriction was applied. The scope consisted of microbiome‐directed interventions relevant to the target population, including FMT and related approaches (microbiota‐derived products, live biotherapeutics), strategies aimed at restoring microbial diversity or function (probiotics, prebiotics, synbiotics, postbiotics, dietary interventions), and care practices that may indirectly modify the microbiome (e.g., antimicrobial exposure patterns and decolonization strategies). Titles and abstracts were screened for relevance to adult patients with malignant hematological diseases, potentially relevant articles were then assessed in full text. Priority was given to randomized controlled trials, prospective interventional studies, controlled observational studies, clinically informative cohort studies, and systematic or narrative reviews addressing microbiome manipulation in hematology or HSCT. When direct hematology‐specific evidence was limited, mechanistic, translational, or non‐hematology studies were included, if they supported biologically plausible concepts relevant to the review question. Studies focusing on pediatric populations, animal models not directly translatable to humans, and reports without sufficient patient outcome data were excluded from the clinical evidence synthesis, although selected preclinical studies were retained when they informed mechanistic hypotheses. Conference abstracts were considered only when they reported relevant data on emerging microbiome‐based interventions or regulatory‐stage products of ongoing clinical trials, for which full peer‐reviewed publications were not yet available.

Study selection was performed iteratively by the authors during manuscript development. From included reports, we extracted study design, clinical setting, population and treatment context, intervention characteristics, donor screening and manufacturing details for FMT or microbiota products, follow‐up duration, comparator use, safety signals, and clinically meaningful outcomes, such as GvHD, bloodstream infections, MDRO colonization, recurrent CDI, and mortality. As this manuscript was designed as a narrative review, formal risk‐of‐bias scoring or GRADE assessment was not performed. Instead, a structured qualitative appraisal was performed, explicitly considering study design, clinical setting, sample size, comparator use, outcome type, follow‐up duration, and directness of evidence when interpreting the literature. Approximately 150 publications and regulatory or guidance documents were reviewed and retained for narrative synthesis. When evidence was conflicting, we prioritized peer‐reviewed full‐text publications over abstracts, controlled and randomized studies over uncontrolled reports, larger and multicenter studies over small single‐center series, and clinically direct evidence from hematology or HSCT populations over indirect evidence from other populations. The manuscript was prepared following the SANRA Reporting System, established for narrative reviews. 8

ESTABLISHED AND EXPERIMENTAL INDICATIONS FOR FMT

The clinical maturity of FMT differs substantially across indications relevant to hematology. The most established indication remains recurrent CDI, where FMT and standardized microbiota‐based products have demonstrated efficacy in broader adult populations, although hematology‐ and HSCT‐specific evidence is still largely derived from small case series and observational studies, as well as immunocompromised subgroup data. 9 , 10 Therefore, in patients with malignant hematological diseases, FMT for recurrent CDI may be considered the indication with the strongest clinical rationale, but its use still requires careful patient selection, donor screening, and institutional expertise.

By contrast, MDRO decolonization should be considered a promising but incompletely validated indication. In a single‐center prospective cohort of 10 adult patients with hematological malignancies colonized with multidrug‐resistant bacteria before or after allogeneic HSCT, Battipaglia et al. reported MDRO decolonization in approximately 70% of recipients during follow‐up. 11 In another single‐center case series comparing eight MDRO‐colonized allogeneic HSCT recipients who received FMT with 11 colonized patients who did not receive FMT, Innes et al. reported lower decolonization rates and suggested possible clinical benefit, but the small sample size, non‐randomized design, and potential for spontaneous decolonization limit causal interpretation. 12 Overall, the durability of MDRO clearance remains uncertain, because studies differ in sampling frequency during follow‐up, and whether recurrent colonization or recolonization was systematically assessed.

FMT for steroid‐refractory or steroid‐dependent gastrointestinal GvHD is another area with encouraging but still non‐definitive evidence. Initial single‐center case series, including the four‐patient single‐center report by Kakihana et al. and subsequent small single‐center case series using upper or lower gastrointestinal routes, described clinical responses after FMT in selected allogeneic HSCT recipients with severe gastrointestinal GvHD. 13 , 14 Later evidence from prospective single‐arm studies, retrospective or non‐randomized comparative cohorts, and early phase interventional trials suggested that FMT or pooled microbiota products may be feasible and biologically active in this setting, with some studies reporting improvements in diarrhea, GvHD severity, microbial diversity and immune‐cell profiles. 11 , 12 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 However, most studies remain limited by small sample size, heterogeneous patient populations, variable timing relative to corticosteroids or ruxolitinib, differences in route and formulation, and limited controlled data. Therefore, FMT for steroid‐refractory gastrointestinal GvHD should currently be framed as a promising investigational or specialized‐center strategy rather than a well‐established standard‐of‐care.

ENTERAL MICROBIOME MANIPULATION AS A THERAPEUTIC MODALITY

Therapeutic framing, exposure, and response assessment

A useful conceptual shift is to regard enteral microbiome manipulation not merely as replacement of a depleted microbial community, but as a biologically active therapeutic intervention. In this framework, dysbiosis represents a modifiable disease‐associated state characterized by loss of microbial diversity, pathobiont domination, impaired colonization resistance, reduced production of immunoregulatory metabolites, and disruption of epithelial barrier function. 6 , 7 , 22 , 23 , 24 FMT and standardized microbiota‐based products may therefore be interpreted as mechanism‐based interventions designed to restore ecological and functional properties of the intestinal ecosystem. 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 This approach is particularly relevant in malignant hematology, where chemotherapy, mucosal injury, alloimmune inflammation, and repeated broad‐spectrum antimicrobial exposure converge on the gut microbiome‐barrier‐immune axis. 29 , 30 , 31

Unlike conventional pharmacological agents, the therapeutic exposure of FMT is not defined only by an administered mass or concentration of an active substance. Instead, exposure depends on whether viable and functionally active microbial communities reach the relevant intestinal niche, survive competing ecological pressures, and generate measurable biological effects. Key exposure‐related concepts include engraftment, persistence, and functional activity. 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 Engraftment refers to the establishment of donor‐derived or product‐derived microorganisms in the recipient gut; persistence describes the durability of this effect over time, while functional activity refers to restoration of microbial pathways, such as SCFA production, bile‐acid transformation, tryptophan metabolism, and colonization resistance. These concepts are highly relevant in hematology, because ongoing systemic antibiotics and conditioning‐related mucosal injury, along with immunosuppression, gastrointestinal GvHD, and nutritional restriction may all influence engraftment and therapeutic durability. 15 , 21 , 29 , 36 Consequently, repeat dosing or sequential microbiome‐directed treatment may be required in selected patients, particularly when dysbiosis is profound or when the first intervention is administered during systemic antimicrobial exposure. 18

Route and formulation are central determinants of microbiome exposure. Lower gastrointestinal delivery may provide direct colonic deposition but can be limited by mucosal friability, bleeding risk, and severe diarrhea, or procedural instability in HSCT recipients with gastrointestinal GvHD. In contrast, upper gastrointestinal administration has been used in single‐center case series and cohort studies of HSCT recipients but may introduce concerns regarding aspiration, gastric acidity, and small‐bowel contamination. 19 , 37 , 38 , 39 Oral capsules are increasingly preferred due to their non‐invasiveness, scalability, and compatibility with protective isolation workflows. In the randomized, double‐blind phase II trial by Rashidi et al. among allogeneic HCT recipients and patients with AML after neutrophil recovery, oral capsule‐based FMT was feasible and improved microbiome end points, although it did not significantly reduce infections. 21 However, capsule efficacy depends on capsule stability, gastric acid resistance, microbial viability, and delivery to the colon. 13 , 27 , 40 Formulation also matters, as fresh, frozen, lyophilized, pooled‐donor, spore‐based, and defined‐consortium products differ in microbial complexity, viability, stability, manufacturing reproducibility, and their theoretical safety profile. Thus, the clinical effect of microbiome manipulation should always be interpreted not only in terms of the patient‐level indication, but also in relation to product composition, route, and dosing schedules. 20 , 26 , 37 , 38 , 40 , 41 , 42 , 43 , 44

Future clinical development will probably require biomarkers that can define patient eligibility, quantify biological activity and predict clinical response. According to the literature, candidate microbiome biomarkers for eligibility include baseline intestinal alpha diversity, domination by Enterococcus or other pathobionts, depletion of obligate anaerobes such as Blautia, Faecalibacterium, or other SCFA‐producing taxa, and the presence of antimicrobial‐resistance genes within the intestinal resistome. 6 , 22 , 45 , 46 , 47 Functional biomarkers, such as stool concentrations of acetate, propionate, and butyrate, secondary bile‐acid profiles, tryptophan‐derived indoles, metagenomic pathways related to colonization resistance and markers of epithelial repair, may be even more informative. 48 , 49 , 50 , 51 , 52 , 53 , 54 , 55 , 56 Finally, microbiome readouts may be complemented by host‐response biomarkers, such as inflammatory cytokines, intestinal permeability markers, and established GvHD‐associated biomarkers including ST2 and REG3α. 14 , 24 , 57 , 58 , 59 Importantly, these biomarkers should not be treated merely as exploratory end points. Rather, they might serve as pharmacodynamic indicators of microbiome‐directed therapy and could ultimately guide dose selection, repeat administration, or escalation in the future.

Therapeutic development of microbiome‐based products

Product standardization is another prerequisite for the maturation of microbiome manipulation into a therapeutic field. Conventional donor‐derived FMT is intrinsically variable because donor microbiome composition, donor diet and medication exposure, stool collection procedures, such as processing and storage, route of delivery, and recipient ecology all influence the final biological exposure. From a drug‐development standpoint, this variability creates challenges that are uncommon for conventional small molecules but central to microbiome‐based therapeutics: defining the active substance, ensuring batch‐to‐batch consistency, establishing potency assays, controlling bioburden and transmissible pathogens, preserving viability, and linking product attributes to clinical outcomes. 27 , 38 , 41 , 43

Donor variability is one of the major determinants of product heterogeneity. In conventional FMT, each donor represents a complex ecological source material with distinct bacterial, fungal, viral, metabolomic, and resistome features. Even when donor screening is rigorous, the functional composition of donations may vary over time. Therapeutic‐development strategies that aim to reduce this variability may include repeated donor qualification, centralized stool banking, pooled‐donor manufacturing, defined microbial consortia, spore‐enriched preparations, and synthetic or rationally selected bacterial communities. 34 , 41 These approaches do not eliminate biological complexity, but they may improve reproducibility and allow the product to be characterized using predefined compositional, functional, and safety‐related quality attributes.

Release criteria are particularly important for microbiome products intended for immunocompromised recipients and may need to be stricter than in general populations. At minimum, they should include pathogen exclusion, donor eligibility verification, absence of clinically relevant MDROs, and documentation of manufacturing traceability. For more advanced products, release testing may also include viable microbial count, microbial diversity or taxonomic composition, absence of predefined resistance genes, limits for contaminants, stability after storage, and functional potency assays. Possible functional readouts include restoration of colonization resistance, production of SCFAs, bile‐acid transformation, suppression of pathobiont expansion, or other mechanism‐linked assays relevant to the intended indication.

Formulation stability is another key development issue. Fresh stool preparations may preserve a broader viable ecosystem but are logistically demanding and more difficult to standardize. Frozen preparations enable stool banking and batch testing, but require validated cryopreservation, storage, and transport conditions. Lyophilized and encapsulated formulations may improve scalability, patient acceptability, and compatibility with protective isolation workflows, but require evidence that processing does not compromise viability, ecological function, or delivery to the intended intestinal niche. Spore‐based products, such as SER‐109/Vowst, illustrate a different strategy: selecting a more stable microbial fraction that can be administered orally and manufactured with greater reproducibility. By contrast, rectally administered products such as RBX2660/Rebyota and pooled‐donor full‐ecosystem products such as MaaT013 aim to deliver broader microbial communities, but face different challenges regarding donor variability, storage, administration, and release testing. 40 , 44

Clinical end points should also be phase‐specific. In early phase studies, end points should prioritize safety, feasibility, and tolerability, as well as engraftment with proof of persistence, pharmacodynamic activity, and changes in microbiome or metabolomic function. In intermediate‐phase studies, end points should link biological activity to clinical signals, such as CDI recurrence, MDRO carriage density, bloodstream infection, diarrhea volume, steroid tapering, GvHD response, etc. Late‐phase trials should use clinically meaningful and indication‐specific end points, such as sustained CDI recurrence prevention, durable MDRO decolonization with reduced infection risk, day‐28 gastrointestinal response in acute GvHD, failure‐free survival, non‐relapse mortality, overall survival, or other validated composite end points. Importantly, end points used for recurrent CDI cannot be directly transferred to GvHD or MDRO decolonization because the biological target, expected time course, competing risks, and acceptable safety profile differ substantially between these indications.

Regulatory oversight is therefore inseparable from clinical development. In the United States, the FDA's guidance on live biotherapeutic products emphasizes chemistry, manufacturing, and control information for investigational products, including identity, purity, potency, manufacturing process, and stability considerations. 60 In recurrent CDI, the approvals of SER‐109/Vowst and RBX2660/Rebyota illustrate the transition from empirical FMT toward regulated microbiota‐based products: SER‐109/Vowst is an orally administered fecal microbiota spores product, while RBX2660/Rebyota is a rectally administered fecal microbiota product, both indicated for prevention of recurrent CDI in adults following antibacterial treatment. 40 , 44 However, these approvals cannot be extrapolated directly to hematology, HSCT, or GvHD indications without dedicated clinical evidence. In Europe, Regulation (EU) 2024/1938 on substances of human origin establishes a quality and safety framework relevant to substances intended for human application, while microbiome‐based medicinal products may also fall within medicinal‐product development pathways depending on product composition, processing, and intended use. 61 This evolving regulatory landscape moves the field toward more formalized quality systems, pharmacovigilance and cross‐border consistency, while also highlighting the requirement to distinguish conventional FMT and related stool‐bank‐derived preparations from industrially manufactured microbiome‐based medicinal products.

THE PATHOBIOLOGICAL BASIS FOR MICROBIOME MANIPULATION

Metabolic and immune dysregulation

The intestinal epithelium consists of a dynamically renewing layer of intestinal epithelial cells (IECs), which constitutes the first line of defense against enteric pathogens. The processes of epithelial cell proliferation, differentiation, and migration are regulated by a complex interplay of local growth factors, genetic determinants, environmental influences, and are highly dependent on the availability of cellular energy substrates. Substantial evidence indicates that SCFAs are critical modulators of these processes. 48 , 62 The principal SCFAs, such as acetate, propionate, and butyrate, are produced by commensal fiber‐fermenting bacteria through the fermentation of dietary substrates that are otherwise indigestible by the host. 48 , 49 Butyrate is a critical energy source for IECs and has well‐described immunomodulatory properties, promoting regulatory T‐cell differentiation and limiting the production of pro‐inflammatory cytokines. 63 SCFAs also display pronounced anti‐inflammatory properties through their function as histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitors. 64 , 65 In parallel, indole and related tryptophan‐derived metabolites activate the aryl hydrocarbon receptor, enhancing epithelial barrier function and stimulating interleukin‐22 (IL‐22) mediated mucosal repair. 50 Experimental studies have demonstrated that SCFAs can induce the expression of genes such as the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) and hypoxia‐inducible factor 1α (HIF‐1α). These effects are mediated by activation of the G protein‐coupled receptor 41 (GPR41) on the cell surface and concurrent inhibition of HDAC activity, ultimately promoting interleukin‐22 (IL‐22) production by CD4+ T lymphocytes and innate lymphoid cells (ILCs). 66 In vivo murine transplant models have also shown that loss of these microbial metabolites could associate with more severe GvHD, whereas supplementation appears protective. 59

Key gut microbial metabolites therefore have direct effects on mucosal integrity and immune regulation. Observational and translational studies in HSCT recipients, including single‐center and multicenter cohorts with serial stool sampling, have shown that the gut microbiome after transplantation is highly dynamic. In particular, depletion of SCFA‐producing taxa, including members of the Lachnospiraceae family, and between‐group differences in SCFA levels have been linked to an overall reduction in SCFA concentrations and to the development of GvHD. 6 , 22 , 29 , 46 , 51 , 52 , 67 , 68 In a translational observational study of allogeneic HSCT recipients with acute GvHD published in Blood Advances, Payen et al. reported a marked decline in SCFA production during acute GvHD and linked specific microbial taxa to the clinical course of the disease. Disease severity correlated inversely with several obligate anaerobes, including members of the Lachnospiraceae family and particularly the genus Blautia, as well as taxa within the Ruminococcaceae family. Consistent with this microbial depletion, patients with acute GvHD showed profound reductions in the major SCFAs: acetate (−75.8%), propionate (−95.8%), and butyrate (−94.6%). 47 A subsequent translational study published in 2025 supported these observations, suggesting that dysregulation of SCFA‐metabolizing bacterial communities may contribute to GvHD pathogenesis. 69 In return, dysbiosis in these patients may precipitate uncontrolled inflammatory responses, thereby facilitating the progression of GvHD. 30 For example, SCFAs are involved in the metabolic regulation of regulatory T cells (Tregs) through HDAC inhibition, which enhances histone H3 acetylation. This epigenetic modulation leads to increased expression of the transcription factor FoxP3 and induces the proliferation of Treg cells via activation of the GPR43 receptor, thereby augmenting the secretion of the anti‐inflammatory cytokine interleukin‐10 (IL‐10). 70 , 71 Furthermore, dendritic cells and macrophages also express G protein‐coupled receptors capable of interacting with SCFAs, resulting in attenuation of pro‐inflammatory cytokine production while simultaneously enhancing anti‐inflammatory immune responses. 72 Finally, butyrate and propionate supplementation have been reported to attenuate chronic GvHD in mice, and indole derivatives to reduce the severity of acute gastrointestinal GvHD. 53 , 54

FMT may help reconstitute metabolite‐producing taxa, thereby restoring SCFA production and other beneficial metabolic pathways. Mechanistically, this could shift the intestinal immune milieu toward a more tolerogenic state, favoring expansion of FoxP3+ regulatory T cells and dampening pathogenic Th1/Th17 responses. In the first single‐center clinical case series of FMT for steroid‐resistant acute gastrointestinal GvHD, Kakihana et al. reported that responders demonstrated an increase in circulating regulatory T cells concurrent with clinical improvement, supporting the hypothesis that FMT can promote immune tolerance in this setting. 14 Although comprehensive cytokine profiling remains limited in human studies, it has been postulated that microbiome restoration may reduce concentrations of circulatory pro‐inflammatory mediators implicated in GvHD‐associated tissue injury (TNF‐α, IL‐6, and IL‐1β) by re‐establishing commensal‐driven immunoregulatory feedback loops. In addition, improved colonization resistance after FMT may reduce the relative abundance of endotoxin‐producing Gram‐negative bacteria, potentially lowering systemic endotoxemia resulting in inflammation. 15

Gut barrier dysintegrity and microbiome‐drug interactions

The intestinal epithelium and mucus layer form a critical barrier that prevents bacterial translocation from the gut lumen into the bloodstream and lymphoid tissues. Both dysbiosis and GvHD can compromise this barrier. GvHD induces epithelial apoptosis and depletion of mucus‐producing goblet cells, whereas dysbiosis can favor the expansion of mucus‐degrading taxa. 23 , 24 , 73 Restoration of a more “eubiotic” microbiome through FMT may, therefore, support barrier repair via several complementary mechanisms. Commensal bacteria, particularly butyrate‐producing taxa, have been implicated in promoting mucus production and enhancing tight‐junction integrity, thereby helping the restoration of the compromised epithelial lining. 55 , 56 In addition, FMT may reduce the relative abundance of mucinolytic pathobionts. For example, the antibiotic‐driven expansion of Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron has been shown to erode the colonic mucus layer and exacerbate GvHD in murine models. 73 By reintroducing a diverse microbial community, FMT may limit the dominance of any single mucus‐degrading organism, preserving the epithelial function. A more intact barrier, in turn, would be expected to reduce systemic exposure to microbial products such as lipopolysaccharide or flagellin, which can amplify inflammatory cascades. 57 , 74 , 75 While human data are still emerging and are derived mainly from small clinical studies and translational analyses, it is plausible that restoration of the gut barrier may contribute to the clinical signals reported after FMT in gastrointestinal GvHD and infection‐related outcomes.

A particularly important, yet still underdeveloped mechanistic dimension is pharmacomicrobiomics, referring to the bidirectional interactions between the gut microbiome and drug disposition, efficacy, and toxicity. This concept is highly relevant in malignant hematology and HSCT because patients are exposed to dense and overlapping pharmacological pressures, including systemic corticosteroids, cytotoxic chemotherapy, and conditioning regimens, as well as antibacterial, antiviral and antifungal agents, and supportive medications. These drugs may reshape the intestinal ecosystem, while microbial communities may in turn influence drug absorption, metabolism, enterohepatic recirculation, and gastrointestinal toxicity. 26 , 31 , 76 , 77 , 78 , 79 , 80 Thus, microbiome manipulation should not be viewed in isolation, but rather as part of a dynamic host‐microbiome‐drug network.

The best characterized example in the HSCT setting concerns mycophenolate mofetil. Mycophenolic acid undergoes glucuronidation and biliary excretion, followed by intestinal deconjugation of mycophenolic acid glucuronide by bacterial β‐glucuronidase enzymes, enabling enterohepatic recirculation. In an observational pharmacokinetic‐microbiome study of allo‐HCT recipients, Saqr et al. reported that reduced enterohepatic recirculation of mycophenolate was associated with lower blood concentrations and distinct stool microbiome profiles, including enrichment of selected Bacteroides taxa in patients with higher enterohepatic recirculation. 76 Subsequent pharmacomicrobiomic work, including clinical analyses in kidney transplant recipients and mechanistic enzyme‐level studies, has further supported the concept that microbiome‐derived β‐glucuronidase activity may explain interindividual variability in mycophenolic acid recirculation and exposure, with specific microbial species and enzyme variants contributing to mycophenolic acid reactivation. 77 , 78 These observations are clinically relevant because underexposure may compromise GvHD prophylaxis, whereas overexposure may increase gastrointestinal toxicity, cytopenias, and infectious complications. Accordingly, future studies may need to integrate functional β‐glucuronidase activity of the microbiome composition into pharmacokinetic models of mycophenolate exposure in HSCT recipients. Other immunosuppressants may also be affected by microbiome‐dependent mechanisms, although the evidence is strongest in solid‐organ transplantation and remains less developed in HSCT. Tacrolimus has high interindividual pharmacokinetic variability that is incompletely explained by host pharmacogenomics and clinical covariates. Evidence from in vitro bacterial metabolism experiments and clinical pharmacokinetic studies in kidney transplant recipients suggests that gut bacteria can convert tacrolimus into less active metabolites, while microbial metabolites may also regulate intestinal ABCB1/P‐glycoprotein expression and thereby influence tacrolimus absorption. 79 , 80 , 81 Although direct HSCT‐specific data remain limited, these findings are conceptually important because calcineurin inhibitor exposure is a critical determinant of both GvHD prevention and its drug‐related toxicity. In the future, microbiome‐informed dosing models could potentially complement therapeutic drug monitoring, especially in patients with severe dysbiosis and gastrointestinal GvHD.

Antibacterial agents represent another major pharmacomicrobiomic pressure. Their relevance extends beyond the well‐known risk of dysbiosis, because antibiotic‐induced depletion of obligate anaerobes and SCFA‐producing taxa may modify the biological response to immunosuppression and GvHD‐directed therapy. In translational studies combining human allo‐HCT cohorts with murine transplant models, as well as in clinical observational studies of HSCT recipients, broad‐spectrum antibiotics with anti‐anaerobic activity have been associated with reduced microbial diversity, dominant loss of protective commensals, and increased GvHD‐related mortality. 24 , 30 , 31 , 82 , 83 More recently, a large single‐center retrospective cohort study of 2023 allo‐HCT recipients showed that antibiotic choices and timing during the early transplant period were associated with subsequent acute GvHD rates, with carbapenems and β‐lactam/β‐lactamase inhibitor combinations in the first post‐transplant weeks showing particularly strong associations. 84 Antifungal prophylaxis and the intestinal mycobiome add an additional layer of complexity. Patient with malignant hematological diseases frequently receive azoles, echinocandins or amphotericin‐based prophylaxis or treatment, depending on local epidemiology, treatment phase and prior fungal history. While these strategies are essential for prevention of invasive fungal disease, they may also influence fungal ecology, bacterial‐fungal interactions, and recovery of the intestinal mycobiome. 17 , 85 , 86 In a longitudinal observational study of allo‐HCT recipients with serial intestinal mycobiome profiling, fungal communities changed dynamically after transplantation, and expansion of Candida, particularly Candida parapsilosis complex species, was associated with worse overall survival and higher transplant‐related mortality, independently of candidemia. 85 In a phase 1 interventional study of FMT for GvHD, profiling of the fungal microbiome appeared feasible, but donor‐to‐recipient transfer of fungal taxa was limited or inconsistent. 17 These observations suggest that future microbiome‐directed studies should also aim to evaluate fungal engraftment and ecosystem dynamics, particularly in patients exposed to prolonged antifungal prophylaxis.

The relationship between the microbiome and corticosteroid response in gastrointestinal GvHD is another emerging area. Corticosteroids remain first‐line therapy for acute GvHD, yet a substantial proportion of patients with lower gastrointestinal involvement become steroid‐refractory. Because steroid response depends not only on systemic immunosuppression but also on epithelial repair, microbial metabolite production, and the control of pathobiont expansion, the intestinal microbiome may plausibly influence treatment responsiveness. 14 , 24 , 47 , 52 , 53 , 54 In addition, an exploratory shotgun metagenomic analysis of patients with lower gastrointestinal acute GvHD suggested that fecal microbiome features may help distinguish steroid‐responsive from steroid‐refractory disease, although a subsequent clinical cohort study did not identify consistent pre‐onset taxonomic differences between steroid‐refractory and steroid‐sensitive gastrointestinal GvHD, noting that the evidence remains limited. 84 , 87 In parallel, early clinical evidence from single‐center case series and prospective interventional studies of FMT in steroid‐refractory or steroid‐dependent gastrointestinal GvHD suggests that restoration of microbial diversity can coincide with clinical improvement, reduced inflammatory activity, and changes in immune‐cell composition. 16 , 18

Finally, the interaction between the microbiome and antineoplastic therapy should be considered as a future research priority. For example, preclinical data indicate that the intestinal microbiota can modulate the anticancer immune effects of cyclophosphamide, while broader translational literature suggests that microbiome composition may influence chemotherapy‐associated gastrointestinal toxicity and treatment efficacy. 88 , 89

In sum, mechanistic studies support the notion that FMT and related microbiome‐directed interventions may exert pleiotropic benefits, such as reconstitution of metabolite‐producing communities and the promotion of immunoregulatory pathways, with reduction of pro‐inflammatory microbial products and reinforcement of gut barrier integrity, and in return, potential modulation of host‐microbiome‐drug interactions. These effects are likely positioned on the continuity of the microbiome‐barrier‐immune‐drug axis, which is central to several complications of intensive hematology care. Ongoing work in germ‐free and antibiotic‐perturbed murine transplant models, complemented by integrated metagenomic, metabolomic, microbiome, pharmacokinetic, and clinical analyses from human interventional studies, may help clarify which microbial taxa and functional pathways are most relevant to patient benefit. 24 , 36

SAFETY AND CHALLENGES OF FMT

General safety profile

Ensuring safety is a priority when performing FMT in immunocompromised patients with hematological diseases. Overall, the published literature suggests that FMT is feasible and can be performed with an acceptable safety profile in this vulnerable population, provided that a strict donor screening and standardized procedures are applied. Across multiple single‐center case series, prospective cohorts, and early phase interventional studies collectively including > 100 transplanted patients, FMT has not been associated with excess mortality or an unexpected signal of infectious complications compared with comparator groups, although the available evidence remains underpowered for rare events. 25 Moreover, a systematic review and meta‐analysis of FMT across heterogeneous patient populations concluded that immunocompromised patients appeared to tolerate FMT with adverse event rates broadly similar to those reported in immunocompetent individuals. 90

Reported adverse events (AEs) are most often mild and self‐limited, typically consisting of gastrointestinal symptoms (e.g., abdominal cramping, transient diarrhea, flatulence) or short‐lived fever shortly after administration. For example, in a single‐center case series of seven HSCT recipients receiving FMT for recurrent CDI, Webb et al. observed no serious AEs, as minor bloating and urgency were the only events reported. 39 Likewise, in a case series from two German tertiary centers including 11 patients treated with FMT for refractory intestinal GvHD, Goeser et al. reported no FMT‐attributable infections or toxicities. 19 Nevertheless, rare but serious risks have been documented and have prompted heightened regulatory scrutiny. In 2019, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration issued a safety alert after two immunocompromised adults developed invasive infections with multidrug‐resistant Escherichia coli following investigational FMT, in a setting where donor material had not been adequately screened for resistant organisms. This incident highlights that pathogen transmission remains on of the principal hazards of FMT. Consequently, regulatory agencies now mandate comprehensive donor screening, including stool cultures and fecal PCR for MDROs, to minimize transmission risk. 91

Viral transmission represents an additional concern. In a randomized, double‐blind phase II FMT trial conducted among allogeneic HCT recipients and patients with AML, a fatal adenovirus infection was reported in temporal association with FMT in a steroid‐refractory GvHD patient. 26 It was unclear if the virus came from the donor or was reactivated in the host, but it highlights the need to screen donors for latent viruses, and to use nucleic acid amplification tests for relevant enteric viruses. Accordingly, donor screening typically includes detailed health questionnaires to exclude risk factors (e.g., recent antibiotic exposure, high‐risk behaviors, travel‐associated risks), serologic testing (e.g., HIV, hepatitis A/B/C, syphilis), and stool testing for enteric pathogens (e.g., bacterial culture/PCR for Salmonella, Shigella, Campylobacter, C. difficile, ova and parasites, norovirus, among others). 39 Many protocols also exclude donors with carriage of ESBL‐producing or carbapenem‐resistant organisms. 91 In practice, only a small proportion of potential donors, often in the range of 5–10%, meet all eligibility criteria. This rigorous selection vastly reduces infection risk: for instance, in the study mentioned earlier, donors were screened for 32 different pathogens, and none of the recipients experienced infectious complications during follow‐up. 39

Beyond infectious complications, additional safety considerations include procedure‐related risks, most notably aspiration when FMT is administered via an upper gastrointestinal route, and theoretical immunological concerns. In allogeneic HSCT recipients, one hypothesis is whether FMT could transfer immunogenic material (e.g., residual host cells, antigens or microbial determinants) that might precipitate or amplify alloreactivity, thereby triggering de novo GvHD or graft‐versus‐host responses directed against the intestinal ecosystem. In practice, stool filtrates are largely acellular, and de novo GvHD directly attributable to FMT has not been convincingly documented. Although in the randomized, double‐blind phase II trial by Rashidi et al. among allogeneic HCT recipients and patients with AML the incidence of acute GvHD was numerically higher in the FMT arm (21% vs. 8%), the clinical significance of this imbalance is uncertain. 21 This finding may reflect chance variation, baseline differences between groups, or unmeasured factors related to immune reconstitution rather than a causal effect of FMT. Overall, available evidence and expert consensus suggest that, when performed under rigorous protocols, FMT does not appear to meaningfully increase the risk of GvHD or other immune‐mediated complications, although definitive conclusions will require larger controlled studies.

Regulatory and logistical challenges

In many jurisdictions, FMT is regulated as a biological medicinal product or an equivalent category. In the United States, the FDA has permitted the use of FMT for recurrent CDI under an enforcement‐discretion framework. However, use outside this indication generally requires an investigational new drug pathway and explicit informed consent. This regulatory landscape can limit access and complicate delivery outside formal clinical trials. Across Europe, regulatory approaches remain heterogeneous. Some countries have implemented centralized stool banks and established pathways that facilitate access for selected indications, whereas in others, case‐by‐case authorizations and additional institutional or regulatory approvals are required. 92 Beyond regulation, the standardization of FMT material is another challenge, as FMT preparations are inherently donor‐ and batch‐dependent, resulting in variability in composition and potency. To address these limitations, multiple groups are developing more standardized microbiome‐based therapeutics, including defined bacterial consortia and encapsulated formulations, which may improve quality control and regulatory acceptability in the future. 37 , 41

From a practical perspective, administering FMT to acutely ill hematology patients requires coordination and institutional infrastructure. For example, many HSCT recipients are in protective isolation units, and the handling and delivery of stool‐derived material require strict infection‐prevention procedures, clear workflows and close collaboration among clinicians, infection control, pharmacy, and nursing staff. The route of administration is also a consideration. Lower gastrointestinal delivery via colonoscopy or enema is often not feasible in severe GvHD because of mucosal friability, bleeding risk, and procedural burden, therefore, upper gastrointestinal delivery (e.g., naso‐gastric/naso‐jejunal tubes) or oral capsules are often favored. Notably, in the randomized, double‐blind phase II trial among allogeneic HCT recipients and patients with AML, Rashidi et al. successfully administered oral FMT capsules after neutrophil recovery without an unexpected infectious safety signal, supporting the feasibility of capsule‐based delivery in selected patients. 21 In small single‐center case series of gastrointestinal GvHD, rectal delivery via enema or colonoscopy has been used once mucosal integrity improved. 18 Each route has distinct advantages and limitations, but in many center capsules or tube‐based administration are the most practical options for severely ill patients.

Timing and integration with concurrent therapies remain clinically challenging. Ongoing systemic antibiotics may impair engraftment by suppressing organisms within the transplanted community, and consequently, several protocols recommend withholding non‐essential antibiotics for a defined interval before and after FMT. Concomitant medications may also influence microbiome dynamics. For example, antifungal prophylaxis could affect the mycobiome, and broader host‐drug‐microbiome interactions may complicate engraftment. Finally, both patients and providers may have reservations about FMT, particularly in high‐risk settings such as HSCT and GvHD. Clear counseling is therefore also essential, framing FMT as a form of microbiome restoration, with transparent discussion of rationale, benefits, uncertainties, and safety.

In summary, the main safety challenges associated with FMT in hematology, most notably pathogen transmission, are increasingly being mitigated through rigorous donor screening, standardized processing procedures, and evolving oversight. When such safeguards are implemented, available clinical data suggest that FMT can be performed with an acceptable safety profile, even in profoundly immunosuppressed patients. 25 Nonetheless, future trials should incorporate systematic safety surveillance to detect any potential FMT‐related transmission events.

COMPARISON TO ALTERNATIVE MICROBIOME MODULATION THERAPIES

Probiotics

An alternative approach to modulating the gut microbiome is the administration of probiotics, which are live microorganisms (most commonly selected strains of Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, or Saccharomyces) intended to confer a health benefit. Probiotics are appealing because they are widely available and familiar to both clinicians and patients. However, the ability of conventional probiotics to restore profound post‐HSCT dysbiosis is probably constrained by ecological and formulation‐related limitations. Most probiotic products contain only one or a small number of strains, whereas HSCT‐associated dysbiosis is characterized by broad loss of taxonomic and functional diversity. Human studies have shown that probiotic mucosal colonization is variable and host‐dependent and that probiotic administration after antibiotics may result in delayed or incomplete recovery of the indigenous microbiome compared with autologous FMT. 93 , 94 In the allogeneic HSCT setting, a randomized trial of Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG did not detect an appreciable probiotic‐related change in the gut microbiome or protection against GvHD. 95 By contrast, autologous FMT after allo‐HSCT‐associated antibiotic injury and strain‐level analyses of FMT in other clinical settings indicate that transfer of complex microbial communities can restore broader microbial diversity and donor‐ or baseline‐derived microbial taxa. 33 , 34 , 96 Thus, probiotics are unlikely to reconstitute the microbial diversity to the extent achievable with FMT. Moreover, the sustained engraftment of probiotic strains in a severely perturbed gut ecosystem may be limited, particularly when ecological niches are dominated by pathobionts or when antimicrobials suppress newly introduced organisms. Finally, although outside hematology, Spencer et al. reported that probiotic use in patients with melanoma receiving PD‐1 blockade was associated with lower gut microbial diversity and less favorable immunotherapy‐related outcomes, providing a cautionary example that probiotic supplementation may be context‐dependent and should not be assumed to be uniformly beneficial. 97

Nevertheless, early clinical studies suggest that probiotics and related interventions may exert some immunomodulatory effects in selected settings. For example, in a non‐randomized clinical study among allogeneic HSCT recipients, Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG administration was associated with shorter duration of diarrhea and a possible reduction in GvHD severity. 98 More recently, in a randomized trial among allogeneic HSCT recipients, a multi‐strain prebiotic/probiotic intervention was associated with increased regulatory T‐cell frequencies and a lower incidence of severe acute GvHD in the intervention arm. 99 These findings might suggest a positive clinical effect, but caution is warranted, as probiotic use in neutropenic or severely immunosuppressed patients has been associated with invasive infections, including case reports of Lactobacillus bacteremia and Saccharomyces fungemia attributable to probiotic organisms. 100 , 101 , 102 , 103

Prebiotics and dietary interventions

Prebiotics are fermentable dietary fibers or compounds that selectively promote the growth and metabolic activity of beneficial gut microbes. Common examples include fructo‐oligosaccharides, galacto‐oligosaccharides, inulin, and resistant starch. 104 In the allogeneic HSCT setting, the rationale is that supplying these substrates may help restore a more eubiotic microbial community, particularly by supporting SCFA‐producing taxa. In this context, in a clinical study of allogeneic HSCT recipients, Yoshifuji et al. administered a prebiotic mixture and reported an association with protection against acute GvHD alongside preservation of microbial diversity. 98 However, the efficacy of prebiotics is dependent on the presence of responsive commensal populations. Therefore, in states of profound dysbiosis where key fermentative taxa are markedly depleted, prebiotics alone may have limited capacity to reconstitute microbiome composition or function.

Similarly, dietary interventions can shape the microbiome. In observational dietary and microbiome studies of HSCT recipients, higher dietary fiber intake has been associated with greater gut microbial diversity and lower inflammation, while experimental and translational studies have suggested that lactose availability may promote Enterococcus expansion in susceptible post‐transplant settings. 105 , 106 , 107 Accordingly, some centers are evaluating structured post‐transplant dietary strategies, such as fiber‐enriched regimens or targeted carbohydrate restriction. Dietary modulation is probably a safe, although slow, method to influence the microbiome compared to the direct microbial introduction of FMT.

Antibiotic‐mediated microbiome modulation

Interestingly, antimicrobial agents, despite being a major driver of dysbiosis, have also been considered as potential tools for targeted microbiome modulation. One proposed strategy is the use of selective antibiotics to suppress specific taxa implicated in adverse outcomes, with the intent of allowing recovery of the broader microbiome. 108 For example, ultra‐narrow spectrum antibiotics or minimally absorbed agents such as oral rifaximin have been evaluated in early single‐center clinical studies as a means to limit Gram‐negative overgrowth while relatively sparing obligate anaerobes. Although results showed trends toward reduced GvHD prevalence, robust clinical evidence is lacking. 82 A more immediately actionable approach is to refine prophylactic and empiric antibiotic practices to reduce collateral damage to commensal communities, as observational allo‐HSCT cohort studies and translational models have linked anti‐anaerobic antibiotic exposure to loss of gut microbial diversity and adverse HSCT outcomes. 31 , 83

Consequently, some centers have sought to minimize antibiotics with broad anti‐anaerobic activity when clinically appropriate, to reconsider routine fluoroquinolone prophylaxis, and to shorten the duration of systemic antibiotics whenever feasible. More broadly, antimicrobial stewardship, the intervention of emphasizing appropriate indications, spectrum selection, and treatment duration, represents a feasible strategy to mitigate dysbiosis in HSCT care. In line with this, Henig et al. highlighted the potential of using agents with less anaerobic activity, alongside adjunctive measures such as prebiotic supplementation, to help preserve the intestinal microbiota during HSCT. 24

Postbiotics and bacteriophage therapy

Rather than transferring whole microbial communities, an alternative approach is to target key downstream effector pathways of an eubiotic microbiome through the administration of microbial metabolites or bioactive components, often referred to as postbiotics. Although postbiotics do not constitute microbiome restoration, they may hold selected functional benefits for an intact microbiome in a more standardized manner. For example, supplementation with short‐chain fatty acids, particularly butyrate, or with butyrate‐producing defined consortia has been proposed as a strategy to mimic aspects of FMT while reducing variability inherent to donor‐derived products. 109 , 110 Accordingly, encapsulated butyrate or propionate formulations are being investigated for GvHD and other inflammatory gastrointestinal conditions. 45 , 111 , 112 Another emerging concept is bacteriophage therapy, aiming to selectively reduce pathobiont taxa while sparing the broader ecosystem. In principle, phages targeting organisms such as Enterococcus faecalis or Clostridioides difficile could lower their intestinal burden and facilitate recovery of protective commensals. 113 , 114 However, in hematology and HSCT recipients, bacteriophage‐based strategies remain experimental, with limited clinical evidence to date.

Emerging targeted microbiome therapies

Pharmaceutical development is increasingly focused on standardized microbiota‐based therapeutics as alternatives to conventional donor‐derived FMT. The recurrent‐CDI field provides the most mature regulatory precedent. SER‐109/Vowst, an orally administered purified bacterial spore product, demonstrated efficacy in a phase 3 randomized trial for prevention of recurrent CDI and is approved in the United States for prevention of recurrence after antibacterial treatment. RBX2660/Rebyota, a rectally administered fecal microbiota product, also demonstrated efficacy in the phase 3 PUNCH CD3 trial and is approved in the United States for prevention of recurrent CDI after antibiotic treatment. 40 , 42 , 44 , 115 Although these products illustrate how microbiome therapeutics can be developed using defined manufacturing processes, recurrent CDI approvals should not be interpreted as proof of efficacy in hematology.

In the allogeneic HSCT setting, pooled‐donor microbiome ecosystem products are being developed to address severe dysbiosis‐associated complications. MaaT013 is a pooled‐donor, standardized microbiota ecosystem product administered rectally, whereas MaaT033 is an oral capsule formulation designed for ambulatory use. In a multicenter, single‐arm phase 2 trial, MaaT013 showed encouraging response rates in steroid‐resistant gastrointestinal acute GvHD, supporting further investigation. 116 More recently, MaaT013 was evaluated in the multicenter, single‐arm, open‐label pivotal phase 3 ARES study as third‐line treatment for severe gastrointestinal acute GvHD refractory to corticosteroids and ruxolitinib. Although reported response and survival outcomes are encouraging, the single‐arm design and reliance on historical benchmarks mean that these data should be interpreted as promising but not definitive until peer‐reviewed full publication and regulatory assessment are available. 117 MaaT033 is being evaluated in the ongoing PHOEBUS randomized phase 2b trial in allo‐HSCT recipients, with survival and microbiome‐related end points, and interim safety reviews reported as favorable. 20 , 116 However, until controlled peer‐reviewed data and strict regulatory assessment are available, these results should be rather interpreted as promising, but not universally definitive. More broadly, additional groups are developing defined bacterial consortia intended to restore colonization resistance and reduce infection risk during profound immunosuppression. 118 If these approaches demonstrate consistent clinical benefit across homogenous patient cohorts, they could probably complement traditional FMT in the future.

In summary, although lower‐intensity interventions such as probiotics and prebiotics can modulate the intestinal microbiome and have shown encouraging signals with a favorable safety profile, they are generally viewed as adjunctive strategies rather than substitutes for FMT in the setting of profound dysbiosis, because they rarely reproduce the breadth and functional depth of microbiome restoration achieved by FMT. Importantly, these approaches are not mutually exclusive and may be synergistic when applied in a rational sequence. A plausible future framework could combine microbiome‐sparing antimicrobial strategies to minimize iatrogenic injury, FMT or standardized microbiota‐based products for rapid restoration when severe dysbiosis‐associated complications, such as recurrent CDI or steroid‐refractory gastrointestinal GvHD develop, and maintenance measures, such as dietary optimization, prebiotics, or repeat microbiome‐directed therapies, to support durable engraftment and functional resilience.

FUTURE DIRECTIONS AND RESEARCH OPPORTUNITIES

Optimizing timing and indications

A central unresolved issue is how to position microbiome manipulation within a therapeutic algorithm for malignant hematology and HSCT. Rather than only asking whether FMT is safe or feasible, future studies should aim to define which dysbiosis‐associated clinical states are microbiome‐responsive, which patients are most likely to benefit, and which biological exposure is required for a durable effect. It remains unclear whether microbiome‐directed therapies should be used prophylactically in all HSCT recipients, pre‐emptively in patients with high‐risk microbiome signatures, or therapeutically in established dysbiosis‐associated syndromes such as recurrent CDI, MDRO domination, steroid‐refractory gastrointestinal GvHD, or recurrent bloodstream infection. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 14 , 16 , 18 , 21 , 25 , 26 , 39

Current evidence does not support universal prophylactic FMT for all HSCT recipients. In a randomized, double‐blind phase II trial conducted among allogeneic HCT recipients and patients with AML after neutrophil recovery, Rashidi et al. found that third‐party FMT improved microbiome‐related end points, but did not significantly reduce the primary clinical end point of infections, supporting feasibility and biological activity rather than routine prophylactic efficacy. 21 Accordingly, prophylactic or pre‐emptive microbiome‐directed therapy should remain investigational and preferably be studied in a biomarker‐guided allocation described earlier, such as patients with profound loss of diversity, Enterococcus or Proteobacteria domination, depletion of SCFA‐producing anaerobes, or adverse metabolomic signatures. Future studies should define which dysbiosis‐associated states are microbiome‐responsive, which patients are most likely to benefit, and which biological exposure is required for durable effect.

Timing relative to transplantation and disease course is also being debated. While some investigators advocate for early administration after engraftment to facilitate reconstitution of commensal communities and potentially reduce GvHD risk, others favor initiating FMT once acute GvHD is partially controlled, with the goal of remission consolidation and the reduction of steroid dependence. 29 , 119 Whether a single FMT administration is sufficient also remains uncertain, particularly in hematology and HSCT recipients who frequently require repeated or prolonged systemic antibiotics. Strain‐level analyses have shown that donor bacterial engraftment after FMT is variable and context‐dependent, while clinical reports in gastrointestinal GvHD have often used repeated administrations to achieve or maintain clinical and microbiological effects. 18 , 33 , 34 Consequently, future trials should prospectively define whether FMT is intended as an early vs. late strategy, and single‐induction intervention or as a repeatable microbiome restoration strategy, and should also incorporate longitudinal stool sampling to assess engraftment, persistence, and loss of effect after antibiotic re‐exposure.

Product standardization and personalization

The field is increasingly shifting toward standardized microbiota‐based therapeutics, sometimes called “next‐generation” biotherapeutics, as scalable alternatives to donor‐derived FMT. Efforts are underway to refine formulation and delivery, such as lyophilized preparations, capsule‐based platforms, and defined or synthetic bacterial communities, enabling more personalized approaches over time. 28

A complementary concept is autologous microbiome preservation, whereby a patient's own stool is collected and cryopreserved prior to intensive therapy and later re‐administered (auto‐FMT). In a single‐center pilot study of patients with acute leukemia after intensive chemotherapy, autologous FMT demonstrated feasibility for microbiome reconstitution, and this strategy may be feasible in other high‐risk clinical scenarios, including HSCT and cellular therapies. 96 From a therapeutic‐development perspective, product standardization should include both compositional and functional criteria. Compositional criteria may include microbial diversity, relative abundance of key anaerobic taxa, absence of transmissible pathogens and clinically relevant resistance determinants, and batch‐to‐batch reproducibility. 37 , 38 , 41 , 61 , 91 , 92 Functional criteria may include the ability to restore colonization resistance, the production of SCFAs and transformation primary into secondary bile acids, the generation of immunoregulatory tryptophan metabolites, and the suppression of pathobiont expansion in relevant experimental systems. 47 , 48 , 49 , 50 , 52 , 53 , 54 , 55 , 56 , 66 For hematology‐specific indications, additional quality attributes may be required, including suitability for neutropenic or mucositis‐prone patients, compatibility with antimicrobial exposure, and validated safety testing for viruses and MDROs. This type of framework would allow microbiome products to be evaluated not simply as stool‐derived material, but as complex biological therapeutics with measurable quality attributes and pharmacodynamic effects.

In theory, adjunctive prebiotics may enhance engraftment after FMT by providing substrates that preferentially support beneficial taxa. A related strategy is pre‐conditioning the gut ecosystem before FMT, by using selective antibiotics or bacteriophages to suppress specific pathobionts and open ecological niches for donor microbes. Conceptually, this resembles conditioning prior to HSCT. Personalization may be another promising direction. Donor selection and matching could, in principle, be tailored to the recipient's dysbiotic state. For example, a selection of donors enriched in SCFA‐producing taxa (e.g., Faecalibacterium) and diverse Bacteroides species for recipients whose microbiome is dominated by Enterococcus, with the aim of restoring metabolic function and colonization resistance could be preferred. Computational approaches, including machine‐learning models, are being explored to predict donor‐recipient compatibility and optimize engraftment probability in individual recipients. 39

Large‐scale clinical trials and mechanistic study approaches

To establish efficacy more definitively, adequately powered randomized controlled trials are needed (Table 1). To date, most published studies have been limited by small sample sizes. The randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled phase II trial by Rashidi et al. is one of the most important controlled FMT studies in this field. 21 In this single‐center study, allogeneic HSCT recipients and patients with AML after induction chemotherapy were randomized 2:1 to receive standardized oral encapsulated third‐party FMT or placebo after neutrophil recovery, with repeat administration after subsequent antibacterial antibiotic courses and follow‐up for 9 months. The primary end point was the 4‐month all‐cause infection rate, which was not met: infection density was numerically lower with FMT in both the HCT cohort (0.74 vs. 0.91 events per 100 patient‐days) and AML cohort (0.93 vs. 1.25 events per 100 patient‐days), but without statistically significant differences. Nevertheless, the trial demonstrated biological activity, as donor‐derived bacterial sequences accounted for approximately 25–30% of the fecal microbiota after FMT, with improved post‐antibiotic recovery of microbiota diversity, restoration of depleted obligate anaerobes, and reduction of expanded genera such as Enterococcus, Streptococcus, Veillonella, and Dialister. Several factors may explain why microbiome improvement did not translate into fewer infections. The end point of all‐cause infection is clinically relevant but biologically heterogeneous, including infections that may not be gut microbiome mediated. In addition, recurrent antibiotics, immunosuppression, mucosal injury, central venous catheters, and administration after neutrophil recovery may have limited the measurable clinical effect. Therefore, the negative primary end point should not be interpreted as absence of biological effect, but rather as evidence that future trials may require biomarker‐enriched high‐risk populations, optimized timing and end points more directly linked to gut dysbiosis.

Based on this, larger trials may be better formulated to detect clinically meaningful benefits that earlier studies were underpowered to identify. In parallel, a deeper translational work would be essential to refine clinical application. Integrated analyses of stool, blood, and tissue samples from FMT‐treated patients may identify biomarkers of response or correlates of benefit. For instance, increases in stool butyrate production or reductions in systemic inflammatory markers after FMT could plausibly predict GvHD improvement. Metagenomic and metabolomic profiling before and after FMT will also help link clinical outcomes to microbial functions, enabling a shift from purely taxonomic descriptions to functional readouts. 26 Finally, the contributions of the mycobiome and virome also warrant focused investigation. Because FMT transfers fungi and bacteriophages in addition to bacteria, and because HSCT recipients frequently receive antifungal and antiviral agents, the impact of these non‐bacterial components remains uncertain.

Long‐term follow‐up and integration into standard care

As evidence accumulates, a critical question is whether microbiome‐directed interventions confer durable clinical benefit beyond short‐term microbiological or symptom‐based end points. For this reason, the authors feel that longitudinal follow‐up of FMT recipients at 12 months and beyond will be essential in future trials. Key clinical outcomes should probably include the incidence of recurrent CDI, bloodstream infections, durability of MDRO decolonization, as well as trajectories of immune reconstitution, GvHD incidence and severity, relapse of the underlying malignancy, antimicrobial exposures, hospitalizations, and survival.

As shown earlier, observational allo‐HSCT cohort studies have suggested associations between dysbiosis and adverse clinical outcomes, including mortality and possibly relapse risk. However, these associations do not firmly establish that microbiome restoration will improve long‐term oncological outcomes. If microbiome restoration were ultimately shown to improve overall survival not only by reducing treatment‐related complications, but also by influencing relapse risk, it would represent a shift in how microbiome‐based care is conceptualized among patients with hematological malignancies. 10

Therefore, integration into routine hematology or transplant care should be considered premature at the moment, outside established indications such as recurrent CDI. For other indications, including prophylactic post‐HSCT microbiome restoration, MDRO decolonization, and steroid‐refractory gastrointestinal GvHD, implementation should ideally occur within well‐designed clinical trials, prospective registries, or experienced centers using standardized protocols and systematic safety surveillance. CONCLUSION

In conclusion, enteral microbiome manipulation is evolving from an empirical rescue strategy into a mechanism‐based therapeutic modality for selected patients with malignant hematological diseases. The strongest clinical rationale currently exists for recurrent CDI, whereas MDRO decolonization, steroid‐refractory gastrointestinal GvHD, and post‐HSCT microbiome restoration approaches remain supported mainly by small cohorts from early phase studies and non‐randomized comparisons, or mechanistic data. FMT, standardized microbiota‐based products, and adjunctive microbiome‐sparing strategies may interrupt the cycle of dysbiosis, impaired colonization resistance, epithelial injury, and alloimmune inflammation that characterizes several complications of intensive hematology care. Future studies should define patient selection and timing, therapeutic engraftment and persistence, repeat‐dosing strategies, route and formulation, as well as biomarkers of response, manufacturing standards, and regulatory pathways. If these elements are addressed in adequately powered clinical trials with integrated translational end points in the future, microbiome‐directed therapy may become a structured component of hematology and transplant care rather than a purely experimental supportive intervention.
