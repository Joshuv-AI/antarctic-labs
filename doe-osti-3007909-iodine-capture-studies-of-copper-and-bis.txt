 ABSTRACT

The release of radioiodine, one of several radionuclides of concern when recycling used nuclear fuel
(UNF), is an important consideration in the fuel cycle. In this study, two sorbent materials, Cu0-
polyacrylonitrile (PAN) and Bi0-PAN, were tested as solid sorbent candidates for iodine capture.
Experiments using a thin bed of sorbent material were exposed to vaporized iodine for over 300 hours (~2
weeks) under varied conditions in a dynamic flow environment. The overall performance was monitored
in real-time using thermogravimetric analysis, and the materials were subsequently characterized for
surface and bulk analysis using scanning electron microscopy – energy-dispersive spectroscopy (SEM-
EDS) and powder x-ray diffraction (pXRD), respectively.

Iodine (in the form of I2) is expected to be released primarily in the dissolver off-gas (DOG) stream;
therefore, this study demonstrates the effects of elemental iodine (I2), water vapor (H2O), and nitrogen
dioxide (NO2). When exposed to ‘ideal’ conditions (in which I2 is carried by dry air), Cu0-PAN and Bi0-
PAN behave differently, with TGA analysis indicating that Cu0 sorption performance is higher than that
of Bi0, as evidenced by a larger mass change. Under ‘harsh’ conditions—such as a gaseous feed
containing I2, H2O, and NO2 vapors carried by air,—iodine capture performance for both Cu0- and Bi0-
PAN are affected. SEM-EDS and pXRD analysis of these materials is discussed herein.

 1. INTRODUCTION

When used nuclear fuel (UNF) is recycled, many volatile and radioactive fission products are expected to
be released into the facility off-gas system. Specifically, iodine (129I) is a species of significant concern
because of its long half-life (1.57 × 107 years), high biological activity, high chemical reactivity, and high
vapor pressure as I2.1 Furthermore, the release of this compound into the local environment has shown
significant environmental implications.2 A closer look at how iodine is currently captured from recycling
facilities across the globe was recently discussed,3 acknowledging that iodine is typically captured using
two methods consecutively: (a) wet-scrubbing methods using caustic media4-6 and (b) solid sorbent
methods using materials such as reduced silver mordenite (Ag0Z).7-10 When leveraging these two methods
in a facility, the bulk of radioiodine is expected to be captured using a wet scrubbing method, yet solid
sorbents provide an essential polishing step to safeguard against low concentrations of radioiodine
emissions that may still exceed the limitations of EPA regulations.11

Although silver mordenite and other silver zeolites have been used in UNF recycling off-gas streams for
the capture of iodine, many studies reveal disadvantageous conditions that hinder Ag-based sorbent
performance. These conditions include the presence of water vapor and corrosive NOx fumes, high total
iodine concentration in the vapor phase, and high temperatures.12, 13 For example, the reactivity of Ag0Z
with NO2, volatilized from UNF dissolution in nitric acid,14 leads to the formation of silver nitrate
(AgNO3), thus accounting for the observed decrease in Ag-I reactivity with increasing exposure to NO2.15
An additional disadvantage of using Ag0Z in the United States is that silver is a regulated substance under
the Resource Conservation and Recovery Act (RCRA, 40 CFR 261), posing an accountancy and long-
term storage challenge once saturated with radioiodine.

To address this issue, investigations using alternative metal sorbents seek to identify if copper (Cu), lead
(Pb), cobalt (Co), bismuth (Bi), and other metals can outperform silver-based materials.16-19 In a recent
investigation of alternative metal sulfides, Cu2S (embedded in a polymer bead) outperformed Ag0Z for
iodine capture under harsh conditions. This report presents new results collected from sorbents that
contain Cu0 and Bi0 getters to study the greater impact of initial metal oxidation state on sorbent
performance as a part of a continued effort to evaluate new options for metallic sorbents that can
participate in iodine capture and potentially replace Ag0Z.

 2. MATERIALS AND METHODS (ORNL)

2.1 SORBENT SAMPLE PREPARATION

2.1.1    Cu0-PAN and Bi0-PAN

Samples were prepared as described by Riley and coworkers.20

2.2 EXPERIMENTAL METHODS

2.2.1 Thin-Bed Testing

Thin bed testing of iodine (I2(g)) adsorption by Cu- and Bi-bearing sorbents was performed using a
custom-built thermogravimetric analyzer (TGA) and manifold at the Iodine Off-gas Testing and
Abatement Laboratory (IOTA Lab) at Oak Ridge National Laboratory (Figure 1). A thin bed of sorbent
material (~1 – 2 g) was contained within a temperature-controlled oven set to 135°C. Actual temperatures
at this set point were recorded at 125 ± 3 °C and are reported as such within this study. This apparatus is
designed to weigh the sorbent continuously as it is exposed to air. In all tests, iodine vapor was added to a
dry air stream, and in some of those tests humid air and NO2(g) were mixed as described below.

  Figure 1. Photo of experimental flowing thin bed (customized furnace set above an analytical balance; or
custom TGA) and ancillary manifold delivering iodine vapor (I2) and/or H2O and NO2 from a packed column
 carried by air.

The TGA allows iodine loading of the sorbent to be observed in real-time. Once the weight gain was
observed to be less than 5 mg I/g sorbent for a period of 24 h or had been exposed for 2 weeks, the
sorbent was assumed to be spent. The TGA was then purged with dry air for an additional 18 h to remove
any physisorbed species.

2.3 INSTRUMENT METHODS

2.3.1 Powder X-Ray Diffraction (pXRD)

Cu0- polyacrylonitrile (PAN) sorbents were analyzed on a Rigaku Miniflex 600 diffractometer with a 5
mm × 0.2 mm cavity zero background sample holder. The instrument has a copper source at 40 kV and 15
mA and operates in Bragg-Brentano geometry. Samples were scanned between 3–90 degrees at 0.2
degrees per minute and a step size of 0.01 degrees. Bi0-PAN sorbents were analyzed on a Malvern
Panalytical Empyrean diffractometer with a 16 mm × 0.8 mm cavity sample holder. The instrument has a
copper source at 30 kV and 10 mA and operates in Bragg–Brentano geometry. Reacted samples were
compressed with a laboratory spatula until they were reduced to a powder and scanned between 5–120
degrees at 0.2 degrees per minute and a step size of 0.013 degrees.

Data analysis includes implementing CrystalDiffract smart background detection and subtraction. The
collected data were compared to known compounds patterns provided by the International Centre for
Diffraction Data PDF-5+ software.

2.3.2 Scanning Electron Microscopy – Energy Dispersive Spectroscopy (SEM-EDS)

Sorbent materials were dry mounted on double-sided carbon tape affixed to an aluminum SEM stub.
When possible, sorbents were cut in half with a spatula and mounted onto double-sided carbon tape with
the interior portion of the sample exposed to allow for imaging of cross sections. Samples were not
sputter coated. The SEM analysis was performed on a Hitachi 3400N SEM at an accelerating voltage of
20kV and a working distance of 10 mm. They were also analyzed with an EDAX Element EDS detector
(30 mm2 silicon drift detector) equipped with APEX software for semi-quantitative determination of
elemental composition at the sample’s surface. Image scale is based on instrument calibration from the
manufacturer.

2.3.3 Digital Microscopy

The same samples that were prepared for electron microscopy were also analyzed via digital microscopy.
Digital images were collected on a Keyence VHX-7000 microscope using full ring illumination at
magnifications ranging from 30× to 1000×.

 3. RESULTS

3.1 STARTING MATERIAL CHARACTERIZATION

All starting materials were characterized using digital microscopy, SEM-EDS, and pXRD for the
purposes of comparison with the reacted materials. Additional characterization of this batch of materials
can be found in a report distributed earlier in 2025.21

Cu0-PAN beads are approximately 2 mm in diameter, with a light gray color and orange inclusions on the
surface (Figure 2), and they contain carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, and copper. Inspection of the interior using
digital microscopy reveals a porous structure with a tan core and a heterogeneous distribution of orange
grains (Figure 2). Elemental analysis via energy-dispersive x-ray spectroscopy (EDS) combined with
scanning electron microscopy (SEM) indicates that embedded inclusions in the PAN bead contain copper,
while most of the bead contains predominantly oxygen, nitrogen, and carbon (Figure 3a). Powder x-ray
diffraction displays several well‐defined peaks, which confirm the predominantly crystalline nature of the
sample and are a close match to the published diffraction pattern of metallic copper with minor
contributions of copper(I) oxide and copper (II) oxide (Figure 3b).

Bi0-PAN beads are approximately 2.5 mm in diameter, with a yellowish-gray exterior with gray
inclusions on the surface (Figure 2), and they contain bismuth, carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen. The porous
interior of the bead has a color gradient ranging from charcoal gray to pale yellow and includes a porous
and channeled structure. Elemental analysis identifies that the interior of the bead contained bismuth,
carbon, oxygen, and nitrogen (Figure 4a). Powder x-ray diffraction reveals a predominantly crystalline
pattern, which closely matches bismuth(III) oxide and metallic bismuth (Figure 4b).

3.3 SAMPLE CHARACTERIZATION AFTER EXPOSURE

3.3.1 Surface Characterization of PAN sorbents

3.3.1.1 Metallic Copper

Reacted Cu0-PAN beads show a dramatic change in both the color of the bead matrix as well as the color
of the surface and core inclusions after treatment with both I2 and I2/NO2/H2O. After treatment with I2
only, the bead appears tan both on the surface and in the core, and white inclusions are present on the
surface and in the interior of the bead (Figure 6, middle). For the sample reacted with both I2/NO2/H2O,
the particle appears brownish-orange with white and gray inclusions forming on the interior and exterior
of the PAN bead (Figure 6, right).

Powder X-Ray diffraction was performed on the reacted copper materials as a bulk characterization
technique. In both samples, diffraction patterns display several well-defined peaks that, when compared to
the diffraction database, match the expected pattern of copper(I) iodide. Other minor contributions match
well with copper (II) oxide, copper (I) oxide, or metallic copper, all of which were identified within the
starting material samples (Figure 14 and 15).

 4. DISCUSSION

Thin-bed tests of alternate metal sorbents were completed to determine the overall performance of iodine
sorption under consistent conditions. Ag0Z’s well documented performance reveals that the addition of
NO2 and H2O into the process gas stream has significant negative effects on the ability to sorb iodine
vapor. In order to compare new sorbent materials to this ‘baseline material’ and gain an understanding of
performance, two dynamic flow test types were selected and performed: (1) an ‘ideal’ test, where I2 vapor
is carried by a dry air stream, and (2) a ‘harsh’ test, where a mixed air stream contains the same [I2] as
well as H2O and NO2 vapors. The second test is designed such that it simulates a process off-gas that may
be generated in a UNF recycling facility.

Online monitoring of capture onto the sorbent materials is collected gravimetrically through the entirety
of the experiment. Over the duration of several days, Cu0-PAN exhibits a significantly larger mass gain
than Bi0-PAN in either an ‘ideal’ or ‘harsh’ environment. In fact, Bi0-PAN sorption over the course of
both experiments shows no appreciable change, as it does not exceed performance of 0.2 g/g in either test.
On the contrary, Cu0-PAN gains nearly 7× more mass than the bismuth derivative in an ‘ideal’
environment, reaching an equilibrium at ~1.4 g/g sorbent. This performance is also an order of magnitude
increase from the performance observed from Ag0Z, which was previously documented as 0.1 g/g23 and
tested under all of the same conditions except at 150 oC (or at 0.086 g/g at the same temperature).
However, the Ag0Z material only has 8-9% (w/w) Ag loaded onto the engineered form compared to the
85% (w/w) Cu onto the Cu0-PAN from the current study, rendering efficiency difficult to compare
without quantification data. A similar but less prominent trend is observed during the ‘harsh’ tests, where
Cu0-PAN sorption performance is observed as 0.320 g/g sorbent. As anticipated, this is significantly
decreased as a function of the ‘harsh’ environment where NO2 and H2O are introduced. Ag0Z exposed to
the same ‘harsh’ test environment also decreases in iodine capture ability, however the Cu0-PAN
outperforms the recorded Ag0Z performance (0.041 g/g)23 by nearly 8×.

Upon each test completion, the materials were prepared for surface and bulk characterization to identify
the compounds adsorbed to the surface of the materials. Gaseous treatment of the sorbent beads from
‘ideal’ tests leads to a distinct change in the surface appearance of both sorbents with changes in color and
morphology, particularly that of the inclusions embedded within each bead, and suggests the formation of
secondary Cu or Bi phases as inclusions on or in the beads. In the case of Cu0-PAN, iodine reacts with the
exposed surface containing copper grains to form a white compound that contains oxygen, copper, and
iodine (Figure 6). The effect is also visible on the surface of the Bi0-PAN, albeit to a lesser degree, with
orange inclusions on the surface and interior of the sample (Figure 11), which are also shown to contain
Bi (Figure 12). Given the tendency of iodine to collocate with either copper or bismuth in the elemental
mapping, the metal sites appear to be crucial for sorption of iodine within the samples. Bulk material
analyses of both Cu0-PAN and Bi0-PAN exposed to iodine under ‘ideal’ conditions complement the
analyses of the surfaces, revealing matching experimental data to patterns of copper(I) iodide and

bismuth(III) oxide, respectively. A diffraction pattern that matches bismuth(III) iodate is difficult to
discern from the data collected (Figure 14 and 16).

When subjected to the ‘harsh’ tests, the added NO2/H2O mixture inhibits the sorption of iodine within the
sample, as shown by the decrease in iodine density in the elemental maps. This corresponds to an increase
in both the oxygen and nitrogen concentration in the treated samples, leading to the hypothesis that the
NO2 competes with I2 for the available chemically reactive sites within the sorbent beads. In addition to
the elemental mapping, the inclusions in the samples treated with I2 only differ significantly in
morphology from those in the samples treated with NO2 and I2, suggesting a different chemical phase that
would result in a unique crystallographic form. Bulk material analyses of both Cu0-PAN and Bi0-PAN
exposed to iodine under ‘harsh’ conditions complement the analysis of the surface, revealing that the
experimental data match best to patterns of copper(I) iodide, copper(II) oxide, and bismuth(III) oxide,
respectively. A diffraction pattern that matches bismuth(III) iodate remains difficult to discern from the
data collected. Interestingly, the presence of nitrated copper or bismuth is absent from the bulk
characterization (Figure 15 and 17).

Due to the promising results of Cu0-PAN from these tests, its performance was plotted against the TGA
test results previously gathered for Cu2S-PAN and Ag0Z exposed to the same ‘ideal’ and ‘harsh’
conditions. As can be seen from the figure below, Cu0-PAN adsorbs significantly more mass as a function
of time and does so at a notably faster rate than either previous sorbent. There is significantly more Cu0
doping within these materials, and they have noticeably more Cu0 contained within their core than what
was previously observed with Cu2S-PAN. For those reasons, SEM-EDS analysis identifies an increased
presence of iodine within the core of this material (Figure 8), complementing the overall mass gain from
the TGA results (Figure 5 and 18).

 5. CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE WORK

Although decisions on the studied materials’ advancement as candidates for radioiodine capture are
ultimately determined based on their sorbent performance and efficiency, studying the overall
mechanistic influences of these solid–gas phase reactions can also answer and inform further R&D in this
field. Therefore, in addition to online thermogravimetric analysis to assess sorbent performance, digital
microscopy provided the ability to visually convey observations of physical changes throughout this
study. X-ray diffraction then confirms the chemical changes that have occurred on the surface, providing
a larger picture of the sorbent–iodine reaction mechanism.

The ‘ideal’ dynamic test reveals that Cu0-PAN reacts with iodine, however there is little evidence that
Bi0-PAN shares similar reactivity. NAA analysis may provide additional information for Bi0-iodine
reactivity. With little competing gaseous constituents, the conditions used here provide an understanding
of performance under favorable conditions. In contrast, the ‘harsh’ dynamic test reveals that Cu0-PAN
continues to capture iodine to a significant extent in an environment where other reactions (like
conversion to copper(I) nitrate or oxide) take place.

Although it is difficult to determine performance efficiency, it is likely that Cu0-PAN is a competitive
option against Cu2S-PAN and Ag0Z. Neutron activation analysis will provide the necessary quantitative
analysis to reveal the higher performing sorbent. In addition to this pending characterization, future
investigations for developing alternative metal sorbent materials may include the following activities:

 1. Analysis on Cu-I reaction kinetics and the influence of temperature
 Investigating the kinetics of the heterogeneous reaction of copper and iodine would provide
 additional fundamental insight into leveraging the environment of the capture system to enhance
 sorbent performance. For example, an intentional study that identifies an optimized temperature.
 The temperature selected for this work (125 °C) was used to avoid degradation of the PAN
 material, as it is expected to decompose at temperatures higher than 150 °C.

 2. Testing Cu-based sorbents for alkyl iodide reactivity (Vessel Off-Gas or VOG conditions)
 With Cu-based materials exceeding performance metrics at a rapid rate, their performance for
 organoiodide sorption would add to their potential value as robust sorbent materials and
 technology readiness.

 3. Alternatives to PAN matrices
 The PAN material acts as a support for the metal getters highlighted in these studies. The design
 of other porous materials that can withstand a DOG/VOG environment and/or increase the
 effective reactivity of sorbent materials can be the next step in revealing the structure-property
 relationships of sorbent materials.
