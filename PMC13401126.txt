Comparison between liraglutide and dulaglutide on the incidence or progression of eye and kidney complications
Authors: Aki Katamine, Junko Oya, Yuichiro Kondo, Yukiko Hasegawa, Tomoko Nakagami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsTo evaluate and compare the risk of diabetic retinopathy and kidney disease between once‐daily liraglutide and once‐weekly dulaglutide in Japanese participants with type 2 diabetes (T2D).Materials and MethodsA total of 341 participants newly initiated on once‐daily liraglutide 0.9 mg or once‐weekly dulaglutide 0.75 mg between 2015 and 2020 were included in this study. We used propensity score‐based inverse probability of treatment weighting to adjust for baseline characteristics between the two groups. The participants were divided into three cohorts according to retinopathy, albuminuria, and glomerular filtration rate (GFR). The primary outcomes were the incidence or progression of diabetic retinopathy or kidney disease (albuminuria or reduced estimated GFR (eGFR) <60 mL/min/1.73 m2). The incidence rate and risk of outcomes were compared between the two groups using Poisson regression and Cox proportional hazard models. [Correction added on September 7, 2026, after first online publication: The total number of participants in the Materials and Methods section has been corrected from 331 to 341.]ResultsOver a mean follow‐up of 4.4 and 3.6 years in the liraglutide and dulaglutide groups, respectively, there were no differences between the two groups in terms of the risk of incidence of retinopathy and reduced GFR (hazard ratio (HR): 0.98 [0.60–1.60] and 0.82 [0.36–1.84]). Dulaglutide showed a lower incidence or progression rate but similar risk of albuminuria compared to liraglutide (54.7 vs 25.8 per 1,000 person‐years (P = 0.04), HR: 0.51 [0.25–1.05]).ConclusionsOur study showed that once‐daily liraglutide and once‐weekly dulaglutide have similar risks for retinopathy, albuminuria, and reduced GFR.Keywords: diabetic complications, dulaglutide, liraglutide

INTRODUCTION

Type 2 diabetes (T2D) causes microvascular complications, such as diabetic retinopathy and kidney disease, which lead to a decline in quality of life and an increase in mortality. Recently, placebo‐controlled cardiovascular outcome trials (CVOTs) have shown that glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1 RAs) reduce cardiovascular risk and all‐cause mortality in people with T2D 1 . Some studies also investigated retinopathy and renal outcomes as secondary endpoints. Semaglutide showed an increased risk of severe retinopathy 2 , whereas liraglutide 3 and dulaglutide 4 showed non‐significant risk increases in severe retinopathy compared to placebo. However, GLP‐1 RAs have not been associated with an increased risk of incident diabetic retinopathy compared to other antidiabetic drugs 5 . Regarding diabetic kidney disease, liraglutide and dulaglutide have been reported to be associated with reduced composite renal outcomes, especially with a lower risk of developing macroalbuminuria compared to the placebo 6 , 7 .

GLP‐1 RAs are categorized as short‐ and long‐acting according to their pharmacokinetic profiles; however, no studies have compared GLP‐1 RAs with respect to the incident risk of diabetic retinopathy and kidney disease. The different pharmacological half‐life (e.g., liraglutide for 12 h and dulaglutide for 120 h) 8 , molecular structure and pharmacological properties may be influenced by the risk of microvascular complications. While liraglutide and semaglutide partially cross the blood–brain barrier and act in the central nervous system, dulaglutide, due to its larger molecular weight, does not directly bind to GLP‐1 receptors in the brain 9 . Furthermore, previous studies were conducted using multinational data, which differed from the doses approved in Japan (liraglutide 0.9 mg daily (until 2019) and dulaglutide 0.75 mg weekly). In a phase III study in Japan, once‐weekly dulaglutide 0.75 mg showed a significant improvement in HbA1c compared to liraglutide 0.9 mg daily at 52 weeks 10 , whereas there was no significant difference in HbA1c obtained with dulaglutide and liraglutide in a real‐world retrospective study 11 .

Therefore, we compared the effects of liraglutide and dulaglutide on the incidence and progression of diabetic retinopathy and kidney disease in Japanese patients with T2D.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Participants

We conducted a single‐center, retrospective observational study using the electronic medical records of a hospital. The records of 378 participants with T2D who were newly prescribed liraglutide 0.9 mg/day or dulaglutide 0.75 mg/week between September 2015 and December 2020 were extracted. Participants undergoing hemodialysis or post‐kidney transplantation (n = 16) and steroid therapy (n = 2), those admitted into the hospital (n = 1), those with missing baseline data (n = 4), and those who discontinued these drugs within 6 months (n = 14) were excluded. The remaining 341 participants were included in this study. The participants were divided into three cohorts according to diabetic complications: retinopathy (n = 281), albuminuria (n = 288), and glomerular filtration rate (GFR) (n = 194) (Figure 1). The retinopathy, albuminuria, and GFR cohorts included participants who were not diagnosed with proliferative retinopathy, those who were not diagnosed with macroalbuminuria, and those whose estimated GFR (eGFR) was above 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 at baseline, respectively. Participants were monitored until the incidence or progression of diabetic retinopathy or kidney disease (albuminuria or reduced eGFR (<60 mL/min/1.73 m2)), end of visitation for any reason, discontinuation of the drug or switch to other drugs, death from any cause, or end of December 2022. Collection of clinical and laboratory data

Data on age, sex, body mass index (BMI), type of diabetes, anthropometric measurements, HbA1c, eGFR, and albumin creatinine ratio (ACR) levels, duration of diabetes, and use of antihypertensive and glucose‐lowering agents were obtained from medical records. Diabetic retinopathy was defined according to Fukuda's classification 12 by an ophthalmologist and assigned to three categories: no retinopathy, simple retinopathy (microaneurysm, retinal dot, blot, or flame‐shaped hemorrhage, hard or soft exudates, or retinal nonperfusion area), and proliferative retinopathy (including pre‐proliferative status, neovascularization, vitreous, or preretinal hemorrhage). HbA1c levels were measured using high‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC; Adams A1c HA‐8160; Arkray) and reported as percentages according to the National Glycohemoglobin Standardization Program 13 . Normo‐, micro‐, and macroalbuminuria were defined as ACR <30, 30–299, and ≥300 mg/gCr, respectively. GFR was estimated using the following equation proposed by the Japanese Society for Nephrology: eGFR (mL/min/1.73 m2) = 194 × serum creatinine (mg/dL) −1.094 × age (years) −0.287 × [if female] × 0.739 14 .

Outcome measurements

The primary outcomes were the incidence or progression of diabetic retinopathy (no retinopathy to simple retinopathy or simple to proliferative retinopathy (including pre‐proliferative status)) or kidney disease (albuminuria: normoalbuminuria to microalbuminuria or microalbuminuria to macroalbuminuria or reduced eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m2) in participants in the liraglutide and dulaglutide groups. Secondary outcomes were the incidence of proliferative retinopathy and macroalbuminuria in each group.

Statistical analysis

To adjust for the baseline characteristics between the two groups, we used propensity score‐based inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) to construct a weighted cohort of participants treated with different drugs 15 . To calculate the stabilized propensity scores, multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed with dulaglutide treatment as the dependent variable, including the following confounding variables: age, sex, BMI, duration of diabetes, blood pressure, HbA1c, eGFR, low density lipoprotein cholesterol, retinopathy and albuminuria status, use of antihypertensive and lipid lowering medication, insulin, metformin, dipeptidyl peptidase‐4 inhibitor, sulfonylurea, and sodium‐glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitor. Participants who received dulaglutide were assigned a weight of 1/ (propensity score), and those who received liraglutide were assigned a weight of 1/ (1 − propensity score) 16 . Weights were stabilized and trimmed at the 0.25th and 99.75th percentiles to reduce the estimate variability. Balance among covariates was assessed using the standard mean difference (SMD), and a difference of ≤0.1 was considered to be a well‐balanced result.

Continuous variables are presented as means and standard deviations, and categorical data are expressed as proportions. The incidence of endpoint events was reported per 1,000 person‐years. Poisson regression modeling was used to compare the endpoint events between the two groups. The cumulative incidence rate of endpoint events in the two groups was calculated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and group comparisons were performed using the log‐rank test. Cox proportional hazard models were used to estimate the hazard ratios (HRs) between the two groups and endpoint events, along with the corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Similar analyses were performed for secondary outcomes.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Austria). The P‐values were based on two‐sided tests, and the cutoff point for statistical significance was set at 0.05.

Sensitivity analysis

Dulaglutide has been commercially available since July 2015 in Japan; therefore, there may be a limit to the number of insulin prescriptions in the first year. Hence, physicians may have been more likely to prescribe liraglutide during that period. We therefore conducted sensitivity analyses restricting to 232 participants who initiated liraglutide or dulaglutide between September 2016 and December 2020.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Tokyo Women's Medical University (approval no. 2025‐0174; November 21, 2025). All clinical investigations were conducted in accordance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Incidence or progression of diabetic retinopathy and kidney disease

The incidence and progression of diabetic retinopathy and kidney disease are presented in Table 3. The cumulative incidence rates across the two groups are shown in Figures 2a and 3a,c. Over a mean follow‐up of 4.4 ± 2.5 and 3.6 ± 2.0 years, 20.5 and 17.9 cases of retinopathy event appeared in the liraglutide and dulaglutide groups, respectively. There was no significant difference in the incidence rates between the two groups (37.3 vs 43.1 per 1,000 person‐years, P = 0.70), and similar results were observed in the cumulative incidence curve (log‐rank test, P = 0.68). There was no difference between liraglutide and dulaglutide on incidence or progression of retinopathy (HR 0.98 [95% CI 0.60–1.60]). In the ACR cohort, 31.5 and 14.9 cases of events with an incidence rate of 54.7 and 25.8 per 1,000 person‐years were observed in the liraglutide and dulaglutide groups, respectively, with a significant difference (P = 0.04). Kaplan–Meier cumulative incidence curve analysis indicated that the dulaglutide group had a lower cumulative incidence or progression of albuminuria, with borderline significant differences compared to the dulaglutide group (log‐rank test, P = 0.05). There was no difference between liraglutide and dulaglutide on incidence or progression of albuminuria (HR 0.51 [0.25–1.05]). In the GFR cohort, the incidence rates of reduced eGFR were 46.6 and 41.7 per 1,000 person‐years in the liraglutide and dulaglutide groups, respectively (P = 0.27), and there was no difference between the two groups in the cumulative incidence curve (log‐rank test, P = 0.59). There was no risk difference between the two groups on the incidence of reduced GFR (HR 0.82 [95% CI 0.36–1.84]). There were no differences on incidence of cardiovascular disease between two groups in all cohorts.

Incidence of proliferative retinopathy and macroalbuminuria

The cumulative incidence rates of proliferative retinopathy and macroalbuminuria in the two groups are presented in Figures 2b and 3b. There was no significant difference observed in the cumulative incidence curve (log‐rank test P = 0.19 and P = 0.16, respectively). Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis was performed on 71 and 161 participants who initiated liraglutide and dulaglutide, respectively. In the retinopathy cohort, over a mean follow‐up of 3.9 ± 2.0 and 3.6 ± 1.9 years, 4.4 and 14.0 cases of retinopathy event appeared in the liraglutide and dulaglutide groups, respectively. There was no significant difference in the incidence rates between the two groups (22.8 vs 38.5 per 1,000 person‐years, P = 0.98). Moreover, there was no difference between liraglutide and dulaglutide on the incidence or progression of retinopathy (HR 1.49 [95% CI 0.73–3.04]). In the ACR cohort, there was no significant difference in the incidence rates and HR between the two groups (42.6 vs 34.8 per 1,000 person‐years, P = 0.21, HR 0.73 [0.29–1.84]). In the GFR cohort, there was no significant difference in the incidence rates and HR between the two groups (40.6 vs 47.1 per 1,000 person‐years, P = 0.39, HR 0.81 [0.27–2.44]). These findings were almost consistent with the main results except the incidence rate in the ACR cohort.

DISCUSSION

The present study revealed no differences between liraglutide and dulaglutide on the risk of incidence or progression of retinopathy and albuminuria and reduced GFR after several years of follow‐up. There were no significant differences in the risk of severe microvascular complications between the liraglutide and dulaglutide groups. To our knowledge, this is the first study to compare the incidence of diabetic microvascular complications between liraglutide 0.9 mg daily and once‐weekly dulaglutide 0.75 mg in Japanese patients with T2D.

A randomized phase III study in Japan reported that once‐weekly dulaglutide 0.75 mg decreased HbA1c significantly from baseline compared to once‐daily liraglutide 0.9 mg at 52 weeks, with a −0.20% least squares mean difference 10 . Conversely, a real‐world retrospective study in Japan showed a similar effect on HbA1c between once‐daily liraglutide 0.9 mg (including a few participants administered 1.8 mg) and once‐weekly dulaglutide 0.75 mg 11 . A previous report showed that once‐weekly dulaglutide had higher adherence and was more persistent than once‐daily liraglutide 17 .

The Liraglutide Effect and Action in Diabetes: Evaluation of Cardiovascular Outcome Results (LEADER) trial 3 and the Researching Cardiovascular Events with a Weekly Incretin in Diabetes (REWIND) trial 4 showed that the incidence of severe retinopathy (defined as photocoagulation, vitreous hemorrhage, and blindness) was non‐significantly higher in the liraglutide and dulaglutide groups than in the placebo group, with HRs of 1.15 and 1.24, respectively. These randomized clinical trials were primarily designed to test for CVOTs, and the retinal outcomes were severe. Our study compared the incidence of retinopathy from non‐ or simple to severe status between the liraglutide and dulaglutide groups, with no risk difference observed. Similar results were obtained when the study was limited to the incidence of proliferative retinopathy. There are several explanations for how GLP‐1 RAs may affect the incidence of retinopathy: rapid improvement of blood glucose levels, which affects osmotic pressure and increases soft exudates 18 ; synergistic action of drugs and vascular endothelial growth factor on blood vessels in the retina 18 ; and direct action on the retina due to abundant expression of GLP‐1 R in human retinal cells 19 . In a phase III trial 10 , there was no difference in HbA1c levels between liraglutide and dulaglutide in the early stages of administration, suggesting that there was no difference in the incidence of retinopathy due to blood glucose changes. Moreover, the results indicated that differences in the half‐life and molecular weight of these drugs 9 did not affect the incidence of retinopathy. In this study, rapid improvement in HbA1c levels (a decrease of 1% or more within the first 4–5 months 20 ) was observed in 24.8 and 20.0% for liraglutide and dulaglutide group, respectively, with no significant difference. HRs between rapid improvement of HbA1c and incidence of retinopathy were 0.79 (0.32–1.96). Therefore, in this study, the impact of rapid blood glucose changes on the incidence of retinopathy was not significant.

In the LEADER trial 6 , once‐daily liraglutide 1.8 mg showed a lower risk of new‐onset macroalbuminuria (HR 0.74 [95% CI 0.60–0.91]) and a slightly slower decline in eGFR (estimated trial‐group ratio 1.02 [95% CI 1.00–1.03]) compared to the placebo group. In the REWIND trial 7 , once‐weekly dulaglutide 1.5 mg showed a lower risk of incidence of macroalbuminuria (HR 0.77 [95% CI 0.68–0.87]) and a similar risk of decline in eGFR ≥30% (HR 0.89 [95% CI 0.78–1.01]) compared to the placebo group; however, dulaglutide decreased the risk of decline in eGFR ≥40%. Previous reports have demonstrated the protective effect of GLP‐1 RAs on eGFR decline in patients with more severe renal status 6 , 21 , 22 and that the renoprotective effects of GLP‐1 RAs are dose‐dependent 23 . The GFR cohort in our study was selected from participants with eGFR ≥60 mL/min/1.73 m2 at baseline, and the dosage of liraglutide and dulaglutide was half of the maximum dose in previous reports. Therefore, the liraglutide and dulaglutide groups may demonstrate a similar influence on eGFR. By contrast, our study showed that the dulaglutide group had a significantly lower incidence and progression rate of albuminuria compared to the liraglutide group. Although liraglutide and dulaglutide have been reported to have a lower risk of macroalbuminuria, our albuminuria outcome included micro‐ and macroalbuminuria, which may have influenced our results. Pharmacological differences 8 , 9 and differences in adherence depending on the administration method 17 may also contribute to the explanation. One reason for the different results in the ACR and GFR cohorts in our study is that one report showed that the risk of albuminuria onset and declining eGFR differs in Japanese people with T2D 24 . Several renoprotective effects of GLP‐1 RAs have been reported, including stimulating the release of anti‐inflammatory cytokines 25 and ameliorating oxidative stress 26 , which lead to the protection of vascular endothelial cells and glomeruli; inhibiting intestinal sodium‐hydrogen exchanger isoform 3 activity 27 , which promotes natriuresis and diuresis; and improving renal hemodynamics and reducing markers of renal renin‐angiotensin‐aldosterone system activation, leading to glomerulus injury 28 . In a sensitivity analysis with equivalent follow‐up periods, there was no difference in the incidence rate of albuminuria between the two groups. This suggests that the difference in incidence rate of albuminuria in the main result was largely due to the observation period, which is consistent with no differences in cumulative incidence rate and HR of albuminuria.

This study has some limitations. First, this was a retrospective observational study conducted at a single institution, and the sample size was small; therefore, it may be difficult to generalize the results to populations with different characteristics. Second, the follow‐up period was too short to obtain sufficient outcomes in patients with diabetic retinopathy and kidney disease. Third, this study could not assess the adherence to or satisfaction with each medication used. Fourth, although all participants in the liraglutide group used 0.9 mg daily at baseline, some may have changed the dose during the follow‐up period. Similarly, we did not have data on whether other antidiabetic drugs and antihypertensive drugs were included, discontinued, or had a dose change. Fifth, in recent years, GFR slope has been used as a surrogate marker to assess the effect of drugs on renal function. It has been reported that decline of 0.5–1.0 mL/min/1.73 m2 per year in the GFR slope is associated with risk reduction of clinical outcomes especially in early‐stage renal disease 29 . This study focused on the incidence of renal complications rather than the therapeutic effect on renal function; therefore, changes in CKD classification were used as the marker. Additionally, though we focused on participants with mild CKD, it has been reported that the renoprotective effects of GLP‐1 are more pronounced in individuals with moderate‐to‐severe CKD than in individuals with mild CKD 30 . Further studies are needed to compare the effect on renal function between these drugs by GFR slope or other metrics in individuals with moderate‐to‐severe CKD. Despite these limitations, this study compared the effects of liraglutide and dulaglutide on the incidence of diabetic retinopathy and kidney disease in approximately 340 Japanese patients with T2D. [Correction added on September 7, 2026, after first online publication: In the last sentence, the number of Japanese patients was corrected from 330 to 340.]

In conclusion, this retrospective observational study showed that liraglutide and dulaglutide have similar risks for the incidence and progression of retinopathy, albuminuria, and reduced GFR. Prospective studies with a larger number of participants and longer follow‐up periods are necessary to confirm these results.
