Tissue-resident colon macrophages shape the microbiome and regulate mucosal healing via ferroportin
Authors: Bhupendra Singh Rawat, Jaehyeon Kim, Waleed Mujib, Toni-Ann Bravo, Alexandros Skouris, Garam Choi, Nishali Shah, Ariel Tjitropranoto, Alexander Lemenze, Yosuke Kumamoto, Lea Ann Chen, Nicholas J Bessman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYInflammatory bowel diseases (IBDs) are complex disorders marked by microbial dysbiosis and dysregulated iron. Iron homeostasis is controlled by endogenous hepcidin, which inhibits iron export through ferroportin, to promote mucosal healing via nutritional immunity. However, the relevant site of ferroportin activity and the clinical potential of this pathway in IBD remain unknown. Using unbiased profiling, we demonstrate that functional ferroportin is expressed by discrete colon macrophage subsets in both mice and humans. In mice, ferroportin is highly expressed in a rare, colon-resident macrophage population. We further identified that colon-specific hepcidin delivery can target ferroportin on colon macrophages without disrupting iron homeostasis, modulate microbiome composition, and accelerate recovery after intestinal inflammation. Altogether, these findings outline a model where dysbiotic bacteria critically depend on macrophage-derived iron to inhibit mucosal healing in IBD. Identification of a site-specific therapeutic window for ferroportin inhibition reveals a strategy to promote mucosal healing in IBD.

INTRODUCTION

A delicate balance between commensal microbes and mammalian host tissues underlies health and disease, particularly in the intestine. Multiple overlapping barrier mechanisms limit physical contact between commensal bacteria and the host immune system. In the intestine, barrier dysfunction allows commensal bacteria to activate an immune response and inflammation, which then causes ongoing barrier damage in a feed-forward loop of chronic disease known as inflammatory bowel disease (IBD).1 Achieving disease remission for IBD patients requires the restoration of the intestinal barrier in a process termed mucosal healing.2

The pathogenesis of IBD is tightly linked with gut microbial dysbiosis and iron deficiency anemia (IDA). IBD is associated with reduced gut microbial diversity, which in turn renders the host more susceptible to proliferation of pathobionts and infecting pathogens.3,4 IBD patients display alterations in the relative abundances of specific taxa, such as a decrease in Faecailbacterium prausnitzii,5,6 or increases in Ruminococcus gnavus, Escherichia coli, or Parasutterella species,7–9 and selective modification of the microbiome has shown promise in treating IBD.10 Anemia in IBD patients is frequently treated with oral iron supplementation, which may cause gastrointestinal discomfort and worsen microbial dysbiosis.11–16 Thus, a better understanding of iron homeostasis and dysbiosis may improve treatment options in IBD. We previously reported that endogenous hepcidin, produced by dendritic cells (DCs) in the intestine, can limit iron availability to intestinal bacteria to promote mucosal healing in a mouse model of IBD.17 Hepcidin works by inhibiting ferroportin (Fpn), and Fpn inhibitors have recently shown promising clinical results for multiple hematologic indications.18–20 However, the relevant cellular targets and the clinical potential of Fpn inhibition for IBD remain unknown.

Here, we report that discrete tissue-resident colon macrophage populations express high levels of functional Fpn during homeostasis and inflammation, and its expression is regulated by the microbiome. scRNA-seq and immunostaining analysis of biopsies from IBD patients identified Fpn+ macrophage populations in the human intestine, highlighting macrophage Fpn as a relevant clinical target in IBD. In both mice and humans, macrophage Fpn expression correlates with selenoprotein P. Intestinal inflammation was found to drive iron deficiency in a tissue-restricted manner, and exogenous hepcidin delivered to the colon facilitated mucosal healing after inflammation. Exogenous hepcidin targeted colon macrophage Fpn without inducing systemic iron deficiency and suppressed pro-inflammatory bacteria within the gut. Collectively, these data demonstrate that local macrophage Fpn inhibition promotes mucosal healing in the colon without exacerbating pre-existing hypoferremia. These findings outline a previously unappreciated colon macrophage population that regulates host-microbiome symbiosis, with important implications for clinical management of iron homeostasis and chronic inflammatory disease.

RESULTS

Colonic macrophages express functional Fpn during health and inflammation

We previously reported that inhibition of LysM+ macrophage and/or neutrophil Fpn by endogenous hepcidin critically facilitates mucosal healing after intestinal inflammation.17 However, the individual contribution of macrophage- and neutrophil-intrinsic Fpn remains unexplored. To define the expression of Fpn in the colon lamina propria (cLP), we first used unbiased gating for Fpn expression, exploiting a recently developed antimouse Fpn antibody (1C7, Amgen).21–23 In both the healthy and inflamed cLP, macrophages were the primary cell type expressing Fpn (Figures 1A and 1B), although significant Fpn expression was also detected in neutrophils during inflammation, and in other CD45+ immune cells. cLP macrophages were broadly defined by the following surface marker patterns: (i) CD11b−F4/80+, (ii) CD11b+F4/80+, or (iii) F4/80−CD11b+CD64+. F4/80+CD64+ macrophages were detected in the spleen but not in the cLP (Figures S1A and S1B).

Splenic macrophage Fpn plays a critical role in systemic iron homeostasis, but its regulation and impact in intestinal inflammation is undefined.24,25 Flow cytometric analysis of the splenic myeloid cell compartment demonstrated that acute intestinal inflammation did not alter macrophage frequency, whereas neutrophils were notably increased (Figure S2A). A significant fraction (~40%) of splenic macrophages expressed Fpn, although this proportion was reduced during inflammation, as expected (Figure S2B).26,27 A fraction (~9%) of splenic neutrophils also expressed Fpn, which was increased during intestinal inflammation (Figure S2B). In spleen, the surface expression of Fpn was significantly higher in macrophages in comparison to neutrophils both during health and inflammation (Figure S2C). Thus, macrophages are also the primary site of Fpn expression in the spleen, as expected, but expression is downregulated during intestinal inflammation.

Next, we interrogated the relationship between inflammation and Fpn expression in the cLP macrophages and neutrophils. Acute inflammation resulted in a rapid expansion of macrophage (CD11b+CD64+ and CD11b+F4/80+) and neutrophil populations, whereas CD11b−F4/80+ macrophages were reduced as a percentage of hematopoietic cells (Figure S2D). In healthy colon, macrophage subsets expressed variable Fpn levels and, as in spleen, the Fpn+ fractions for all the subsets were decreased during inflammation (Figure 1C). The level of Fpn surface expression on Fpn+CD11b+F4/80+ and Fpn+CD11b−F4/80+ macrophages was reduced during inflammation (Figure 1D). Hepcidin produced by DCs,17 and/or downregulation of macrophage Slc40a1 transcription due to increased TLR signaling in the colon28,29 during intestinal inflammation, may explain the apparent downregulation of Fpn. Neutrophils present in inflamed colon also showed detectable Fpn expression (Figure 1C). Collectively, these data demonstrate that both macrophage and neutrophil populations in the colon express Fpn at varied levels, and myeloid colon Fpn is dynamically regulated during inflammation.

To test whether macrophage- or neutrophil-intrinsic Fpn is functionally relevant as a target of exogenous hepcidin, we injected FITC-labeled hepcidin directly into the colon lumen. The labeled hepcidin was actively taken up by macrophages in healthy colon, as verified by an increase in the proportion of CD11b−F4/80+ macrophages with detectable FITC (Figure 1E). Further, in mice with intestinal inflammation, labeled hepcidin specifically accumulated in macrophages but not in neutrophils (Figure 1F). Importantly, the labeled hepcidin was not taken up systemically, as evidenced by no change in FITC signal in macrophages and neutrophils of the mesenteric lymph nodes (mLNs) and spleen, both in healthy and DSS-treated mice (Figures S2E and S2F), indicating exogenous hepcidin functions locally. Together, these data suggest intestinal macrophages, especially the CD11b−F4/80+ population, express high levels of functional Fpn and serve as a specific target for exogenous hepcidin.

Fpn is expressed in selenoprotein P/complement-expressing intestinal macrophages in IBD patients

Our mouse studies indicated Fpn is highly expressed in CD11b−/F4/80+ macrophages in the colon, and previous work indicates endogenous hepcidin-mediated Fpn inhibition can promote mucosal healing after inflammation.17 Thus, we next sought to determine whether Fpn is expressed in intestinal macrophages in human Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC) patients. We first analyzed published single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) data from two independent studies.30,31 We found that macrophage and “cycling monocyte” populations in UC (Figure 2A),31 and cell cluster 11 in CD patients (Figure 2B),30 specifically expressed Slc40a1, which encodes Fpn. Cluster 11 in CD patients represent macrophages, as confirmed by Cd68 and Ccl3 expression (Table S1). Cluster 6 and 15 represent monocytes, as they were defined by Cd14 and Fcgr3a, respectively, and both lack Slc40a1 expression (Table S1). Other immune and epithelial populations did not appear to express Fpn, although neutrophils were not captured in these datasets. In the UC dataset, Slc40a1 expression was comparable between healthy patients and inflamed and non-inflamed tissue from UC patients (Figure S4).

Since our mouse studies showed striking differences in Fpn expression across macrophage populations, we used an expression correlation analysis to better understand which macrophage populations express Fpn in human IBD patients. Across the two independent studies encompassing UC and CD patients, gene correlation analysis showed a strong conserved correlation of macrophage Slc40a1 expression with the gene SEPP1 in both UC and CD patients (Figures 2C–2E; Figure S3). Notably, SEPP1 is an established marker of anti-inflammatory “M2” macrophages, which play critical roles in tissue repair.32,33 In CD, macrophage Slc40a1 gene expression was also highly correlated with macrophage complement gene expression (Figure 2E). To assess heterogeneity in Fpn expression among UC patients, we plotted Fpn levels in macrophages (Figure 2F) and cycling monocytes (Figure 2G) in each patient. Both cell types showed apparent variability in Fpn expression, suggesting that any therapeutic targeting of Fpn for IBD may benefit from prior screening for Fpn expression.

Since Fpn is tightly regulated at the post-transcriptional level,25,34 we also used antibody staining to probe for Fpn protein in IBD patients (see Table S2 for sample information). Immunostaining of small intestinal sections from healthy individuals and CD patients with the macrophage marker CD68 and Fpn antibody showed apparent cellular co-staining within the mucosa (Figure 2H). Together, these data demonstrate that intestinal macrophages in the intestine of IBD patients noticeably express Fpn, and that SEPP1 and Fpn expression might be correlated.

Colonic Fpn+ macrophages in mice co-express iron handling genes, and complement and selenoprotein genes, and are regulated by the microbiome

In our mouse studies we observed varied Fpn expression across different macrophage subsets (defined by CD11b, F4/80, and CD64) in the colon (Figures 1A–1D). Prior work has characterized a highly heterogeneous and microbiome-dependent composition of colonic macrophages.35 To transcriptionally define mouse macrophage populations and understand how mouse and human Fpn+ macrophages are related, we performed scRNA-seq analysis on FACS-sorted Fpn+ macrophages from the cLP (Figure 3A). We generated 1,395 high quality single cell transcriptomes from sorted Fpn+ macrophages, pooling cells in the CD11b−F4/80+, CD11b+F4/80+, and CD11b+CD64+ gates. The transcriptome of each cell was projected into a two-dimensional uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) space. The UMAP projection of single cell transcriptomes is partitioned into ten clusters. Reference-based annotation, using SingleR, identified that clusters 0, 1, 2, 7, and 8 represented macrophages, accounting for 60% of the single cell profiles, while other clusters included contaminating granulocytes, monocytes, T cells, activated fibroblasts, and B cells (Figures 3B and 3C).

Fpn is highly expressed in splenic red pulp macrophages (RPMs) and is essential for iron recycling from damaged or senescent red blood cells (RBCs).25 The RPM iron recycling transcriptional program is driven by heme-mediated induction of the transcription factor Spic, which subsequently induces multiple genes including the heme oxygenase, Hmox1, and Slc40a1, encoding Fpn.36–38 Interestingly, cluster 7 and 2 showed the highest relative expression of Hmox1, with lower levels of Slc40a1 (Figure 3D), consistent with a heme/Spic-directed iron recycling transcriptional program. Importantly, cluster 7 and 2 were lacking high expression of Itgam transcript (encoding CD11b), consistent with CD11b−F4/80+ macrophages as the site of highest Fpn expression and hepcidin binding (Figure 3D and 1C–1F). As the sensitivity of scRNA-seq is low,39,40 we could not detect robust signals for SpiC and Adgre1 (encoding F4/80) transcripts in these clusters (Figure 3D). Further, the high expression of Selenop (known as SEPP1 in humans) and C1qa by cluster 7 and 2 (relative to other clusters) correlated with human UC and CD patient data, where Slc40a1 was correlated with these transcripts (Figures 2C–2E and 3D). Other genes implicated in active iron handling such as, Trf, Pcbp1, and Ednrb were also identified as unique cluster markers in cluster 7 and/or 2 (Table S3).41–45 These data together indicate that macrophages associated with clusters 7 and 2 are specialized in actively handling iron in the cLP and are conserved in mice and humans.

Next, since macrophage iron handling in the intestine remains poorly defined, we sought to understand how macrophage Fpn expression is regulated. We first explored whether the Fpn expression in macrophages is impacted by bacterial TLR signaling, since it was reported that TLR signaling suppresses macrophage Fpn expression.27,28,46,47 We generated in vitro macrophages through bone marrow culture and stimulated them with lipopolysaccharide (LPS). LPS treatment resulted in a significant downregulation of Slc40a1 transcript as expected (Figure 3E).26,27 However, the expression of surface Fpn was not reduced upon LPS treatment, perhaps due to insufficient hepcidin or slow Fpn turnover in the culture conditions (Figure S5A). In human data, we observed that different IBD patients showed differential Fpn expression (Figure 2F), and a critical role of the microbiome in defining the transcriptional profile of colonic macrophages has been described previously.35 Thus, we asked whether Fpn expression in cLP macrophages is impacted by the microbiome. We colonized germ-free (GF) mice by cohousing them with a mouse from our specific pathogen-free (SPF) facility for 3 weeks (Figure 3F). Microbiome colonization of GF mice led to a significant increase in cLP neutrophil fraction in comparison to GF control mice, whereas all the macrophage subsets and other cell fractions were unaltered (Figure S5B). Colonization of GF mice was associated with a significant reduction of Fpn+ cell fractions for all the cell types tested including macrophages (Figures 3G and 3H), when compared to controls that remained GF. Furthermore, the surface Fpn level was specifically reduced in CD11b−F4/80+ macrophages (Figure 3I). These data altogether suggest that while Fpn+ cLP macrophages are highly heterogeneous, a specific transcriptional program associated with iron recycling genes, selenoprotein P, and complement is conserved in mice and humans, and that Fpn expression in cLP is dynamically regulated by the microbiome.

Iron-recycling colon macrophages are tissue-resident and not derived from monocytes

Two observations suggested that CD11b−Fpn+ colon macrophages may be tissue-resident: first, intestinal macrophages with low CD11b expression were previously identified as tissue-resident48; next, the macrophage clusters 2 and 7, which were enriched for iron-handling genes (Figures 3D; Table S3), were also enriched for Lyve1, Timd4, and Folr2. These genes have been extensively characterized as markers of long-lived tissue-resident macrophages in multiple tissues, including the intestine.49–51 To better understand macrophage heterogeneity and ontogeny in our Fpn+ scRNA dataset, we filtered out contaminating non-macrophages and re-clustered the macrophage transcriptomes (Figure 4A). Filtered macrophage cluster 3 was identified as the likely tissue-resident, iron-handling macrophage population in the colon, due to the appearance of Ednrb and Lyve1 among the top five most-enriched genes in this cluster (Figure 4B). Further, cluster 3 was significantly enriched for markers of tissue residency (Lyve1, Timd4, Folr2) and iron-handling (Ednrb, Bach1, Cd163, Hmox1, Tfrc), as well Selenop, the mouse homolog of human SEPP1, which was highly correlated with macrophage Slc40a1 expression across IBD datasets (Figures 4C; Table S4). Cluster 1 also showed significantly enriched expression for Selenop, Cd163, and Hmox1, suggesting clusters 1 and 3 may be functionally or developmentally related. Meanwhile, clusters 2 (Cd80, Cd86, Cx3cr1, Il1b, H2-Aa, H2-Ab, H2-DMb1), 4 (H2-Aa, H2-Ab), and 5 (Ccr2, Arg1) showed enrichment for genes involved in a wide array of macrophage biology (Table S4).

While markers of tissue residency may be induced over time in monocyte-derived macrophages, the lifetime of monocyte-derived macrophages in the colon is short, leading us to hypothesize that Folr2/Timd4/Lyve1-expressing Fpn+CD11b− macrophages are not monocyte-derived. We used a recently developed mouse strain, Ms4a3Cre-RosaTdT, to definitively identify monocyte-derived colon macrophages.52 CD11b−F4/80+ macrophages, which express the highest levels of Fpn in the colon, were weakly labeled in Ms4a3Cre-RosaTdT mice (Figures 4D and 4E). By contrast, CD11b+CD64+ and CD11b+F4/80+ macrophages were efficiently labeled (Figures 4D and 4E). These data are consistent with a model where Fpn+CD11b−F4/80+ macrophages are long-lived, rare, tissue-resident cells that are not continuously replenished by blood monocytes.48–50 In further agreement with this model, DSS is known to induce rapid recruitment and differentiation of monocyte-derived macrophages53; however, while DSS significantly increased the relative population of CD11b+CD64+ and CD11b+F4/80+ macrophages in the colon, CD11b−F4/80+ macrophages actually became less abundant (Figure S2D).

Intestinal hepcidin delivery does not impact systemic iron homeostasis

Iron-deficiency anemia is a challenging complication in IBD patients that limits quality of life.54 Despite data suggesting that Fpn inhibition in the intestine can promote mucosal healing,17 Fpn inhibition can also exacerbate iron deficiency,19,20,55 so a better understanding of local and systemic iron homeostasis during intestinal inflammation is critical before Fpn inhibition can be applied in the clinic. Thus, we measured total iron levels in intestinal tissues as well as spleen and liver (sites of systemic iron storage) and the serum during DSS-induced acute inflammation. As expected,56–58 DSS treatment suppressed serum iron levels (Figure 5A). Total spleen iron levels and normalized iron levels (on weight/weight basis) in the liver or small intestine were not impacted with DSS treatment (Figure 5A). In contrast, iron levels within large intestine were lower in the DSS-treated group (Figure 5A). Thus, acute inflammation in the colon drives iron deficiency in local colon tissue and in the serum, but not in liver or spleen. Next, we investigated the impact of systemic hepcidin, delivered intra-peritoneally (IP), on iron homeostasis in healthy C57BL/6J mice one hour post injection. Consistent with previous work,55 IP hepcidin administration significantly reduced serum iron levels causing hypoferremia, whereas spleen and liver iron levels were not altered (Figure 5B). As in healthy mice, IP hepcidin administration in DSS-treated mice further exacerbated hypoferremia, whereas spleen and liver iron levels were not impacted (Figure 5C). These data imply that systemic Fpn inhibition with exogenous hepcidin risks exacerbating hypoferremia and iron-deficiency anemia in IBD patients.

As an alternate strategy, we evaluated how hepcidin delivered directly into the colon regulates iron homeostasis. We delivered hepcidin directly to the colon lumen via intracolonic instillation and measured iron levels one hour after injection. Hepcidin delivered directly into the colon lumen of healthy mice did not reduce iron levels in serum, spleen, liver, small intestine, or large intestine (Figure 5D). Likewise, colon-specific hepcidin delivery in DSS-treated mice did not impact serum or tissue iron levels (Figure 5E), suggesting this approach will not exacerbate hypoferremia and iron deficiency. Taken together, these data show that DSS-induced acute inflammation specifically reduces serum and colon iron levels, and hepcidin delivery directly into the colon lumen spares serum iron.

Intestinal hepcidin delivery promotes mucosal healing by acting on macrophage Fpn

Next, we sought to test whether delivery of exogenous hepcidin into the colon promotes mucosal healing. To deliver hepcidin into the colon, we adapted an established method to allow oral delivery of peptides by encapsulation in pectin-hydrogel beads, which protect peptides from digestive proteases.59,60 These pectin hydrogel beads are degraded in the colon by the microbiota, and as a result the encapsulated peptides are released specifically in the colon. We delivered intestinal hepcidin via pectin-hydrogel beads after the onset of severe DSS-induced intestinal inflammation to model a newly diagnosed IBD case prior to treatment. In this context, exogenous intestinal hepcidin delivery showed earlier signs of recovery in body weight in comparison to unloaded bead controls (Figure 6A). Importantly, hepcidin-mediated enhancement in weight recovery was not associated with reduction of serum iron levels (Figure 6B), suggesting it promotes mucosal healing without deteriorating pre-existing hypoferremia. These data suggest that colon-specific Fpn inhibition may be a promising strategy to promote mucosal healing in IBD patients.

Prior studies have implicated macrophages as drivers of intestinal inflammation in IBD,61,62 and also suggested that endogenous hepcidin produced by DCs can support an anti-inflammatory microbiome composition and promote clearance of tissue bacteria during healing.17 To determine whether exogenous hepcidin functions by limiting intestinal inflammation, we examined the inflammatory marker lipocalin-2 (Lcn2) in the feces. The Lcn2 levels were comparable between hepcidin and control groups on day 10 and day 14 post DSS-treatment (Figure 6C), indicating no direct impact of exogenous hepcidin on inflammation. We also found no impact of hepcidin treatment on T cell or neutrophil populations in the colon (Figure 6D), nor on expression of inflammatory cytokines (Figure 6E).

To investigate whether exogenous hepcidin regulates the microbiome, we profiled the microbiome community at three distinct phases. First, in healthy mice, we found no impact of three daily doses of exogenous hepcidin on the microbiome at the genus level (Figure 6F). By contrast, on day 10, after three successive treatments with hepcidin or control beads, we found hepcidin treatment was associated specifically with a relative expansion of Monoglobus spp. and a contraction of Parasutterella spp. (Figure 6G). Notably, Parasutterella has been extensively associated with IBD and intestinal inflammation in prior studies,8,63–65 suggesting a causative role in exacerbating intestinal inflammation. Finally, on day 14, Dubosiella spp. were significantly less abundant in hepcidin-treated mice compared to controls (Figure 6H). Thus, exogenous delivery of hepcidin to the colon modulates the microbiome only in the setting of acute inflammation, and may promote healing by limiting pathogenic Parasutterella without directly impacting inflammation.

Finally, we investigated whether intestinally delivered exogenous hepcidin functions via myeloid cells such as macrophages and/or neutrophils, as has been suggested for endogenous DC-derived hepcidin.17 We employed a Slc40a1C326Y–LysM mouse model66 that expresses a hepcidin-resistant Fpn variant, Slc40a1C326Y, in both macrophages and neutrophils through LysMCre mediated recombination. Oral administration of hepcidin- or control beads into Slc40a1C326Y–LysM mice after DSS-induced intestinal inflammation displayed indistinguishable recovery in weights, indicating that the therapeutic effect of hepcidin was lost (Figure 6I). As non-monocytic CD11b−F4/80+ macrophages express the highest levels of Fpn in the colon, the exogenous hepcidin likely functions specifically via this rare Fpn+ population. Altogether, our data suggest that exogenous hepcidin delivered into the colon lumen can promote recovery from inflammation by inhibiting Fpn on macrophages, and subsequently limiting pathogenic bacteria, without inducing systemic hypoferremia (Figure S6).

DISCUSSION

In the current study, we delineate the Fpn expression pattern in the intestinal and spleen myeloid compartment during health and disease, and we identify unique markers and regulation of an iron-handling intestinal macrophage population found in mice and humans. In healthy mice, intestinal macrophage populations express varying levels of Fpn, which is downregulated during DSS-induced acute inflammation. Fpn expression was also detected on neutrophils from inflamed intestine. The downregulation of macrophage Fpn might be the result of increased hepcidin production17 and/or TLR signaling28,29 during intestinal inflammation. Alternatively, DSS-elicited, newly recruited monocyte-derived macrophages may intrinsically express lower Fpn compared to homeostatic resident macrophages.

Several studies have described discrete macrophage subsets in the colon35,49,67–71 and have demonstrated a role of commensal microbiota in regulating colon macrophage differentiation and/or maintenance.48,50,67 However, intestinal macrophage iron homeostasis remains largely unexplored. Our scRNA-seq data on Fpn+ macrophages isolated from the cLP displayed significant heterogeneity.35 We identified distinct Fpn+ cLP macrophage clusters that express multiple genes implicated in iron handling such as Hmox1, CD163, and Tfrc. These genes are involved in heme-iron catabolism, iron storage, and intracellular iron trafficking, respectively, and are prominently expressed by splenic red pulp macrophages that recycle iron from senescent red blood cells.42,44,72 These colon iron-handling macrophages also showed relatively low levels of Itgam (which encodes CD11b), but high levels of Selenop and C1qa, which were also correlated with Fpn expression in macrophages from IBD patients. A very recent study in Crohn’s patients specifically identified enrichment of a Lyve1+/Folr2+/CD163+/C1q+ macrophage population within fistula, supporting the relevance of this population in IBD pathology.73 Additionally, another study identified a microbiota-dependent macrophage population marked by Ccl2, Ccl7, CD163, CD36, and Lyve1,35 and iron-handling colon macrophages in our study share enriched expression of these markers. Finally, a very recent study identified that Ednrb marks iron-rich tumor associated macrophages,45 and we find Ednrb is also uniquely expressed in colon iron-handling macrophages. Altogether, our study identifies a unique, tissue-resident, non-monocyte-derived colon macrophage population co-expressing a microbiome-dependent iron-recycling transcriptional program, along with markers of tissue-repair-associated macrophages. The low level of CD11b expression and the rarity of this population may explain why this population has been overlooked to this point.74

What is the functional relevance of iron recycling macrophages in the intestine? In prior studies, we discovered that mice lacking the endogenous Fpn inhibitor, hepcidin, in DCs display impaired mucosal healing after intestinal inflammation.17 Since IBD patients are not known to be deficient for endogenous hepcidin production, in the current study we explored whether additional, exogenous Fpn inhibition in the colon can promote mucosal healing in hepcidin-sufficient mammals. Therapeutic potential for localized Fpn inhibition has been suggested for treating group A Streptococcus-derived necrotizing fasciitis as it prevented bacterial dissemination,75 and systemic Fpn inhibition was recently FDA-approved to treat polycythemia vera.18,20 Here, we identify that exogenous macrophage Fpn inhibition in the colon promotes recovery after intestinal inflammation. The therapeutic effect was associated with suppression of proinflammatory Parasutterella, which has been linked to CD, irritable bowel syndrome, microbial dysbiosis, and mucosal barrier penetration.8,64,76 Crucially, colon-specific Fpn inhibition had no impact on systemic iron levels, which should avoid worsening IBD-associated anemia.54,77 Thus, we conclude that macrophage iron recycling via Fpn in the colon crucially regulates the microbiome and mucosal healing, and the specificity and detailed mechanisms at play will be the subject of future study. A recent study reported the development of a gut bacterium engineered to produce hepcidin,78 and our work suggests this strain may be therapeutic in IBD.

These studies shed new light on iron, colon macrophages, and host-microbiome symbiosis. While it is appreciated that intestinal bacteria drive the functional diversification of macrophages,35 the impact, mechanics, and cellular specificity of myeloid cell sampling of lumen contents remains incompletely understood.79–83 Our work implicates a CD11b−F4/80+ myeloid population in sampling luminal hepcidin during homeostasis and inflammation, and in subsequent regulation (via iron export) of microbial colonization and mucosal healing after inflammation. Conversely, we find that microbiome colonization also regulates the expression of the iron exporter Fpn in macrophages. These findings add to recent reports that microbial colonization increases Fpn and ferritin protein levels in the entire colon,84 and that gut microbiota-derived metabolites such as reuterin and 1,3-diaminopropane decrease duodenal Fpn expression and iron absorption in an iron overload mouse model.85 Previous studies have also demonstrated cytokine- and LPS-mediated Fpn down-regulation in monocytes and macrophages, suggesting a potentially distinct hepcidin-independent mechanism of improving IBD resolution through transcriptional regulation.26,28 These findings together with our study demonstrate a nuanced reciprocal regulation of iron homeostasis between mammals and their intestinal microbiome, with critical impact on inflammatory disease.

Broadly, our studies identify a functional population of tissue-resident, iron-recycling macrophages in the colon, with Fpn expression dynamically regulated during health and disease. Fpn expression was also detected in macrophages from inflamed intestinal biopsies of human IBD patients, and inhibition of mouse Fpn with exogenous hepcidin promoted mucosal healing without causing systemic hypoferremia. Altogether, these data highlight colon macrophage Fpn as a promising pre-clinical target to promote mucosal healing in IBD.

Limitations of the study

We demonstrated that colon macrophage Fpn inhibition via exogenous hepcidin promotes mucosal healing in a mouse model of colitis. However, the kinetics of exogenous hepcidin, and whether it directly downregulates colon macrophage Fpn and increases iron retention within them remains unclear. A critical limitation of the study is the lack of functional experiments such as loss-of-function approaches, restricting our ability to definitively conclude the causal relationship of microbiome changes with observed phenotype. Studies in gnotobiotic mice with defined microbial communities will be required to better define this microbiome-dependent mechanism. A comprehensive analysis for delineating the ontogeny of Fpn+CD11b− F4/80+ macrophages is needed to confirm their tissue-resident identity, and the regulation of Fpn expression within this population. Future studies investigating these questions will deepen the mechanistic understanding of this population.

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

Animals

C57BL/6J mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratories and used at 8–12 weeks of age. Slc40a1C326Y–Floxed mice were originally generated by Dr. Lakhal-Littleton.66 Ms4a3Cre and Ai14 tdTomato strains (Jackson Laboratories) were crossed to generate Ms4a3Cre-RosaTdT mice. Both male and female mice were used in this study, and all individual experiments were sex-matched. The influence of mouse sex on results can not be strictly determined, since experimental design was agnostic to sex. However, no experiment showed significant differences between biological replicates of female and male mice cohorts, when wdata for both were available. All mice were bred on a C57BL/6J background and maintained in a 12h light/dark cycle in a specific pathogen-free facility at Rutgers University. All animal work was performed according to the approved protocol by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at Rutgers University.

Human samples

Healthy samples were obtained from Biochain, and CD patient samples were obtained during surgical re-section, following IRB-approved study protocols (see Table S2 for sample information). The influence of sex and geneer on results can not be determined from this study. Individual samples were not assigned to experimental groups for this study.

METHOD DETAILS

In vitro treatment of bone marrow derived macrophages

For in vitro macrophage experiments, bone marrow derived macrophages (BMDMs) were generated by culturing bone marrow mononuclear cells from C57BL/6J mice in RPMI 1640 (Corning) supplemented with 20ng/mL macrophage colony stimulating factor (M-CSF) (ThermoFisher), 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Sigma), and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Sigma). BMDM cultures were treated with 100ng/mL of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) (Sigma) on Day 6 for 16 h and cells were collected for flow cytometry and qPCR analysis.

Isolation of cells from spleen, mLN and cLP

The tissues were analyzed from healthy and inflamed mice on day 7 of DSS-treatment. Spleen and mLN were collected at necropsy in ice-cold RPMI 1640 (Corning) having 2% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Sigma) and mechanically disrupted through 70μm nylon mesh to prepare single-cell suspensions. For colons, remaining fat tissue was cleaned and colons were cut open longitudinally. All luminal contents were removed by gentle scraping with forceps and subsequently washed with cold PBS (Corning). Epithelial cells were dissociated by incubation on a shaker in PBS containing 1mM EDTA (Invitrogen), 1mM DTT (Sigma), and 5% FBS (Sigma) for 30 min at 37°C. Subsequently, samples were vortexed, and the epithelial fraction was discarded. To obtain lamina propria lymphocytes, enzymatic digestion of the remaining tissue was performed with 0.5mg/mL collagenase/dispase (Roche) and 20μg/mL DNaseI (Sigma) for 40 min at 37°C.

DSS administration and oral hepcidin treatment

‘Colitis-grade’ dextran sodium sulfate (DSS) with average MW of 36,000–50,000 Da (MP Biomedicals) was added to drinking water at day 0. In all the DSS experiments sex- and age-matched mice were used. DSS was given ad libitum for 7 days to induce significant inflammation, confirmed by ~10% weight loss. Mice were weighed at the same time of day at indicated time points. After DSS treatment for 7 days, mice were switched to water and allowed to recover for next 7 days. During the recovery phase mice were treated orally for three days (on day 7, day 8, and day 9) with either hepcidin-loaded pectin hydrogel beads or unloaded beads as a control. To make the pectin hydrogel beads, we adapted a previously published protocol.60,87 Briefly, human hepcidin-25 peptide (VWR) re-suspended in phosphate buffered saline (PBS), or PBS alone (for unloaded bead control), was diluted into 6% pectin (Sigma) dissolved in ultrapure water. This pectin solution was then added dropwise through a 23-gauge needle into a 1% Zein (Sigma)/0.5% (w/v) CaCl2 solution in 85% ethanol. The resulting pectin hydrogel beads were then washed in ultrapure water before use. Beads were administered via oral gavage at a dose corresponding to either 25μg or 0μg hepcidin. For experiments with Slc40a1C326Y–LysM mice, littermate mice were used as controls. The health of mice was monitored by measuring weight recovery daily and visual observation of mobility and grooming behavior.

Acute hepcidin dosing

For hepcidin tracing experiments, healthy mice or DSS-treated mice on day 7 of DSS-treatment, were injected intra-peritoneally (IP) or into the colon lumen (3cm beyond the anus) with either 100μg of FITC-hepcidin (Vivitide) in PBS or only PBS for the hepcidin-treated or control groups, respectively. Similar regimen was also used for unlabelled hepcidin administration experiments for iron measurements. Mice were euthanized one hour after hepcidin administration and serum was collected for iron measurement, and colon, mLN, and spleen were collected for flow cytometry. For iron measurement, spleen, liver, and small and large intestines were collected after perfusion with PBS.

Flow cytometry and sorting

Single-cell suspensions from tissues (cLP, spleen, and mLN) and BMDMs from in vitro cultures were stained with conjugated antibodies on ice in PBS containing 2mM EDTA and 2% FCS. Prior to staining, Fc receptors were blocked with unlabelled anti-CD16/32 (clone 2.4G2, BD biosciences) antibody. To exclude dead cells from the analysis, Live/Dead Aqua viability kit (BioLegend) was used. Antibodies used for flow cytometry are mostly from BioLegend and BD Biosciences. The following antibodies were used CD45-BUV395 (30-F11), CD11b-APC-Cy7 or BV605 (M1/70), Ly6G-AF647 or PE-Dazzle594 (1A8), CD64-BV421 (X54-5/7.1), CD4-BV785 (GK1.5) and F4/80-AF700 (BM8). The anti-Fpn antibody (1C7) was kindly provided by Amgen21 and was labeled with Alexa Fluor 594 labeling kit (Invitrogen). All antibodies were used at 1:200 dilution except Fpn, which was used at 1:100. Data were acquired on a custom-figured 5-laser, 18-color BD Fortessa and analyzed with FlowJo software (Tree Star). In sorting for scRNA-seq, Fpn+ positive CD11b− F4/80+, CD11b+F480+, and CD11b+CD64+ macrophage subsets were collected in a single tube pooled from twelve C57BL/6J mice and subjected to scRNA-seq.

RNA isolation and quantitative real-time PCR

RNA from control and LPS treated BMDMs was extracted using a Direct-zol RNA microprep kit (Zymo Research) with on-column DNase I treatment. For all DSS-treated tissues, RNA was purified via lithium chloride extraction. cDNA was prepared with Superscript reverse transcriptase II (ThermoFisher) according to manufacturer’s instructions. For quantitative real-time PCR, the amplification of cDNA was monitored with SYBR green (ThermoFisher) on the CFX Opus 384 real-time PCR system (Bio-Rad). The transcript levels were normalized with Hprt1, and relative expressions were displayed. Primers used in quantitative real-time PCR are: Hprt1 For: CTTGCTGGTGAAAAGGACCTCTC, Rev: GAAGTACTCATTATAGTCAAGGGCA, Slc40a1 For: TTGCAGGAGTCATTGCTGCTA, Rev: TGGAGTTCTGCACACCATTGAT,88 Tnfa For: CACGCTCTTCTGTCTACTGA, Rev: ATCTGAGTGTGAGGGTCTGG, Il6 For: TCCAGTTGCCTTCTTGGGAC Rev: GTGTAATTAAGCCTCCGACTTG, Il17 For: AAGGCAGCAGCGATCATCC, Rev: GGAACG GTTGAGGTAGTCTGAG.

scRNA-seq data analysis

For human scRNA-seq data, raw or processed data were accessed from the respective repository (GSE134809 and SCP 259).30,31 All subsequent processing was performed using the Seurat package (v4.0.3) within R (v4.1.0). GSE134809 was provided as a raw matrix. Low quality cells were filtered from the dataset and read counts were normalized using scTransform method.89 Cells with percentage of reads of mitochondrial origin >10%, with percentage of reads of ribosomal origin >45%, with <200 feature counts, with >6000 feature counts were defined as low quality cells. Samples were integrated with the Seurat integrate function90 and clustered via uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) according to nearest neighbors. The data from SCP 259 were pre-filtered so the raw counts were directly normalized using the scTransform method89 and clustered via UMAP according to nearest neighbors. For GSE134809, cluster markers were identified using a wilcoxon rank-sum test within Seurat (FindAllMarkers) with a log2FC threshold of 0.5 and minimum percentage difference of 0.3.

For mouse scRNA-seq data, raw reads were barcode deconvoluted and aligned to the reference genome (mm10) via cellranger (v8.0.1). All subsequent processing was performed using the Seurat package within R (v4). Low quality cells (cells with percentage of reads of mitochondrial origin >10%, with percentage of reads of ribosomal origin >45%, with <100 feature counts, with >6000 feature counts) were filtered from the dataset, and read counts were normalized using the scTransform method.89 Samples were clustered via UMAP according to nearest neighbors and cell type were assigned using SingleR91 against the MouseRNASeqData database built from 358 bulk RNA-seq datasets available on GEO.92

Immunofluorescence assay

The formalin-fixed paraffin embedded (FFPE) small intestinal tissue sections on glass slides were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated in decreasing concentration of ethanol. Antigen retrieval was performed at 95°C for 10 min with citrate buffer (pH 6), followed by blocking with 2% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 30 min. After blocking, tissue sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C and then secondary and tertiary antibodies, each for 30 min at room temperature. The following antibodies were used: AlexaFluor-555-labeled anti-human CD68 (Clone KP1, Abcam); AlexaFluor-488-labeled anti-human Fpn (clone 31A5), kindly provided by Amgen21; Rabbit Anti-AlexaFluor-488 (ThermoFisher A-11094); AlexaFluor-647-labeled anti-rabbit Fab (ThermoFisher A-21246). Slides were mounted with diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (ThermoFisher) and sealed. Images were taken with a laser confocal microscope (Nikon) and fluorescence intensities and colocalization were analyzed with NIS-Elements Viewer software 5.21.

Iron measurements

Serum iron levels were measured with ferrozine-based colorimetric assay (Pointe Scientific). Iron levels in spleen, liver, small and large intestines were measured with graphite furnace atomic absorption spectrometry (GFAAS). The tissues were first digested in 3 mL (liver, small and large intestines) or 1 mL (spleen) of nitric acid (ThermoFisher), overnight, followed by heating at 95°C for 2h. Subsequently, equal volume of hydrogen peroxide (ThermoFisher) was added, and the mixture was incubated at 95°C for 1h. These samples were further diluted and used for iron estimation on GFAAS.

Lipocalin-2 (Lcn2) detection

To assess inflammation, Lcn2 levels were estimated with ELISA (R&D systems) on day 10 and day 14 fecal samples.

Microbiome profiling

For determining hepcidin-dependent microbiome changes, samples were collected on day 10 and day 14 from DSS-treated mice. On day 10, fecal samples were collected from live mice, and at day 14, mice were euthanized, and complete colon contents were collected. For the healthy mouse group, mice were treated with hepcidin-loaded or unloaded control beads on day −3, −2, and −1, and fecal samples were collected on day 0. Genomic DNA was isolated using the PowerFecalPro kit (Qiagen), and 16S rRNA genes were sequenced after amplification using standard primers (F: CCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG and R: GACTACH VGGGTATCTAATC-C).86 All 16S rRNA profiling was performed with mice that were randomized between cages prior to the bead treatment, to eliminate confounding cage effects. Amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were quantified utilizing dada2 (v1.22.0) and assigned taxonomic classifications using the SILVA (v138) database. At each time point, sequences from hepcidin-treated and corresponding empty bead-treated controls were compared using PCoA (phyloseq v1.38.0) and differential abundance analyses (DESeq2 v1.34.0).

The following notations are used to indicate statistical significance: * = p-value < 0.05, ** = p-value < 0.01, *** = p-value < 0.001 and **** = p-value < 0.0001. Absence of asterisk means no significance compared to control.
