DENSITY FUNCTIONAL THEORY ANALYSIS OF CANDIDATE TRITIUM PERMEATION BARRIERS
FOR VANADIUM ALLOYS AS A FUSION STRUCTURAL MATERIAL – Mary Alice Cusentino, Anne Davis
(Sandia National Laboratories)

OBJECTIVE

Vanadium alloys are being considered as a candidate structural material for the breeder blanket component
of future fusion reactors due to their good thermal conductivity, low activation, and chemical compatibility
with FLiBe [1]. However, vanadium has a high hydrogen solubility, resulting in hydrogen diffusion and
trapping in the vanadium structural alloy and lower than expected tritium breeding ratios. To mitigate the
permeation of hydrogen into the structural alloy, a tritium permeation barrier will be coated onto the
vanadium alloy. Multiple materials have been proposed as candidate tritium permeation barriers, but the
extent to which each of these materials impacts hydrogen diffusion and trapping is still unknown. In this
project, we perform atomic-scale calculations to narrow down the list of potential tritium permeation barrier
candidates and to understand the mechanisms through which hydrogen diffusion is affected in these
materials.

SUMMARY

We have performed density functional theory (DFT) calculations on thirteen candidate tritium permeation
barrier (TPB) materials, which include a selection of oxides, carbides, nitrides, and metals. The Vienna Ab
initio Simulation Package (VASP) [2] was used to calculate hydrogen solution energies at common
interstitial sites, adsorption energies on the lowest energy surfaces, and migration barriers using Nudged
Elastic Band (NEB) methods. The lowest energy sites and diffusion barriers for each material were identified
and ranked from highest to lowest energy to select the most promising materials. We have selected two
materials to develop atomic cluster expansion (ACE) machine learned interatomic potentials (ML-IAP) [3]
to perform large-scale molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of hydrogen diffusion and irradiation damage
in the future.

PROGRESS AND STATUS

Density Functional Theory Simulation Methods

 We performed DFT calculations using the projector augmented wave potential method [4,5]
implemented in VASP [2]. The generalized gradient approximation was used to handle electron exchange
and correlation. A kinetic energy cutoff of 500 eV was implemented for the plane-wave basis. We used k-
point grids of various densities depending on the size of the unit cell of the material and the property being
calculated. Our grids ranged in size between 2x2x2 and 4x4x4 k-points. For adsorption energy calculations,
only 1 k-point was used in the z-direction. The Monkhorst-Pack method was used to generate the k-point
grid. The structural optimization was truncated when the force converged to less than 0.02 eV/Angstom.
For Cr2O3, we employed spin-polarized calculations. The system was initialized in an anti-ferromagnetic
state with an initial magnetic moment for chromium of 2.935, as taken from the Materials project [6]. We
also included a U term of 3.7, as taken from the Materials project [6], to better simulate the insulating nature
of Cr2O3.

 Three properties of interest were calculated for each candidate material: hydrogen solution
energies, hydrogen adsorption energies, and hydrogen migration barriers. For solution energy and
migration barrier calculations, supercells varied in size between 128-640 atoms. The initial atomic positions
were optimized before calculation to ensure that the lattice parameters were a good match to previous
literature data. To calculate solution energies, hydrogen was place into various interstitial sites within the

crystal structures, identified from previous studies. The structures with hydrogen were minimized. Solution
energies were calculated using Equation 1,

 Esolution = Ebulk+interstitial – Ebulk – n*Einterstitial (Eq. 1)

where Ebulk+interstitial is the total energy of the structure with hydrogen inserted into the interstitial site, Ebulk is
the energy of the initial supercell, n is the number of hydrogen atoms (1 for these calculations) and
Einterstitial is the energy of a single hydrogen atom. Einterstitial is obtained by placing an H2 molecule in a box,
relaxing the box, and dividing the energy by 2. To calculate adsorption energies, the crystal structures for
each material were sliced to reveal various low angle surfaces using the ASE package [7]. Supercells of
these surfaces contained a minimum of 10 layers of atoms with an equivalently sized vacuum layer.
Supercells varied in size from 100-400 atoms. The initial atomic positions were optimized, and the surface
energies were calculated according to Equation 2,

 Esurface = (Eslab – nslab*Ebulk)/(2*A) (Eq. 2)

where Eslab is the total energy of the supercell, nslab is the number of atoms in the supercell, Ebulk is the per
atom energy of the bulk material, and A is the surface area. Once the surface with the lowest energy is
identified, hydrogen atoms are placed at various sites on the surface. We then freeze the bottom half of the
atoms from moving and minimize the structure, allowing the hydrogen atom to relax on the surface.
Adsorption energies were calculated using Equation 3,

 Eadsorption = Eslab+adsorbate – Eslab – Eadsorbate (Eq. 3)

where Eslab+adsorbate is the total energy of the supercell with the adsorbed hydrogen, Eslab is the energy of the
supercell, and Eadsorbate is the energy of an isolated hydrogen atom. For migration barrier calculations, we

Results

 We report the results of the solution energy calculations, which indicate the change in energy of
the crystal structure when a hydrogen atom is added to a specific interstitial site. Positive solution energies
indicate that adding a hydrogen atom is energetically unfavorable, while negative values indicate the
opposite. We present the results from our solution energy analysis of 13 candidate tritium permeation
barrier materials in Figure 1. For each material, we list the most energetically favorable interstitial site for
hydrogen that we calculated, which would be the most likely site to find hydrogen in a pristine crystal
structure. The materials have been ordered in the table from highest solution energy to lowest. Materials
located closer to the top are better suited as tritium permeation barriers. Based on our results, the best
performing materials are BeO, Al2O3, AlN, CaO, and SiC. In Figure 1, we have also attached images of
hydrogen sitting in the most favorable interstitial sites for BeO and Al2O3.

 Next, we report the results of the hydrogen adsorption energy calculations, which indicate the
change in energy when a hydrogen atom is adsorbed at a specific site on a surface. Similar to the solution
energies, lower values indicate more energetically favorable sites for hydrogen to be adsorbed. We present
the results for the 13 candidate materials in Figure 2. In the table, we report the lowest energy surface we
calculated for each material as well as the most favorable surface site for hydrogen, with materials again
ordered from highest to lowest adsorption energy. Materials near the top of the table are better suited to be

 This DFT work helps narrow down the list of tritium permeation barrier materials. Although many
materials have been proposed as good potential barrier materials, a full comparison of their material
properties at the atomic scale has not been previously performed to our knowledge. In this work, we not
only identify the materials with the best performance in these three metrics, but we also gain mechanistic
understanding of hydrogen behavior at the atomic level in these materials.

FUTURE WORK

 We will continue calculating migration barriers for the nine remaining tritium permeation barrier
materials. In the meantime, we will move forward with building an ACE ML-IAP for two of the most promising
candidate materials, Al2O3 and SiC. We will use the DFT data generated in this study as training data to
build the potentials. Additionally, we will generate new data to create a more complete training set with
many different structures for the machine-learned model. The ML-IAPs will be used to run large-scale

hydrogen permeation simulations using MD to better understand tritium diffusion and trapping in these two
candidate materials.
