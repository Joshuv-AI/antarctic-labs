 ABSTRACT
The Advanced Materials and Manufacturing Technologies program aims to accelerate the development,
qualification, demonstration, and deployment of advanced materials and manufacturing technologies,
such as additive manufacturing (AM), to enable reliable and economical nuclear energy. However,
the characteristic process-structure-property relationships of AM materials pose challenges for the
qualification and certification of AM nuclear components. In particular, component-scale variations
in microstructure and properties can be driven by localized changes in melt pool dynamics due to how
process parameters interact with different part geometries. Computational modeling tools can play a
crucial role in predicting and controlling this variability. This report presents final results on process
modeling tools designed to predict microstructure variability in additively manufactured stainless steel
316 (SS316) parts. It details the software packages and physical modeling approaches employed to
simulate an AM component within an automated process modeling workflow. Results are demonstrated
through comparisons between predicted microstructures and experimental measurements across various
representative processing conditions. The report concludes by discussing identified challenges and future
opportunities for connecting the developed simulation workflow with mechanics simulations for prediction
of part performance.

 1. INTRODUCTION
Metal additive manufacturing (AM) processes enable the creation of complex geometries and offer unique
material properties that are beneficial for various applications, including in the nuclear energy sector. AM
components have the potential to deliver enhanced performance and reduced lead times and strengthen
domestic supply chains. Despite these benefits, the adoption of AM in nuclear energy applications faces
substantial challenges primarily because of qualification and certification barriers. Unlike conventional
wrought processes, in which material microstructure and properties are often assumed to be uniform
throughout a component, AM materials typically exhibit heterogeneous microstructures and properties
that vary by location [1, 2]. These variations arise from the localized nature of energy deposition during
the fusion of powder layers, which leads to a close relationship between the heat source path and a
component’s geometry on the resulting distribution of microstructure and properties. Consequently, the
origins of microstructure and property variation in AM components need to be better understood to predict
and control the effects of variation on component performance.
Within the Advanced Materials and Manufacturing Technologies (AMMT) programs [3], a combination
of in situ process sensing and physics-based computational modeling are being pursued to elucidate the
variability in microstructure and mechanical properties in AM materials. Previous work has demonstrated
that the grain structure of stainless steel 316 (SS316) is sensitive to both processing and composition
(i.e., 316-L vs. 316-H). Consequently, the thermal creep properties for AM material exhibits significantly
larger variability as a function of AM system and process conditions than is expected for conventional
wrought material. The purpose of the present research is therefore to provide a computational modeling
workflow capable of estimating this level of variability within a typical engineering component, in which
microstructure and properties may vary spatially.
Previous AMMT research in this area focused first on reviewing the requirements for effectively modeling
grain structure in AM processing[4]. That work showed that appropriately resolving the melt pool
characteristics was critical for accurate determination of grain structure, but that larger scale thermal
effects with respect to geometry and scan path were also necessary to consider. These characteristics of

 1

AM lead to a substantial computational challenge, where it is necessary to bridge length scales between
the component geometry and localized microstructure evolution. Several major gaps in the state-of-the art
were identified. First, computational expense for melt pools models varies dramatically depending on the
selection of physics included. A balanced approach that can achieve accurate melt pool shape predictions
without incorporation computationally intensive phenomena such as liquid-gas free surface evolution
or laser ray tracing was identified as a key enabling capability for accurately predicting representative
microstructure. Similarly, at the microstructure scale, dendrite resolved solidification simulations using
phase field, although common and scientifically valuable, do not scale to representative grain structures.
Instead, a grain scale approach, such as cellular automata (CA) [5] which simplifies dendrite scale growth
kinetics, was identified as a reasonable approach. Lastly, existing models in the literature were only
loosely coupled to processing data, and tended to neglect complex heat source scan paths in favor of
simplified descriptions of process conditions. A coupled and automated workflow between manufacturing
process and computational models was identified as a critical need for real-world engineering applications.
In FY24, this work package sought to construct this computational modeling workflow[6]. AdditiveFOAM
[7] was selected as the melt pool model, which uses a flat surface assumption and simplified volumetric
heat source to balance computational expense with accurate molt pool geometry predictions. ExaCA[8],
a GPU-capable CA model, was selected for prediction of grain structure in response to the solidification
thermal conditions predicted by AdditiveFOAM. Additionally, a python library called Myna [9] was
implemented to interface with the AMMT Multi-Dimensional Data Correlation (MDDC) framework[10],
enabling extraction of realistic machine parameters and scan paths for experimental AM builds. An initial
demonstration of the workflow functionality was described in a previous report.
In FY25, the purpose of this work package was to determine the accuracy and validity of the coupled
physics-based modeling approach. The corresponding heat transfer and microstructure models were
extended to incorporate key physics features associated with laser AM and microstructure evolution in
SS316 alloys. These models were then systematically calibrated against available experimental data and
then applied to coupons fabricated with various process conditions to create datasets for quantitative
comparison against experiments. This report describes the model development and calibration for laser
powder bed fusion (L-PBF) SS316, as well as the validation of simulations using the calibrated models by
comparison to experiments.

The samples use a cylindrical sample geometry with a bulk region and fin region, with one side of
the cylinder truncated to provide a registration surface. To prepare the metallographic specimens, the
samples were mounted with the registration surface aligned with the top of the mount. The surface was
then polished down using a series of coarse and fine grit sizes to a cross-section that cuts through both
the bulk and features in the fin region. The mirror-polished samples were initially electron backscatter
diffraction (EBSD) scanned to obtain an Inverse Pole Figures (IPF) map of a small region at the border,
in the bulk, or in a thin section. The EBSD scan was performed on a Zeiss Gemini 450 scanning electron
microscope equipped with a Bruker EBSD detector and Esprit acquisition software. Scans were acquired
at an accelerating voltage of 25 kV, a beam current of 20 nA, and 250× magnification with a step size of
1 µm. The collected data were post-processed using a custom MATLAB script incorporating the MTEX
toolbox, and grain reconstruction was carried out using a 4° misorientation threshold. The samples were
then electrolytically etched in a solution of 10 g oxalic acid dissolved in 100 mL of distilled water under
direct current at 2.0 V, applied for 90 s to 316L and 10 s to 316H builds. Etching was continued until grain
boundaries and dendritic structures became visible, with each alloy requiring a different reaction time
to reveal the microstructural features. Optical characterization of the entire cross-sectional surface was
subsequently performed using a Leica DMi8M microscope equipped with a Leica DMC4500 camera, and
large-area images were automatically stitched with Leica LAS-X software.
The cross-section was then registered to the part geometry in order to compare directly with the in situ data
captured during the build by the Manufacturing Demonstration Facility (MDF) Peregrine software [13] and
the simulated results. From the optical images, ImageJ [14] was used to measure the mean and standard
deviation of the half-width and depth of melt pools the top-surface of the bulk region.

The predictive accuracy of the model is derived from the adaptive parameterization of its axial
components, σz and k. Self-consistency of the melt pool model is enforced by dynamically coupling the
energy penetration depth, σz , to the calculated melt pool depth, d. At each time step, σz is set to the value
of d from the preceding step. This coupling is defined by the constant 3 in the exponential term, which
establishes that the energy intensity at the melt pool’s maximum depth is a finite value of exp(−3) ≈ 5% of
its peak. This non-zero energy flux at the solid-liquid interface represents the physical mechanism driving
the phase change and ensuring stable propagation of the melting front. In conjunction with this depth
adaptation, the axial shape factor, k, is dynamically calculated to reflect the dominant physical melting
mode.
The parameter k controls the axial distribution of energy input, transitioning from a diffuse,
surface-weighted profile (small k) in the conduction mode to a sharp, depth-focused profile (large k)
that represents the energy contained within a keyhole. This is achieved by defining k as a function of the
normalized melt pool depth, a = d/(2σ). The form of this function is established via empirical calibration
against experimental data, and is discussed in detail in a subsequent calibration section.

2.3 GRAIN STRUCTURE MODEL DESCRIPTION
ExaCA, a performance-portable CA code built on the Kokkos library and developed as part of the Exascale
Computing Project, was used to predict as-solidified grain structure from input time-temperature history
data [6]. ExaCA predicts the competitive solidification of dendritic grains for metals with cubic crystal
structures using the decentered octahedron algorithm, which approximates the preferential ⟨1 0 0⟩ growth
of dendrites of arbitrary crystallographic orientation on a regular grid. The local solidification increment
per time step in a CA cell at the edge of an octahedral grain envelope depends on the local undercooling
∆T of the liquid in the cell (from the input thermal history) and an interface response function V(∆T ),
which approximates the expected behavior at the scale of individual dendrite tips (solute partitioning
and transport, interfacial energy effects, etc). Heterogeneous nucleation of new grains is governed by
distributing nucleation sites with a number density N0 randomly across the CA cells, and assigning a
random crystallographic orientation and an activation undercooling ∆T N to each potential nucleation site,
where ∆T N values are sampled from a Gaussian distribution with mean ∆T µ and standard deviation ∆T σ .
Additional details of ExaCA’s algorithm, validation, computational performance, and model sensitivity is
documented in recent publications [17, 18, 19, 8].
ExaCA, as of version 2.0, considers only single phase solidification, governed by a single V(∆T ). While
the 316 series stainless steels are austenitic, it has been shown by Godfrey et al. that both ferrite and
austenite phases may appear during SS316-L solidification, as indicated by variations in Ni and Cr
segregation patterns across an LPBF build [20]. Despite the single-phase nature of SS316-L when at
equilibrium at room temperature, the presence of competing phases during solidification may affect the
observed grain structure [9] and a two-phase solidification model is therefore needed to predict the grain
structure for SS316 series steels of arbitrary composition.
As a rules-based method, ExaCA required new state variables and transition rules to emulate the physics
of the problem at hand. Figure 1 shows the grain structure of three SS316-L parts built using the three
energy densities from Table 1 and while the exact grain structure varies based on energy density and
location within the part, certain common features are observed from the EBSD and overlaid optical
microscopy data. Grains that first appear in the as-solidified ferrite region also tend to appear in the
next layer’s as-solidified austenite region (dark bands around melt pool edges), but rarely appear in the
as-solidified ferrite region of the next layer. This manifests as a grain structure discontinuity between the
top of each dark etched region and the successive bright etched region, leading to a microstructure where
few grains advance through more than one or two layers. A two-phase CA model of this material should
therefore account for the epitaxial growth of either phase from the existing material with consideration
of the transformation of as-solidified ferrite into austenite after solidification, but not allow instantaneous
switching of a phase during the solidification of a given grain. This was accommodated with an additional
variable tracking the as-solidified phase ID of a given cell in ExaCA, where the phase ID of the parent
cell is inherited during the conversion of a liquid cell to active via a cell capture event (i.e., the advance
of a solidifying grain). Conversely, cell activation at melt pool edges always consists of assigning the
austenite phase regardless of the as-solidified phase ID originally present, due to the fact that as-solidified
ferrite is assumed to transform into austenite shortly after solidification is complete. The ferrite to
austenite transformation is not explicitly handled, as there is insufficient data to separately calibrate
nucleation parameters for both the liquid-solid and solid-solid transformations, and it is assumed that
each as-solidified ferrite grain transforms into one austenite grain with a new crystallographic orientation
following solidification.
ExaCA’s extension to two phases requires separate interface response functions for ferrite (δ) and austenite
(γ) in both SS316-L and SS316-H. These functions are used both for grain envelope growth in a given cell
based on its phase ID as well as phase selection during nucleation, determined by selecting the larger of

2.4 COORDINATION OF SIMULATIONS WITH BUILD DATA
Build data for the validation simulations was extracted from the MDDC framework by using an Oak Ridge
National Laboratory (ORNL)-developed software tool called Myna [9]. Myna is a Python package that

2.5 MELT POOL MODEL CALIBRATION
Calibration of the melt pool model was performed against against experimental data obtained from etched
optical microscopy taken from the top of a SS316-H build, sectioned perpendicular to the build direction.
The resulting melt pool cross-sections are shown in Figure 2, and the measured melt pool depths for each
process parameter combination are summarized in Table 5.
The primary objective of this calibration is to establish an empirical relationship for the axial shape factor
k in the volumetric heat source model which adapts to the melt pool geometry in response to changes
in processing conditions. To manage the highly non-linear nature of this parameter with respect to the
normalized melt pool depth (a = d/2σ), which can span several orders of magnitude when transitioning
from conduction to keyhole mode, we re-parameterize the problem such that k = 2n . This transformation
allows the calibration to be performed on the exponent n, mapping the exponential behavior of k to a more
constrained and linearly-scaled parameter space, which is ideal for robust parameter fitting.

 8

Following this re-parameterization, the relationship for the exponent n was determined to be a logarithmic
function of the normalized melt pool depth:

                                            n(a) = Alog2 (a) + B,                                          (6)

where the fitting constants A and B were determined through Bayesian inference against the experimental
data in 5. The base-2 logarithm is selected for mathematical convenience, as it simplifies the full
expression to the direct power-law relationship k(a) = C · aA , where C = 2B . The final calibrated function
was determined to be:
                                        n(a) = 4.11 log2 (a) − 0.016.                                      (7)
This calibrated relationship is plotted in Figure 3, which also shows two example simulation results. These
examples for Parameter Set B (transition mode) and Parameter Set C (keyhole mode) visually demonstrate
how increasing the value of n produces the deeper, narrower melt pools characteristic of the keyhole
regime, validating the physical basis of the adaptive model parameterization.

2.6 MICROSTRUCTURE MODEL CALIBRATION
Calibration of ExaCA used etched optical microscopy data taken from the top of the SS316-L build
corresponding to label E in Table 1, sliced perpendicular to the build direction. The cross-section in
Figure 4 shows the presence of dark and light regions, which were attributed by Godfrey et al. to a local
difference in chemical composition due to preferential segregation of Cr and Ni during solidification of
ferrite and austenite phases [20]. The dark etched region, confirmed to be austenite by Godfrey at al.,

 10

appears preferentially along the edges of melt pools while the light etched region, which solidified as
ferrite and later transformed into austenite, appears around melt pool centers. From the microstructure
of the final deposited layer, two observable quantities were extracted and used to calibrate ExaCA: the
area fractions of as-solidified ferrite and austenite (i.e., ratio of light or dark etched region to the total area
highlighted on the right hand side of Figure 4) and the mean grain area for the as-solidified ferrite region
(i.e., mean area of the grains highlighted on the right hand side of Figure 4). The fraction of as-solidified
austenite Aa was measured to be 0.32, and the mean grain area of as-solidified ferrite µF was measured to
be 296.5 µm2 .

 From the microstructure of the final deposited layer, the area fraction of as-solidified ferrite (light

As discussed in Section 2.3, ThermoCalc and the multicomponent KGT model were used to obtain
dendrite tip velocities V and corresponding interface temperatures T for the ferrite and austenite phases
in both SS316-L and SS316-H. To use this data in ExaCA, it was necessary to convert the calculated V
and T into ferrite and austenite interface response functions Vδ (∆T ) and Vγ (∆T ) for both alloys. First, for
each alloy, temperature was converted into a relative undercooling ∆T defined with respect to the phase
with the higher T l (this was ferrite for both alloys). Next, the four V(∆T ) datasets were fit to functional
forms that ExaCA could use. While the austenite data was fit to power law functions with R2 > 0.96,
the ferrite data required the definition of a new functional form in ExaCA to achieve a reasonable fit;
fitting the ferrite data to exponential functions (i.e., V = a · exp(b∆T ) provided R2 of around 0.98. The
KGT model data is plotted alongside the fitted functions in Figure 5 for SS316-L and SS316-H. While
the functional forms are defined for all ∆T (dotted lines in Figure 5), the KGT model predicts a stability
limit for the dendritic morphology at around V = 0.25 m/s for ferrite and V = 0.15 m/s for austenite for
both SS316-L and SS316-H (solid lines in Figure 5). From Trivedi and Kurz, a metastable morphology
consisting of alternating bands of planar and dendritic solidification modes is expected at V beyond the
dendritic stability limit [23]. However, given that ExaCA is based on the assumption that solidification
is cellular or dendritic, values for V in ExaCA are truncated at the V corresponding to the KGT model
prediction of the lowest stable T for dendritic growth. This approximation will be revisited in the future,
as this may lead to artificially slow solidification near the melt pool center (as the solidifying grains cannot
keep up with the rate of isotherm motion) and affect grain competition.

parameters N0 and ∆T µ as the inputs of interest, fixing ∆T σ at 0.5 K. Reasonable bounds for N0 and ∆T µ
were chosen based on prior simulations. Tasmanian, the Toolkit for Adaptive Stochastic Modeling and
Non-Intrusive Approximation, was used to sample the 2D input space using a level 3 resolution and a

 3. RESULTS
The calibrated AdditiveFOAM and ExaCA models were used to simulate the build conditions for the
three regions with different volumetric energy densities in the 2024-01-19 AMMT Challenge Problem
build. The simulations were driven by Myna, as described in Section 2.4, which used the build data
collected by Peregrine and stored within the MDDC framework to coordinate the simulation results with
specific regions of the build. This coordination is shown in Figure 7 with the simulated regions being
registered to the specific part and layers of the build by utilization of the as-built scan path for the melt
pool simulations. Each of the AdditiveFOAM simulations (60 in total) was run on one node of the Oak
Ridge Leadership Computing Facility (OLCF) Frontier supercomputer with 56 processors, taking between
5-30 minutes per part-layer. The ExaCA simulations were run using three nodes with 24 GPUs total and
tool between 2-10 hours. The VED 135 J/mm3 part took the longest due to increased energy density
resulting in larger melt pools.

3.1 COMPARISON OF SIMULATIONS AND EXPERIMENTS
Figure 8 shows the simulation results for each of the three regions relative to location in X-ray
Computed Tomography (XCT) and EBSD of the test specimen. A qualitative comparison of the
simulated microstructures with the corresponding experiments indicates that the calibrated melt pool and
microstructure models are generally able to produce the same trends observed experimentally. Two notable
trends as the VED increases from 52 J/mm3 to 135 J/mm3 are the increase in grain size and the increase in
the strength of the crystallographic (100) poles with the build direction (red in the IPF-Z coloring).
Figure 9 shows the cumulative distribution function of the grain areas for both the simulated cross-sections
and the corresponding experiments. For the 52 and 71 J/mm3 parts, the grain area distributions match
closely with the experiments. For the 135 J/mm3 part, the simulated microstructure grain area was smaller
than the experiment and also decreased relative to the 71 J/mm3 part. While the nucleation parameters
were calibrated for SS316, the plateau of possible mean nucleation undercooling values shown in Figure
6 indicates that another combination of ∆T µ and N0 might have provided results closer to the experiment.

experiment grain areas. In the simulated microstructure, all grain boundaries are known as an output of

3.2 CHALLENGES AND FUTURE DIRECTION
Despite some differences in the grain area, the agreement between simulations and experiments shows
that the combined effects of process parameters, scan path design, and part geometry on microstructure
morphology and crystallographic texture are being captured in the melt pool and microstructure models.
This enables capabilities for subsequent simulations for mechanical properties and creep performance, and
exposes some challenges for future work.
Simulated microstructures output three-dimensional representations of the microstructures, which
are necessary inputs to crystal plasticity simulations to determine mechanical properties and creep
performance. While statistical methods, such as 2-point statistics, can be used to reconstruct
3D-representations from 2D EBSD information, common implementations of these methods decouple
correlations between texture and grain morphology. Therefore, simulations provide a critical ability
to obtain 3D digital representations of microstructures that are similar to real L-PBF microstructures.
Preliminary work has been done to extend the present simulation workflow in Myna to pass ExaCA
microstructure to a crystal plasticity simulation tool for creep performance developed at Argonne National
Laboratory [25, 26]. However, there are various open questions about the coupling of these models, for
example, the sensitivity of the results the conversion of the voxelized microstructure to the finite element
mesh representation required by the crystal plasticity model.
Future work on improving the fidelity of the 3D microstructure data passed to subsequent property
simulations could focus on sub-grain features that are not considered in the current grain-scale
solidification CA model, notably intragranular misorientations, porosity, and interdendritic elemental
segregation. Intragranular misorientations have been shown to accumulate with L-PBF SS316 [27]
and could have an effect on the deformation of the alloy during creep and the heat treatment response
due to spatially varying dislocation densities. Porosity due to lack of fusion defects and key hole

 16

instabilities can lead to disruptions in the microstructure development and serve as stress concentration
sites when the material is under load and would be expected to be important to include in crystal
plasticity simulations. Lastly, spatially-varying interdendritic elemental segregation could occur due
to differences in the solidification conditions throughout the melt pool or due to different processing
conditions. These concentration variations could drive differences in precipitation kinetics under long-term
elevated temperature exposure. Additionally, as the microstructure features that are most important
to the performance of a part are determined, additional analysis tools for quantitative comparison of
microstructures are needed.

 4. CONCLUSIONS
In this work, the ability to capture machine-, material-, and parameter-specific behavior by calibration
of the the melt pool heat transfer and grain-scale solidification model parameters was confirmed via
comparison of the simulated microstructure to corresponding measurements of as-built SS316-H parts.
Newly developed capabilities in the AdditiveFOAM melt pool model and ExaCA microstructure model
enables the development of calibration approaches that can be reasonably extrapolated to new processing
conditions and material compositions. The MDDC framework was used to run simulations for the as-built
conditions of the parts for calibration and validation, which provided continuity between the numerical
conditions in the simulations and the processing conditions in the experiments.
With reasonably accurate capabilities to predict grain-scale microstructure morphology and
crystallographic texture, numerical investigations into the effect of process-structure-property relationships
for L-PBF SS316-H creep performance can be expanded to include more realistic as-built microstructures.
Additionally, there is now an opportunity to integrate higher-fidelity, sub-grain microstructure features
into simulated three-dimensional digital representations of the microstructure. Such capabilities are
required for using physics-informed modeling to downselect the most promising samples to undergo
time-consuming creep tests, as well as for forecasting long-term creep performance. This can provide
accelerated pathways for qualifying creep performance by reducing the number of iterations of creep
experiments.
