Impact of common cleaning protocols on the surface integrity and nanoscale topography of PET-G clear aligners: an AFM and profilometric study
Authors: Armen Zohrab Yeretsian, Ahmad S. Burhan, Mohammad Y. Hajeer, Ibrahim Alghoraibi, Fehmieh R. Nawaya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate the scale-dependent surface response of PET-G clear aligners to routine home-care cleaning protocols using contact profilometry (macroscopic) and atomic force microscopy (nanoscale).MethodsEighty PET-G samples were divided into four groups: Control, Listerine® mouthwash, Corega® cleansing tablets, and Toothbrush with Colgate® toothpaste. Cleaning protocols were applied once daily for 14 days. Surface roughness (Ra) was measured by profilometry, whereas the nanoscale parameters (Sa, Sq, St, Sp, Sv) were ascertained via AFM. For between-group comparisons, one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s HSD post-hoc test (α = 0.05) was used for normally distributed variables, and the Kruskal–Wallis test with Mann–Whitney U pairwise testing (Bonferroni correction, adjusted α = 0.0083) was used for nonnormally distributed variables. Test-retest repeatability was assessed by repeating measurements after 24 h and computing intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC).ResultsOverall, a statistically significant difference across groups was observed for the profilometric Ra (p = 0.027(. Tukey’s HSD post-hoc tests revealed a statistically significant pairwise difference between the Listerine® and control groups (mean difference = 0.30 μm; Tukey’s p = 0.028), whereas other pairwise comparisons were not significant at α = 0.05. In contrast, all the AFM-derived nanoscale parameters (Sa, Sq, Sp, Sv, St) showed clear and statistically significant differences across groups (p < 0.001). AFM images and metrics indicated that chemically cleaned specimens (Listerine® and Corega® groups) showed smoother and more uniform nanostructures, whereas mechanically cleaned specimens (Toothbrush group) exhibited localized micro-scratches and irregular topographic features consistent with mechanical abrasion.ConclusionCommon cleaning protocols were associated with scale-dependent surface effects. Listerine® was associated with higher macroscopic Ra compared to the control, whereas AFM revealed that chemically cleaned specimens (Listerine® and Corega®) showed substantially smoother nanoscale surfaces. Brushing was associated with nanoscale micro-scratches without significant macroscopic Ra change.

Introduction

Clear aligners have revolutionized orthodontic treatment by providing a nearly invisible, removable alternative to fixed braces that improves patient comfort and facilitates oral hygiene [1]. These thin thermoplastic appliances, which are commonly made of polyurethane or polyethylene terephthalate glycol (PET-G), are valued for their aesthetic transparency and mechanical strength [2, 3]. Indeed, PET-G is now the most commonly used aligner material because of its excellent impact and tear resistance, chemical stability, and optical clarity [2, 3]. Each aligner typically remains in the mouth for two weeks before being replaced by the next one, resulting in gradual tooth movement [4]. A significant concern with these devices is the stagnation of salivary flow, which makes their internal surfaces susceptible to plaque buildup and discoloration [5]. Therefore, patients need to maintain a high standard of oral hygiene and ensure thorough cleaning of any debris from the aligner surfaces [5].

Typical cleaning approaches include mechanical methods (e.g., brushing with a soft toothbrush and non-abrasive toothpaste, ultrasonic vibration) and chemical methods (soaking in solutions such as chlorhexidine, detergents, or effervescent cleaning tablets) [6]. Systematic reviews and trials agree that multi-step regimens work best. Charavet et al. reviewed four trials and concluded that no single method outperforms all others; nevertheless, a combination of mechanical and chemical cleaning seems to be the most effective approach [6]. That said, the relative contribution of each step remains unclear, since few studies have compared single-step protocols [6, 7].

However, cleaning methods can themselves alter the aligner’s surface [3]. For example, vigorous brushing (especially with abrasive toothpaste) roughens the plastic. Scientific reports have shown that both “toothbrush” and “toothbrush + toothpaste” treatments significantly increase the surface roughness of a 3D-printed polyurethane aligner surface [8]. In contrast, chemical soaking with effervescent tablets preserved smoother surfaces by dissolving deposits without mechanical abrasion [8]. These findings mirror earlier observations: aligners worn for only two weeks can already exhibit microcracks, abraded/delaminated areas, and calcified deposits [9], indicating that both wear and cleaning can create new surface irregularities.

Clinically, increased roughness may promote biofilm retention [10] and staining [11], whereas surface defects may compromise material integrity and force transmission [12]. Chromogenic agents such as coffee, tea, and wine tend to adsorb more readily onto roughened aligner surfaces, leading to persistent discoloration that impairs aesthetics despite routine cleaning [13]. Rough surfaces on aligner materials may trap pigments and create niches that could favor bacterial adhesion and biofilm maturation [10]. Studies have identified a roughness threshold of Ra = 0.2 μm (Ra, the average roughness along a line), suggesting that below this level bacterial accumulation is minimal and above it plaque retention may increase sharply [14]. Indeed, these devices can induce changes in the ecosystem of the oral cavity, which, in turn, can increase the risk of oral diseases, such as periodontal disease, halitosis, and caries lesions caused by bacterial growth [9]. Proper hygiene protocols are therefore recommended to prevent discoloration and microbial growth on aligners [6].

Despite these insights, important gaps remain for modern aligner materials and nanoscale assessment. Most published work has focused on older thermoplastic systems, particularly polyurethane-based appliances (e.g., Invisalign), and many studies on cleaning protocols and material properties have used these materials [6, 8]. By comparison, PET-G is a newer, popular, clear thermoplastic aligner, but it has received little attention. One recent study is an exception: Simunović et al. tested four brands of PET-G and reported that both thermocycling and brushing significantly increased PET-G roughness [15]. However, few studies have systematically evaluated the effects of common cleaning methods on PET-G surfaces, particularly at the nanoscale. PET-G specific data remain sparse. Furthermore, prior research has often focused on macroscopic properties (such as bulk flexibility and translucency) rather than nanoscale surface characteristics [16, 17]. In fact, most studies have measured roughness via profilometry or optical methods, but have not examined nanoscale topography; as Porojan et al. noted, PET-G surface deterioration at the nanometer scale remains “an open question” [3].

The present study therefore aimed to investigate how typical cleaning procedures affect the surface quality of PET-G clear aligners (Clear Smile) using both contact profilometry and AFM. A central question was whether cleaning protocols produce scale-dependent effects, that is, different changes at the micrometric (profilometric Ra) versus nanometric (AFM Sa, Sq, etc.) levels. Three common home-care protocols were tested: brushing with toothpaste, soaking in an effervescent tablet solution (Corega®), and rinsing with an antiseptic mouthwash (Listerine®).

The null hypothesis was that there would be no significant difference in surface roughness (Ra) or nanoscale topography parameters (Sa, Sq, St, Sp, Sv) among the four cleaning protocols after 14 days of daily application.

Materials and methods

Study design and settings

This study was a controlled, in vitro experimental investigation performed at the Department of Orthodontics, Faculty of Dentistry, Damascus University, Damascus, Syria. The primary objective was to compare the effects of three common home-care cleaning protocols (Listerine® mouthwash, Corega® cleansing tablets, and manual tooth-brushing with Colgate® toothpaste) on the surface morphology of PET-G clear aligner specimens (Clear Smile, Damascus, Syria). The complete experimental workflow, including specimen preparation, pre-conditioning, daily cleaning cycles, and outcome measurements, is illustrated in Fig. 1. This study, which was conducted between July 2024 and August 2025, was funded by the University of Damascus (ID: 501100020595).

Estimation of the sample size

The sample size was calculated a priori using G*Power version 3.1.9 (Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany). A one-way ANOVA test was selected with the following assumptions: significance level (α) = 0.05 and study power (1 − β) = 90%. The effect size (f = 0.65) was estimated from previously published data by Huang et al. (2025) [7] for the surface roughness (Ra) outcome. Therefore, the power analysis was formally anchored to Ra. The AFM parameters (Sa, Sq, St, Sp, Sv) were not included in the sample size calculation and thus not formally powered. Using these parameters, the required sample size was 40 specimens for the variable of interest, which were equally distributed into four main groups, with 10 specimens allocated to each group. Thus, 40 specimens (10 per group) were used for profilometric Ra measurements, and the other 40 (10 per group) for AFM analysis.

Sample preparation

The study sample consisted of 80 specimens obtained from clear aligners with a thickness of 0.8 mm, which were fabricated from PET-G material (Clear Smile, Damascus, Syria) using the thermoforming pressure technique, according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Ministar S®, Scheu-Dental GmbH, Iserlohn, Germany). A single gypsum model was 3D scanned to produce a resin model, onto which a transparent aligner sheet was thermoformed. The molded section was trimmed and separated, and then cut into flat rectangular strips with dimensions of 10 × 10 mm for testing. All specimens were cut from the region corresponding to the dental alveolus to obtain flat surfaces as much as possible, which is essential for reliable profilometric and AFM measurements. This region also minimized potential thermoforming gradients, as it is relatively central. Specimens were obtained from 30 thermoformed sheets, with four specimens taken from each sheet. All 80 specimens were randomly allocated to the four cleaning groups, each consisting of 20 samples, using a computer-generated random number sequence. Within each cleaning group, specimens were further randomly assigned to either profilometry (n = 10) or AFM (n = 10) subgroups using the same method. Profilometry and AFM measurements were performed by two different investigators, both blinded to group allocation. The four cleaning groups were: Group 1 served as the control; Group 2 underwent cleaning with Listerine® mouthwash (Johnson & Johnson, Neuss, Germany); Group 3 was treated using Corega® cleansing tablets (Stafford-Miller, Dungarvan, Ireland); and Group 4 was cleaned with a toothbrush and Colgate® toothpaste (Colgate-Palmolive, Guangzhou, China).

Cleaning protocols

Greenwood’s artificial saliva was prepared following Imai et al. (2019) [18]. Per 100 g of distilled water the solution contained: potassium chloride (KCl) 0.24 g, calcium phosphate (Ca₃(PO₄)₂) 0.06 g, dipotassium hydrogen phosphate (K₂HPO₄) 0.14 g, potassium sulfate (K₂SO₄) 0.09 g, trisodium phosphate (Na₃PO₄) 0.08 g, and albumin 0.5 g. This formulation was used to simulate oral conditions during specimen conditioning and testing. The salts were sequentially dissolved in distilled water using a magnetic stirrer, and albumin was added at 37 °C to ensure proper incorporation. Prepared specimens were initially immersed in plastic containers filled with artificial saliva and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h in a laboratory incubator. After this initial incubation, all specimens were rinsed with distilled water for 1 min to remove any residual artificial saliva before the cleaning procedures. The cleaning regimen described below was applied once daily for 14 consecutive days, reflecting the clinical service time of a single aligner.

Group 1 — Control (no cleaning intervention)

The specimens in the control group received no active cleaning intervention. They were handled and rinsed in the same manner as the experimental groups (distilled water rinse and air-drying) but were otherwise left untreated and stored in artificial saliva between cycles.

Group 2 — Listerine® mouthwash (Johnson & Johnson, Neuss, Germany)

The specimens were immersed in Listerine® mouthwash for 15 min, rinsed with distilled water for 1 min and then air-dried. After drying, the specimens were returned to artificial saliva and incubated at 37 °C until the next daily cycle.

Group 3 — Corega® cleansing tablets (Stafford Miller (Ireland) Ltd., Dungarvan, Ireland)

Corega® tablets were dissolved in warm water according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The specimens were immersed in the prepared solution for 3 min, rinsed with distilled water for 1 min, air-dried before being returned to artificial saliva and incubated at 37 °C.

Group 4 — Toothbrushing with Colgate® toothpaste (Colgate-Palmolive, Guangzhou, China)

The specimens in this group were manually cleaned using a soft-bristle Colgate toothbrush and Colgate toothpaste. A pea-sized amount of toothpaste was prepared as a slurry and applied to the brush head prior to each cleaning cycle. Each specimen was brushed for 30 s per surface (two surfaces per specimen; 60 s total) using circular motion, with the brush head held approximately perpendicular (≈ 90°) to the specimen surface. Brushing force was standardized by using only the weight of the toothbrush as the applied vertical load, without additional manual pressure. All brushing procedures were performed by a single trained operator to ensure consistency. The approximate target force was equivalent to the weight of the brush head (~ 1–2 N), which is consistent with gentle manual brushing. To further reduce variability, brushing time (30 s per surface) was controlled with a stopwatch, and the toothpaste slurry was refreshed between specimens. After brushing, specimens were rinsed with distilled water for 1 min, air-dried, subsequently returned to artificial saliva and incubated at 37 °C until the next cycle.

The cleaning protocols were adapted from previously published studies [3, 7, 16].

Surface roughness test (Profilometer)

Surface roughness test (Profilometer)

Surface roughness test (Profilometer)

The surface roughness was measured via a contact profilometer (TR200™ Surface Roughness Tester, Time Group Inc., China). The device operates on the basis of the stylus method and measures the arithmetical mean surface roughness (Ra). The stylus had a tip radius of 5 μm and traversed the surface under a constant vertical force of 4 mN. A cut-off length of 0.8 mm and an evaluation length of 4.0 mm were used in accordance with ISO 4287 standards. Profilometric surface roughness measurements have been widely used in dental materials research [14].

The device was calibrated before use according to the manufacturer’s instructions, using the provided standard calibration block. Due to the relatively flat but non-uniform surface of the aligner specimens, a single surface roughness measurement was performed per sample at a carefully selected representative area. The measurement site (central region, away from edges) was standardized for all specimens to ensure comparability. This approach minimized variability from surface curvature and ensured consistent, comparable data across all samples.

Microstructural analysis

The microscale surface topography of the PET-G aligner specimens was examined using an atomic force microscope (AFM) (Nanosurf easyScan2, Nanosurf, Switzerland) operated in tapping mode, under controlled ambient laboratory conditions. A Tap190 Al G cantilever and tip (NanoSensors™, Switzerland) with a sensitivity of 0.2 nm were employed. The scanner resolution was set to 0.2 nm, and each measurement was performed over a 10 μm × 10 μm scan area at a scan rate of 0.5 Hz, corresponding to one frame every 20 s. Data acquisition included both height and phase images at a resolution of 256 × 256 pixels. AFM has been successfully applied to characterize nanoscale topography of orthodontic aligners [3].

Before each measurement, the AFM was calibrated on a standard silicon calibration grating (pitch 3 μm; depth 200 nm). To reduce tip wear and maintain the accuracy of the data, each specimen was scanned once in a representative surface area. The scan site (central region, away from edges) was standardized for all specimens to ensure comparability. From the 10 × 10 μm² topographic images obtained, the arithmetical mean height (Sa), root mean square roughness (Sq), maximum height of the surface (St), maximum peak height (Sp), and maximum pit depth (Sv) were derived with the instrument’s analysis software. The single-scan AFM parameters for each specimen were then statistically compared among the four cleaning method groups.

Blinding

Blinding during cleaning interventions was not feasible because of the inherent characteristics of the applied protocols. However, blinding was implemented during the outcome assessment. All the specimens were coded prior to surface analysis to conceal group allocation. AFM measurements were performed by an investigator who was not involved in specimen cleaning or coding, thereby ensuring allocation concealment for nanoscale analysis. Profilometric measurements were conducted on coded specimens by a separate investigator who had no access to the allocation table. The specimens were analyzed in randomized order following standardized rinsing and drying procedures to ensure uniform surface conditions before measurement. Although certain cleaning protocols may induce subtle visible surface alterations, investigators had no access to allocation data at the time of testing.

Assessment of test-retest repeatability

An exploratory test-retest assessment was performed on forty specimens to evaluate the precision and agreement of the measured variables. All the measurements were repeated after 24 h under identical laboratory conditions. Systematic (bias) error was assessed using paired-samples t-tests at a 95% confidence level, whereas random error and inter-measurement agreement were quantified using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC, two-way mixed model, absolute agreement).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM® SPSS Statistics version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) unless otherwise specified. The normality of each variable within groups was evaluated with the Shapiro–Wilk test. The surface roughness parameter (Ra) showed a normal distribution and was therefore analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s HSD post hoc test for pairwise comparisons (α = 0.05). In contrast, the AFM surface roughness parameters (Sa, Sq, St, Sp, and Sv) did not meet the normality assumption and were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. When significant differences were detected, pairwise comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test with Bonferroni correction for multiple testing. Because the study included four groups, six pairwise comparisons were possible. Therefore, the Bonferroni-adjusted significance level was set at α = 0.05/6 = 0.0083 to control the familywise error rate (FWER).

Results

Assessment of test-retest repeatability

No statistically significant systematic differences were observed between the first and second measurements for any of the evaluated variables (all P > 0.05; Tables 1 and 2), indicating the absence of systematic errors. Reliability was excellent across all the assessed parameters: surface roughness (ICC = 0.998). The AFM topographic parameters (Sa, Sq, St, Sp) had ICC values between 0.994 and 0.995, while Sv reached an ICC of 1.000. These findings confirm the high repeatability of the instruments and procedures used.

Surface roughness

Shapiro–Wilk tests confirmed normality of Ra values within each group (Control, p = 0.731; Listerine®, p = 0.583; Corega®, p = 0.851; Toothbrush, p = 0.933). One-way ANOVA revealed that surface roughness (Ra) differed significantly among the four cleaning groups (p = 0.027), with a large effect size (η² = 0.222), indicating that approximately 22.2% of the variance in surface roughness was attributable to the cleaning protocol. The highest mean Ra was observed in the Listerine® group (0.58 ± 0.36 μm), followed by the Corega® group (0.46 ± 0.14 μm) and the Toothbrush group (0.34 ± 0.17 μm); the lowest mean Ra was recorded in the Control group (0.28 ± 0.15 μm) (Table 3). Post-hoc pairwise comparisons using Tukey’s HSD test indicated a significant difference between the Listerine® and Control groups (mean difference = 0.30 μm; 95% CI for the difference: 0.024 to 0.572; p = 0.028). No other pairwise comparisons were statistically significant.

Surface microstructure

2D and 3D AFM images (Fig. 3) demonstrate the differential nanoscale topographical alterations associated with the different cleaning regimens. Because the AFM data were not normally distributed, non-parametric analyses (Kruskal–Wallis followed by Mann–Whitney U tests with Bonferroni correction) were used. Statistical AFM parameters (Sa, Sq, St, Sp, Sv) confirmed statistically significant difference across groups (p < 0.001, Kruskal–Wallis). Large effect sizes were observed for all AFM parameters (ε² range: 0.527–0.757), indicating that cleaning methods were associated with substantial alterations in nanoscale surface integrity. Confidence intervals for mean differences were provided for the parametric analyses (Ra), whereas non-parametric AFM comparisons were summarized using effect sizes and adjusted pairwise significance testing. These findings agreed with visual surface morphology. The control group (Fig. 3A) exhibited an irregular and rough surface with prominent asperities, deep grooves, and random orientation. In contrast, the Listerine® group (Fig. 3B) presented the most uniform and even topography among all specimens, with substantially reduced peak–valley amplitudes. Compared with the control group, the Corega® group (Fig. 3C) also demonstrated strong surface improvement, with fewer deep depressions and reduced roughness parameters. However, isolated micro-pitting was sometimes observed. Conversely, the Toothbrush group (Fig. 3D) presented inhomogeneous micro-abrasion patterns, linear striations, and localized scratches.

Discussion

The increasing use of clear aligners in orthodontics reflects their superior aesthetics and near-invisibility, and patients treated with removable aligners generally demonstrate good oral hygiene and high compliance, which helps reduce plaque-related disease [19]. Nonetheless, clinicians must provide clear, evidence-based home-care instructions for aligner maintenance because surface quality, which is a composite of color, roughness, and topography, depends on both material and processing and is altered by the oral environment and cleaning regimens [3]. Polyethylene terephthalate-glycol (PET-G) is widely used for thermoformed aligners because of its transparency, chemical resistance, and formability, yet its surface topography can be modified in service (by staining, biofilm, or hygienic actions); therefore, its profilometric roughness and AFM microstructure have been evaluated to capture both macroscopic wear and nanoscale damage relevant to plaque retention and aesthetics. This study reveals that cleaning protocols can produce opposing effects on PET-G surface topography depending on the measurement scale, a pattern consistent with scale-dependent polymer surface modification.

Based on the results, the null hypothesis is rejected: significant differences in both Ra (p = 0.027) and all AFM parameters (p < 0.001) were observed among the four cleaning protocols. Therefore, the alternative hypothesis, that PET-G surface topography differs among cleaning protocols, is accepted.

From a polymer surface-science perspective, PET-G clear aligner surfaces should not be viewed as inert; in glassy polymers, small-molecule uptake can modify near-surface free volume and chain mobility, leading to transient plasticization and surface swelling, whereas more severe exposure can contribute to crazing or other micro-damage [20]. Accordingly, the cleaning regimens examined in this study may have shifted the balance between chemical softening and superficial leveling on the one hand and mechanical abrasion on the other, which is consistent with the observed changes in aligner morphology and roughness [21]. Under harsher or prolonged chemical exposure, chain scission is also a plausible degradation pathway, as PETG chains can shorten during hydrolytic degradation [22]. These general mechanisms will be considered when interpreting our specific findings below.

Surface changes are traditionally measured by contact profilometry, reported as Ra [8]. While profilometry is simple and standard, it samples only linear profiles and uses a relatively large stylus (∼5 μm tip) [3]. In contrast, advanced microscopy methods such as atomic force microscopy (AFM) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) provide much more detail. SEM imaging can qualitatively reveal surface topography but is inherently two-dimensional [3]. AFM, however, generates full 3D height maps at the nanoscale (with a ∼0.01 μm tip) [3]. Studies now combine profilometry and AFM: profilometry yields standardized Ra values, while AFM yields 3D roughness parameters such as the arithmetical mean height (Sa), which is defined as the average absolute height deviation from the mean plane over a scanned area, and reveals fine peaks and valleys [3, 8]. This multidimensional assessment reveals subtle damage that Ra alone might miss [3, 8]. It is important to note that contact profilometry (Ra) and AFM (Sa, Sq, etc.) assess surface topography at fundamentally different length scales (macroscopic versus nanoscale), and therefore their results may not correlate directly.

In light of these methodological considerations, the current findings showed a clear divergence between macroscopic and nanoscopic surface metrics. Profilometric (Ra) and AFM-based surface roughness measurements revealed evident surface change among the four cleaning regimens. Contact profilometry revealed that the Listerine® group presented the highest mean surface roughness (Ra = 0.58 ± 0.36 μm) and differed significantly from the control group, which presented the lowest mean surface roughness (Ra = 0.28 ± 0.15 μm). The observed increase may be attributed to potential chemical interactions between the Listerine® components and the PET-G polymer matrix. This finding is broadly consistent with some prior observations. For example, Wible et al. reported that Listerine® produced the largest increase in Ra (although still < 0.5 μm) among several cleaning methods for Essix PET-G retainers [23]. In contrast, other studies on different polymers have reported little or no roughness change from Listerine®. Altaie et al. reported no significant Ra increase for a heat-cured PMMA denture resin after daily 8-hour Listerine® soaks for one month [24], and Ayatollahi et al. found no Ra change in bulk-fill or nanohybrid resin composites exposed to Listerine® [25]. These discrepancies likely reflect material differences: PET-G (a copolyester) may be more susceptible to ethanol/EO effects than highly crosslinked acrylics or filled resins.

However, AFM topography provided a more sensitive nanoscale evaluation, revealing that Listerine® and Corega® groups presented the most even overall surfaces with the minimum Sa and Sq values. While the AFM data confirm that specimens exposed to Listerine® exhibited a substantially smoother nanoscale surface compared to the untreated control, this finding should be interpreted as an active modification of the PET-G surface rather than passive preservation of its native topography. Several candidate mechanisms may explain this observation. First, the ethanol content of Listerine® (approximately 21% v/v) may induce transient plasticization of the superficial PET-G layer. Upon evaporation, this could facilitate polymer chain reflow and leveling of pre-existing nanometric asperities. Second, the essential oils and alcohol mixture might selectively dissolve or soften a thin, amorphous, and potentially less-ordered surface layer generated during thermoforming (the ‘skin layer’), thereby exposing a smoother underlying polymer matrix. Third, the control specimens may inherently possess a thermoforming-induced asperity layer, and the chemical action of Listerine® simply removes this layer without otherwise altering the bulk polymer. Fourth, we cannot entirely exclude the possibility of AFM scan-site selection bias or tip convolution artifacts, although standardized scanning protocols and the consistent topographical patterns across all Listerine® specimens make systematic bias less likely. Distinguishing among these mechanisms would require complementary surface characterization techniques such as X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) to assess chemical changes, nanoindentation to map near-surface mechanical property gradients, or time-resolved AFM during solvent exposure. Nevertheless, regardless of the precise mechanism, the present data unequivocally demonstrate that specimens exposed to Listerine® exhibited a different nanoscale topography compared to untreated controls, indicating that Listerine® exposure was associated with a modification of the surface rather than mere preservation.

A further consideration is whether the apparent Ra-AFM divergence reflects a genuine physical change or a measurement artifact. Two interpretations exist. First, the profilometer (4 mm scan, 5 μm tip) might detect micrometric waviness (e.g., uneven swelling) that the AFM (10 × 10 μm window) cannot see. Second, Listerine® could simultaneously increase longer-wavelength roughness and decrease nanoscale asperity, reflecting a true bi-scalar effect. Our data do not fully separate these possibilities. However, the AFM images show flat, uniform surfaces without hidden waviness. Furthermore, the significant reductions in nanoscale parameters (Sa, Sq, St, Sp, Sv) are consistent across multiple specimens, with low standard deviations, supporting a genuine topographical modification rather than a sampling artifact. However, a decisive test would require larger-area AFM scans (50–100 μm) or optical profilometry, which were beyond the scope of this study.

The observed pattern, wherein mechanically cleaned specimens showed greater alterations in surface topography than mild chemical immersion did, aligns with findings reported in several previous studies. Porojan et al. reported that mechanical cleaning and exposure to staining agents produce measurable nanoscale alterations in thermoplastic appliances, whereas effervescent tablets tend to have less abrasive impact [3]. Nanoscale AFM studies, e.g. [12]., have shown that AFM can reveal localized peaks and pits after abrasive treatment that are not always captured by single-line profilometry, which helps explain why a specimen may appear relatively smooth by Ra yet show deleterious nanostructural features by AFM. Recent work by Šimunović and co-workers similarly revealed that the rank order and magnitude of effects depend on material type and cleaning protocol, supporting our observation that Listerine® and Corega® may behave differently on different PET-G formulations [8, 26].

This apparent contradiction between profilometry (Ra) and AFM metrics indicates complementary roles of the two techniques: profilometry (Ra) quantifies large-scale and superficial waviness in millimeter-length profiles, whereas AFM parameters (Sa, Sq, St, Sp, Sv) quantify nanoscale height variations and therefore allow more sensitive discrimination between mechanical abrasion and chemical smoothing. AFM 3D micrographs revealed that the Toothbrush group displayed localized striations and abrasive scratch marks, resulting in elevated St and Sp values, whereas the Corega® and Listerine® groups presented homogeneous, flattened nanoscale surfaces with decreased peak-to-valley amplitudes, indicating effective cleaning without mechanical loss. Therefore, whereas profilometry revealed a perceived increase in macro-roughness due to swelling of the surface or adsorption of chemicals, AFM confirmed that chemical regimens exhibited a nanoscale surface with reduced peak-to-valley amplitude, indicating a significant topographical modification from the untreated control. Regarding the clinical relevance of this roughness metrics, the Bollen threshold (Ra = 0.2 μm) was established using contact profilometry and applies to the macroscopic 2-D Ra metric. All groups exceeded this threshold in Ra (0.28–0.58 μm), indicating potential for biofilm retention at the macroscopic level. However, AFM-derived Sa values are orders of magnitude smaller and should not be directly compared to the same numerical cut-off; they reflect nanoscale integrity without a predefined clinical benchmark. Taken together, these results indicate that profilometry integration with AFM offers a holistic assessment of aligner surface integrity, where AFM is better at discriminating minor morphological damage not uncovered by Ra alone.

Clinically, these in vitro findings suggest caution when routine toothbrushing of aligners is recommended: although brushing effectively removes deposits, it may accelerate the formation of nanoscale features that favor plaque formation and staining and reduce clarity. Conversely, gentle chemical soaking protocols (used according to the manufacturer’s instructions) appear less likely to compromise the PET-G microstructure. Therefore, based on these in vitro observations, chemical soaking (effervescent tablets or a short alcohol-containing rinse) may be considered for routine cleaning, as it was associated with minimal nanoscale abrasion, whereas mechanical brushing could be reserved for visible deposits that chemical soaking fails to remove, using a soft brush without abrasive toothpaste. Clinicians may balance the immediate cleaning benefits of mechanical action against its potential to increase biofilm retention and aesthetic deterioration.

The PET-G aligners used in this study (Clear Smile, Damascus) are a specific regional product. As shown by Šimunović et al. [8, 15], inter-brand variability in crystallinity and processing is substantial. Therefore, our findings should not be extrapolated to other PET-G brands or polyurethane systems without brand-specific replication.

Finally, we note methodological considerations. We adopted a 15-minute daily soak in Listerine® protocol based on a previously published protocol by Agarwal et al. [16], who evaluated the effects of mouth rinses on comparable polymeric materials, thereby providing methodological precedent. However, that study was performed on polyurethane retainers, not on PET-G, which limits direct comparability. Additionally, this exposure period was chosen to simulate an intensified daily chemical challenge that would allow measurable changes to be detected within a limited experimental timeframe (14 days). Notably, this duration was selected for experimental standardization purposes and does not represent a direct clinical recommendation for patient use. Similarly, the brushing protocol (30s per surface with toothpaste) represented a worst-case mechanical challenge. Thus, our findings represent an intensified model rather than exact clinical replication. We used a single measurement per specimen for both contact profilometry and AFM; measurement sites were standardized (central flat region, edges excluded) and all procedures followed predefined criteria. Reliability was assessed by repeating measurements on 40 specimens after 24 h; no systematic differences were detected and the agreement was excellent. Given the small AFM scan area (10 × 10 μm²) and relatively long acquisition time (~ 20 s/frame at 256 × 256 pixels), multiple scans per specimen would have greatly increased instrument time and exposure to cantilever tip wear, which can alter tip–sample interactions and introduce additional variability if probes are not replaced frequently. For these reasons, a pragmatic balance between spatial representativeness and measurement consistency was adopted under standardized conditions.

Limitations

This in vitro study cannot fully reproduce the complex oral environment (salivary pellicle, enzymatic activity, thermal cycling, masticatory forces and dietary challenges). AFM analysis was limited to a single 10 × 10 μm² scan per specimen and profilometry was limited to a single linear trace per specimen, which may underrepresent whole-specimen spatial heterogeneity. Although a 24-hour test-retest repeatability assessment indicated high measurement agreement, this quantifies instrument consistency at the same locations and does not capture spatial heterogeneity across different regions of the same specimen. The single-scan/trace approach remains a pragmatic compromise; these methodological constraints may contribute to the Ra–AFM discrepancies and limit generalizability. Consequently, the reported differences between cleaning groups could be either overestimated or underestimated depending on the localized surface features captured by the single trace/scan. However, standardized sampling and random allocation limit this concern for group comparisons. An additional methodological limitation concerns the study design. All comparisons were made between independent groups at a single time point (after 14 days of cleaning), without baseline measurements of each individual specimen before exposure. However, the random allocation of specimens from a single thermoformed sheet and the relatively large sample size (n = 10 per group) reduce the likelihood that baseline differences systematically biased the between-group comparisons. Future studies should include multiple non-overlapping AFM scans, standardized triplicate profilometer traces per specimen, long-term aging or in vivo simulations, and a broader sampling of materials to better capture within-sample variability and clinical relevance.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that common home-care cleaning protocols were associated with scale-dependent effects on PET-G aligner surfaces. Contact profilometry showed that the Listerine® group exhibited significantly higher macroscopic roughness (Ra) compared to the control group, whereas AFM revealed that chemically cleaned specimens (Listerine® and Corega®) displayed smoother nanoscale surfaces (lower Sa) and that mechanically brushed specimens showed micro-scratches and increased peak-to-valley heights. The nanoscale smoothing after chemical soaking is consistent with surface reflow, while the abrasive features after brushing are consistent with mechanical wear. Clinically, based on these in vitro findings, daily chemical soaking (effervescent tablets or a short alcohol-containing rinse) may be considered for routine cleaning, as it was associated with less nanoscale abrasion than mechanical brushing; brushing could be limited to visible deposits that chemical soaking fails to remove, using a soft brush without abrasive toothpaste. It should be noted that these findings are derived from an in vitro setting and are specific to the tested protocols and durations. Future studies should use a paired design with repeated measurements on the same specimens and include larger-area AFM scans to capture spatial heterogeneity, as well as test other PET-G brands and polyurethane aligners.
