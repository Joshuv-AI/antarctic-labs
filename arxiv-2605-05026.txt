Local Intrinsic Dimension Unveils Hallucinations in Diffusion Models
Bartlomiej Sobieski, Matthew Tivnan, Dawid Płudowski, Michał Jan Włodarczyk, Pengfei Jin, Przemyslaw Biecek, Quanzheng Li, [0.2cm] Centre for Credible AI, Warsaw University of Technology, University of Warsaw

Abstract
Diffusion models are prone to generating structural hallucinations - samples that match the statistical properties of the training data yet defy underlying structural rules, resulting in anomalies like hands with more than five fingers. Recent research studied this failure mode from several viewpoints, offering partial explanations to their occurrence, such as mode interpolation. In this work, we propose a complementary perspective that treats hallucinations as instabilities on the model-induced manifold. We begin by showing that a hallucination filter based on such instabilities matches or exceeds the performance of the recently proposed temporal one. By tracing the source of these instabilities, we identify local intrinsic dimension (LID) as their primary driver and propose Intrinsic Quenching (IQ), a direct corrective mechanism that deflates it to alleviate hallucinations. IQ consistently outperforms standard hallucination reduction baselines across a wide array of benchmarks and offers a highly promising solution for enforcing anatomical consistency in downstream medical imaging tasks.

Background

DMs can be formulated through the framework of stochastic differential equations (SDEs):

\displaystyle\text{d}\mathbf{x}_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{F}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}\text{d}t+\mathbf{G}_{t}\text{d}\mathbf{w}_{t},

(1)

\displaystyle\text{d}\mathbf{x}_{t}

\displaystyle=[\mathbf{F}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{G}_{t}\mathbf{G}_{t}^{\top}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}{\log{p(\mathbf{x}_{t})}}]\text{d}t+\mathbf{G}_{t}\text{d}\overline{\mathbf{w}}_{t},

(2)

where
\mathbf{F}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}
is a linear
drift term
with
\mathbf{x}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and time-dependent matrix
\mathbf{F}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,
\mathbf{G}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is a matrix-valued
diffusion
coefficient,
\mathbf{w}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\overline{\mathbf{w}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
are the Wiener processes running forward and reverse in time respectively,
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}{\log{p(\mathbf{x}_{t})}}
is the
score
function of the time-parameterized distribution
p(\mathbf{x}_{t}),t\in[0,1]
, where
p(\mathbf{x}_{0})
denotes the data and
p(\mathbf{x}_{1})
a terminal distribution, typically chosen to be Gaussian. A DM, parameterized by
\boldsymbol{\theta}

Equation

1

forward
process, which gradually corrupts data samples into Gaussian noise, whereas
eq.

2

reverse
process, which generates the data through iterative denoising. The Probability Flow ODE (PF-ODE) is the deterministic counterpart of
eq.

2

\mathbf{F}_{\text{ODE}}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)\triangleq\frac{\text{d}\mathbf{x}_{t}}{\text{d}t}=[\mathbf{F}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{G}_{t}\mathbf{G}_{t}^{\top}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}{\log{p(\mathbf{x}_{t})}}].

Substituting the
\boldsymbol{\theta}

as

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})=\mathbf{x}_{1}-\int_{0}^{1}\mathbf{F}_{\text{ODE}}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)\text{d}t,

(3)

denoting the model-based mapping of a sample
\mathbf{x}_{1}\sim p(\mathbf{x}_{1})
to the approximate data distribution
p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})
given by the DM. At the center of our reasoning, we rely on the following assumption.

Assumption 1
.

We follow the stratified manifold hypothesis
(
25
)
, which posits that high-dimensional data resides on a disjoint collection of low-dimensional submanifolds, and assume that a DM is able to learn a statistical approximation of these strata.

Based on
1

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\left\{\mathbf{x}_{0}\ \middle|\ \exists_{\mathbf{x}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathbf{x}_{0}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})\right\}

(
20
;
53
;
67
)
.

Training DMs relies on a tractable formula for the forward kernel
p(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{0})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{H}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t})
, where
\mathbf{H}_{t}=\mathbf{\Phi}(t,0)
with
\mathbf{\Phi}(t,s)=\exp\left(\int_{s}^{t}\mathbf{F}_{u}\text{d}u\right)

\mathbf{F}_{t}

\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{\Phi}(t,\tau)\mathbf{G}_{\tau}\mathbf{G}_{\tau}^{\top}\mathbf{\Phi}(t,\tau)^{\top}\text{d}\tau

\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}{\log{p(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{0})}}
as target or utilize equivalent objectives such as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{DSM}}(\mathbf{x}_{0},t,\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})}\left[\left\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\right\|_{2}^{2}\right],

(4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{ISM}}(\mathbf{x}_{0},t,\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})}\left[\operatorname{Tr}{(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t}))}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\right\|_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t}}^{2}\right]

(5)

where
\mathbf{x}_{t}=\mathbf{H}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{0}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t}^{\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
, in each case marginalizing over
\mathbf{x}_{0}
and
t
to learn the underlying score function
(
29
;
75
;
79
)
. We refer to

\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}

Thanks to the Tweedie’s formula

\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\triangleq\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_{0}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t}]=\mathbf{\Phi}(t,0)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{x}_{t}+\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}{\log{p(\mathbf{x}_{t})}}\right).

(6)

We denote by
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})
the result of replacing the true score function in
eq.

\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}

Arising from the manifold hypothesis,
\operatorname{LID}

Empirical Investigation

The initial hypothesis given by
2
suggested that DMs’ hallucinations stem from interpolating between the modes of the data distribution and thus amplifying the regions of low probability. To filter out hallucinations post-generation, the authors proposed the Trajectory Variance Filter (
\operatorname{TVF}
) defined as
\operatorname{TVF}(\mathbf{x}_{0})=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\left\|\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})-\overline{\hat{\mathbf{x}}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0,t_{1}:t_{2}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\text{d}t,

where
\overline{\hat{\mathbf{x}}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0,t_{1}:t_{2}}}
is the average predicted posterior mean over the
[t_{1},t_{2}]
interval. This represents the
temporal
view, which is used to analyze hallucinations using the intermediate properties of the reverse process. We propose to shift that perspective and instead focus on the
spatial
view by analyzing the local geometry of the DM on the induced manifold.

We define the Local Manifold Instability (
{\operatorname{LMI}}
) of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
for an initial point
\mathbf{x}_{1}
as

{\operatorname{LMI}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})\triangleq\operatorname{Tr}(\operatorname{Cov}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{1}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})))=\operatorname{Var}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{1}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})),

(7)

where
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\beta^{2}\mathbf{I})
for some small
\beta>0
, often also referred to as
local sensitivity

(
12
)
. Intuitively,
{\operatorname{LMI}}
measures the total spatial spread of a small spherical region of noise after it is transported to the data space, providing a proxy for exploring the local geometry of a given DM.

Geometric perspective.
We begin with a core question:
If structural hallucinations can be partially captured by temporal variance during the reverse process, do these failure modes also manifest as localized spatial instabilities on the model-induced manifold?
To assess it, we compare the performance of
\operatorname{TVF}
and
{\operatorname{LMI}}
as hallucination filters on a benchmark of hand images from the
11kHands
dataset
(
1
)
, where they are easily recognizable as hands with deformed, missing or additional fingers. We use the capable EDM model
(
32
)
trained by
71
with a deterministic 40-step Euler solver to generate 128 samples. To decide whether a given sample is correct or represents a structural hallucination, we conduct a user study with independent human annotators to label the samples in a binary manner.
For
\operatorname{TVF}
, we collect
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})
for each
t
throughout generation and then optimize
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
for best performance. For
{\operatorname{LMI}}
, we estimate it with 32 perturbations per sample for a chosen
\beta
. For more details, see
section

B.2
.

Figure

{\operatorname{LMI}}

To better understand the predictive ability of
{\operatorname{LMI}}
, we provide a proposition that connects it with the
\operatorname{LID}
of the generated sample on the model-induced manifold, which we denote as
\operatorname{LID}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}

Proposition 1
.

Let
\mathbf{x}_{0}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})
and assume a sufficiently small perturbation scale
\beta>0
such that a first-order linear approximation holds. Let
\sigma_{1}\geq\sigma_{2}\geq\dots\geq\sigma_{n}\geq 0
denote the singular values of the generator’s Jacobian
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{1}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})
, where
n
is the ambient dimension. Then it holds that

{\operatorname{LMI}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})\approx\beta^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{\operatorname{LID}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})}\sigma_{i}^{2}.

(8)

We provide the proofs and full formal statements of all theoretical results in
appendix

.

1
implies that
\operatorname{LID}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})
measures the
effective
dimensionality (significantly non-zero singular values) of
\mathbf{x}_{0}
at
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
when considering a theoretically full-rank
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}

Proposition

over-estimating the true singular values (of the true stratified manifold), thus resulting in more rapid but plausible modifications under perturbation, or

With

Let

as a hallucination filter with
\operatorname{TVF}

From a geometric perspective, this result reveals the primary origin of the local instabilities; hallucinations occur when the DM overestimates the complexity of the generated samples by inflating their

Theoretical Foundation

While the empirical evidence justifies a hallucination filter based on
\operatorname{LID}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, filters themselves remain only a partial solution to the hallucination problem, as they inevitably require generating multiple invalid samples. In what follows, we propose a corrective mechanism for the sampling process inspired by the evidence that one should deflate
\operatorname{LID}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
for particular samples to avoid hallucinations.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\mathbf{x}_{0}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})
. Assume that
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})
is decomposed as
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\leq\tau}\circ\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{>\tau}
for sufficiently small time
\tau>0
, where
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{>\tau}:\mathbf{x}_{1}\mapsto\mathbf{x}_{\tau}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\leq\tau}:\mathbf{x}_{\tau}\mapsto\mathbf{x}_{0}
. Let the singular values of the macroscopic flow Jacobian
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{1}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{>\tau}(\mathbf{x}_{1})
be monotonically ordered as
\sigma_{1}^{>\tau}\geq\sigma_{2}^{>\tau}\geq\dots\geq\sigma_{n}^{>\tau}\geq 0
. For sufficiently small
\beta>0
, it is true that

{\operatorname{LMI}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})\lessapprox\beta^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{\operatorname{LID}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{\tau}))}(\sigma_{i}^{>\tau})^{2}.

(9)

The contribution of
theorem

1
is two-fold. First, it preserves the connection between local instability and the sample’s
\operatorname{LID}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, while only requiring the posterior mean
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{\tau})
for some small
\tau>0
instead of the final
\mathbf{x}_{0}
. Second, it replaces the global singular values from
proposition

1
with a spectral bottleneck: a summation where the macroscopic variance (
t>\tau
) is strictly truncated by the intrinsic dimensionality of the terminal projection (
t\leq\tau
). Therefore, actively steering the trajectory to minimize
\operatorname{LID}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{\tau}))
dynamically drops variance terms from this summation, provably decreasing the upper bound of
{\operatorname{LMI}}
for the resulting sample. We formalize the implied sampling process below.

Theorem 2
.

Assume
t\leq\tau
, where
\tau>0
is sufficiently small. Replacing the standard trained score function
\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})
in the reverse process (
eq.

2
) with

\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})=\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})-\lambda_{t}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x}_{t}),

(10)

where the energy
\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x}_{t})=\operatorname{LID}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t}))
, is equivalent to sampling from the ideal Boltzmann distribution over the true terminal states,
p_{t}^{\boldsymbol{\theta},\lambda_{t}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\propto p_{t}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\mathbb{E}_{p^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{0}|\mathbf{x}_{t})}[\exp(-\lambda_{t}\operatorname{LID}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{0}))]
.

Theorem

2
guarantees that, for small enough
t
, correcting the reverse process with the gradient of
\operatorname{LID}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
for the posterior mean
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})
moves the resulting sample along
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
towards terminal states of lower
\operatorname{LID}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
,
i.e.
, a lower-dimensional stratum. We obtain the gradient through
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\operatorname{LID}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t}))\approx\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{DSM}}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t}),t,\boldsymbol{\theta})
, where the approximation comes from estimating the DSM expectation with
k
i.i.d noise samples.

Probabilistic perspective.
The resulting correction represents a projected gradient descent scheme on
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
. However, utilizing the
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DSM}}
estimator does not immediately reveal the probabilistic effect of that correction on the generated sample
\mathbf{x}_{0}
. To address that, we provide
corollary

1
showing that one may interchangeably use either the
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DSM}}
- or
\mathcal{L}_{\text{ISM}}
-based estimator.

Corollary 1
.

The gradients
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{DSM}}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t}),t,\boldsymbol{\theta})
and
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{ISM}}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t}),t,\boldsymbol{\theta})
are exactly collinear and
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{DSM}}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t}),t,\boldsymbol{\theta})=2\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{ISM}}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t}),t,\boldsymbol{\theta})
.

This collinearity allows us to reason about the probabilistic effect of the stable and computationally cheap
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DSM}}
gradient through the theoretical lens of
\mathcal{L}_{\text{ISM}}
.

Corollary 2
.

Assuming
\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\log p_{t}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})
,
eq.

5
can be equivalently reformulated as
\mathcal{L}_{\text{ISM}}(\mathbf{x}_{0},t,\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})}\left[\operatorname{Tr}{(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}^{2}\log p_{t}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t}))}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\right\|_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t}}^{2}\right]
. Thus, as
t\to 0
, minimizing the energy from
eq.

10
steers towards stationary points (
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\log p_{t}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\to\mathbf{0}
) with maximal negative curvature (
\operatorname{Tr}{(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}^{2}\log p_{t}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t}))}\ll 0
) of the model-induced probability distribution
p_{t}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})
, inducing a mode-seeking behavior.

By mitigating mode interpolations,
corollary

2
directly connects our optimization scheme with the work of
2
. As the proposed correction essentially aims at eliminating unstable, low-probability states by ‘cooling’ them and shifting towards stable local maxima, we term our approach as
Intrinsic Quenching
(
\operatorname{IQ}
).

From theory to practice.
Both the theoretical assumptions and the separability performance from
fig.

2
indicate that
\operatorname{IQ}
should be only used for small times
t<\tau
. We thus apply it solely within a narrow interval
[t_{1},t_{2}]
of the reverse process, where
t_{2}
is small. This focused intervention is also empirically justified when inspecting the energy gradients
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x}_{t})
(
fig.

3
). By overlaying them over the final generated image, we discover a smooth transition from global, structural attributes through localized, highly-specific features correlated with the hallucinatory content, up to dispersed but cohesive states. This observation naturally aligns with multiple prior studies that decompose the diffusion process into semantically interpretable phases
(
52
;
61
;
77
)
. In this case, however,
\operatorname{LID}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
provides an inherent attribution map
(
45
;
69
)
that allows quantifying such effects, as it indicates pixels with the highest contribution to the model’s error at time
t
.

To ensure that the modified updates behave stably, we compute the
\lambda_{t}
scale dynamically for each
t
by ensuring that the magnitude of the energy term projected into data space (
\left\|\lambda_{t}\mathbf{\Phi}(t,0)^{-1}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\right\|_{2}
) always equates to a fixed ratio
\lambda
of the natural update. For example, in the EDM framework, where the network is trained directly to predict
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})
(and
\mathbf{\Phi}(t,0)=\mathbf{I}
), we use
\lambda_{t}=\lambda\frac{\left\|\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})-\mathbf{x}_{t}\right\|_{2}}{\left\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\right\|_{2}+\epsilon}
, where
\epsilon
ensures numerical stability.

The DM’s score function naturally interacts with the energy gradient from
\operatorname{IQ}
; the former pushes the sample towards
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
with decreasing
t
, while the latter shifts the sample along the manifold to regions of lower
\operatorname{LID}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, suppressing the most volatile directions. To ensure that this intervention selectively targets hallucinations rather than uniformly restricting all samples, we employ
\operatorname{LID}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
as a dynamic, mid-generation filter. Specifically, we disable the correction (
\lambda_{t}=0
) for stable samples satisfying
\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x}_{t})<q_{t}
, where
q_{t}

\mathbf{x}_{t}
across the active interval
[t_{1},t_{2}]
, and set

to the
q
-th percentile of these empirical energy values at each timestep. We provide the pseudocode of
\operatorname{IQ}

Hallucination reduction.

(

Because automated evaluations struggle with semantic perception, we quantify HR on image datasets via a blinded user study (

Results.
Quantitative and qualitative results are summarized in

confirms its theoretical validity: it minimally deviates from the baseline while maximally reducing hallucinations by eliminating mode interpolations.

On

\operatorname{IQ}

We hypothesize the concurrent improvement in feature-based metrics stems from the specific nature of these failures: generating extra shapes creates natural outliers that inflate FID. Since these anomalies dominate baseline generation, their removal naturally increases valid diversity. This is corroborated on
MNIST

formulation: an optimal DM must bound its factors of variation, and while
\operatorname{IQ}

On natural images, where human-centric UP and HR are the primary performance indicators, differences in feature-based metrics shrink. Crucially, on

that such metrics fail to accurately capture human preference. On
FFHQ
and

\operatorname{IQ}

\operatorname{IQ}
successfully cuts hallucinations by around 50% on
FFHQ
and 15% on the highly challenging
AFHQV2

\operatorname{IQ}

Hallucinations in medical diagnosis.
Generative hallucinations pose severe risks in real-world decision-making, particularly in medical imaging where models restore high-frequency details to enable low-dose radiation
(
78

. To demonstrate
\operatorname{IQ}

We formulate the inverse problem
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\sigma\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
, where
\mathbf{x}
represents ground truth
RSNA
brain CT scans
(
6
)

\mathbf{A}
is the forward projection matrix (X-ray line integrals),
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})

\mathbf{y}
is the measurement sinogram. By zeroing the singular values of
\mathbf{A}
below a threshold
\xi

(
63

\mathbf{H}_{t}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{A}^{+}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\alpha_{t}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{A}^{+}\mathbf{A})\mathbf{x}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t}=\gamma_{t}\mathbf{A}^{+}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}{\mathbf{A}^{+}}^{\top}+\beta_{t}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{A}^{+}\mathbf{A})
, utilizing the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse
\mathbf{A}^{+}
. Because SDB maps measurements
\mathbf{y}
to reconstructions
\mathbf{x}
via a matrix-valued diffusion process, it allows for direct integration of the evaluated hallucination reduction methods. Throughout this experiment, we strictly adhere to the original SDB experimental setup on
RSNA

(

)

7
Discussion and Limitations

Our work serves as a direct counterpart to
59
, who demonstrate how the collapse of
\operatorname{LID}
values provides a direct indicator of
memorized
samples. These complementary findings offer empirical evidence for an intuitive understanding of
\operatorname{LID}
: it serves as a proxy for the
creativity
of a DM, where hallucinations (the model being overly creative) and memorization (a lack of creativity) span the full generative spectrum.

Evaluation persists as the primary limitation of
\operatorname{IQ}
and other hallucination reduction methods. While human annotators generally agree when identifying structural failures, disagreements remain common, highlighting the subjective nature of the task. Constructing fully objective, large-scale benchmarks with automated evaluation would streamline the development of more effective methods. Currently, deciding whether the observed drops in DSV and IV, proxy metrics for diversity, in datasets like
11kHands
and
FFHQ
are strictly caused by the removal of hallucinated samples remains highly probable but difficult to definitively isolate.

Furthermore, methods
intrinsic
to the model, such as RODS and
\operatorname{IQ}
, still induce a moderate computational overhead. Discovering cheaper estimators for
\operatorname{LID}
would lead to immediate practical improvements for
\operatorname{IQ}
. Finally, while unconditional generation is the cornerstone of generative modeling, hallucinations pose the highest risk in conditional settings involving real-world decision-making and diagnosis
(
8
)
, highlighting a critical direction for future investigation.

A.1
Proposition

1

Proposition 1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
be the deterministic generative mapping of a diffusion model from the latent noise space to the induced data manifold
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, evaluated at an initial state
\mathbf{x}_{1}
. Assume the perturbation scale
\beta>0
is sufficiently small such that a first-order approximation holds. Let
\sigma_{1}\geq\sigma_{2}\geq\dots\geq\sigma_{n}\geq 0
denote the singular values of the generator’s Jacobian
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{1}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})
. Let
\operatorname{LID}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})
denote the local intrinsic dimensionality of the generated data point
\mathbf{x}_{0}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})
with respect to the induced manifold
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
. The Local Manifold Instability (
{\operatorname{LMI}}
) is approximately equal to the scaled sum of its top
\left\lfloor\operatorname{LID}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})\right\rfloor
squared singular values:

{\operatorname{LMI}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})\approx\beta^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{\left\lfloor\operatorname{LID}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})\right\rfloor}\sigma_{i}^{2}.

(11)

By definition, the Local Manifold Instability (
{\operatorname{LMI}}
) at an initial state
\mathbf{x}_{1}
is given by
{\operatorname{LMI}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})\triangleq\operatorname{Tr}(\operatorname{Cov}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{1}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon})))
, where
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\beta^{2}\mathbf{I})
for a perturbation scale
\beta>0
. Applying a first-order Taylor expansion to the generative mapping
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
around
\mathbf{x}_{1}
yields:

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{1}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon})=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})+\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{1}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})\boldsymbol{\epsilon}+\mathcal{O}(\left\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\right\|_{2}^{2}).

(12)

Substituting this expansion into the covariance expression and recognizing that
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})
is a deterministic constant with respect to
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
, it vanishes from the computation. Letting
\mathbf{J}=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{1}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})
, we obtain:

\operatorname{Cov}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{1}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}))=\mathbf{J}\operatorname{Cov}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon})\mathbf{J}^{\top}+\mathcal{O}(\beta^{3})=\beta^{2}\mathbf{J}\mathbf{J}^{\top}+\mathcal{O}(\beta^{3}).

(13)

Applying the trace operator to calculate the total variance (
{\operatorname{LMI}}
):

{\operatorname{LMI}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})=\operatorname{Tr}(\beta^{2}\mathbf{J}\mathbf{J}^{\top})+\mathcal{O}(\beta^{3})=\beta^{2}\left\|\mathbf{J}\right\|_{F}^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\beta^{3}).

(14)

For a sufficiently small noise scale
\beta
, the linear regime dominates and the higher-order remainder
\mathcal{O}(\beta^{3})
can be safely neglected, yielding the first-order approximation:

{\operatorname{LMI}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})\approx\beta^{2}\left\|\mathbf{J}\right\|_{F}^{2}.

(15)

Our next objective is to relate this squared Frobenius norm to the effective spectral properties of the mapping. The squared Frobenius norm of any matrix is algebraically equivalent to the sum of all its squared singular values:

\left\|\mathbf{J}\right\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}^{2}.

(16)

We address the geometric dimensionality of the generation process. The generator
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
continuously maps the
n
-dimensional ambient noise space into a lower-dimensional induced data manifold
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
. The local intrinsic dimensionality at the specific generated data point
\mathbf{x}_{0}
, denoted as
\operatorname{LID}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})
, represents the effective number of independent, structurally significant directions in which the model’s output varies.

While deep neural networks practically possess full-rank Jacobians (meaning all
\sigma_{i}>0
),
1
implies that the mapping highly contracts the space along off-manifold directions. Consequently, the singular values corresponding to the remaining
n-\left\lfloor\operatorname{LID}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})\right\rfloor
directions are infinitesimally small, representing negligible off-manifold numerical noise rather than structurally meaningful degrees of freedom. By treating these tail singular values as negligible (
\sigma_{i}\approx 0
for
i>\left\lfloor\operatorname{LID}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})\right\rfloor
), we can approximate the full spectral sum by truncating it at the effective dimensionality:

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}^{2}\approx\sum_{i=1}^{\left\lfloor\operatorname{LID}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})\right\rfloor}\sigma_{i}^{2}.

(17)

Finally, substituting this spectral truncation back into
eq.

15
yields the completed theoretical approximation:

{\operatorname{LMI}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})\approx\beta^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{\left\lfloor\operatorname{LID}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})\right\rfloor}\sigma_{i}^{2}.

(18)

This demonstrates that, under a localized linear regime, the spatial instability of the generator is closely approximated by the variance of the injected noise scaled by the structurally significant spectral mass of the local manifold.
∎

A.2
Proposition

2

Proposition 2
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
be the
\operatorname{LID}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})
-dimensional manifold induced by a fully trained diffusion model parameterized by
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, embedded in an
n
-dimensional ambient space, with probability density
p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})
. Assume
p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})
is locally uniform on
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
with negligible curvature at the limit
t\to 0
. Let the forward process be defined by an arbitrary drift matrix
\mathbf{H}_{t}
and a strictly positive-definite covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t}
. As
t\to 0
, the expected DSM loss evaluates exactly to the local intrinsic dimension
\operatorname{LID}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})
, and the expected ISM loss evaluates exactly to
-\frac{1}{2}(n-\operatorname{LID}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{0}))
.

Let the forward diffusion state be
\mathbf{x}_{t}=\mathbf{H}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{0}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t}^{\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
, where
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})
and
\mathbf{x}_{0}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
. By Tweedie’s formula applied to the model’s induced distribution, the predicted score function
\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})
defines the model’s posterior mean estimate
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})
via:

\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})=-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{H}_{t}\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})).

(19)

The predicted noise is defined as
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\triangleq-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})
. Substituting the predicted score yields:

\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{H}_{t}\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})).

(20)

To evaluate the DSM expectation
\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\left[\left\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]
, we express the target forward noise as
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{H}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{0})
. The residual error inside the norm becomes:

\boldsymbol{\epsilon}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{H}_{t}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})-\mathbf{x}_{0}).

(21)

As
t\to 0
, the model’s posterior mean
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})
acts as the optimal projection of the noisy state back onto the locally flat model-induced manifold
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
. Because the forward process introduces spatial drift
\mathbf{H}_{t}
and covariance
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t}
, the projected state minimizes the Mahalanobis distance
\left\|\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{H}_{t}\mathbf{x}\right\|_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t}^{-1}}^{2}
for
\mathbf{x}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{x}_{0}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
be the tangent space of the model’s manifold at
\mathbf{x}_{0}
. The projection onto this mapped tangent space
\mathbf{H}_{t}\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{x}_{0}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
under the Mahalanobis inner product
\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t}^{-1}}=\mathbf{u}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t}^{-1}\mathbf{v}
is governed by an oblique projection matrix
\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{T}}^{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t},\mathbf{H}_{t}}
. Therefore, the deviation of the model’s posterior mean mapped through the drift is exactly the oblique projection of the forward perturbation:

\mathbf{H}_{t}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})-\mathbf{x}_{0})\approx\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{T}}^{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t},\mathbf{H}_{t}}(\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{H}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{0})=\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{T}}^{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t},\mathbf{H}_{t}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t}^{\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}.

(22)

Substituting this geometric relation back into
eq.

21
, the DSM expectation becomes:

\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}[\mathcal{L}_{\text{DSM}}(\mathbf{x}_{0},t,\boldsymbol{\theta})]=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\left[\left\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{T}}^{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t},\mathbf{H}_{t}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t}^{\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right].

(23)

Define the transformed operator
\mathbf{M}_{\mathcal{T}}\triangleq\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{T}}^{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t},\mathbf{H}_{t}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t}^{\frac{1}{2}}
. This operator represents the tangent space projection mapped into the whitened isotropic noise space. We prove
\mathbf{M}_{\mathcal{T}}
is a standard Euclidean orthogonal projector by checking symmetry. By definition,
\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{T}}^{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t},\mathbf{H}_{t}}
is self-adjoint with respect to the Mahalanobis inner product, meaning
(\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{T}}^{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t},\mathbf{H}_{t}})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t}^{-1}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t}^{-1}\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{T}}^{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t},\mathbf{H}_{t}}
. Thus:

\mathbf{M}_{\mathcal{T}}^{\top}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{T}}^{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t},\mathbf{H}_{t}})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t}^{-\frac{1}{2}}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t}^{-1}\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{T}}^{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t},\mathbf{H}_{t}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t})\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t}^{-\frac{1}{2}}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{T}}^{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t},\mathbf{H}_{t}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t}^{\frac{1}{2}}=\mathbf{M}_{\mathcal{T}}.

(24)

Because
\mathbf{M}_{\mathcal{T}}
is symmetric (
\mathbf{M}_{\mathcal{T}}^{\top}=\mathbf{M}_{\mathcal{T}}
) and idempotent (
\mathbf{M}_{\mathcal{T}}^{2}=\mathbf{M}_{\mathcal{T}}
), it is a valid Euclidean orthogonal projector. Its rank is strictly preserved under similarity transformations and the non-singular linear mapping
\mathbf{H}_{t}
, meaning
\text{rank}(\mathbf{M}_{\mathcal{T}})=\text{rank}(\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{T}}^{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t},\mathbf{H}_{t}})=\operatorname{LID}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})
.

Applying the standard identity for the expected squared norm of an orthogonally projected standard Gaussian, we evaluate the trace:

\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}[\mathcal{L}_{\text{DSM}}(\mathbf{x}_{0},t,\boldsymbol{\theta})]=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\left[\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{\top}\mathbf{M}_{\mathcal{T}}^{\top}\mathbf{M}_{\mathcal{T}}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\right]=\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{M}_{\mathcal{T}})=\operatorname{LID}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{0}).

(25)

Finally, by Stein’s Lemma, the generalized score matching objectives satisfy the exact algebraic identity
\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}[\mathcal{L}_{\text{DSM}}(\mathbf{x}_{0},t,\boldsymbol{\theta})]=n+2\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}[\mathcal{L}_{\text{ISM}}(\mathbf{x}_{0},t,\boldsymbol{\theta})]
. Rearranging this relationship and substituting the evaluated DSM expectation directly yields:

\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}[\mathcal{L}_{\text{ISM}}(\mathbf{x}_{0},t,\boldsymbol{\theta})]=-\frac{1}{2}(n-\operatorname{LID}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})),

(26)

proving geometric and algebraic consistency for arbitrary drift and covariance schedules exclusively within the model-induced geometry.
∎

A.3
Theorem

1

We include a more general formulation of
theorem

1
that is not limited to isotropic noise as
t\rightarrow 0
. This allows us to provide theoretical guarantees also for frameworks like SDB.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\mathbf{x}_{0}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})
. Assume that
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})
is decomposed as
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\leq\tau}\circ\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{>\tau}
for sufficiently small time
\tau>0
, where
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{>\tau}:\mathbf{x}_{1}\mapsto\mathbf{x}_{\tau}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\leq\tau}:\mathbf{x}_{\tau}\mapsto\mathbf{x}_{0}
. Let the singular values of the macroscopic flow Jacobian
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{1}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{>\tau}(\mathbf{x}_{1})
be monotonically ordered as
\sigma_{1}^{>\tau}\geq\sigma_{2}^{>\tau}\geq\dots\geq\sigma_{n}^{>\tau}\geq 0
. Let
K=\left\|\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{\tau}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\leq\tau}(\mathbf{x}_{\tau})\right\|_{2}^{2}\geq 1
represent the squared spectral norm of the terminal oblique projection. For sufficiently small
\beta>0
, it is true that

{\operatorname{LMI}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})\lessapprox K\beta^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{\left\lfloor\operatorname{LID}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{\tau}))\right\rfloor}(\sigma_{i}^{>\tau})^{2}.

(27)

Applying the multivariate chain rule to the decomposed generative flow
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\leq\tau}\circ\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{>\tau}
, the total generator Jacobian
\mathbf{J}=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{1}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})
is the matrix product of the stage-wise Jacobians:

\mathbf{J}=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{\tau}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\leq\tau}(\mathbf{x}_{\tau})\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{1}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{>\tau}(\mathbf{x}_{1})=\mathbf{J}_{\leq\tau}\mathbf{J}_{>\tau}.

(28)

Recall from
proposition

1
the first-order approximation for
{\operatorname{LMI}}
for sufficiently small
\beta
, which evaluates to the scaled squared Frobenius norm of the total Jacobian:
{\operatorname{LMI}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})\approx\beta^{2}\left\|\mathbf{J}\right\|_{F}^{2}
. Substituting the chain rule expansion into this norm yields:

\left\|\mathbf{J}\right\|_{F}^{2}=\left\|\mathbf{J}_{\leq\tau}\mathbf{J}_{>\tau}\right\|_{F}^{2}.

(29)

For sufficiently small
\tau
, the terminal integration step
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\leq\tau}
behaves as a projection onto the induced manifold
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
. In the general case of anisotropic terminal noise (such as the embedded covariance structure in SDB), this operation acts as an
oblique
projector rather than a standard Euclidean orthogonal projector.

Consequently, its Jacobian
\mathbf{J}_{\leq\tau}
possesses exactly
\left\lfloor\operatorname{LID}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})\right\rfloor
non-zero singular values. We denote these terminal singular values as
\sigma_{i}^{\leq\tau}
and bound them by the squared spectral norm of the oblique projector:
(\sigma_{i}^{\leq\tau})^{2}\leq\left\|\mathbf{J}_{\leq\tau}\right\|_{2}^{2}\triangleq K
. The remaining
n-\left\lfloor\operatorname{LID}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})\right\rfloor
singular values are strictly equal to
0
(annihilating variance along normal directions).

To decouple the two generative regimes, we apply the singular value product inequality (von Neumann trace inequality) to bound the Frobenius norm of the product. The sum of the squared singular values of the product is bounded by the sum of the products of their individual squared singular values:

\left\|\mathbf{J}_{\leq\tau}\mathbf{J}_{>\tau}\right\|_{F}^{2}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\sigma_{i}^{\leq\tau})^{2}(\sigma_{i}^{>\tau})^{2}.

(30)

Substituting the bounded spectrum of the terminal oblique projector (
\sigma_{i}^{\leq\tau}\leq\sqrt{K}
for
i\leq\left\lfloor\operatorname{LID}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})\right\rfloor
and
0
otherwise) acts as a strict algebraic cutoff for the summation, scaled by the projection constant
K
:

\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\sigma_{i}^{\leq\tau})^{2}(\sigma_{i}^{>\tau})^{2}\leq K\sum_{i=1}^{\left\lfloor\operatorname{LID}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})\right\rfloor}(\sigma_{i}^{>\tau})^{2}.

(31)

Substituting this strict upper bound back into the
{\operatorname{LMI}}
approximation provides an intermediate theoretical guarantee:

{\operatorname{LMI}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})\lessapprox K\beta^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{\left\lfloor\operatorname{LID}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})\right\rfloor}(\sigma_{i}^{>\tau})^{2}.

(32)

Next, we address the topological equivalence of evaluating the intrinsic dimensionality at the unobserved terminal state
\mathbf{x}_{0}
versus the predicted posterior mean
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{\tau})
. At any intermediate time
\tau>0
, the state
\mathbf{x}_{\tau}
is corrupted by forward process variance. Consequently,
\mathbf{x}_{\tau}
has full support in the
n
-dimensional ambient space, meaning
\mathbf{x}_{\tau}\notin\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
.

To evaluate the topological complexity of the target manifold from
\mathbf{x}_{\tau}
, we compute the Tweedie estimate derived from the predicted score function,
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{\tau})
. By the fundamental theorem of estimation, this conditional expectation acts as the Minimum Mean Square Error (MMSE) estimator. Because
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
generally possesses curvature, the expectation of points on the manifold strictly resides within the convex hull of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, meaning
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{\tau})\notin\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
in the general case.

However, we establish asymptotic equivalence between this estimation and the terminal generative mapping. The terminal state is obtained by integrating the Probability Flow ODE from
\tau
to
0
:
\mathbf{x}_{0}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\leq\tau}(\mathbf{x}_{\tau})
. As the integration interval
\tau\to 0
, the distribution concentrates locally, the exact ODE integration converges to the analytic score-based projection, and the Euclidean distance to the manifold vanishes:

\lim_{\tau\to 0}\text{dist}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{\tau}),\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=0.

(33)

This implies that for a sufficiently small
\tau
, the Tweedie estimate
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{\tau})
and the true terminal state
\mathbf{x}_{0}
reside within the exact same infinitesimal local neighborhood.

Under the assumption that
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is a smooth manifold, the local intrinsic dimensionality
\operatorname{LID}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})
is a locally constant topological invariant. Because
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{\tau})
approaches
\mathbf{x}_{0}
sufficiently close, their geometric dimensionalities strictly align:

\operatorname{LID}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{\tau}))=\operatorname{LID}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{0}).

(34)

Substituting this topological equivalence into the intermediate bound (
eq.

32
) yields the completed operational approximation:

{\operatorname{LMI}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})\lessapprox K\beta^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{\left\lfloor\operatorname{LID}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{\tau}))\right\rfloor}(\sigma_{i}^{>\tau})^{2}.

(35)

∎

A.4
Theorem

Theorem 2
.

Let
p_{t}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})
be the model-induced marginal density at time
t\leq\tau
. Assume the network parameterizes the exact score of its induced distribution, such that
\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\log p_{t}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})
. We define the ideal geometrically-guided distribution by re-weighting the marginal over the true terminal states:
p_{t}^{\boldsymbol{\theta},\lambda_{t}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\propto p_{t}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\mathbb{E}_{p^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{0}|\mathbf{x}_{t})}[\exp(-\lambda_{t}\operatorname{LID}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{0}))]
. Assume
\tau
is sufficiently small such that the posterior variance
\sigma_{t}^{2}\to 0
. Under these conditions, guiding the reverse diffusion with the modified score
\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})=\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})-\lambda_{t}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\operatorname{LID}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t}))
is mathematically equivalent to sampling from the ideal distribution
p_{t}^{\boldsymbol{\theta},\lambda_{t}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})
, shifting probability mass toward lower-dimensional target strata.

We define the ideal guided target density by re-weighting the model-induced marginal
p_{t}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})
with the expected terminal energy:

p_{t}^{\boldsymbol{\theta},\lambda_{t}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})=\frac{1}{Z_{t}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}p_{t}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\mathbb{E}_{p^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{0}|\mathbf{x}_{t})}\left[\exp(-\lambda_{t}\operatorname{LID}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{0}))\right],

(36)

where
Z_{t}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is the partition function.

Taking the logarithm and differentiating with respect to
\mathbf{x}_{t}
yields the ideal modified score:

\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\log p_{t}^{\boldsymbol{\theta},\lambda_{t}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\log p_{t}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})+\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\log\mathbb{E}_{p^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{0}|\mathbf{x}_{t})}\left[\exp(-\lambda_{t}\operatorname{LID}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{0}))\right].

(37)

To render this tractable, we analyze the domain
t\leq\tau
. As the noise scale diminishes (
\sigma_{t}^{2}\to 0
), the model’s posterior distribution
p^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{0}|\mathbf{x}_{t})
concentrates into a Dirac delta distribution centered at the Tweedie estimate
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})
. Consequently, the log-expectation of the energy converges exactly to the energy of the expectation:

\lim_{\sigma_{t}^{2}\to 0}\log\mathbb{E}_{p^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{0}|\mathbf{x}_{t})}\left[\exp(-\lambda_{t}\operatorname{LID}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{0}))\right]=-\lambda_{t}\operatorname{LID}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})).

(38)

While the theoretical intrinsic dimension of a smooth manifold is a locally constant integer (which would yield a trivial zero gradient), our operational definition of
\operatorname{LID}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
relies on the generalized score-matching estimator
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DSM}}(\mathbf{x},t,\boldsymbol{\theta})
. Because this estimator evaluates the model’s spatial error, it is a continuous, non-linear, and differentiable function of its inputs, parameterized by the neural network
\boldsymbol{\theta}
. Therefore, applying the spatial gradient operator yields a well-defined, non-zero vector field:

\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x}_{t})=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\operatorname{LID}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t}))\approx\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{DSM}}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t}),t,\boldsymbol{\theta}).

(39)

Substituting this limit and the assumed model score function (
\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\log p_{t}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})
) into the target gradient yields the operational guidance rule:

\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})=\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})-\lambda_{t}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\operatorname{LID}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})).

(40)

By substituting
\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})
into the reverse probability flow ODE, the generated trajectory simulates sampling from the ideal terminal-weighted distribution
p_{t}^{\boldsymbol{\theta},\lambda_{t}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})
. Because this guided distribution is multiplicatively bounded by the original prior
p_{t}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})
, the integration strictly preserves the topological validity dictated by the model-induced distribution while explicitly minimizing the local intrinsic dimension of the expected final sample via the continuous gradient signal.
∎

B.1
Experimental setup

As the domain of hallucination reduction currently lacks a single unified evaluation scheme, we reimplement all of the methods within our codebase. For DG, we train 6 noisy classifiers from scratch, one for each dataset, using ResNets of different sizes adapted to the complexity of the data for image datasets and a 3-layer MLP for
GaussianGrid
. For AAM, we adapt the temperature gradient descent scheme for all DMs and train a separate PatchCore anomaly detection model
[

for each dataset. To find optimal hyperparameters, we perform a large grid search for AAM, DG and RODS on all datasets, except
11kHands
,
FFHQ
and

[

. We report the hyperparameters of all methods in

.

B.2
User study

For each of the five image datasets, the user study consisted of two subsequent phases: the
calibration
phase and the
labeling
phase. The former involved presenting 128 images from the original dataset in batches, aiming to calibrate the user’s perception of structures and correlations that appear in the true data distribution. Although this phase might initially seem redundant,
fig.

5
presents several examples of hand images from the
11kHands
dataset that contain atypical hand and finger placements, highlighting the diversity and complex relationships that should not be labeled as hallucinations. Hence, this phase ensures that annotators are aware of what a structural hallucination is in each considered case.

The labeling phase involved presenting the annotator with a grid of images, each generated with a different sampling method from the same starting point
\mathbf{x}_{1}
of the diffusion process. The placement of all methods was fixed across grids, and the study was blinded;
i.e.
, the annotators were not given the names of the methods, which were replaced by
Image n
captions, with
n
being the method index (see
fig.

5
).

The user study relied on two independent annotators, both of whom were machine learning researchers. The task was first briefly described to them to ensure that the term
structural hallucinations
was understood correctly. At each iteration of the labeling phase, the annotators first had to indicate the indices of images that contained a structural hallucination (which could also be none) and then (for natural image datasets) the indices of images that were of the highest quality according to their own perception.

Because the labeling process was highly time-consuming, the study utilized a different number of images for each dataset, chosen a priori based on our visual assessment and the subjective advantage of
\operatorname{IQ}
. This approach ensured that statistically significant results would be obtained (with a high probability) using a minimal number of images to reduce the burden on the annotators, which could have otherwise negatively impacted the study. The numbers of starting points (
i.e.
, the number of grids presented to each annotator) were as follows: 128 for
11kHands
, 1024 for
AFHQV2
and
FFHQ
each, 512 for
MNIST
and
SimpleShapes
each. Each annotator spent approximately 8 hours in total (across two sessions) labeling all of the provided samples.

At last, we note that the initial empirical investigation (
section

3
) uses the labels provided by one of the annotators on the
11kHands
dataset.

B.6
Ablation studies

In the following subsections, we provide an ablation study of each hyperparameter of
\operatorname{IQ}
on the
11kHands
dataset, where hallucinations are easiest to judge subjectively. For each hyperparameter combination, we manually labeled 128 samples, resulting in a total of 4096 labeled cases, and reported feature-based metrics using 2048 samples. Because the original annotators did not take part in these studies, the HR differs from the main results in
table

1
. In each study, we freeze all of the hyperparameters and vary only the one of interest. We use the settings from
table

11
as the optimal configuration.

We note that while feature-based metrics are not the optimal choice for evaluating image quality when hallucinations are present, they serve as a useful proxy; large collapses or expansions in these metrics indicate an inevitable change in quality. Crucially, in the following experiments, we also observe that diversity metrics (IV and DSV) consistently inflate (suggesting
improvements
) even when FID grows exponentially and HR achieves almost 100%. This observation further highlights the misalignment between feature-based metrics (for diversity in this case) and human perception.

B.6.3
Ablating filtering

To ablate the filtering parameter
q
, we sweep it across the
[0,1]
range, where
q=0
implies that
\operatorname{IQ}
is applied unconditionally across all samples, while
q=1
means that it is never applied.
Figure

9
visualizes the resulting dependency between
q
, feature-based metrics, and HR, leading to two conclusions. First, there is an evident correlation between
q
and the metrics (negative for FID, positive for IV and DSV). Second, there appears to be an optimal choice of
q
at around 0.4, where the HR achieves satisfactory values while not significantly altering feature-based metrics. This once again provides evidence that
\operatorname{IQ}
might be trading the inevitable cost in feature-based metrics to decrease hallucinations.

The effect of filtering is also clear when analyzing the
GaussianGrid
results (
fig.

16
). With
q=0
(depicted as
\operatorname{IQ}^{*}
), all of the generated samples squeeze at the Gaussian modes, almost entirely zeroing out the variance of each Gaussian component. By applying filtering (
\operatorname{IQ}
), this effect is eliminated and the original variance is almost matched.

B.6.4
Ablating constant scaling

To assess the validity of adaptive step size
\lambda_{t}
, we replace it with constant scaling and sweep its values between 0.0 and 10.0 (based on the range of values we observed from the adaptive approach).
Figure

10
visualizes the results with an additional dimension for the presence of artifacts in the generated samples,
i.e.

The results confirm the instability of constant scaling, showing that within the optimal range of the trade-off between HR and other metrics, artifacts start to appear. This behavior is not observed when using the adaptive size (
