Prediction of decline in ovarian function in Turner syndrome

Commentary

Primary ovarian insufficiency (POI) due to gonadal dysgenesis is a cardinal feature of Turner syndrome (TS) (1, 2), and infertility is a source of considerable distress for individuals and their families (3). Ovarian reserve is often severely diminished at birth due to accelerated germ cell loss resulting in absent or incomplete puberty. Although spontaneous thelarche is described in nearly 30% to 40%, most progress to POI (4). Spontaneous menarche is reported in 20% of individuals with TS, and the presence of a mosaic 46,XX cell line is associated with a higher likelihood of preserved ovarian function, spontaneous menarche, and fertility (4). Despite genotype–phenotype correlations, the karyotype alone cannot reliably predict ovarian function in any individual patient. Counseling expectant families prenatally or predicting the trajectory of ovarian function during childhood remains challenging (5). With the advent of universal noninvasive prenatal screening, individuals with a less severe TS phenotype, including many with 45,X/46,XX mosaicism, and those with preserved ovarian function may be diagnosed early, affording an important opportunity for recognition and preservation of fertility.

The underlying mechanisms responsible for ovarian dysgenesis in TS remain incompletely understood. Accelerated oocyte depletion is thought to begin during fetal life, but the relative contributions of X chromosome haploinsufficiency, meiotic defects, impaired germ cell survival, and ovarian stromal abnormalities have not been fully elucidated (2).

The concern for rapid loss of ovarian reserve without the ability to predict an accurate timeline for potential decline of ovarian function in an individual with TS often requires families to make decisions on fertility preservation before the child is developmentally mature to participate in the process. Oocyte cryopreservation may be offered as a fertility preservation option in adolescents with TS if they have had spontaneous menarche but requires psychological maturity to cooperate with procedures for controlled ovarian stimulation and oocyte aspiration (2). Ovarian tissue cryopreservation (OTC) may be offered to prepubertal children with TS but involves an invasive procedure and is regarded as experimental since the efficacy is yet to be established in TS (5). Determining the optimal timing and appropriate candidates for OTC is challenging since OTC should ideally be offered to individuals who are likely to still have primordial ovarian follicles present yet are unlikely to retain ovarian function long enough to pursue the more clinically established oocyte cryopreservation procedures at a later age. Biomarkers or a prediction model that could forecast the trajectory of ovarian follicular loss in a particular individual with TS would greatly enhance the ability to identify, counsel, and appropriately time fertility-preservation strategies for those with more immediate risk of POI.

Hormonal markers such as FSH, LH, or anti-Mullerian hormone (AMH) likely represent the most informative indicators of future ovarian function. Based on longitudinal data of 33 individuals, Sawyer et al suggested that an FSH <37.4 uiu/mL and LH < 0.5 uiu/mL at <3 years of age accurately predicted spontaneous menarche in adolescents with TS (6). These findings suggest that hormonal markers during the minipuberty of infancy may be helpful in early prediction of future ovarian function but need further validation. AMH is also shown to be higher in individuals with spontaneous menarche in TS (7) with an AMH threshold of 4 pmol/L (0.56 µg/L) proposed as a predictor of absent puberty in prepubertal girls with TS, and POI in adolescents and adults with TS (8). However, both biological and analytical variability make AMH values prone to volatility and serial measurements in the same laboratory are likely more informative than a single value.

The recent study by van der Coelen et al (1) presents one of the larger cohorts to date, comprising 365 individuals with longitudinal assessment of hormonal markers (FSH, LH, AMH, inhibin B) in relation to puberty and ovarian function in TS but is still limited by a relatively small number of prepubertal measurements. The study confirms that elevated prepubertal FSH was associated with absence of spontaneous menarche; however, a normal FSH could not distinguish POI from preserved ovarian function due to the relative quiescence of the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis in childhood. Inhibin B and LH similarly lacked discriminatory value in prepubertal individuals. The AMH values reported in the study were primarily obtained between 8 and 12 years of age, and a measurable AMH was associated with a sensitivity of 0.88 and a specificity of 0.92 for predicting spontaneous thelarche. Analysis of the first AMH measurement between 10 and 13 years, following spontaneous thelarche, suggested that an AMH of 0.1 to 1 µg/L was associated with a 12.8× higher likelihood (hazard ratio) of subsequent POI compared with AMH >1 µg/L. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution given the substantial statistical uncertainty surrounding the Kaplan–Meier estimates for the 0.1 to 1 µg/L AMH group. The authors attempted to develop a joint prediction tool for timing of POI using longitudinal FSH and AMH data from ages 4 to 30 years in individuals with TS and spontaneous thelarche. The model performed reasonably well (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, 0.83) utilizing data up to age 12 years in predicting the risk of POI at 18 years. Although a larger dataset is necessary for validation before clinical utility, these findings highlight the potential of this prediction model for quantitative prediction of POI risk and timing in TS.

The most recent TS consensus guidelines recommend offering AMH measurements to all individuals with TS and annual monitoring if fertility preservation is considered (2). Measurement of LH, FSH, and AMH at 8 to 9 years and annually until 11 to 12 years is also recommended to facilitate timely referral for fertility preservation (2). Integrating AMH measurements from birth, including minipuberty labs, along with a joint prediction model such as one developed by the authors may improve identification of the optimal timing of fertility preservation in TS. Ethically approved research protocols that incorporate OTC, when clinically appropriate, together with histologic and molecular analyses of ovarian tissue, represent a unique opportunity to advance our understanding of ovarian biology in individuals with TS (2). Such translational studies may also help identify biomarkers of residual ovarian reserve, clarify the pathways leading to follicular loss, and ultimately inform the development of preventative or fertility-preserving approaches, while ensuring that investigational procedures are conducted with appropriate ethical oversight (2).
