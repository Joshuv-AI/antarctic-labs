Introduction

Uveal melanoma (UM) is the most common primary intraocular malignancy in adults, yet it remains a biologically and clinically distinct entity within the melanoma spectrum. Although its incidence is relatively low, approximately 5-7 cases per million annually in Western populations, UM poses disproportionate therapeutic challenges due to its unique metastatic tropism, molecular profile, and microenvironmental constraints. The liver is the predominant site of metastasis, involved in over 90% of disseminated cases, and once hepatic spread occurs, median survival drops precipitously to under one year. Current treatment strategies focus on local control through radiotherapy, which preserve the eye in many cases but have limited impact on systemic disease. Systemic therapies effective in cutaneous melanoma, including checkpoint inhibitors and targeted agents, have shown minimal efficacy in UM, reflecting fundamental differences in tumor biology, immune contexture, and therapeutic vulnerabilities.

At the molecular level, UM is defined by a highly conserved set of driver mutations, most notably in GNAQ and GNA11, which activate downstream signaling through the MAPK and Hippo-YAP pathways. Co-occurring alterations in genes such as BAP1, SF3B1, and EIF1AX further stratify prognostic risk and metastatic potential. Crucially, UM tumor cells must adapt to two highly divergent tissue niches: the immune-privileged ocular environment of the primary tumor, and the immunologically dynamic and metabolically demanding hepatic microenvironment, where metastases predominantly develop. These tissue contexts impose distinct, yet overlapping, cellular stresses-including hypoxia, nutrient deprivation, oxidative stress, and proteostasis imbalance-that tumor cells must overcome to survive and evolve.

Cellular stress responses constitute an integrated and dynamic network of pathways that enable tumor cells to maintain homeostasis and resist environmental insults. In UM, these include hypoxia-inducible signaling, metabolic reprogramming, mitochondrial and oxidative stress adaptation, endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and the unfolded protein response (UPR), the integrated stress response (ISR), and autophagy. These stress-adaptive mechanisms not only support tumor cell survival under microenvironmental pressure but also facilitate immune evasion, metastatic seeding, and resistance to therapy. Notably, the activation patterns and functional consequences of these stress pathways in UM diverge markedly from those observed in cutaneous melanoma, underlining the disease’s distinct regulatory landscape.

Emerging evidence indicates that the rewiring of stress-response networks plays a central role in shaping UM’s unique tumor ecology-linking oncogenic signaling, metabolic liability, immune suppression, and therapy resistance. As such, dissecting the molecular basis of stress adaptation in UM holds great promise for identifying context-specific vulnerabilities. Recent advances in multi-omics platforms, spatial transcriptomics, organoid and organ-on-chip systems, and nanomedicine are now enabling unprecedented insight into the spatiotemporal dynamics of stress responses in UM. Harnessing these technologies to unravel the interplay between stress signaling and microenvironmental adaptation will be essential for designing more precise and effective therapeutic interventions. Ultimately, reframing UM as a stress-dependent malignancy may not only clarify its biological distinctiveness but also unlock new avenues for clinical translation in this historically intractable disease.

In this Review, we first discuss how canonical UM driver events, including GNAQ/GNA11 activation and BAP1 loss, establish signaling states that predispose tumor cells to stress-adaptive dependencies. We then examine how sequential exposure to the primary ocular niche and the hepatic metastatic niche imposes distinct but overlapping pressures, including hypoxia, immune constraint, lipid exposure, oxidative stress, and nutrient competition. Building on this ecological framework, we review hypoxia and metabolic remodeling, redox and mitochondrial stress, UPR/ISR-mediated proteostasis control, and autophagy/mitophagy as interconnected adaptive modules. Finally, we discuss how these stress-response circuits shape ferroptosis, pyroptosis, apoptosis, immune escape, and therapeutic resistance. By organizing UM biology through this sequence, we aim to show how genetic drivers and tissue-specific stressors converge on a reprogrammable stress-adaptation network that creates both metastatic fitness and actionable vulnerabilities.

Main Body

Clinically, UM risk stratification currently relies primarily on cytogenetic and transcriptional classification frameworks. Established high-risk features include monosomy 3, chromosome 8q gain, and BAP1-associated aggressive states, whereas alterations such as SF3B1 and EIF1AX are linked to distinct prognostic subsets. In parallel, gene expression profiling (GEP)-based classification is widely used to distinguish tumors with lower versus higher metastatic risk. In this context, the stress-response-related signatures discussed in this Review are not intended to replace existing prognostic systems; rather, they may provide a complementary layer of biological information by capturing dynamic stress-adaptive states, metastatic competence, and potentially actionable therapeutic liabilities that are not fully represented by static cytogenetic or transcriptional classifiers alone.

Genetic drivers of UM and its “high stress dependency” biological characteristics

In this Review, we use the term stress-dependent malignancy in an operational rather than purely descriptive sense. Specifically, we define such a malignancy as a tumor state in which malignant fitness depends not only on initiating oncogenic lesions, but also on sustained reliance upon stress-response circuits that buffer intrinsic and microenvironmental stress, enable adaptive state transitions, and create exploitable therapeutic liabilities. In UM, this concept is supported by the recurrent engagement of hypoxia adaptation, metabolic rewiring, redox control, UPR/ISR signaling, and autophagy/mitophagy across disease progression.

For the purposes of this Review, a minimal stress-dependency framework in UM includes three features:[1] recurrent activation of major stress-response modules as a non-incidental component of UM biology;[2] functional evidence that these modules contribute to survival, metastatic adaptation, immune escape, or treatment resistance; and[3] evidence that perturbing these stress-buffering circuits generates disproportionate loss of tumor fitness or increases therapeutic vulnerability. This framework allows diverse mechanisms discussed below to be interpreted as components of a reprogrammable stress-adaptation network rather than as isolated parallel pathways.

Molecular mechanisms and signal pathway activation driven by GNAQ/GNA11 driver genes

Activating mutations in the GNAQ and GNA11 genes, which encode the alpha subunits of heterotrimeric G proteins Gq and G11 respectively, represent key oncogenic drivers in UM, found in approximately 80-90% of cases. These mutations, most commonly missense substitutions at codon Q209 (e.g., Q209L, Q209P), lead to constitutive activation of G protein signaling independent of receptor stimulation, resulting in aberrant downstream signaling cascades that promote tumorigenesis [[1], [2], [3]]. The mutant GNAQ/GNA11 proteins induce persistent activation of phospholipase C beta (PLCβ), which catalyzes the hydrolysis of phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate into diacylglycerol (DAG) and inositol trisphosphate (IP3). DAG activates protein kinase C (PKC), while IP3 mobilizes intracellular calcium stores, collectively triggering multiple oncogenic pathways [3,4].

One critical downstream pathway activated by mutant GNAQ/GNA11 is the PKC-MAPK axis. PKC activation leads to stimulation of the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway, which drives cell proliferation and survival. However, clinical trials targeting PKC alone have yielded modest responses, partly due to compensatory activation of parallel pathways such as PI3K/AKT, which sustains tumor cell survival despite MAPK inhibition [5,6]. Indeed, combined inhibition of PKC and PI3K/AKT signaling synergistically induces apoptosis in UM cells harboring GNAQ/GNA11 mutations, underscoring the complexity of the signaling network downstream of these oncogenes [5].

In addition to the PKC-MAPK pathway, GNAQ/GNA11 mutations activate the Hippo pathway effector Yes-associated protein (YAP), a transcriptional co-activator promoting oncogenic gene expression. YAP activation is mediated via PKC-dependent and independent mechanisms, contributing to tumor cell proliferation, survival, and immune evasion. For instance, mutant GNAQ/GNA11-driven YAP activation upregulates PD-L1 expression, facilitating immune checkpoint-mediated tumor immune escape[7]. Targeting YAP, either genetically or pharmacologically (e.g., Verteporfin), suppresses PD-L1 expression and enhances T cell-mediated cytotoxicity, highlighting YAP as a promising therapeutic target in GNAQ/GNA11-mutant UM [7,8].

Another layer of complexity arises from the role of inositol phosphate metabolism in modulating calcium signaling and cell survival. The phosphatase INPP5A degrades IP3, thereby regulating calcium oscillations induced by mutant GNAQ/GNA11. INPP5A is upregulated in UM cells and is essential for preventing calcium overload and cell death, representing a synthetic lethal vulnerability in GNAQ/GNA11-driven tumors [4,9]. Pharmacological inhibition of INPP5A leads to increased cytosolic calcium and p53-dependent apoptosis, suggesting potential therapeutic avenues.

Furthermore, post-translational modifications of GNAQ/GNA11, such as palmitoylation and farnesylation, are critical for their membrane localization and downstream signaling. Loss of palmitoylation impairs oncogenic signaling and tumor cell survival, and inhibitors targeting these modifications (e.g., 2-bromopalmitate, farnesyltransferase inhibitors) show promise in disrupting GNAQ/GNA11-driven oncogenesis [10,11].

Importantly, GNAQ/GNA11 activation and BAP1 loss should not be viewed as creating fully autonomous stress dependencies in isolation. Rather, these driver events establish signaling and chromatin states that prime UM cells for later stress-adaptive reliance. GNAQ/GNA11 mutations configure calcium flux, PKC/MAPK signaling, YAP activity, and redox-sensitive survival pathways, whereas BAP1 loss reshapes chromatin regulation, DNA damage responses, lipid tolerance, proteostasis, and ferroptotic thresholds. These genetically primed states become functionally stress-dependent when UM cells encounter ocular and hepatic microenvironmental pressures such as hypoxia, oxidative stress, nutrient limitation, lipid exposure, immune surveillance, and therapy-induced damage. Thus, UM stress dependency is best understood as driver-primed and niche-shaped rather than as a direct consequence of mutation status alone.

Collectively, the constitutively active GNAQ/GNA11 mutants orchestrate a complex network of signaling pathways including PKC, MAPK, YAP, and calcium signaling, which synergistically promote UM cell proliferation, survival, and immune evasion. These pathways also enhance cellular adaptability to metabolic and oxidative stress, establishing a “high stress-dependent” tumor state. This stress adaptation involves activation of survival pathways such as PI3K/AKT and modulation of calcium homeostasis, enabling tumor cells to thrive in the hostile microenvironment [5,9]. BRCA1-associated protein 1 (BAP1) deficiency and regulation of tumor invasion and stress response

BRCA1-associated protein 1 (BAP1) functions as a critical tumor suppressor gene whose loss or mutation is strongly linked to the malignant transformation and metastatic progression of UM, particularly its liver metastasis, which is the predominant cause of mortality in UM patients. The tumor-suppressive role of BAP1 is underscored by its involvement in multiple cellular processes including DNA repair, chromatin remodeling, and regulation of cellular stress responses. BAP1 deficiency disrupts these pathways, thereby facilitating tumor aggressiveness and adaptation to adverse microenvironments. Mechanistically, BAP1 loss impairs the DNA damage response by downregulating DNA repair genes, resulting in genomic instability that promotes tumor progression. Furthermore, BAP1 modulates chromatin structure by deubiquitinating histone H2A at lysine 119 (H2AK119ub1), a modification essential for maintaining proper Polycomb-mediated transcriptional repression. Loss of BAP1 leads to widespread accumulation of H2AK119ub1 and aberrant chromatin compaction, which in turn disturbs gene expression programs critical for cellular homeostasis and stress adaptation [12]. In discussing BAP1, it is important to distinguish UM-established biology from broader BAP1-dependent stress mechanisms described in other cancers. In UM, BAP1 loss is firmly associated with high metastatic risk, poor prognosis, aggressive transcriptional states, and enhanced adaptation to the hepatic niche, including lipid tolerance and resistance to lipotoxic stress. By contrast, mechanisms such as SLC7A11-dependent ferroptosis regulation, broad proteostasis control, and certain DNA repair or chromatin-associated stress responses are supported by BAP1 studies across multiple cancer types and may be relevant to UM, but their precise functional contribution in UM remains to be fully defined. We therefore interpret these mechanisms as biologically plausible extensions rather than uniformly established UM-specific pathways.

In the context of cellular stress, BAP1 deficiency alters the expression of genes involved in the UPR and ER stress pathways. This modulation affects the sensitivity of UM cells to ER stress and proteotoxic insults, potentially contributing to their survival under tumor microenvironmental stressors. Additionally, BAP1 loss influences the regulation of ferroptosis, a form of regulated cell death driven by lipid peroxidation. BAP1 suppresses the expression of SLC7A11, a cystine/glutamate antiporter crucial for glutathione synthesis and redox balance, through epigenetic mechanisms involving histone deubiquitination. Consequently, BAP1-deficient cells exhibit altered ferroptotic thresholds, which may confer resistance or susceptibility to oxidative stress depending on the cellular context [13,14].

Moreover, BAP1 mutations frequently co-occur with alterations in splicing factor genes such as SF3B1 and EIF1AX, which further compound the dysregulation of RNA processing and translation control. These combined genetic aberrations exacerbate chromosomal instability and disrupt protein homeostasis, thereby elevating cellular stress and modifying the threshold for stress-induced cell death. Notably, the mutual exclusivity observed between BAP1 and SF3B1 mutations in UM suggests distinct but convergent pathways influencing tumor behavior and stress responses. Co-occurrence experiments have demonstrated that combined BAP1 deficiency and SF3B1 mutation induce cellular senescence via impaired DNA repair, reducing tumor cell invasiveness and growth, which may partially explain their mutual exclusivity in clinical cases [15].

The impact of BAP1 loss extends to the tumor microenvironment and metastatic niche adaptation. BAP1-deficient UM cells exhibit enhanced lipid tolerance and resistance to lipotoxicity, particularly in the liver microenvironment, which is rich in fatty acids. This metabolic reprogramming is partly mediated by epigenetic regulators such as ASXL2, which cooperate with BAP1 to modulate lipid homeostasis pathways. The resulting lipotoxicity resistance supports metastatic colonization and survival in the hepatic niche, highlighting a novel mechanism linking BAP1 deficiency to organ-specific metastasis [16].

Clinically, BAP1 loss is associated with poor prognosis, increased metastatic risk, and aggressive tumor phenotypes in UM and other malignancies such as clear cell renal cell carcinoma and malignant mesothelioma. The presence of BAP1 mutations or deletions serves as a prognostic biomarker and may inform therapeutic strategies. For example, BAP1-deficient tumors often exhibit altered sensitivity to chemotherapeutic agents and targeted therapies, including MEK inhibitors and proteasome modulators. Furthermore, BAP1 status influences the cellular response to oxidative and ER stress, suggesting that modulation of stress response pathways could be exploited for precision therapy in UM [[17], [18], [19]].

In summary, BAP1 deficiency in UM orchestrates a complex network of molecular alterations that promote tumor invasion, metastasis, and adaptation to cellular stress. By impairing DNA repair, remodeling chromatin landscapes, and modulating stress response gene expression, BAP1 loss has been linked to altered stress tolerance and may influence ferroptosis sensitivity, although the direction and extent of this effect appear to be context-dependent. The interplay between BAP1 loss and mutations in splicing factors further disrupts protein homeostasis, contributing to tumor progression. Understanding these mechanisms provides critical insights into UM pathogenesis and highlights potential avenues for targeted therapeutic intervention.

Intraocular and hepatic microenvironment dual stress impact on UM cell stress status

The dual stress imposed by the intraocular hypoxic environment and the metabolic challenges within the liver critically shapes the stress adaptation of UM cells, enhancing their survival and metastatic potential. The liver’s unique sinusoidal architecture, along with Kupffer cells and sinusoidal endothelial cells, creates a complex “high metabolism-high immune surveillance” milieu that subjects UM cells to additional stress pressures. This dual microenvironmental stress compels UM cells to activate intricate molecular stress response pathways, enabling them to maintain metabolic homeostasis despite fluctuating oxygen and nutrient levels. Simultaneously, these stress responses facilitate immune evasion by modulating signaling networks that suppress immune detection and destruction. Collectively, the interplay between ocular hypoxia and hepatic metabolic-immune stress fosters a resilient UM phenotype, capable of thriving during dissemination and colonization in the liver, underscoring the importance of targeting these adaptive mechanisms in therapeutic strategies (Fig. 1).

Distinct stress landscapes and adaptive programs of UM across the ocular-hepatic axis.

The primary ocular and hepatic metastatic niches impose distinct but overlapping stress landscapes. In the eye, UM cells arise in a spatially confined and immune-privileged environment where limited immune surveillance, regional hypoxia, restricted nutrient availability, and local oxidative pressure shape early tumor adaptation. In contrast, the liver exposes disseminated UM cells to a lipid-rich and metabolically active milieu, fluctuating oxygen gradients, oxidative stress, nutrient competition, sinusoidal shear and adhesion constraints, and immune-rich but tolerogenic surveillance mediated by Kupffer cells, liver sinusoidal endothelial cells, macrophages, and lymphocytes. Successful metastatic colonization therefore requires more than preservation of the primary tumor state; it requires reprogramming of stress-response modules that coordinate HIF signaling, mitochondrial metabolism, lipid handling, ROS buffering, UPR/ISR activity, autophagy/mitophagy, and immune escape. This ocular-to-hepatic transition provides the ecological basis for viewing UM as a reprogrammable stress-dependent malignancy.

Taken together, the genetic architecture of UM does more than initiate oncogenic signaling. GNAQ/GNA11 activation and BAP1-associated state changes collectively preconfigure a tumor that depends on coordinated buffering of calcium flux, redox imbalance, proteostatic burden, and metabolic stress. In this sense, UM driver events do not merely promote proliferation; they establish the molecular preconditions for a high stress-adaptation state that is subsequently shaped by ocular and hepatic niche pressures.

The role and mechanism of hypoxia/metabolic stress in UM

Activation and regulation of hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF) signaling pathway

In the hypoxic microenvironment characteristic of UM, the stabilization and activation of hypoxia-inducible factor-1 alpha (HIF-1α) play a pivotal role in tumor adaptation and progression. Under normoxic conditions, HIF-1α is rapidly degraded; however, hypoxia inhibits its degradation, leading to accumulation and nuclear translocation where it functions as a transcription factor regulating genes involved in glycolysis, angiogenesis, and cell survival. This molecular adaptation facilitates UM cells’ metabolic reprogramming to enhance glycolytic flux, promoting energy production despite oxygen scarcity, and stimulates angiogenic factors such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), which supports neovascularization critical for tumor growth and metastasis [[20], [21], [22]]. The expression of HIF-1α in UM tissues has been correlated with tumor size, gene expression profiles, and clinical prognosis. For instance, immunohistochemical analyses demonstrated that high HIF-1α expression associates with increased pigmentation and loss of BAP1, a tumor suppressor gene implicated in UM metastasis, indicating its potential as a prognostic biomarker [23]. Furthermore, chromosomal aberrations such as monosomy 3 and gains in chromosome 8q contribute to aberrant activation of HIF signaling pathways, including HIF-2α, which has been linked to vasculogenic mimicry (VM) formation, a process where tumor cells form vessel-like structures independent of endothelial cells, enhancing tumor perfusion and dissemination [24]. Experimental evidence supports that hypoxia-induced HIF activation has been associated with increased invasiveness and migration in UM models through downstream signaling cascades such as Notch and MAPK pathways. These pathways are activated under low oxygen tension, facilitating epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and enhancing the invasive phenotype of UM cells [20,24]. Additionally, hypoxia-induced metabolic regulators like BNIP3 mediate mitophagy, maintaining mitochondrial function and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), which in turn modulate HIF-1α stability and glycolytic activity, underscoring a complex feedback mechanism in UM metabolic adaptation [25]. Therapeutically, targeting HIF signaling has shown promise; for example, inhibitors such as KCN1 suppress hypoxia-induced expression of collagen-modifying enzymes (P4HA1/2), disrupting extracellular matrix remodeling and tumor invasion [20], while combined inhibition of HIF-2α and focal adhesion kinase (FAK) reduces VM and tumor growth in UM xenografts [24]. Moreover, hypoxia preconditioning studies using in vivo models like the chick embryo chorioallantoic membrane (CAM) reveal that while hypoxia upregulates HIF-1α, it does not necessarily enhance metastatic dissemination, suggesting nuanced roles of oxygen dynamics in UM progression [26]. In UM, HIF signaling should be interpreted not merely as a canonical response to oxygen deprivation, but as a context-specific adaptation module that links ocular hypoxia to metastatic competence. By coordinating glycolytic remodeling, mitochondrial quality control, vasculogenic mimicry, extracellular matrix remodeling, and potentially immune suppression, HIF activation may help UM cells survive the transition from the immune-privileged eye to the metabolically demanding and immune-active liver niche. Importantly, the strength of evidence differs across these axes: HIF-associated metabolic and vasculogenic changes are supported by UM-focused studies, whereas the contribution of HIF signaling to immune escape and liver colonization remains an emerging area requiring direct functional validation in UM models. Collectively, these findings highlight the centrality of HIF-1α stabilization and its downstream regulatory network in UM’s adaptation to hypoxia, metabolic reprogramming, angiogenesis, and invasiveness, offering multiple molecular targets for precision therapy aimed at mitigating UM aggressiveness and improving patient outcomes.

Metabolic stress and energy metabolism remodeling

UM cells exhibit profound metabolic plasticity, enabling them to adapt to metabolic stress through the dynamic regulation of key energy-producing pathways such as glycolysis, OXPHOS, and lipid metabolism. This metabolic remodeling is critical for supporting the rapid proliferation of UM cells and their ability to metastasize, particularly to the liver, which is the most common site of UM metastasis. Studies have shown that UM tumors, especially those with monosomy 3, display enhanced glycolytic capacity and pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) activity, which facilitate the generation of ATP and biosynthetic precursors necessary for tumor growth. For instance, the overexpression of the rate-limiting glycolytic enzyme PFKP in metastatic UM correlates with increased glucose uptake and utilization, while interventions such as quercetin that impair glucose metabolism can inhibit UM cell proliferation and viability by increasing oxidative stress and disrupting ATP synthesis [27]. This highlights the reliance of UM cells on glycolysis as a metabolic adaptation to sustain their energetic and anabolic demands.

In addition to glycolysis, mitochondrial OXPHOS plays a significant role in UM metabolism. Unlike many solid tumors that predominantly rely on aerobic glycolysis (the Warburg effect), UM cells demonstrate high OXPHOS activity, which supports their energy needs and contributes to therapy resistance. Pharmacological activation of mitochondrial proteases such as CLPP by imipridones (ONC201 and ONC212) has been shown to decrease OXPHOS effectors, induce metabolic stress, and promote apoptosis in UM cell lines, leading to reduced tumor burden and improved survival in orthotopic liver metastasis models [28,29]. These findings underscore the importance of mitochondrial metabolism in UM progression and suggest that targeting OXPHOS could be a promising therapeutic strategy.

Lipid metabolism also plays a pivotal role in UM metabolic adaptation, particularly in the context of liver metastasis. BAP1 loss, a common genetic alteration in metastatic UM, is associated with enhanced lipid processing and resistance to lipotoxicity. BAP1-deficient UM cells exhibit increased tolerance to lipid peroxides, which are markers of ferroptotic stress, enabling their survival in the lipid-rich hepatic microenvironment. Disruption of lipid metabolism using agents such as atorvastatin reduces tumor burden in BAP1-mutant UM models, indicating that lipid metabolic pathways are critical for metastatic colonization and growth [16]. Moreover, the epigenetic regulator ASXL2, which correlates positively with BAP1 expression, modulates lipid homeostasis, and its depletion phenocopies the lipotoxicity resistance seen in BAP1-mutant UM, possibly through altered PPAR signaling pathways. This suggests a mechanistic link between genetic alterations and lipid metabolic remodeling in UM cells adapting to the liver microenvironment. Collectively, these metabolic adaptations-upregulated glycolysis and PPP, elevated OXPHOS, and reprogrammed lipid metabolism-enable UM cells to meet the heightened bioenergetic and biosynthetic demands required for rapid proliferation and successful metastasis. The interplay between these pathways also contributes to oxidative stress management, as UM cells balance reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and antioxidant defenses to maintain redox homeostasis during metabolic stress [30]. Understanding these complex metabolic networks provides a foundation for developing precision therapies that exploit the unique metabolic vulnerabilities of UM cells, such as inhibitors of glycolysis, mitochondrial function, or lipid metabolism, thereby improving treatment outcomes for patients with this aggressive malignancy [31].

Interaction between metabolic stress and immune microenvironment

Metabolic stress significantly influences the immune microenvironment within UM, with accumulating metabolic byproducts such as lactate playing a pivotal role. Elevated lactate levels acidify the tumor milieu, impairing the function of effector immune cells like cytotoxic T lymphocytes and natural killer cells, thereby fostering an immunosuppressive environment that facilitates tumor progression. This metabolic reprogramming not only supports tumor cell survival under adverse conditions but also actively modulates immune checkpoints and cytokine profiles, promoting immune evasion. Furthermore, metabolic stress intricately regulates immune cell phenotypes and activities; for instance, it can skew macrophage polarization toward a pro-tumoral, immunosuppressive state and inhibit dendritic cell maturation, collectively undermining effective anti-tumor immunity. Recent investigations into metabolic inhibition have revealed its capacity to induce non-classical forms of cell death, such as ferroptosis, within UM cells. Specifically, disrupting the metabolic-stress adaptation circuitry triggers inflammatory cell death pathways, suggesting that “metabolic stress overload” could be exploited therapeutically. This emerging evidence provides a mechanistic foundation for precision strategies that combine metabolic modulation with immune activation to overcome UM’s immune resistance and improve clinical outcomes.

Overall, hypoxia signaling and metabolic remodeling in UM should not be viewed as isolated adaptations to low oxygen or nutrient limitation. Rather, they form an integrated module that supports survival across shifting ecological constraints, from the immune-privileged ocular environment to the metabolically demanding liver niche. This module also creates liabilities, as dependence on flexible bioenergetic routing and redox-balanced mitochondrial function may expose UM cells to metabolic and oxidative therapeutic stress.

The role of oxidative stress and mitochondrial stress in the occurrence and development of UM

To clarify the functional role of ROS in UM, we reorganize oxidative stress biology into three interconnected states. First, moderate ROS acts as an adaptive signaling input that amplifies oncogenic and stress-response pathways, including calcium-dependent signaling, PI3K/Akt/mTOR activity, mitochondrial metabolism, and potentially YAP-associated survival programs. Second, UM cells deploy ROS-buffering mechanisms, including antioxidant defenses, BNIP3-mediated mitophagy, autophagy, and UPR/ISR signaling, to prevent oxidative damage while preserving redox-dependent signaling. Third, when ROS production exceeds this buffering capacity, UM cells enter a lethal overload state characterized by mitochondrial dysfunction, lipid peroxidation, ferroptosis, pyroptosis, apoptosis, or immunogenic cell death. This three-state model provides the mechanistic basis for the proposed “moderate ROS maintenance-ROS overload lethality” vulnerable window.

ROS generation and signal transduction

In UM cells, elevated levels of ROS play a dual role, acting both as signaling molecules that promote tumor proliferation and as agents capable of inducing cellular damage. Several studies have elucidated the complex interplay between ROS generation and the regulation of signaling pathways that influence UM biology. For instance, the loss of the tumor suppressor LKB1 in UM cells leads to enhanced proliferation and survival via inhibition of SIK2 and upregulation of the sodium/calcium exchanger SLC8A1, resulting in increased intracellular calcium and mitochondrial ROS production-two cancer hallmarks that drive tumor progression [32]. This ROS elevation acts as a signaling amplifier, modulating multiple downstream pathways (Fig. 2).

Hypoxia within the UM tumor microenvironment further complicates ROS dynamics. Hypoxia-induced BNIP3 promotes mitophagy, which alleviates mitochondrial dysfunction and enhances OXPHOS while reducing mitochondrial ROS production. This metabolic reprogramming impairs HIF-1α stability and inhibits glycolysis, thereby promoting UM progression and metastasis [25]. Thus, ROS levels are finely tuned in UM cells to balance survival and proliferation under hypoxic stress.

Therapeutic strategies exploiting ROS modulation have shown promise. Ultrasound-responsive nanoparticles induce a ferroptosis-apoptosis combined anticancer effect by activating intracellular Fenton reactions, generating high ROS levels that cause mitochondrial damage and apoptosis, effectively inhibiting UM tumor growth and metastasis [33]. Similarly, biomimetic nanoparticles delivering photosensitizers generate ROS upon laser stimulation, inducing tumor cell death and modulating the tumor microenvironment to improve drug delivery [34]. These approaches underscore the centrality of ROS in therapeutic interventions.

At the molecular signaling level, ROS act as second messengers regulating pathways such as PI3K/Akt/mTOR, as demonstrated by carbon dot-induced ROS promoting UM cell tumorigenicity via activation of mTOR signaling and glutamine metabolism [35]. Conversely, compounds like pristimerin induce ROS accumulation leading to mitochondrial dysfunction and apoptosis through inhibition of the PI3K/Akt/FoxO3a pathway in UM cells [36]. The balance between ROS-mediated proliferative signaling and ROS-induced cytotoxicity is critical in UM pathophysiology.

ROS also influence epigenetic and immune regulatory mechanisms. Co-delivery of HDAC inhibitors and glutamine metabolism inhibitors in ROS-responsive nanoparticles induces a lethal ROS storm, triggering pyroptosis and enhancing anti-tumor immunity in UM [37]. Activation of the cGAS-STING pathway by ROS-mediated DNA damage further stimulates systemic anti-tumor immune responses [38].

Moreover, ROS generation is implicated in the efficacy of photodynamic therapies (PDT) for UM, where photosensitizers produce ROS upon light activation, leading to apoptosis and necrosis in UM cells [39]. The modulation of ROS levels is thus a pivotal mechanism by which PDT exerts its anti-tumor effects.

In summary, elevated ROS levels in UM cells serve as critical signaling molecules that promote proliferation, metabolic adaptation, and survival, while excessive ROS can trigger cell death pathways. ROS amplify multiple signaling cascades, including calcium signaling, PI3K/Akt/mTOR, and immune-related pathways, thereby influencing tumor biology and therapeutic response. Targeting ROS generation and signaling represents a promising avenue for precision treatment strategies in UM.

Mitochondrial dysfunction and stress response

Mitochondrial dysfunction plays a pivotal role in the cellular stress response by disrupting energy metabolism and activating apoptotic signaling pathways, which are critical determinants of cell fate in UM. Under stress conditions, mitochondria can become impaired, leading to a decline in ATP production and an increase in ROS, which in turn triggers intrinsic apoptotic pathways. UM cells have evolved mechanisms to regulate mitochondrial homeostasis to maintain survival despite these adverse conditions. For instance, the modulation of mitochondrial iron-sulfur (Fe-S) cluster proteins, such as CISD2, which coordinates 2Fe-2S clusters, is crucial for preserving mitochondrial function and redox balance. CISD2 has been shown to influence mitochondrial morphology and function by controlling ROS levels and iron homeostasis. In non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), CISD2 downregulation results in mitochondrial dysfunction, increased mitochondrial ROS accumulation, and disrupted mitochondrial morphology, which collectively induce apoptotic signaling and inhibit tumor proliferation and invasion. Although this study focuses on NSCLC, the parallels in mitochondrial regulation and stress response mechanisms provide valuable insights into UM, where similar mitochondrial adaptations may support tumor survival under stress by maintaining energy metabolism and preventing apoptosis [40].

Furthermore, mitochondrial stress is intricately linked with autophagy, a cellular degradation process that removes damaged organelles and proteins to restore cellular homeostasis. In UM cells, mitochondrial dysfunction can activate mitophagy, a selective form of autophagy targeting damaged mitochondria, which serves as a protective mechanism to mitigate mitochondrial stress and prevent cell death. The crosstalk between mitochondrial stress and autophagy modulates cell fate decisions, balancing survival and apoptosis. When mitochondrial damage is moderate, autophagy facilitates the removal of dysfunctional mitochondria, thus preserving cellular energy production and promoting tumor cell survival. However, excessive mitochondrial damage or impaired autophagy can lead to the accumulation of dysfunctional mitochondria, overwhelming the cell’s capacity to maintain homeostasis and triggering apoptotic pathways. The coordination between mitochondrial stress and autophagy is therefore a critical adaptive response in UM, enabling tumor cells to survive in hostile microenvironments characterized by hypoxia, oxidative stress, and nutrient deprivation. Targeting this interplay between mitochondrial dysfunction and autophagy holds potential for precise therapeutic strategies aimed at disrupting UM cell survival mechanisms under stress conditions [40].

Oxidative stress and mechanisms of therapeutic resistance

Oxidative stress plays a pivotal role in the development of therapeutic resistance in UM, particularly resistance to targeted therapies and chemotherapy. UM cells often exhibit activation of oxidative stress-mediated signaling pathways that promote survival and adaptation under therapeutic pressure. For instance, oxidative stress-related genes (OSGs) are differentially expressed in UM, with enrichment in pathways such as HIF-1, FoxO, and PI3K-Akt, which are known to mediate cell survival, proliferation, and apoptosis evasion [41]. The activation of these pathways under oxidative stress conditions enables UM cells to withstand the cytotoxic effects of treatments, thereby contributing to resistance. Moreover, oxidative stress can induce autophagy and ER stress responses, which further support UM cell survival during therapy. Rather than invoking treatment paradigms centered on non-canonical UM drivers, we now emphasize that stress-adaptive responses in UM are most plausibly engaged under therapeutic pressures that challenge proteostasis, mitochondrial fitness, redox balance, or survival signaling within the GNAQ/GNA11-dominant context [42]. Similarly, oxidative stress-induced activation of the SIRT1/FoxO3a signaling axis has been implicated in UM cell survival, where modulation of this pathway by agents like Elaiophylin induces oxidative stress and autophagic cell death, suggesting a complex interplay between oxidative stress and resistance mechanisms [43]. Additionally, epigenetic regulators such as JMJD2C mediate chemoresistance by modulating the MDM2/p53/IL5RA axis, linking histone demethylation and oxidative stress responses to cisplatin resistance in UM [44]. These findings underscore the multifaceted role of oxidative stress in activating compensatory survival pathways, including metabolic reprogramming and stress response signaling, which collectively contribute to therapeutic resistance in UM.

Targeting the antioxidant defense mechanisms that UM cells employ to counteract oxidative stress has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy. For example, CALM1, an oxidative stress-related gene, has been shown to protect UM cells from H2O2-induced oxidative damage and apoptosis, suggesting that inhibition of such antioxidant proteins could sensitize tumor cells to oxidative stress and improve treatment outcomes [41]. Pharmacological agents that induce oxidative stress beyond the buffering capacity of UM cells can overcome resistance; imipridones (ONC201, ONC212), which activate mitochondrial stress and reduce OXPHOS, effectively induce apoptosis and reduce tumor burden in UM models, highlighting the vulnerability of UM cells to metabolic and oxidative stress modulation [28,29]. Moreover, combining inhibitors targeting oxidative stress response pathways with conventional therapies shows synergistic effects. For instance, co-targeting FAK and MEK-ERK pathways, which are involved in oxidative stress signaling and survival, results in enhanced cytotoxicity in UM cells [45]. Similarly, inhibitors of autophagy and lysosomal function, such as chloroquine combined with MEK inhibitors, sensitize GNAQ/11-mutated UM cells to apoptosis by disrupting oxidative stress adaptation [46]. These approaches aim to disable the antioxidant and stress response systems that UM cells exploit to evade therapy-induced oxidative damage.

In summary, oxidative stress-mediated signaling pathways are central to the development of resistance to targeted therapies and chemotherapy in UM. The tumor cells’ ability to activate antioxidant defenses, autophagy, and metabolic adaptations enables survival under therapeutic stress. Therapeutic strategies that disrupt these protective mechanisms, either by enhancing oxidative stress or inhibiting antioxidant responses, hold promise for overcoming resistance. Continued research into the molecular underpinnings of oxidative stress responses in UM will facilitate the development of precision therapies that exploit these vulnerabilities, potentially improving clinical outcomes for patients with this aggressive malignancy.

“Moderate ROS maintenance - overload ROS lethality” vulnerable window

The concept of a “vulnerable window” in UM characterized by the delicate balance between moderate ROS maintenance and lethal ROS overload has emerged as a compelling therapeutic paradigm. This window reflects the tumor cells’ reliance on a controlled ROS environment for survival and proliferation, yet their susceptibility to oxidative stress beyond a threshold that triggers cell death. In particular, UM harboring GNAQ/11 mutations exhibit a distinctive “addiction” to ROS, which can be exploited by agents that induce mitochondrial ROS overload. Elesclomol, a copper ionophore, has been identified as a potent inducer of mitochondrial ROS generation, thereby disrupting redox homeostasis in GNAQ/11 mutant UM cells. Mechanistically, elesclomol facilitates copper transport into mitochondria, catalyzing redox cycling and excessive ROS production. This mitochondrial ROS overload leads to suppression of YAP, a critical transcriptional co-activator in the Hippo pathway that promotes UM cell survival and proliferation. The downregulation of YAP activity by elesclomol-induced oxidative stress culminates in apoptotic cell death, highlighting a direct link between ROS modulation and oncogenic signaling pathways in UM. Importantly, while UM studies support the association between ROS-inducing interventions, reduced YAP activity, and tumor cell death, the precise intermediate signaling architecture remains incompletely resolved.

Supporting evidence from systematic drug screening reveals that digitoxin, another agent elevating ROS levels, preferentially inhibits UM cell proliferation by targeting key transcription factors such as STAT3 and KLF5, underscoring the vulnerability of UM cells to ROS-mediated cytotoxicity[47]. Additionally, studies on other cancer models demonstrate that oxidative stress induced by various compounds triggers ER stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and apoptotic pathways, further validating the concept that ROS overload can selectively eliminate malignant cells [48,49]. The specificity of elesclomol’s action in GNAQ/11 mutant UM is particularly notable, as these mutations drive aberrant signaling that sensitizes cells to redox perturbations.

The therapeutic exploitation of this ROS-dependent vulnerability is further supported by nanocomposite approaches that amplify ROS generation and induce immunogenic cell death through mitochondria-associated ER stress, enhancing antitumor immunity [50]. Moreover, the interplay between ROS and cellular stress responses such as UPR and autophagy has been implicated in determining cell fate under oxidative stress conditions, suggesting that ROS overload can tip the balance toward lethal outcomes in UM cells [51,52]. Importantly, the “addiction” to ROS in UM implies that these cells maintain a precarious redox equilibrium, where moderate ROS levels support oncogenic signaling and proliferation, but surpassing this threshold via agents like elesclomol results in selective cytotoxicity.

In conclusion, the evidence converges on the notion that GNAQ/11 mutant UM cells are uniquely susceptible to mitochondrial ROS overload induced by copper ion carriers such as elesclomol. This vulnerability arises from their dependence on moderate ROS levels for survival and oncogenic signaling, particularly through YAP activity. Therapeutic strategies that exploit this “moderate ROS maintenance - overload ROS lethality” window offer promising avenues for precision treatment of UM by selectively inducing oxidative stress beyond the tumor’s adaptive capacity, thereby suppressing tumor growth and inducing cell death.

These observations support a model in which redox regulation in UM is not simply a byproduct of tumor metabolism, but a central determinant of malignant fitness. UM cells appear to require a calibrated oxidative state sufficient to sustain signaling and adaptation, yet vulnerable to collapse when ROS exceeds a tolerable threshold. Accordingly, oxidative and mitochondrial stress responses represent both a survival platform and a tractable vulnerability within the broader stress-adaptation network.

ER stress, UPR, and ISR

ER stress and activation mechanisms of UPR signaling pathways

In UM cells, ER stress arises when misfolded or unfolded proteins accumulate within the ER lumen and disrupt protein homeostasis. This stress activates the unfolded protein response (UPR), a conserved adaptive program. The UPR is mediated primarily through three canonical branches: protein kinase RNA-like ER kinase (PERK), inositol-requiring enzyme 1 alpha (IRE1α), and activating transcription factor 6 (ATF6). Each sensor detects ER stress and initiates distinct yet interconnected pathways to restore ER function. PERK phosphorylates eukaryotic initiation factor 2 alpha (eIF2α), leading to a transient attenuation of global protein synthesis, thereby reducing the load of nascent proteins entering the ER. Concurrently, PERK selectively enhances the translation of activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4), which regulates genes involved in amino acid metabolism, redox homeostasis, and apoptosis. IRE1α possesses kinase and endoribonuclease activities; upon activation, it splices X-box binding protein 1 (XBP1) mRNA, producing a potent transcription factor (XBP1s) that upregulates genes involved in protein folding, ER-associated degradation (ERAD), and lipid biosynthesis. ATF6, upon sensing ER stress, translocates to the Golgi apparatus where it is cleaved to release its cytosolic domain, which migrates to the nucleus to induce expression of ER chaperones and components of the ERAD machinery. In UM cells, these three arms coordinate to modulate protein folding capacity and degradation pathways, thus maintaining proteostasis under stress conditions. Studies have demonstrated that UM cells exploit UPR signaling to survive and adapt to microenvironmental pressures such as hypoxia, nutrient deprivation, and oxidative stress, which are prevalent in the tumor milieu. For instance, activation of the PERK pathway has been implicated in promoting autophagy and resistance to apoptosis, facilitating tumor cell survival during therapeutic interventions. Moreover, the IRE1α-XBP1 axis has been linked to enhanced metastatic potential and immune evasion in UM, suggesting its role in tumor progression. ATF6 activation further supports cellular adaptation by augmenting ER folding capacity and mitigating proteotoxic stress. Importantly, the dynamic balance between adaptive and pro-apoptotic UPR signaling determines UM cell fate; chronic or overwhelming ER stress can shift the UPR from a protective to a death-inducing program, involving factors such as C/EBP homologous protein (CHOP). Similarly, ER stress signaling may buffer apoptosis under UM-relevant treatment conditions, as suggested by studies showing sensitization to cell death when PERK-associated adaptive responses are disrupted in combination with pro-apoptotic agents. Additionally, combinatorial treatments that exacerbate ER stress, such as artemisinin derivatives with mitochondrial uncouplers, induce autophagic cell death via enhanced ATF4 and CHOP activity, underscoring the potential of modulating UPR components for UM therapy. Overall, the intricate regulation of the three classical UPR branches in UM cells underscores their pivotal role in maintaining protein homeostasis, promoting tumor cell survival, and facilitating adaptation to the hostile tumor microenvironment, thereby representing critical molecular targets for precision therapeutic strategies [42,[53], [54], [55], [56]].

Regulatory role of ISR in UM

The ISR represents a fundamental cellular adaptive mechanism that orchestrates the response to a variety of stress stimuli by modulating protein synthesis and determining cell fate. ISR functions as a central hub integrating signals from diverse stressors, such as oxidative stress, nutrient deprivation, and ER stress, to recalibrate cellular homeostasis. Mechanistically, ISR activation leads to phosphorylation of the eIF2α, resulting in a global reduction of cap-dependent protein translation while selectively enhancing the translation of specific mRNAs that aid in stress adaptation. This finely tuned modulation of protein synthesis enables cells to conserve resources, repair damage, and decide between survival and programmed cell death pathways. In the context of UM, a highly aggressive intraocular malignancy, ISR plays a critical role in facilitating tumor cell adaptation to the hostile microenvironment characterized by hypoxia, nutrient scarcity, and immune surveillance. Activation of ISR in UM cells promotes their survival under these multifactorial stresses by adjusting metabolic and proteostatic networks, thereby enhancing tumor resilience.

Furthermore, ISR activation in UM contributes to immune evasion, a hallmark of cancer progression. Although direct UM-specific mechanistic evidence remains limited, studies in multiple cancer types indicate that ISR activation can contribute to immune evasion by altering translational output, stress ligand expression, and adaptive survival programs. These observations raise the possibility that analogous ISR-linked immune escape mechanisms may also operate in UM. By modulating the expression of immune-regulatory molecules and stress-induced ligands, ISR can attenuate the recognition and elimination of tumor cells by immune effectors. This adaptive immune escape is crucial for UM cells to persist and metastasize despite the host’s immunological defenses. Recent insights into RNA methylation dynamics, particularly modifications such as N6-methyladenosine (m6A), reveal an epigenetic layer that intersects with ISR pathways, influencing RNA stability and translation efficiency during stress. The homeostasis of RNA methylation is essential for maintaining cellular functions including proliferation, migration, and survival, all of which are pivotal in UM pathophysiology. Dysregulation of RNA methylation patterns under stress conditions may further potentiate ISR-mediated adaptive responses, thereby promoting tumor progression and resistance to therapy.

Collectively, the ISR acts as a master regulator in UM by coordinating multiple stress signals to modulate protein synthesis and cell fate decisions. Its activation equips UM cells with enhanced adaptability to microenvironmental pressures and facilitates immune escape mechanisms. Understanding the molecular intricacies of ISR regulation and its interplay with RNA methylation modifications offers promising avenues for developing targeted therapies aimed at disrupting these adaptive responses, thereby improving clinical outcomes in UM patients [57].

UPR/ISR and their association with immune escape and therapeutic resistance

The UPR and ISR are pivotal cellular adaptive mechanisms activated under conditions of ER stress and other cellular insults. In UM, as in other malignancies, these stress pathways intricately regulate tumor immune evasion and the acquisition of resistance to therapies. One critical mechanism by which UPR/ISR influences immune escape is through the modulation of immune checkpoint molecule expression, thereby reshaping the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME). For instance, ER stress-induced UPR activation has been shown to suppress endogenous antitumor immunity by impairing antigen presentation and promoting immunosuppressive phenotypes in immune cells within the tumor milieu [58]. This is exemplified by the downregulation of MHC class I molecules under hypoxic conditions mediated via the PERK arm of the UPR, leading to diminished antigen presentation and subsequent T cell exclusion [59]. Such modulation of antigen presentation machinery facilitates tumor cells’ evasion from cytotoxic T lymphocyte recognition, a phenomenon likely relevant to UM given its immune-privileged ocular environment and propensity for immune escape.

Moreover, UPR/ISR pathways regulate the expression of immune checkpoint molecules, including PD-L1 and CTLA-4, which are critical mediators of immune suppression. In melanoma, a CD8+ T cell-associated immune gene panel correlated with elevated expression of immune checkpoints and immune escape features, underscoring the role of stress responses in modulating immune evasion [60]. The noncanonical UPR factor CREB3L2, for example, has been implicated in triple-negative breast cancer to drive immune evasion by activating the Hedgehog pathway in CD8+ T cells, thereby repressing their cytotoxic function and conferring resistance to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy [61]. While this study pertains to breast cancer, it highlights a paradigm wherein UPR components directly influence immune cell function and checkpoint regulation, a mechanism that may be extrapolated to UM. However, the extent to which individual UPR/ISR branches directly control checkpoint programs in UM remains to be established.

In addition to immune modulation, UPR/ISR activation confers therapeutic resistance by enabling tumor cells to adapt to the cytotoxic stresses imposed by treatments. In UM, therapeutic stress is more appropriately considered in the context of signaling and survival dependencies linked to the canonical GNAQ/GNA11-driven landscape. Accordingly, ER stress, UPR/ISR activation, and autophagy are now discussed in relation to MEK-pathway inhibition, PKC/MAPK-directed stress, BH3-mimetic sensitization, and metabolic or mitochondrial perturbation, all of which are more directly relevant to UM therapeutic adaptation. UM cells, under therapeutic pressure such as targeted therapy or chemotherapy, can activate ISR pathways, including the eIF2α-ATF4 axis, to reduce global protein synthesis and enhance survival [62]. This adaptive translational control allows cancer cells to endure hostile conditions and evade apoptosis. For instance, in multiple myeloma, ISR-dependent suppression of protein translation and ubiquitination leads to resistance against proteasome inhibitors, illustrating how ISR facilitates drug tolerance [63]. Similarly, in acute myeloid leukemia, pharmacological activation of ISR pathways can overcome resistance to BCL-2 inhibitors by promoting pro-apoptotic signaling [64]. These findings suggest that UM cells may exploit similar ISR-mediated mechanisms to survive therapeutic insults.

Mitochondrial dysfunction and metabolic reprogramming, often intertwined with UPR/ISR activation, further contribute to therapy resistance. ISR activation can induce metabolic shifts that support tumor cell survival under stress, as seen in pancreatic adenocarcinoma where ISR-driven asparagine production fosters resistance to mitochondrial inhibition [65]. Targeting such metabolic adaptations alongside ISR components has shown promise in overcoming resistance. For example, mitoribosome-targeting antibiotics sensitize melanoma cells with elevated ISR activity to MAPK inhibitors, preventing resistance development [66].

Therapeutically, these insights underscore the potential of targeting UPR/ISR components to counteract immune escape and resistance in UM. Strategies include inhibition of key UPR sensors or ISR effectors such as ATF4, PERK, or eIF2α phosphorylation, which may restore immune surveillance and sensitize tumor cells to therapy [58,61]. Additionally, modulating stress response pathways can enhance the efficacy of immunotherapies by reversing immune suppression within the tumor microenvironment. For instance, targeting redox regulators like thioredoxin, which are upregulated in stress conditions and contribute to immune evasion, may improve T cell-mediated tumor killing [67]. Furthermore, combining ISR inhibitors with conventional therapies has demonstrated improved outcomes in preclinical models of infectious and malignant diseases, suggesting a translational avenue for UM treatment [68]. Viewed within the framework of stress-dependent malignancy, UPR and ISR signaling in UM are best understood as proteostasis-centered adaptive circuits that couple translational restraint, stress tolerance, and immune modulation. Their importance lies not only in buffering ER and metabolic stress, but also in linking hostile microenvironmental conditions to therapeutic resistance and potentially reversible immune escape programs.

The dual role of autophagy in UM occurrence, metastasis, and treatment

Because autophagy-related terms are often used interchangeably but refer to biologically distinct processes, we first clarify the terminology used in this Review. "Autophagy activation’ refers to the induction of autophagy-related signaling or autophagosome formation, whereas "autophagic flux" denotes the complete dynamic process of cargo sequestration, autophagosome-lysosome fusion, and lysosomal degradation. Therefore, increased LC3β expression or accumulation of autophagic vacuoles alone does not necessarily indicate enhanced autophagic flux. "Autophagy blockade" refers to interruption of the autophagy pathway, often at the lysosomal degradation stage, which may paradoxically increase LC3-II or p62 accumulation. "Mitophagy" and "ER-phagy" represent organelle-selective autophagy targeting mitochondria and ER compartments, respectively, and may have different consequences for ROS control, proteostasis, calcium homeostasis, and therapy response. Finally, "autophagy-associated cell death’ indicates that autophagy is associated with, or contributes to, cell death, but does not necessarily imply that autophagy is the sole execution mechanism. These distinctions are essential for interpreting apparently conflicting findings in UM.

Basic mechanisms of autophagy and its activation status in UM

Autophagy is a highly conserved intracellular degradation system that maintains cellular homeostasis by recycling damaged organelles, misfolded proteins, and other cytoplasmic components through lysosomal degradation. This catabolic process is crucial for cellular adaptation to various stressors, including nutrient deprivation, hypoxia, and oxidative stress. In the context of UM, autophagy plays a complex and multifaceted role, influencing tumor cell survival, metabolism, and response to therapy. Recent studies have revealed that autophagy is markedly activated in UM cells, supporting their survival and metabolic adaptation under the stressful tumor microenvironment.

Molecular investigations have identified autophagy-related genes (ARGs) and autophagy-associated long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs) as key modulators of autophagic flux in UM. For instance, prognostic models constructed from autophagy-related lncRNAs have stratified UM patients into high- and low-risk groups, with the high-risk group exhibiting enhanced autophagic activity linked to cytoplasmic recycling, energy metabolism, and apoptosis pathways. Similarly, a nine-gene autophagy signature was found to be predictive of UM patient survival and correlated with immune-related pathways, including IL6-JAK-STAT3 signaling and OXPHOS, suggesting that autophagy intersects with immune modulation in UM [69,70].

Ultrastructural analyses comparing normal choroidal melanocytes and UM cells have shown that autophagic vacuoles may be more abundant in normal melanocytes [71]. However, such static ultrastructural observations should not be equated directly with dynamic autophagic flux. Vacuole abundance may reflect basal turnover, altered degradation efficiency, or cell-type-specific physiology rather than stress-responsive flux per se. Accordingly, these findings are not necessarily inconsistent with evidence that UM cells engage autophagy as an adaptive response under tumor-associated stress conditions [71]. Immunohistochemical studies further support the notion that autophagy-related proteins such as LC3 beta are variably expressed among UM histological subtypes, with spindle cell tumors showing higher LC3 beta levels, indicating subtype-specific autophagic activity [71].

Functionally, autophagy in UM serves as a double-edged sword. On one hand, it can act as a tumor suppressor by removing damaged organelles and preventing genomic instability; on the other hand, it promotes tumor progression by enabling metabolic adaptation and resistance to therapeutic stress. For example, the autophagy-related protein Beclin-1 has been associated with favorable prognosis in UM, where higher Beclin-1 expression correlates with reduced metastasis and longer disease-free survival, highlighting its tumor-suppressive role [72]. Conversely, overexpression of autophagy-promoting factors such as ABCB5 enhances UM cell proliferation, migration, and chemoresistance by modulating autophagic pathways, suggesting that autophagy supports tumor survival and therapy resistance [73].

Mechanistically, autophagy in UM is regulated by key signaling pathways including mTOR, PI3K/AKT, and STAT6. The PTK6-SOCS3 axis modulates autophagy by influencing mTOR phosphorylation, where PTK6 inhibits autophagy to promote UM tumorigenesis, while SOCS3 acts as a suppressor of this effect, indicating potential therapeutic targets within this pathway [74]. Additionally, the STAT6/LINC01637 axis promotes UM progression via autophagy, and pharmacological targeting of STAT6 has shown promise in inhibiting tumor growth [75]. The PI3K/AKT pathway is also implicated, as inhibitors like (-)-4-O- (4-O-β-D-glucopyranosylcaffeoyl) quinic acid induce autophagy and suppress UM cell proliferation, underscoring the therapeutic potential of modulating autophagy [76].

Therapeutically, autophagy modulation is being explored to enhance treatment efficacy in UM. For instance, combining MEK1/2 inhibitors with autophagy inhibitors such as chloroquine has demonstrated synergistic cytotoxicity in GNAQ/11-mutated UM cells, indicating that autophagy serves as a survival mechanism upon MAPK pathway inhibition [46]. Moreover, autophagy inhibitors like selamectin have been shown to increase cisplatin sensitivity by downregulating autophagy-related genes, providing a strategy to overcome chemotherapy resistance [77]. Conversely, late-stage autophagy inhibitors such as Elaiophylin induce oxidative stress and autophagic cell death in UM, highlighting the complexity of autophagy’s role in tumor biology and the need for precise therapeutic targeting [43].

In summary, autophagy in UM is an adaptive cellular process that is frequently upregulated, supporting tumor cell survival, metabolic flexibility, and immune interactions within the tumor microenvironment. The activation status of autophagy in UM is influenced by genetic, epigenetic, and microenvironmental factors, and its dualistic role in tumor suppression and promotion necessitates a nuanced understanding for the development of effective autophagy-targeted therapies. Ongoing research into autophagy-related biomarkers and signaling pathways holds promise for improving prognostic stratification and designing precision treatment strategies for UM patients.

Organelle-specific autophagy and its potential role in UM

Organelle-specific autophagy, particularly mitophagy and ER autophagy (ER-phagy), represents a critical cellular quality control mechanism that ensures organelle homeostasis by selectively degrading damaged or dysfunctional mitochondria and ER components. In the context of UM, recent advances underscore the necessity of systematically evaluating these autophagic processes to elucidate their molecular contributions to tumor progression, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance. Mitophagy has emerged as a pivotal regulator in UM pathophysiology, as evidenced by integrative analyses identifying mitophagy-related gene signatures that stratify patient prognosis and predict clinical outcomes. For instance, a six-gene mitophagy signature (including ATG12, CSNK2B, MTERF3, TOMM5, TOMM40, and TOMM70) was shown to effectively classify UM patients into high- and low-risk groups, with the high-risk subgroup exhibiting significantly poorer survival rates and distinct immune infiltration profiles, suggesting mitophagy’s dual role in tumor metabolism and immune modulation [78]. Mechanistically, hypoxia-induced mitophagy mediated by BNIP3 facilitates UM progression and metastasis by reprogramming tumor metabolism-enhancing mitochondrial OXPHOS while reducing ROS production, thereby stabilizing mitochondrial function and impairing glycolysis. Inhibition of BNIP3-driven mitophagy suppresses UM malignancy both in vitro and in vivo, highlighting mitophagy as a potential therapeutic target [25].

Therapeutic interventions targeting mitophagy pathways have demonstrated promising anti-tumor efficacy. Elaiophylin, a late-stage autophagy inhibitor, exerts potent cytotoxic effects on UM cells by suppressing mitophagy and inducing oxidative stress, mediated through the SIRT1/FoxO3a signaling axis. This compound decreases SIRT1 expression, disrupts mitochondrial localization of FoxO3a, and triggers autophagic cell death, culminating in significant tumor growth inhibition in xenograft models [43]. Furthermore, novel nanomaterials such as non-oxidized MXene-Ti3C2Tx quantum dots induce mitophagy-associated ferroptosis in UM cells by generating ROS and lipid peroxidation, leading to tumor cell death without harming normal retinal pigment epithelium cells. This approach exemplifies the therapeutic potential of exploiting mitophagy and oxidative stress pathways to selectively target UM [79].

Despite the burgeoning evidence implicating mitophagy in UM, ER-phagy remains an underexplored area warranting systematic investigation. Given the ER’s central role in protein folding and calcium homeostasis, ER-phagy may critically influence UM cell survival under stress conditions such as hypoxia and nutrient deprivation. Drawing from recent advances in organelle-specific autophagy research in melanoma and other cancers, establishing robust technical platforms-such as transcriptomic profiling combined with functional assays (e.g., autophagosome formation, organelle-specific markers, and live-cell imaging)-is essential to delineate ER-phagy’s contribution to UM biology. Moreover, integrating multi-omics data with clinical parameters could facilitate the identification of ER-phagy-related gene signatures, analogous to mitophagy models, to predict prognosis and guide precision therapies.

In summary, the current landscape of UM research underscores mitophagy as a critical modulator of tumor metabolism, immune microenvironment, and therapeutic response, with compelling evidence supporting its exploitation as a prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target. Concurrently, the role of ER-phagy in UM remains a promising frontier. Systematic evaluation employing advanced molecular and bioinformatic methodologies is imperative to unravel the complex interplay of organelle-specific autophagy pathways in UM pathogenesis and to develop novel, targeted treatment strategies that improve patient outcomes. This integrative approach aligns with the precision medicine paradigm, aiming to tailor interventions based on the unique autophagic and metabolic profiles of UM tumors [25,43], [78], [79].

Crosstalk between autophagy and novel cell death forms such as pyroptosis and ferroptosis

Using “metabolic inhibition-induced ferroptosis” as an example to discuss how autophagy inhibition or overactivation alters UM cell death modalities, providing insights for integrated “autophagy-programmed cell death” interventions

The intricate interplay between autophagy and emerging forms of regulated cell death, notably ferroptosis and pyroptosis, has garnered increasing attention in cancer biology, including UM. Ferroptosis, characterized by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation and distinct from classical apoptosis or necrosis, is tightly regulated by cellular metabolism and redox homeostasis. Autophagy, a lysosomal degradation pathway, can either protect cells by recycling damaged organelles and mitigating oxidative stress or promote cell death by facilitating ferroptosis through selective degradation of ferritin (ferritinophagy), thereby increasing intracellular iron availability and lipid peroxidation. In UM, metabolic stress, such as nutrient deprivation or pharmacological metabolic inhibition, can induce ferroptosis, and the modulation of autophagy plays a pivotal role in determining cell fate under these conditions.

Recent studies have identified a ferroptosis-related gene signature in UM that correlates with prognosis and is significantly associated with autophagy-related gene expression, underscoring the functional crosstalk between these pathways [80]. This signature includes genes such as MAP1LC3C, a key autophagy marker, linking autophagy flux to ferroptosis susceptibility. Metabolic inhibition, which disrupts cellular energy homeostasis, can trigger ER stress and ROS accumulation, activating autophagy as a cytoprotective response. However, excessive or dysregulated autophagy can lead to autophagic cell death or promote ferroptosis by degrading ferritin and increasing labile iron pools, thereby enhancing lipid peroxidation and cell death.

Mechanistically, autophagy-dependent ferroptosis involves selective autophagic degradation of ferritin mediated by nuclear receptor coactivator 4 (NCOA4), which releases iron and amplifies oxidative damage [81]. In UM cells subjected to metabolic inhibition, autophagy modulation can thus shift the balance between survival and death. Autophagy inhibition, for instance by chloroquine or bafilomycin A1, can reduce ferritin degradation, lowering intracellular iron and lipid ROS, thereby attenuating ferroptosis and promoting cell survival. Conversely, autophagy overactivation can exacerbate ferroptotic cell death by facilitating ferritinophagy and enhancing oxidative stress [82,83].

Furthermore, ER stress induced by metabolic perturbations can serve as an upstream regulator of both autophagy and ferroptosis. The UPR pathways, particularly IRE1 and PERK, modulate autophagy induction and ferroptotic sensitivity [84]. In UM, metabolic inhibition-induced ER stress may activate autophagy to mitigate protein misfolding and oxidative damage; however, sustained ER stress can switch autophagy from a protective to a pro-death mechanism, promoting ferroptosis. This dual role suggests that precise modulation of autophagy and ER stress pathways could be exploited therapeutically to induce ferroptosis selectively in UM cells.

Importantly, metabolic inhibition-induced ferroptosis is not isolated but interconnected with other forms of regulated cell death, such as pyroptosis and apoptosis, through shared signaling nodes including ROS, calcium flux, and mitochondrial dysfunction [85,86]. Autophagy acts as a central hub in this network, capable of modulating the threshold and modality of cell death in response to metabolic stress. For example, autophagy can degrade inflammasome components, thereby influencing pyroptotic responses, or regulate mitochondrial quality control, affecting apoptotic sensitivity.

Therapeutically, these insights advocate for integrated interventions targeting both autophagy and programmed cell death pathways in UM. Agents that induce metabolic stress combined with autophagy modulators may synergistically enhance ferroptosis and overcome resistance mechanisms. For instance, combining ferroptosis inducers with autophagy activators or inhibitors has shown promise in other cancers by promoting oxidative cell death while limiting compensatory survival pathways [87,88]. Moreover, targeting upstream regulators such as AMPK/mTOR signaling, which governs autophagy and cellular metabolism, may fine-tune ferroptotic responses [89].

In conclusion, metabolic inhibition-induced ferroptosis exemplifies the complex crosstalk between autophagy and novel cell death forms in UM. Autophagy modulation can decisively alter UM cell death outcomes by regulating iron metabolism, oxidative stress, and ER stress responses. Understanding these molecular interdependencies provides a rationale for developing integrated “autophagy-programmed cell death” therapeutic strategies, potentially improving precision treatment and overcoming therapeutic resistance in UM.

Role of autophagy in liver metastasis of UM

Autophagy plays a critical role in facilitating UM cells to adapt to the unique and challenging microenvironment of the liver, thereby enhancing their metastatic potential. UM is characterized by a high proclivity for liver metastasis, which significantly worsens prognosis. Autophagy, a conserved catabolic process that degrades damaged organelles and proteins to recycle nutrients, is increasingly recognized as a survival mechanism for cancer cells under stress conditions such as nutrient deprivation, hypoxia, and immune attack encountered during metastasis. In the context of UM, autophagy appears to support tumor cell survival and colonization in the liver by enabling metabolic flexibility and resistance to microenvironmental stressors. Immunohistochemical studies have demonstrated that higher expression of autophagy-related proteins such as Beclin-1 correlates with a lower risk of metastasis and improved disease-free survival, suggesting a complex role of autophagy that may vary with tumor stage and context [72]. While Beclin-1 expression is associated with a protective effect in primary tumors, autophagy may later facilitate metastatic progression by enhancing cellular adaptation mechanisms.

Beyond its role in metabolic adaptation, autophagy regulates critical processes involved in cell migration and invasion, which are essential for metastatic dissemination. Autophagy modulates cytoskeletal dynamics and focal adhesion turnover, thereby influencing UM cell motility and invasive capacity. Furthermore, autophagy intersects with immune regulatory pathways, impacting the tumor microenvironment and immune surveillance. A prognostic autophagy-related gene signature identified in UM reveals associations with immune-related functional pathways and immune cell infiltration, indicating that autophagy may contribute to immune evasion mechanisms in metastatic niches such as the liver [69]. This immune modulation potentially facilitates UM cells to escape immune-mediated destruction during metastatic colonization.

Additionally, autophagy influences the response of UM cells to therapeutic interventions, including radiotherapy. Proton beam therapy, a precise radiotherapy modality used for primary UM, induces cellular stress responses including autophagy. Understanding the interplay between autophagy and DNA damage repair pathways in UM cells could help optimize therapeutic strategies to prevent or delay liver metastases [90]. Targeting autophagy pharmacologically may sensitize UM cells to treatment and impair their metastatic capabilities by disrupting their ability to cope with microenvironmental stresses.

In summary, autophagy acts as a multifaceted regulator in the liver metastasis of UM by promoting tumor cell adaptation to the hepatic microenvironment, enhancing migratory and invasive behaviors, and modulating immune interactions. These insights underscore the potential of autophagy-targeted therapies as part of precision medicine approaches to improve outcomes for patients with metastatic UM. Further research is needed to delineate the stage-specific roles of autophagy and to develop effective autophagy modulators that can be integrated into current treatment paradigms.

Conclusion

In conclusion, UM exemplifies a uniquely stress-dependent malignancy, driven by its characteristic oncogenic mutations and the dual microenvironmental pressures within the eye and liver. The intricate interplay of cellular stress responses forms a complex adaptive network that underlies UM tumorigenesis, hepatic metastasis, immune evasion, and therapeutic resistance. A stress-centered framework provides a unifying basis for developing more effective, durable, and personalized strategies for this challenging malignancy. Future work should prioritize defining stage-specific stress dependencies in primary versus metastatic UM, clarifying how stress-adaptive states are rewired during the ocular-to-hepatic transition, and establishing biomarker-guided experimental and therapeutic frameworks to validate actionable stress vulnerabilities.
