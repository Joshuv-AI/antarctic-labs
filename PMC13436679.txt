Successful radiofrequency catheter ablation of an anteroseptal accessory pathway in cardiac dextroposition presenting with aborted sudden cardiac death: a case report

Introduction

Cardiac dextroposition refers to a rightward displacement of a normally oriented heart and is distinct from dextrocardia, where the chamber anatomy is a mirror image.1 This displacement disrupts anticipated thoracic spatial relationships, complicating electrophysiologic interventions, particularly when fluoroscopic reference points do not align with the true cardiac anatomy. Wolff–Parkinson–White (WPW) syndrome can result in life-threatening tachyarrhythmias and, in rare instances, aborted sudden cardiac death.2 We present a case of aborted sudden cardiac death secondary to WPW syndrome complicated by atrial fibrillation (AF) in a patient with cardiac dextroposition, in whom successful radiofrequency (RF) catheter ablation (RFCA) was accomplished using three-dimensional (3D) electroanatomical mapping.

Case presentation

A 57-year-old man, previously told at age 20 that he had dextrocardia, presented after out-of-hospital cardiac arrest. Emergency medical technicians detected ventricular fibrillation (VF) and performed defibrillation, after which the patient arrived at the hospital with return of spontaneous circulation and alert mentation (Figure 1A). During continuous electrocardiographic monitoring in the emergency department, pre-excited AF with a shortest pre-excited RR interval (SPERRI) of less than 200 ms was documented (Figure 1B). The patient was haemodynamically unstable during pre-excited AF, and synchronized direct current cardioversion at 200 J was performed, successfully restoring sinus rhythm.

(A) This electrocardiogram strip demonstrates ventricular fibrillation, as documented by emergency medical services at the moment of defibrillation. (B) This electrocardiogram strip, recorded on arrival to the emergency department, shows findings consistent with atrial fibrillation with pre-excitation.

The anteroposterior (AP) chest radiograph showed that both the cardiac silhouette and aortic arch were present in the right hemithorax, whereas gastric air was identified on the left side (Figure 2). A 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) with reversed lead orientation was obtained. The post-cardioversion ECG demonstrated negative QRS complexes in leads I and augmented vector left (aVL), and positive QRS complexes in leads II, III, augmented vector right (aVR), and agmented vector foot (aVF). Precordial leads displayed a shortened PR interval, the classic delta wave with a broad, slurred initial QRS upstroke, and reversed R-wave progression in the precordial leads (Figure 3A). Transthoracic echocardiography revealed normal chamber sizes, preserved biventricular systolic function with an ejection fraction of 60%, and no regional wall motion abnormalities.

Urgent coronary angiography excluded significant stenosis and demonstrated non-mirror-image coronary anatomy (see Supplementary material online, Video S1, S2).

Cardiac computed tomography (CT) demonstrated normal atrial–ventricular connections, typical spatial relationships of the great vessels, and usual visceral organ arrangement (see Supplementary material online, Video S3). On the basis of these imaging results, a diagnosis of dextroposition of the heart was established.

A repeat 12-lead ECG was recorded after moving the precordial electrodes further towards the right side from their standard locations. This ECG demonstrated a positive P–QRS complex in lead I and a normal axis, whereas the shortened PR interval and delta waves persisted in the precordial leads (Figure 3B).

The patient was ultimately diagnosed with aborted sudden cardiac death attributed to AF with WPW syndrome, with the finding of pre-excited AF during the acute presentation. Given the indication for accessory pathway ablation, RFCA was scheduled. We decided to utilize the CARTO 3 electroanatomical mapping system (Biosense Webster) to ensure precise localization and enhance the safety of the ablation procedure. During the 7-day interval from presentation to ablation, the patient was monitored in the intensive care unit with continuous telemetry and immediate defibrillation capability.

The procedure was performed using standard local anaesthesia. A diagnostic duo-decapolar catheter (Livewire, St Jude Medical) was placed at the high right atrium (HRA)–tricuspid annulus; however, placement into the coronary sinus (CS) was unsuccessful due to a small os (see Supplementary material online, Video S4). An EP star Fix Nogami Curve (Japan Lifeline) was advanced to the bundle of His and right ventricular (RV) apex (Figure 4A and B). Intracardiac echocardiography (ICE) with CartoSound (Biosense Webster) was used throughout to address the anatomical alterations observed in cardiac dextroposition. An 8.5-Fr SR0 long sheath (Abbott) was inserted into the right atrium.

At baseline assessment, no ventriculoatrial (VA) conduction was observed during RV pacing at 600 ms. Spontaneous AF was provoked, but it did not progress to VF throughout the evaluation (see Supplementary material online, Figure S1). SPERRI during AF was 410 ms.

For the purpose of activation mapping and ablation, a Pentaray mapping catheter (Biosense Webster) was employed to identify the His bundle location and precisely define the earliest ventricular activation during atrial pacing. Mapping findings revealed the earliest ventricular signal at the anteroseptal tricuspid annulus (Figure 4C). The Navistar Thermocool SmartTouch navigation & ablation catheter (Biosense Webster) was utilized for mapping and RF application. The targeted ablation site was near the right-sided atrioventricular node, approximately 10 mm from the previously marked His location.

RF energy (20–25 W) was administered four times, which temporarily eliminated the delta wave (see Supplementary material online, Figure S2). Two additional booster ablations were applied at an adjacent site; nonetheless, the delta wave reappeared after 20 min. Further RF energy applications (lasting 30–40 s each) were delivered at the area corresponding to the earliest activation. In total, 10 RF energy deliveries were performed. Following a 30-min observation period, no delta waves were detected (Figure 4D). Activation mapping during pre-excitation consistently showed the earliest ventricular activation at the anteroseptal tricuspid annulus with durable loss of pre-excitation after ablation, and therefore alternative left-sided access was not pursued.

The procedure was completed without complications. At the 2-week and 2-month follow-up visits, the patient remained asymptomatic, and no delta waves were observed on ECG (Figure 5).

With the precordial electrodes relocated further to the right, the 12-lead electrocardiograms showed absence of delta waves and demonstrated preserved R/S progression across the precordial leads.

Discussion

Dextroposition refers to rightward displacement of an otherwise structurally normal heart and should be distinguished from dextrocardia, where both the cardiac chambers and great vessels are mirror images.1,3 Dextroposition can be secondary to congenital or acquired aetiologies, including lung hypoplasia, pneumonectomy, or mediastinal shift.4 In this case, chest CT showed no lung agenesis/hypoplasia or major thoracic structural abnormality, pulmonary function was normal, and there was no history of thoracic surgery or trauma; thus, the aetiology remained uncertain.

WPW syndrome affects an estimated 0.1%–0.3% of the population, but its coincidence with dextroposition is exceptionally rare.5 Although many individuals with WPW remain asymptomatic, pre-excited AF can induce rapid ventricular conduction and VF, as demonstrated in the present case.6,7 Notably, the SPERRI measured on the ECG obtained during the acute presentation was less than 200 ms, whereas the SPERRI measured during the electrophysiological study was 410 ms. This discrepancy may reflect differences in autonomic tone, with heightened adrenergic activity during the acute post-arrest period and lower sympathetic tone during the electrophysiological study.7

Unlike mirror-image dextrocardia, dextroposition causes unpredictable, non-mirror displacement. The lack of predictability complicates catheter manipulation and fluoroscopic interpretation.8 The complexity was further increased by the presence of an anteroseptal accessory pathway, as this anatomical area is inherently associated with a higher risk of atrioventricular nodal injury.9,10 In cardiac dextroposition, conventional fluoroscopic left–right/anterior–posterior landmarks and surface ECG localization may be less reliable; therefore, a patient-specific 3D electroanatomical reconstruction (with CT review and ICE confirmation), including careful His-region tagging to maintain a safety margin during anteroseptal ablation, can improve both localization and procedural safety. Reports describing accessory pathway ablation in dextroposition remain limited; however, previous literature underscores the necessity of individualized procedural approaches rather than relying on mirror-image catheter manipulation.11

Although cardiac dextroposition can reduce the reliability of ECG-based pathway localization algorithms, this case suggests that precordial lead patterns may still provide useful clues when ECG interpretation is adjusted for cardiac position; however, definitive localization should rely on invasive mapping. As limitations, cardiac magnetic resonance imaging was not performed and ajmaline challenge was not available in our region; thus, subtle structural or channelopathic substrates cannot be fully excluded. Because residual risk cannot be entirely ruled out, structured long-term follow-up with periodic ECG and Holter monitoring is planned, with reconsideration of advanced imaging or ICD therapy should pre-excitation recur or unexplained arrhythmia develop.

Conclusion

This case illustrates that RF ablation of an anteroseptal accessory pathway can be performed safely despite substantial distortion of fluoroscopic anatomy due to cardiac dextroposition. A comprehensive anatomical evaluation, including ECG, multimodal imaging, and 3D electroanatomical mapping, was vital for the correct localization and effective elimination of pre-excitation.
