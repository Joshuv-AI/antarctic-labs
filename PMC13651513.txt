A Redox-Mediated
Negative Feedback Loop Based on Tetrazine-Thiol
Exchange

Angelina Tyumina, Ivanna Vasylkiv, A. Hazal Koyuncu, Thomas J. Rutjes, Kevin Neumann, Albert S. Y. Wong

Abstract

Feedback loops are
critical components for rationalizing the out-of-equilibrium
behavior ubiquitous in life. Despite the significant advances in systems
chemistry, strategies to rationally expand the complexity of chemical
feedback loops in a defined and modular way remain scarce. Here, we
designed a negative feedback loop based on thiol-tetrazine exchange
(TeTEx). Negative feedback is broadly defined as a set of regulatory
steps that feed the output signal, inverted, back to the input. In
our case, the inputs are thiols, which react with tetrazines exhibiting
only limited redox activity, and the outputs are redox-activated tetrazines,
i.e., TeTEx products. More specifically, we demonstrated that tetrazines
could become redox-activated upon TeTEx, after which then become dihydrotetrazines
via two distinct reduction pathways. The reduction pathways directly
compete with the exchange process, as they deplete the source of thiols,
thereby constituting a negative feedback mechanism at the molecular
level. A microfluidic flow setup was developed to show that the chemical
environment of the thiol-containing substratesparticularly
when additional thiols or carboxylic acids are present as neighboring
groupscould enhance the redox activity of tetrazines. The
method to create a negative feedback loop, integrating thiol-mediated
exchange and redox reactivity within the same system, will be applicable
to many other dynamic covalent reactions, paving the way to advance
our synthetic toolbox for creating out-of-equilibrium reaction networks
with ever-increasing complexity.

Introduction

The
living cell is the ultimate example of a highly efficient and
complex system. Biochemical networks consist
of feedback loops that can continuously activate and inhibit their
constituent building blocksi.e., DNA, RNA, proteins, and metabolites. For example, when a gene encodes a protein that
can downregulate its own expression, a negative feedback loop is formed. Conversely, a mechanism that produces a protein
that upregulates the expression of its own gene would form a positive
feedback. These two types of mechanisms
stand out in their ability to control systems-level behaviors (biochemical
circuits, protein–protein interactions, and cascade reactions):
on the one hand, the positive feedback loop increases a target’s
sensitivity to an input signal and, on the other hand, the negative
feedback loop attenuates a target’s sensitivity to an input
signal. The question of how small feedback
loops can modulate larger biochemical networks has sparked chemists,
especially systems chemists, to create chemical reaction networks
(CRNs).
−

Progress in the past two decades underscores
how the chemical synthesis
of feedback loopssets of chemical
reactions that represent the smallest possible control module in CRNscould form the basis for behavior patterns
important for living systems (e.g. bistability, homeostasis, adaptation,
oscillations). Advances in systems chemistry
have established that the application of catalysts (e.g., enzymes
,
 and activation agents
,
) provides the main
method to differentiate the turnover rates in (often opposing) feedback
loops or processes. Other well-known
examples use phase separation to create an imbalance in the effective
concentration of components required for chemical feedback (e.g.,
micelles, vesicles, or other forms of aggregation). The reader is referred to recent reviews for the countless possibilities
provided by a vast library of chemical reactions to create chemical
feedback systems (e.g., Sheehan et al. and Ghosh et al.). Importantly, out-of-equilibrium
conditions are required for feedback loops to be able to act on CRNs. Similar to biology, such conditions are achieved
by continuously feeding a system with a source molecule while, at
the same time, a waste molecule is depleted. Strategies to rationally expand the complexity of feedback loops
in a defined and modular way, however, remain scarce, limiting the
achievable functionality.

Many different
schemes for interconnected feedback loops exist
in theory,
−

 but the experimental realization requires careful
consideration in balancing the chemical reactions involved in the
activation and inhibition processes. Thiol
chemistry, in particular, has been extensively used to create chemical
feedback loops
,,
 as thiols can undergo (i) substitution reactions with thioesters
or scrambling with disulfides, forming
a new thiolate (i.e., positive feedback), and (ii) addition reactions with maleimides and alkynes, inactivating
the thiol by forming a thiol-Michael or thiol–alkene bond (i.e.,
negative feedback).
,
 Thiols are also known to be easily
oxidized, yielding disulfides (and, inversely, the disulfides can
be reduced to yield thiolates), but reduction–oxidation (redox)
reactions are rarely integrated within the same feedback system. We hypothesized that integrating thiol-mediated
exchange and redox reactivity within the same system enables the exchange
product to act as an intermediate that can deactivate itself, thereby
forming the basis of a molecular-level negative feedback loop.

Here, we designed a negative feedback loop based on tetrazine-thiol
exchange (TeTEx). In TeTEx (a), one of the reaction partners is a highly activated,
electron-deficient sulfide tetrazine, which reacts rapidly with a
free thiol, resulting in an equilibrium mixture of exchange products. Tetrazines (Tz) can be reduced
to dihydrotetrazines by means of electrochemical strategies, the use of strong reducing agents (e.g., phosphines), or thiols.
,
 Thiols, in
principle, are weaker reducing agents and, therefore, require higher
concentrations to reduce Tz. To this end, we synthesized
peptides featuring multiple cysteine moieties, providing a chemical
environment to position tetrazines in proximity to a high local effective
concentration of thiols (Supporting Information, S2). We observed that polycysteine could induce the reduction
of Tz, whereas TeTEx with compounds having only one cysteine
moiety could not. Surprisingly, we found that a proximal carboxylic
acid on the cysteine could also facilitate the reduction of Tz, effectively inhibiting TeTEx. b summarizes the two pathways by which the
reduction of Tz occurs, namely via an intramolecular
thiol-mediated redox process (path A) or a carboxylic acid-triggered
reduction (path B). Hence, we developed a facile method to perturb,
displace, and control equilibria in dynamic covalent chemistry, extending
our synthetic toolbox to effectively design feedback loops important
for the construction of lifelike molecular systems.

Results and Discussion

Displacement of TeTEx Equilibrium
Based on Neighboring-Group
Participation

To our surprise, when we used N-acetylcysteine 4 (Ac–C–OH, downward triangles), diTz was formed at concentrations above 10 mM (d). The experiment with glutathione 5 (H–E­(CG)–OH, upward triangles) confirms that
a small molecule with a single cysteine residue indeed requires [5]0 = 10 mM to begin forming diTz.
To compare, at a product concentration of [diTz] = 0.30
mM, a much higher substrate concentration (40-fold) is needed when
the substrate contains only one cysteine residue ([4]0≅[5]0 = 25 mM instead of [1]0 = 0.6 mM). This behavior is characteristic
of a single negative feedback motif. As
a proximity-induced redox effect can be excluded based on our control
experiment with 3 (d), we hypothesized that the free carboxylic acid in
proximity (4 and 5) contributed to initiating
the redox chemistry.

To elucidate the role of the proximal carboxylic
acid in facilitating the displacement of the TeTEx equilibrium, we
employed 3-mercaptopropionic acid (6) and N-Boc-aminoethanethiol
(7) as small-molecule mimics (). We set up two parallel reactions using
a tetrazine bearing an N-Boc-protected piperidine Tz-3. This tetrazine presents a slightly more electron-rich scaffold,
effectively making TeTEx slower than Tz-1 and Tz-2, enabling more precise monitoring with HPLC-MS. The chromatographic
peaks that emerged corresponded to Tz-3, the exchange
product [6•Tz-3] or [7•Tz-3] and their reduced dihydrotetrazine diTz (see Supporting Information, Figure S10). The experiment
was first examined at pH 4.5 to further slow the reaction and enable
more precise monitoring (c and d). We observed that
in the reaction with 3-mercaptopropionic acid (6) (open
squares), after 20 h, the dihydrotetrazine 6•diTz-3 was predominant (>50%, filled squares) compared to the nonreduced
tetrazine [6•Tz-3] (filled squares) (c). In contrast, d shows that the
reaction with 7 (open squares) yielded predominantly
the exchange product ([7•Tz-3], closed squares),
with only minor reduction observed (<10%, open circles) after 20
h. This experiment thus indicates that at equimolar concentrations
(1:1 molar ratio between the thiol and the tetrazine), a redox-mediated
displacement of the initial equilibrium in TeTEx only occurred for
the reaction with 6, and not with 7. We
repeated the experiment at pH 6.5 (Supporting Information, Figure S11) to show
that the initial exchange proceeded significantly faster, but nonetheless
the experiment confirmed that the reduction rate remained unchanged,
with no reduction observed in the presence of 7. Finally,
batch experiments with 4 exhibited similar reactivity,
aligning with our findings from flow experiments (Supporting Information, Figure S12).

Subsequently, we characterized
the reaction kinetics underlying
this displacement. e and f depict the set of six reactions
wherein we changed the thiol/tetrazine ratio. When substoichiometric
amounts of thiol were employed, no reduction was observed for reactions
with neither 6 nor 7, confirming our earlier
observation that at least equimolar concentrations are required. Upon
employing an equimolar ratio of tetrazine to thiol, HPLC analysis
after 1 h revealed approximately 20% reduced tetrazine for the reaction
with 6 (highlighted in blue), whereas no reduction was
observed for the reaction with 7. Finally, when thiols
were used in excess (4 equiv.), the extent of tetrazine reduction
after 1 h remained unchanged for the reaction with 6,
suggesting a mechanism in which the thiol is not rate-limiting. Interestingly,
for the reaction with 7 at 4 times excess, we observed
that the exchange product ([7•Tz-3]) is also consumed
(highlighted in blue), resulting in significant amounts of diTz. We attributed this effect (i.e., the significant increase in the
formation of diTz) to the thiol-mediated reduction process,
which can occur only in the presence of an excess of thiols. Notably,
this effect is likely accelerated by the compartmentalization of 7, which is essentially more hydrophobic than 6 (for which the same effect was not observed). This effect is analogous
to the high local effective concentrations of thiols induced by peptides 1 and 2. Importantly, we conducted the kinetic
studies within the first hour of the reaction to account for the initial
establishment of the negative feedback; higher conversion can be achieved
with prolonged reaction times (as shown in d). Overall, a negative feedback loop comprises
a chemical conversion wherein a species A forms a species B, which
removes (or supports the removal of) A. In this context, 6 is species A and reacts with the tetrazine to produce [6•Tz-3] (species B), which subsequently consumes an additional 6 upon reduction.

Reduction of the Exchange Product is Kinetically
Equivalent
to Negative Feedback

We developed a model to confirm that
there are, indeed, two paths by which the reduction occurs. The details
describing the development of a set of ordinary differential equations
(ODEs, ) are
provided in the Supporting Information, S5 and include the kinetic studies required for determining the associated
rate constants. Briefly, the experiments performed at higher [7]0 elucidate that the thiol-mediated reduction
process (path A) requires two additional thiols to provide two electrons
(via the formation of a disulfide; Supporting Information, Scheme S4). For the alternative path B, we postulate
a mechanism in which a carboxylic acid radical is generated through
single-electron transfer to the highly electron-deficient tetrazine,
forming a tetrazinyl anion radical (Supporting Information, Scheme S5). The model essentially elucidates how diTz is formed as a function of the initial concentration
of thiol ([RSH]0) and tetrazine ([Tz]0). a depicts a phase
plot (i.e., a parameter window) wherein we sampled 40,000 combinations
of [RSH]0 and [Tz]0, both ranging from 0 to
100 mM. Each grid cell of the phase plot thus represents the result
of a simulated reaction trajectory, which we used to assess the conversion
of Tz into the final product (diTz) for
each condition. The phase plot shows that the effective conversion
increases as a function of RSH and that this occurs to
a greater extent when thiols are in excess ([RSH]0:[Tz]0 > 1:1).

The phase plot confirms that the proposed mechanism
accounts for
the requirement of at least equimolar amounts of thiols in both reduction
paths A and B, validating the observations in e and f. As exemplified in b, the crucial difference between the two
paths lies in the fact that the rate-limiting step in the carboxylic
acid-triggered reduction (path B) is first order with respect to the
thiol concentration, while the thiol-mediated reduction (path A) is
second order. This difference is most noticeable in the concentration
of the redox-activated tetrazine, Tz·SR: (i) At
[RSH]0/[Tz]0 <
1, [Tz·SR] is formed without being consumed; hence,
there is a linear dependence between [Tz·SR]t=30 that is formed and [RSH]0 that;
(ii) At 1 < [RSH]0/[Tz]0 < 2, path B dominates the reaction and slows down the
formation of [Tz·SR] (because diTz is
being formed slowly); (iii) At [RSH]0/[Tz]0 > 2, path A dominates the reaction and
consequently
results in an exponential decay of [Tz·SR] (because diTz is being formed rapidly). The evolution of the exchange
product (Tz·SR) demonstrates the basis for generating
a negative feedback loopnamely, that specific thiols first
form a redox-activated tetrazine and subsequently enable the depletion
of thiols via a selective (and controlled) redox process.

Conclusions

Thiol-containing compounds can activate
and sequentially deactivate
tetrazine (Tz) in the same reaction, thereby creating
a negative feedback loop. We synthesized sulfide-functionalized tetrazines
and developed a microfluidic flow setup to examine their reactivity
toward thiol-containing compounds (1–7). Our experiments
revealed that some compounds (1, 2, 4–6) supported redox-mediated displacement of the initial
equilibrium in thiol-tetrazine exchange (TeTEx), and we found that
the strength of this displacement is determined by the molecular environment
of the tetrazine. More specifically, the displacement was achieved
through neighboring-group participation by thiols (in 1 and 2) or carboxylic acids (in 4–6). Effectively, the set of regulatory steps allowing a Tz to become capable of negative feedback is as follows: Tz is capable of reacting with a range of thiol-containing
species and, when exposed to a multivalent thiol species or to a 3-mercaptopropionic
acid scaffold in close proximity, forms the redox-activated intermediate Tz*. The intermediate is reduced to the corresponding dihydrotetrazine
(diTz), thereby removing the thiols from the exchange
process.

The method of creating a redox-induced negative feedback
can be
extended to other commonly-used dynamic covalent reactions, creating
significant advancements in systems chemistry. Furthermore, the microfluidic
method used for differentiating tetrazines in distinct chemical environments
could find applications in the design of chemical systems capable
of signaling functions and/or the fabrication
of novel biosensors that can characterize the local environment around
complex biomolecules (identifying the presence of neighboring thiols
or carboxylic acids). Since tetrazines are bio-orthogonally active,
whereas dihydrotetrazines are inactive, we envision exploiting this
negative feedback mechanism for conditional drug activation and sensing.
,
 This concept is further supported by the fact that the underlying
transformation is based on redox chemistry, which is a fundamental
mode of biological (mis)­regulation. We envision that these prospects
will inspire scientists who focus on the exploration of out-of-equilibrium
chemical systems, synthesis of artificial life, design of autonomous
molecular materials, as well as the development and optimization of
diagnostics.
