Nicotinic Acid Restriction Enhances the Therapeutic Benefit of NAMPT Inhibition in Small‐Cell Lung Cancer Models
Authors: Kyoji Tsurumi, Miyuki Nomura, Mai Ouchi, Shigehiro Yagishita, Naohiko Kikuchi, Taku Sato, Mami Morita, Yoji Yamashita, Kayoko Hayashi, Tomoyoshi Soga, Akinobu Hamada, Tatsuro Fukuhara, Nobuhiro Tanuma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSmall‐cell lung cancer (SCLC) is an aggressive malignancy with limited therapeutic options. We previously showed that nicotinic acid riboside (NAR) sustains NAD biosynthesis in vivo and compensates for NAMPT inhibition in SCLC models. Here, we evaluated combining NAMPT inhibition with dietary nicotinic acid (NA) restriction to suppress NAR‐dependent NAD biosynthesis. This combination showed enhanced antitumor activity consistent with synthetic lethality in SCLC CDX models and showed robust efficacy in PDX models, including those derived from chemotherapy‐refractory tumors. In most CDX models examined, combination therapy showed greater antitumor activity than standard chemotherapy with cisplatin and etoposide. Although we did observe transient leukopenia, the treatment was otherwise better tolerated than chemotherapy, with less impact on body weight and platelet counts. These findings support NAD metabolism–targeted therapy as a promising strategy to treat SCLC.

Introduction

Small cell lung cancer (SCLC) is a highly aggressive malignancy characterized by rapid tumor cell proliferation and early metastasis and is associated with a poor prognosis [1, 2, 3]. Moreover, most patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage, making surgery no longer feasible, underscoring the need for systemic therapies. Although the initial response rate to platinum‐based chemotherapy is high, most patients rapidly develop therapy resistance. While immune checkpoint blockade has recently been incorporated to chemotherapy regimens, only a subset of patients derives clinical benefit [4, 5, 6]. Unlike cancers, where substantial progress has been made in developing targeted therapies, advances in SCLC have been limited, due in part to the relative paucity of actionable driver mutations in SCLC [1, 2]. Thus, there is a strong need to develop therapeutic strategies targeting vulnerabilities beyond driver mutations, including metabolic dependencies in this disease.

Cancer cells exhibit a wide range of metabolic characteristics [7, 8, 9] and can become highly dependent on specific metabolites, nutrients, or metabolic pathways that are potentially targetable. For example, we recently found that neuroendocrine carcinomas (NECs), including SCLC, have high dependence on NAD biosynthesis [10]. NAD, a metabolic cofactor, serves as a redox carrier to support energy production and biomass synthesis [11]. NAD also serves as a precursor for NADPH synthesis and as a substrate for NAD‐consuming enzymes, including PARPs and sirtuins [11, 12]. These non‐redox, NAD‐consuming processes reduce the NAD pool, necessitating continuous NAD biosynthesis to maintain essential cellular functions, including cell proliferation.

In most cancers, the NAD salvage pathway represents the major route for NAD biosynthesis. Notably, SCLC is particularly dependent on this pathway, and inhibition of NAMPT, the rate‐limiting enzyme in the pathway, induces robust cell death in vitro [10]. We also identified a non‐canonical NAD biosynthetic pathway in SCLC that operates specifically in vivo and weakens the effects of NAMPT inhibition in animal models of the disease. Specifically, we found that SCLC cells can utilize nicotinic acid riboside (NAR), a nicotinic acid (NA) derivative not found in standard cell culture media, to support NAMPT‐independent NAD biosynthesis (Figure 1A) [10, 13]. We further demonstrated that this pathway can be suppressed in vivo by restricting dietary NA, a precursor for NAR biosynthesis. In mouse models, NA restriction markedly enhanced the antitumor efficacy of NAMPT inhibition in SCLC [10]. Collectively, these findings highlight NAD biosynthesis as a promising therapeutic target in NECs, including SCLC. However, whether this strategy would provide a therapeutic benefit in clinically relevant settings remains to be established.

In this study, we demonstrate that combining NAMPT inhibition with suppression of NAR metabolism via NA restriction induces synthetic lethality in vivo against SCLC and validate its efficacy in patient‐derived xenograft (PDX) models. Furthermore, we compare this therapeutic strategy with standard chemotherapy, including an assessment of associated toxicities.

Materials and Methods

Reagents

GNE‐617 was purchased from MedChemExpress (Monmouth Junction, NJ). Cisplatin (CDDP) and etoposide were purchased from Fujifilm Wako (Osaka, Japan). Acetaminophen (AAP) was obtained from Tokyo Chemical Industry (Tokyo, Japan). Anti KI‐67 antibody was purchased from Roche (Basel, Switzerland).

SCLC and NSCLC Cell Lines

Human SCLC cell lines (H209, Lu‐139, 87–5, DMS79, MS‐1, T3M‐12, and Lu134A) and NSCLC cell lines (A549 and RERF‐LCKJ) were obtained from RIKEN Bioresource Center (Tsukuba, Japan), JCRB Cell Bank (Tokyo, Japan), or ATCC (Manassas, VA). All cell lines were cultured in RPMI‐1640 supplemented with 10% FCS at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. All lines were verified as mycoplasma‐free using a MycoAlert kit (Lonza, Basel, Switzerland). Cell line authentication tests were not performed.

SCLC PDX Lines

Small‐cell lung cancer (SCLC) patient‐derived xenograft (PDX) lines were obtained from the J‐PDX library [14]. Establishment of PDX models was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the National Cancer Center (NCC), and written informed consent was obtained from all patients. Use of these PDX models here was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of National Cancer Center (#2022‐227) and Miyagi Cancer Center (#2022‐36). The following PDX lines were used: #1, a chemotherapy‐treated SCLC from liver metastasis; #2, a chemotherapy‐treated combined SCLC from a lung metastatic lesion; #3, a treatment‐naïve SCLC derived from lymph node metastasis; and #4, a treatment‐naïve combined SCLC from a primary lung tumor. Histopathological diagnoses were confirmed prior to experimental use.

Whole‐Exome Sequencing, Including Mutation Calls, and Copy Number Analysis

Whole‐exome sequencing (WES), mutation calling, and copy‐number analysis were performed as previously described [15]. Briefly, libraries were prepared using the Twist Comprehensive Exome Kit (Twist Bioscience) and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Reads were aligned to the combined human (hg38) and mouse (mm10) reference genomes using Burrows–Wheeler Aligner. Somatic mutations were called using our in‐house pipeline together with MuTect2 and VarScan2, and annotated with SnpEff. Copy‐number alterations were estimated based on log R ratio (LRR) using our in‐house pipeline.

RNAseq Analysis

RNA sequencing (RNA‐seq) was performed as previously described [15]. Libraries were prepared using the NEBNext Ultra Directional RNA Library Prep Kit following poly(A) selection and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Reads were aligned to a combined human (hg38) and mouse (mm10) reference genome using STAR with GENCODE annotations to distinguish human and murine transcripts. Gene expression levels were quantified as transcripts per million (TPM) for human transcripts.

Gene Expression Profiling of SCLC Cell Lines

For qRT‐PCR analyses, total RNAs from H209, Lu139, 87‐5, MS‐1, DMS79, Lu134A, A549, and RERF‐LCKJ cells were reverse‐transcribed using random primers and SuperScript III RTase (Thermo Fisher). qRT‐PCR analyses were performed using a LightCycler 480 (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). LightCycler 480 probes master and TaqMan probes from a universal probe library (Roche) were used for analyses of CHGA (#17), NCAM (#55), SYP (#13), RN18s (#48), ASCL1 (#88), and YAP (#59) (probe number indicated in parentheses). Primers used are listed in Table S1. Analyses of NEUROD1 and POU2F3 were performed using a TaqMan Gene Expression Assay kit (Thermo Fisher), as indicated in Table S1. Data were normalized to RN18S as internal control (Table S2). To integrate qRT‐PCR data with the DepMap RNA‐seq dataset, expression levels of 87‐5 and MS‐1 were mapped and scaled into the DepMap dataset based on a linear regression model utilizing the 5 overlapping cell lines (H209, DMS79, Lu134A, A549, and RERF‐LCKJ) as common references (Figure S3A).

Mouse Experiments

All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the Miyagi Cancer Center Research Institute and conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines. Mice were maintained under specific pathogen‐free conditions with a 12‐h light–dark cycle at controlled temperature and humidity and were fed a normal diet (MF; Oriental Yeast Co., Tokyo, Japan) ad libitum unless otherwise indicated. The niacin‐free diet (NFD) was prepared by Oriental Yeast Co. as described previously [10]. GNE‐617 was formulated at 3 mg/mL and administered daily by oral gavage (0.6 mg per mouse) for four consecutive days. For combination therapy, mice were switched to an NFD immediately after the first dose and returned to the normal diet after 1 week. Chemotherapy consisted of CDDP (5 mg/kg, i.p.) on Day 1 and etoposide (10 mg/kg, i.p.) on Day 2, repeated weekly for 4–6 cycles depending on tumor growth and tolerability.

Establishment of CDX and PDX Models

For the cell line‐derived xenograft (CDX) models 87‐5, H209, Lu139, MS‐1, DMS79, or T3M‐12 cells, we mixed cells (2 × 10^6 for H209, and 1 × 10^6 for others) with Matrigel (Corning, NY) and injected them subcutaneously into female NOG mice (6–8 weeks old). For PDX models, cryopreserved tumors were implanted subcutaneously into 8–10‐week‐old NOG mice and expanded by serial passaging as described [14]. Tumor fragments (2–4 mm3, 2–3 per site) were implanted with Matrigel. Tumor size was measured using calipers, and volume was calculated as (length × width2)/2. When tumors reached approximately 100 mm3, mice were allocated to treatment groups that showed balanced baseline tumor volumes. PDX tumors at passages 3–5 were used for experiments.

Histological Analysis

Tumor and normal tissues were excised, fixed, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned using standard procedures. HE‐staining was performed using conventional methods. Immunohistochemical staining for Ki‐67 was performed using a Ventana instrument and reagents (Roche). Areas with necrotic morphology were identified on HE‐stained sections based on morphological criteria, including disruption of tissue architecture, loss of cellularity, and accumulation of cellular debris, by comparison with viable tumor regions in untreated control tumors. Necrotic areas and the Ki‐67 labeling index were quantified using Hybrid Cell Count software (KEYENCE, Osaka, Japan). The viable tumor area was expressed as a percentage of the total tumor area, calculated by subtracting the necrotic area from the total tumor area.

NAD Measurement in Non‐Tumor Tissues

Female C57BL/6 mice (10 weeks old; obtained from CLEA Japan (Tokyo, Japan)) were treated with GNE‐617, with or without the NFD. Tissues were collected at 27 h after the final dose of GNE‐617. NAD levels were determined by CE‐MS methods as described [16, 17].

Toxicity Assessment

Female C57BL/6 mice (8–10 weeks old; from CLEA, Japan) were treated with GNE‐617 alone and fed a normal diet, GNE‐617 in the presence of a NFD, or chemotherapy and fed a normal diet. Control mice were fed a normal diet and not drug‐treated. In most experiments, mice in the combination group were switched to the NFD 1 day prior to GNE‐617 treatment and maintained on that diet thereafter. For body weight analysis (Figure 4A), however, NFD was initiated concurrently with drug administration, consistent with the CDX/PDX experiments. On the day following the final dose, whole blood was collected into EDTA‐containing tubes for complete blood counts, and plasma was used for biochemical analyses. Complete blood counts were performed using an automated hematology analyzer (Sysmex, Kobe, Japan). Alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) activities were determined using an ALT activity assay kit and an AST activity assay kit (both from Cayman, Ann Arbor, MI). Blood urea nitrogen levels were measured using a BUN colorimetric detection kit (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA). Creatinine levels were measured using a CRE assay kit (FUJIFILM Wako). Levels of creatine kinase MM isoenzyme and skeletal muscle Troponin I were determined by ELISA using a mouse CK‐MM ELISA Kit (Cusabio, Houston, TX) and a mouse TnI ELISA kit (Abcam, Cambridge, UK), respectively. All plasma assays were conducted according to the manufacturers' instructions. For the selected parameters, plasma samples obtained from an AAP‐induced liver injury model and a high‐dose CDDP‐induced muscle injury model were used as positive controls. AAP (1.24 g/kg, p.o.) and CDDP (20 mg/kg, i.p.) were administered as described [18, 19]. Blood glucose levels were measured in tail vein blood using a GlucoCard system (“Oshiete Guruko”; FORA Care Japan, Tokyo, Japan) and compatible test strips.

Statistics and Reproducibility

No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample size. No data were excluded from analyses. Experiments were not randomized, and investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment. Student's t‐test (two‐tailed) and a one‐way ANOVA followed by a post hoc test were used when comparing two groups and multiple groups, respectively. A p value of < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Combining NAMPT Inhibition With NA Restriction Promotes Synthetic Lethality in SCLC Xenograft Models

Previously, we reported that dietary NA restriction lowers circulating NAR levels and significantly enhances antitumor efficacy of NAMPT inhibitors (NAMPTi) in SCLC xenograft mouse models [10]. Importantly, that analysis showed that NAD biosynthesis from NAR was NAMPT‐independent, and that NA restriction per se did not affect SCLC xenograft growth [10]. Together, these observations suggest that combining NAMPTi with NA restriction results in synthetic lethality in tumor cells. To test this hypothesis, we treated mice bearing 87‐5 SCLC xenografts with the NAMPTi GNE‐617, with or without NA restriction (achieved by feeding a NFD) (Figure 1A) and analyzed tumor tissues. Administration of GNE‐617 alone modestly decreased the Ki‐67 proliferation index in cells from those tissues (Figure 1B,C and Figure S1A) but had minimal impact on viable tumor areas (Figure 1B,D and Figure S1A). In contrast, combining dietary NA restriction with GNE‐617 treatment significantly decreased the Ki‐67 index relative to that seen in tumors treated with GNE‐617 but fed a normal diet (Figure 1B,C and Figure S1A). Moreover, in GNE‐617‐treated mice fed an NFD, necrosis‐like cell death was markedly increased compared with GNE treatment alone (Figure 1B and Figure S1A). Quantification of the 87‐5 model revealed that a significantly larger fraction of the total tumor area exhibited necrotic morphology in the combo group, resulting in a corresponding reduction in the percentage of viable tumor area (Figure 1D). We observed similar results in a CDX model using T3M‐12 cells (Figure S1B–D). These findings indicate overall that NAMPT inhibition plus NA restriction synergize to induce synthetic lethality in SCLC tumors.

Efficacy of NAMPT Inhibition Combined With NA Restriction in SCLC PDX Models

Next, we evaluated the efficacy of combining a NAMPTi with a NFD in PDX models using four independent SCLC PDX lines. Based on expression of key transcription factors, SCLC can be classified into four subtypes: SCLC‐A (ASCL1), SCLC‐N (NEUROD1), SCLC‐P (POU2F3), and SCLC‐Y (YAP1) [20, 21]. Among these, SCLC‐A is the most prevalent, followed by SCLC‐N, while SCLC‐P and SCLC‐Y are less common [20, 21, 22]. The A, N, and P subtypes generally retain NE characteristics, while the Y subtype exhibits a low‐ or non‐NE phenotype. Gene expression profiling indicated that all four lines exhibited a neuroendocrine phenotype, and transcription factor expression profiling indicated that three were of the SCLC‐A subtype (#1‐#3), while one displayed mixed SCLC‐A/SCLC‐N features (#4) [20, 21, 22] (Figure 2A and Figure S2A). Two lines (#1 and #2) were derived from chemotherapy‐refractory cases (see Methods for details). Compared with vehicle‐treated controls, GNE‐617 in combination with an NFD significantly suppressed tumor growth across all PDX models. (Figure 2B and Figure S2B). Notably, tumor regression was observed in all PDX models, with the majority of tumors showing substantial volume reduction following treatment initiation (Figure S2C). In the two models (#1 and #2) in which a NAMPTi‐alone group was included, NA restriction significantly reduced tumor burden relative to mice treated with NAMPTi alone, (Figure 2B). These results indicate that combining NAMPTi with NA restriction is effective against SCLC in PDX models, including those derived from chemotherapy‐refractory models.

Comparison of Therapy Targeting NAD Metabolism With Standard Chemotherapy

We then compared the therapeutic efficacy against SCLC of combining the NAMPTi GNE‐617 with an NFD versus the effects of standard chemotherapy in CDX models. Subtypes of CDX models assessed here were determined based on transcription factor and NE marker expression profiles (Figure S3A), as shown in Figure 3A. That analysis suggested that H209, Lu‐139, 87‐5, and DMS79 show characteristics consistent with the SCLC‐A subtype, whereas MS‐1 shows properties of the SCLC‐N subtype. For the chemotherapy group, we fed mice a normal diet and used a regimen of cisplatin (CDDP) with etoposide (ETP) (Figure 3B), based on previous studies [23, 24]. Among five CDX models tested, four (H209, 87‐5, MS‐1, and DMS79) exhibited significantly greater tumor growth suppression with GNE‐617 in combination with an NFD than with chemotherapy (Figure 3C and Figure S3B), whereas the Lu‐139 model showed comparable responses to both treatments. All tumors treated with combined therapy showed ≥ 30% reduction in tumor volume from baseline (defined as tumor volume at treatment initiation), whereas only 12 of 50 tumors treated with chemotherapy met this response criterion (Figure 3D). Furthermore, in the 87‐5 and MS‐1 models, tumors regrew after the first cycle of GNE‐617 treatment combined with NA restriction; however, a second cycle again decreased tumor volume (Figure 3C and Figure S3B). These results suggest that our NAMPTi/NFD strategy provides enhanced antitumor activity relative to standard chemotherapy in most SCLC models tested.

Toxicity and Tolerability of NAD Metabolism–Targeted Therapy in Mice

Finally, we assessed potential adverse effects associated with both treatments. Mice subjected to repeated cycles of chemotherapy showed decreased body weight relative to untreated control mice (Figure 4A). By contrast, mice treated with GNE‐617 and fed a NFD showed body weight comparable to untreated control mice fed a normal diet (Figure 4A). We then measured NAD levels in tissues from mice treated with GNE‐617 with or without NA restriction, compared with those in vehicle‐treated controls. Gastrocnemius muscle (GM) showed decreased NAD levels when mice were treated with GNE‐617 alone (GNE), whereas NAD levels in brain, heart, liver, and kidney were comparable to controls (Figure 4B and Figure S4A). In addition to GM, we observed modestly decreased NAD levels relative to controls after GNE‐617/NFD (Combo) treatment in heart, liver, and kidney, while effects on brain NAD levels were minimal (Figure 4B and Figure S4A). However, we did not observe significant increases in liver enzymes (ALT and AST), renal function markers (BUN and CRE), or muscle injury markers (CK‐MM and Trop‐I) in plasma of mice treated with GNE‐617/NFD, indicating minimal adverse effects on those tissues (Figure 4C and Figure S4B). Hematologic analysis showed no thrombocytopenia following GNE‐617/NFD treatment (Combo), in contrast to chemotherapy (CDDP + ETP; Chemo) (Figure 4D). Leukopenia was more pronounced with Combo therapy than with chemotherapy, but substantial recovery was observed by day 18 (Figure 4D). Finally, blood glucose levels were not significantly affected by GNE‐617 treatment, either with or without NA restriction, relative to control (Figure S4C). Overall, we found the combination therapy well tolerated and exhibited a favorable safety profile in mice.

Discussion

In this study, we provide evidence that NAMPT inhibition in the presence of dietary NA restriction induces synthetic lethality in SCLC, as we proposed previously [10]. We further show that this combination strategy is effective in SCLC PDX models, including those derived from chemotherapy‐refractory cases, and overall results in superior antitumor activity relative to standard chemotherapy in CDX models.

Previously, using various NEC xenograft models, we reported that NAMPT inhibition under dietary NA restriction synergistically reduced NAD levels in transplanted tumors and enhanced the therapeutic efficacy of NAMPT inhibition [10]. Here, we confirm that this treatment approach induces potent synthetic lethality in vivo and establish a mechanistic basis for this activity. Dietary NA restriction converts the effect of NAMPT inhibition from cytostatic to cytotoxic in SCLC in vivo (Figure 5). Notably, combining NAMPT inhibition with NA restriction showed therapeutic efficacy across all SCLC PDX and CDX models tested, suggesting broad therapeutic applicability in SCLC. One limitation of the present study is that cell death in tumor tissues was assessed primarily by histological morphology rather than complementary molecular markers.

Dietary NA restriction converts the effect of NAMPT inhibition from cytostatic to cytotoxic in SCLC in vivo. The schematic in the upper panel and the waterfall plot in the lower panel are adapted from Figures 1A and 3D, respectively.

The antitumor activity we observed across models is consistent with our previous finding that tumor cell NE differentiation is associated with high NAMPT dependence [10, 25]. In this context, non‐NE tumors, including SCLC‐Y, may be less sensitive than NE‐high subtypes, although the current number of models tested is insufficient to confirm subtype‐specific responses. Thus, although most SCLCs exhibit an NE phenotype, NE status may be relevant for patient stratification in potential clinical applications of our findings. Conversely, our findings suggest that combining NAMPT inhibition and NA restriction may exert antitumor activity beyond lung cancer, potentially extending to NECs of other organs [26, 27, 28], as we suggested previously [10]. Moreover, in at least some cancers, tumors can acquire resistance to targeted therapies via transdifferentiation into a NE lineage [29, 30, 31], which could increase their vulnerability to therapeutic strategies proposed here.

In toxicity analysis, we found that in our models, NAMPT inhibition with GEN‐617 induced transient leukopenia, a condition exacerbated by NA restriction but ameliorated within 2 weeks (Figure 4 and Tsurumi et al., unpublished data). Jones et al. have reported that AML leukemic stem cells (LSCs) are vulnerable to NAMPT inhibition, whereas normal hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) are not [32], an observation consistent with the transient nature of leukopenia that we observed. Overall, these findings suggest that early hematopoietic progenitors, such as multipotent progenitors (MPPs) and common myeloid progenitors (CMPs), may be more susceptible to NAMPT inhibition than HSCs, warranting further investigation of these possibilities. On the other hand, we found that NAMPT inhibition under NA restriction reduced NAD levels in various normal tissues, although those decreases were much less than those reported in tumor cells [10]. Significantly, in our study, liver and renal function of our model mice after treatment remained largely normal.

Chemotherapy combined with immune checkpoint blockade is becoming a standard SCLC treatment [4, 5]. Here, we show that in our experimental context, combining NAMPT inhibition with dietary NA restriction has greater antitumor activity than chemotherapy. Thus, future studies should investigate how targeting NAD metabolism affects responses to immune checkpoint blockade. In this context, recent studies indicate that NAD is a key metabolite for proper immune function in many cell types [33, 34, 35, 36, 37]. Paradoxically, inhibition of PARPs, which utilize NAD as a substrate, reportedly enhances antitumor immunity [38]. These findings highlight the complex, context‐dependent roles of NAD metabolism in antitumor immunity. Finally, for potential clinical application, pharmacological targeting of NAR metabolism in tumor cells, for example by inhibiting NMRK1/2, which directly metabolize NAR [39, 40], may represent a feasible alternative to dietary NA restriction.

In summary, we established that NAMPT inhibition under dietary NA restriction induces synthetic lethality in SCLC. Our findings may represent an important stepping stone toward clinical translation of NAD metabolism‐targeted therapy in NECs, including SCLC.
