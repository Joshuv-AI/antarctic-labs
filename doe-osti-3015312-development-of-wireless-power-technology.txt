Executive Summary
Electrochromic (EC) windows, often referred to as smart windows, have the ability to adjust their tint in
response to electrical signals, thereby controlling the amount of sunlight and heat entering a building. This
dynamic modulation can lead to significant energy savings by reducing the reliance on artificial lighting and
minimizing the load on heating, ventilation, and air conditioning (HVAC) systems.
However, the widespread adoption of electrochromic window technology, especially in existing buildings, faces
challenges, particularly concerning the installation cost associated with the need for electrical wiring to power
and control the EC glass (tinting).
In this project, GuRu Wireless demonstrated that it’s mmWave-based wireless power energy transfer technology
can provide the necessary power for operation of electrochromic windows, wirelessly, and address the wiring
challenges of electrochromic windows in retrofit applications.

Background
GuRu Wireless, Inc., a Venture Capital backed for profit Small Business, is the leading provider of adaptive, long-
range wireless energy transfer solutions. The company’s proprietary 24GHz millimeter-wave technology with
GuRu RF Lensing® enables precise and targeted transmission and recovery of milliwatts to kilowatts of power
over significant distances with unmatched performance. Its small, lightweight, tile-able modules, incorporating a
highly integrated custom ASIC, intelligent algorithms and custom software, can be scaled for specific
applications, resulting in solutions that are best-in-class in terms of power/distance and form factor. Founded in
2017 by a team of scientists and engineers from Caltech, the company has successfully completed several proof-
of-concept (PoC) projects with leading customers in industrial, commercial and defense applications.
The concept of RF lensing that enables efficient wireless energy transmission is shown in Figure 1. At the
generator unit (GU), an array of antennas are driven with phased locked sources to create a converging RF wave
that focuses the RF energy on the receiver or Recovery Unit (RU). The RU incorporates an array of rectifying
antennas (rectennas) that each capture and convert the incident RF energy to DC power. The GU electronically
adjusts the focal point of the RF beam via antenna element phase control and maintains efficient energy
transfer.

lends itself to scaling the size and power of transmitter and receiver. Each GU and RU module incorporates

The company received Small Business Innovation Research (SBIR) grant from the U.S. Department of Energy’s
(DOE) Building Technologies Office (BTO) to fund research on integrating wireless power with electrochromic
windows to make energy-efficient building upgrades more accessible and economically viable for a wide range
of commercial and government buildings. It is anticipated that by using GuRu Wireless’ technology, the cost to
retrofit electrochromic windows in existing commercial buildings will be up to 90% lower than current hard-
wired solutions.
 Electrochromic (EC) windows are able to change their visible and/or solar transmittance based on an applied
electrical potential. Several EC window technologies, including transition metal films, organic films, suspended
particle devices, and liquid crystal devices, have been introduced into the market. Within this range of EC
technologies, transition metal film products have become the most prevalent in the architectural market and
are responsible for the bulk of EC windows installed in buildings.

EC windows were identified as a potentially energy-saving technology already in the 1970s (Lampert 1980) and
1980s (Selkowitz and Lampert 1989), as the ability to exert dynamic control over glazing properties was deemed
important for actively managing solar heat gains and daylight admission to a space. In the 21st century, full scale
studies in the laboratory (Lee et al. 2006) and in the field (Lee et al., 2000, Lee et al. 2016, Fernandes et al. 2018)
have demonstrated the performance of EC windows regarding energy use and building occupant comfort.
Measured benefits to south-facing perimeter zones of buildings include reductions in daily and peak HVAC loads
by at least 29% and 25%, respectively (Fernandes et al. 2018). Glare reduction is a key occupant comfort benefit,
although it requires adequate balancing with the need for daylight admission (Lee et al., 2000, Fernandes et al.
2018). These experimental studies have been supplemented by simulation studies documenting the potential
larger-scale benefits (DeForest et al. 2013, Gehbauer et al. 2023, Sellers et al. 2025, Fernandes et al. 2025) of EC
windows in buildings, including HVAC and lighting energy savings, glare control, and enhancing the ability of the
building to respond to changing grid conditions and energy prices. Gehbauer et al. (2023) showed that demand-

response-enabled EC windows can deliver 4.4 W/m2-floor peak demand savings for the median building while
meeting visual and thermal comfort requirements. Fernandes et al. (2025) show that EC window retrofits can
reduce energy use by 7.2% in the median commercial building, with savings as high as 28% for some buildings,
with an estimated 0.25 quads of site energy savings across the United States.

Despite the demonstrated performance potential, EC windows still represent a small part of the window market
in the United States. One reason for that is that installing EC windows requires wiring to power and control the
windows. This is of particular concern when retrofitting existing buildings, where the installation of wiring
through existing infrastructure (including window frames) is particularly disruptive and costly. While mature
wireless communications options exist that could conceivably be deployed for controlling windows, this is not
the case for the delivery of power.

GuRu Wireless’ innovative wireless power transfer (WPT) technology can streamline the installation process and
significantly reduce the cost of integrating these windows by eliminating the need for hardwired installations.
GuRu Wireless used the Phase I SBIR award to develop and customize its wireless power beaming system for a
proof-of-concept deployment in a dedicated government laboratory installation. The successful completion of
Phase I formed the foundation for development of a commercial-grade solution.

Project Objectives
The goal of this SBIR Phase I project was to evaluate the feasibility and effectiveness of wireless power transfer
across room for electrochromic (EC) window applications. Initially, the project aimed to develop and integrate a
custom-built GuRu wireless power transfer system to energize two sets of EC window panes—out of a total of
eight—installed at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory’s (LBNL) Building Technologies Department.
However, during the implementation phase, it was determined that the existing EC window controllers could
not accommodate a separate power input for the two planned sets. Consequently, the project scope was
revised to develop a higher-capacity wireless power system capable of wirelessly powering all eight sets,
covering a total window area of 140 square feet.
The technical objectives of the project was defined as below:
 • Development of a Custom Wireless Power System
 Design and build a GuRu wireless power transfer system specifically for EC window applications.
 • Characterization of EC Window Performance Using Wireless vs. Conventional Powering Methods
 o Transition Speed: Assess the response time of EC windows under wireless and wired power
 sources.
 o Reliability & Consistency: Conduct preliminary tests on operational stability and performance
 over time.
 o Energy Consumption: Measure total energy usage, including daily operation and switching
 cycles.
 • Analysis of Transmitter Positioning Effects
 Evaluate how transmitter placement impacts the efficiency, reliability, and performance of wirelessly
 powered EC windows.
This Phase I study lays the groundwork for wireless power solutions for EC windows, addressing installation
challenges associated with wired connections while advancing energy-efficient smart building technologies.

Description of Activities Performed
The technical activities performed for the purpose of the project can be split into the following stages:

Identify WPT System Requirements
The required power to be delivered and the distance between the transmitter and receiver are two key input
parameters needed for designing the WPT systems. The GuRu Wireless team visited LBNL Advanced Windows
Testbed (AWT) facility to assess these requirements.

The Advanced Windows Testbed (Figure 3) enables investigations of system-level interactions between
innovative façade systems and impacted lighting and HVAC systems using outdoor tests in three full-scale, side-
by-side instrumented test chambers. Each chamber is thermally isolated so that window heat flow
measurements can be made on a comparative basis. The chambers are designed to emulate typical private
offices so that daylighting, comfort, and human factors studies can be conducted as well. The testbed is located
at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory site in Berkeley, California.

The testbed is equipped with a large variety of sensors to thoroughly evaluate window and attachment
performance. This includes:
 • Electric power metering: HVAC, lighting, and equipment at device level.
 • Net window heat flow measurement: chilled water flow and inlet/ outlet temperature, electric
 resistance heating, indoor temperature control to 1°C.
 • Photometric capture throughout room and timelapsed HDR imaging to assess workplane illuminance
 and glare.
 • Environmental conditions: direct normal irradiance, global irradiance, wind speed and direction,
 temperature.
 • Robust data acquisition system, remote data access, can integrate experiment specific instrumentation

Figure 4 shows the layout the AWT facility, which consists of three south-facing rooms, each with an identical
9.8ft x 15.1ft configuration. Two of these rooms are equipped with EC glass windows while the third serves as a
reference room with standard low-E glass windows. The EC window controller is located in the middle room and
manages the tint level of EC windows of both adjacent rooms based on the outdoor and ambient conditions.
The initial project goal was to wirelessly power half of the windows in one room and compare their performance
against the other half which would remain powered by wired connection. However, during the site visit it
became clear that the EC windows controller has only a single power input, making it impractical to separate
and independently power a subset of windows. As a result, the WPT system needed to be scaled up beyond the
original proposal to support wireless power delivery to all EC windows in both rooms.
The power consumption of electrochromic (EC) glass varies throughout the day, influenced by both tint level
adjustments and transition activity, rather than remaining constant. Key parameters impacting the wireless
power transfer (WPT) system design include input voltage, peak power consumption, and maximum daily energy
consumption.
To assess these parameters, measurements were conducted over a full day using a Fluke 287 Cal True-RMS
Electronics Logging Multimeter. The EC controller operates at 24V, and the results, shown in Figure 5, indicate:
 o A peak power consumption of 24.1W during each of its eleven transitions
 o A steady-state consumption of 6W when idle
The total daily energy consumption recorded was 175Wh, comprising:
 o 29Wh from switching events
 o 146Wh from idle-state consumption
Based on historical data from LBNL’s Advanced Windows Testbed (AWT) and measured energy per transition,
the maximum projected daily energy consumption is estimated at 198Wh.

System Design

The schematic of the system designed to wirelessly power EC windows is shown in Figure 6. The setup integrates
the existing SageGlass EC window controller already installed at LBNL with a custom-built GuRu Wireless power
transfer (WPT) system.

To demonstrate that GuRu’s WPT system can serve as a drop-in replacement for existing EC window
installations, no modifications were made to the SageGlass controller. Instead, the system’s original wall-
plugged 24V DC power adapter was simply replaced with the GuRu wireless power system.

Testing revealed that the SageGlass controller requires input voltage stability within ±1V of the nominal 24V.
This stringent requirement rules out directly powering the system with standard 24V rechargeable batteries
(such as Li-Ion or LiFePO₄), which exhibit greater voltage variation across their state of charge (SOC) cycles.

 Page 6 of 15

To address this, a voltage regulator circuit was added between the battery and the controller to ensure
consistent voltage delivery, regardless of SOC. However, the inclusion of this regulator introduced conversion
losses, reducing overall system efficiency by approximately 10%–14%.

System Build and Testing

 Figure 7. GuRu Wireless WPT system for controlling 140sqft of EC Windows. Left: Receiver unit; Right: Generating unit

Figure 6 illustrates the generating and receiving units of the Wireless Power Transfer (WPT) system developed
for this project. The receiving unit comprises two 4”x4” antenna arrays that convert received RF energy into DC
power. The RU controller manages communication with the transmitter unit to request power, regulates the
current and voltage from the antenna arrays, and oversees the charging of the battery pack. This battery pack
consists of two 5Ah, 20V DeWalt batteries (DCB205), capable of sustaining the Electrochromic (EC) window
driver for over 24 hours. The selected battery capacity ensures continuous operation of the EC windows, even
during potential WPT system maintenance shutdowns.
The Wireless Power Transfer (WPT) system underwent a month-long evaluation at GuRu Wireless's laboratory
under conditions mirroring those at LBNL. A Rigol DL3021A 200W Programmable DC Electronic Load was
employed to emulate the power consumption of the EC window driver, as depicted in Figure 5. System
parameters were fine-tuned to ensure the WPT system could deliver the requisite daily energy within a 10-hour
period.

Installation at LBNL

The testbed was configured with SAGE Electrochromic (EC) glass spanning the whole south facade of two test
chambers. This includes an active electrochromic area of 70 ft2 (6 window zones) per chamber, and 140 ft2 (12
window zones) in total. The windows were controlled by the manufacturer’s control system to optimize cooling
energy consumption while providing glare protection. Both rooms were controlled by the same controller, which
was physically located in one of the test chambers.

 Figure 8: SAGE Electrochromic Controller.

The SAGE controller (Figure 8) consists of two modules: (a) the window driver unit (green outline) which drives
the electrochromic glass through a variety of DC to DC converters, and (b) the window controller (purple outline)
which determines the high-level setpoints based on sensor inputs and control logic. For this test it was assumed
that the window controller (b) could be located anywhere within a building and therefore is not subject to be
powered by the GuRu system. This leaves the window driver unit (a) to be powered by the GuRu wireless power
device. The only connection between the window driver and controller is a communication pathway which does

 Page 9 of 15

not exchange power between the modules. This allows power isolation between the units and conduction of
this experiment with the alternative GuRu wireless power system.

The window driver unit is powered by a separate 24 V DC power supply which allows integration with the GuRu
wireless power system by disconnecting the power output and connecting it to the pre-configured GuRu system.
Power measurements prior to installation of the GuRu system showed a peak demand, when ECs were switched,
of about 1 A (24 W) and idle around 0.25 A (6 W).

 Figure 9. Installation layout and picture of GuRu WPT system powering the EC windows at LBNL

Figure 7 illustrates the installation of the Wireless Power Transfer (WPT) system at LBNL. The test room contains
existing sensors utilized by LBNL for electrochromic (EC) window measurements, resulting in limited space for
positioning the GuRu transmitter with a clear line of sight to the WPT receiver mounted on the aluminum
window frame. Consequently, the Generator Unit (GU) was placed on a table adjacent to the side wall of the
room, at a lower height compared to the Recovery Unit (RU), which is installed 5 feet above the floor. The GuRu
WPT transmitter autonomously detects the receiver and utilizes proprietary GuRu RF Lensing™ technology to
focus and steer the RF energy beam toward the receiver, optimizing power transfer efficiency. This feature
facilitates a smooth installation process without the need for manual alignment procedures.

voltage levels, to evaluate applicability to the system. This initial test revealed that even a fully-charged battery,
with a voltage of approximately 21 V, was not sufficient to power the system. Table 2 shows the actual

A follow-up test was conducted to evaluate allowable voltage supply ranges for the SAGE window driver unit. A
variable DC power supply was used to evaluate supply voltages between 17 to 24 V. The only status indicator of
the proprietary SAGE system are colored status LEDs at each of the driver boards. The following voltage ranges
were determined:

 • LED Red at <18 V, indicating low-voltage and stop of operation.

 • LED Orange between 19 to 22 V, indicating partial operation.

 • LED Green at >22 V, indicating full operation of the system.

It was decided that an additional DC to DC converter is required at the output of the GuRu system to provide a
stabilized 24 V output. A third test was conducted and operation over a period of several hours was monitored
and confirmed to be adequate to reliably power the SAGE system.

The GuRu system was set-up on 2025-01-30 and continuous testing was performed for 20 days, from 2025-01-
31 through 2025-02-19. In this time the SAGE window driver unit was powered solely by the GuRu system.

Results from this operational testing are presented in Figure 10. The power consumption profile of the EC
window controller is illustrated in Figure 10 (A). SAGE peak consumption was up to 26.7 W, and idle at 5.5 W. As
the EC glass draws energy from the battery, the battery voltage decreases until reaching approximately 18V
(equivalent to 40% SOC). At this threshold, the wireless power transfer (WPT) beam activates to recharge the
battery. Charging continues until the battery voltage reaches around 21V (approximately 80% SOC).
Figure 10 (B) displays the battery voltage across charge and discharge cycles during the test, indicating a total of
16 charging cycles. The transmitter's input power and the corresponding DC power recovered by the receiver
are detailed in Figure 10 (C) and (D), respectively.
After the battery reached its target charge level, the RF beam was designed to deactivate. However, due to a
firmware issue, the transmitter failed to enter sleep mode after charging completion. Consequently, this
resulted in elevated transmitter power consumption between 790.0 and 808.0 W of electricity, depicted by the
navy-blue curve instead of the intended gray curve in Figure 8 (C), ultimately more than doubling the
transmitter's energy use during the test period.

Over the 17 day experiment period the GU system delivered over 2.85KWh of energy to the battery, while the
window driver consumed 2.5KWh. The difference represents the power loss in battery and voltage regulator
used to maintain a stable 24V output. The losses can be improved by redesigning the window controller to
directly accept the output from the battery pack.
During the same period, the GU consumed 298KWh. The largest portion of energy consumption resulted from
an issue with deep sleep function of the system. Had the deep sleep feature operated normally, the GU would
have consumed power only during the battery charging periods, reducing its total power consumption to
128KWh.
Another factor that resulted in higher than necessary energy consumption and can be optimized further is the
EC window driver. Out of 2.5KWh energy consumed by the driver is from its standby power consumption of 6W
which can be substantially reduced by introducing smart power management techniques used in battery
operated devices. This can reduce the total energy requirement of the window driver from 2.5KWh to 0.45KWh
which in turn would reduce the battery size required to operate the EC driver and WPT system power
consumption. Furthermore, the transmitter power efficiency can be further improved through use of more
advanced semiconductor processes resulting in lower power consumption of system.

To further investigate the operation of the EC windows with the different systems, the tint behavior under
varying solar conditions is shown in Figure 11. The y-axis shows the global horizontal irradiance (GHI), in Watts
per square meter, and the x-axis shows the binned tint states of an EC window as box-plots for all times where
GHI is greater than zero. The GuRu period, in blue, includes the full testing period, and the Grid period, in
orange, includes data for two weeks in April 2024. The tint of the EC window is darkened in respect to higher
solar irradiation. The actual tint level is determined by a proprietary SAGE algorithm, and may vary based on
time of day, position of the sun, or other influences, hence the variation in Tint 1 and 2 regions between the
different modes of operation. However, one key finding is that for both the GuRu and grid-powered operation,
the window was never in the Clear (i.e., un-tinted) state greater GHI levels, with a threshold of 200 W/m2,
indicating that the system was operating properly to regulate daylight and glare in the room when the sun was
present.

In summary, the observed data indicates that the GuRu system was successfully able to wirelessly power the
SAGE window driver units throughout the testing period.

Conclusions and Recommendations
The WPT system was able to successfully power two sets of full-scale EC windows throughout the three weeks of
the test period. Window tint data indicates that the windows operated in the manner that would be expected
had they been powered via wires, without any noticeable deviations. For future development of wireless-
powered EC windows, it is recommended that power delivery and storage be optimized in order to best take
advantage of the periods during which the windows aren’t switching, in order to minimize power losses. This can
be done, e.g., by enabling the transmitter to sleep whenever power transmission is not needed and by
optimizing the energy consumption of EC energy drivers.

Lessons Learned
The GuRu’s Wireless Power Transfer System can be utilized to power EC Windows. For retrofit applications,
such a system can reduce the cost of adoption for EC Windows. Further development of the WPT system can
improve the cost and efficiency of the WPT system.
