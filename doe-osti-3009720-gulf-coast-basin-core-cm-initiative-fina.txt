 GULF COAST CORE-CM INITIATIVE
Executive Summary
The Bureau of Economic Geology at the University of Texas at Austin (UT-BEG) is leading the Gulf
Coast Carbon Ore, Rare Earth, and Critical Minerals (CORE-CM) Initiative to assess the potential to
produce critical minerals (CMs), including rare earth elements (REEs) from coal, coal ash, and
produced water related to oil and gas production, and related materials (alumina processing
waste [red mud], heavy mineral sands, graphite, and zeolite) within the Gulf Coast Basin. This
project represents the first phase in a long-term program and provides reconnaissance data that
will be foundational for future work by assessing resources and suggesting plans to be conducted
in future work and expanding stakeholder engagement. The project includes several tasks
designed to identify, characterize, and assess several necessary aspects for development of CMs
and REEs in the Gulf Coast Basin.
Task 1 focused on quantifying coal resources in the Gulf Coast and related CM and REE
concentrations. The Gulf Coast is a major lignite-bearing basin within the United States (US).
Previous estimates of coal resources were updated by incorporating data on coal thickness from
~31,200 drill holes and related data points throughout the Gulf Coast and conducting detailed
geostatistical analysis to quantify and map cumulative coal thickness throughout the Gulf Coast.
The estimated total coal resource in the Gulf Coast is about 83 Gt in the upper 90 m (~ 300 ft) of
the subsurface. Texas accounted for 40 percent (32 Gt) of the total resource, followed by
Mississippi (24 %, 20 Gt), and Louisiana (14 %, 12 Gt).
Analysis of CMs and REEs was conducted, primarily on archived coal samples from the U.S.
Geological Survey (USGS), with additional samples collected from active mines in Texas and
outcrops in Alabama and Arkansas. Results show that total REE+yttrium (REY) concentrations (dry
coal basis) are comparable to those of the upper continental crust (UCC) with localized hot spots,
such as the Texas Gibbons Creek mine (REY≤~2,860 ppm). Ratios of REY concentrations to UCC
concentration (169 ppm, dry coal basis) range from 0.1 to 17 (median ratio 0.6). Median REY
concentration as oxides is 129 ppm and results in an estimated value of Rare Earth Oxides (REOs)
of $262 billion based on 83 Gt of coal in the Gulf Coast Basin. REEs that are important for
permanent magnets (Pr+Nd+Tb+Dy) have a median concentration of 28 ppm as oxides resulting in
an estimated value of $216 billion for Gulf Coast lignite. REY extractability from lignites is high
(median: 63– 93%) using environmentally benign weak acids. Although the estimated Gulf Coast
coal resources are high (83 Gt), there are only five active coal mines that produced ~ 15 million
tonnes in 2023. REE production from coal would likely require coproducts, such as activated
carbon and/or humic acids, to attain economic viability. Mining of these coals for CMs and REEs
could provide additional co-benefits such as activated carbon and other uses because power
generation using coal has sharply decreased in past decades.
Potential waste streams that could be reused in the Gulf Coast Basin to extract CMs and REEs
include coal ash in power plants, red mud from alumina processing, heavy mineral sands in frac
sand operations, and graphite and zeolite from mining operations.
Coal ash resources were estimated for all power plants in the United States based on coal
production (52 Gt, 1950 – 2021) that resulted in 10% coal ash at power plants (5.3 Gt) and an
estimated 70% potentially accessible (i.e., not sold or used but disposed in landfills and ponds).
 7

Using literature values of REEs in coal ash for Appalachian, Illinois and Powder River Basin coals
along with extractability of REEs resulted in a value of $8.4 billion for all coal ash derived from
these basins, and $0.65 billion for coal ash from these basins in Gulf Coast power plants.
Considering REEs for permanent magnets (Pr+Nd+Tb+Dy) resulted in a total value of $5.2 billion
for all coal ash in the US with $0.4 billion from coal ash from these basins in the Gulf Coast.
Potentially accessible coal ash derived from lignite in the Gulf Coast totaled 0.26 Gt, with a
median REY concentration as oxides of 395 ppm, ~ 4 times the concentration in the raw coal
because REEs are nonvolatile and are concentrated in the ash after burning. However,
extractability of REEs from coal ash is estimated to be ~ 30%, much lower than extractability from
raw Gulf Coast lignite (63 – 93%). The value of REYs as oxides from lignite in the Gulf Coast is $1.1
billion based on 30% extractability. The corresponding value of the REEs used for permanent
magnets (Pr+Nd+Tb+Dy) as oxides is $0.9 billion. However, many of the ash landfills and ponds
have been reclaimed, making it difficult to access ash from these ponds. Production of REEs from
coal ash could offset remediation costs for ash ponds that could be contaminating nearby surface
water bodies or underlying aquifers.
Oilfield waters also represent potential sources of CMs, such as lithium (Li). Data on Li
concentrations were obtained from ~2450 water samples in states along the greater Gulf Coast
(NM, TX, OK, LA, AR, and MS) (Darvari et al., 2024). The dataset contains analyses from samples
spanning >70 years, including the USGS National Produced Waters Geochemical Database (v2.3),
recent data previously published by the authors, recently collected samples and archived samples,
and data from U.S. Bureau of Mines reports. Background Li concentrations ranged from 0 – 20
mg/L with hotspots (≥80 mg/L) restricted to Jurassic Smackover Formation, a well-known brine
deposit in Arkansas (300+ mg/L Li), but also along the Gulf Coast in Texas, Louisiana, and
Mississippi (several samples ≥100 mg/L Li); and Cretaceous Edwards Formation of South Texas
(several samples ≥100 mg/L Li). Host rocks for high levels of Li in brines are generally carbonates,
likely due to their low sorption capacity. The Permian Basin in Texas and New Mexico is
characterized by high volumes of produced water (~6.6 billion barrels in 2023); however, Li
concentrations are generally low (median ~ 15 mg/L) resulting in an estimated 16,000 tonnes of Li
for 2023 or a value of $126 million using current Li prices.
Bauxite processing for alumina has been conducted at a number of sites along the Gulf Coast in
Texas, Louisiana, and Alabama. The red mud that results from alumina processing has variable
concentrations of REEs and CMs. Based on limited sampling of the two sites in Texas, median REY
concentrations range from 380 ppm in Point Comfort to 1988 ppm at the Copano site. Preliminary
estimates of the value of REEs from the Copano site range from $4.5 billion for REY oxides based
on 120 million tonnes of red mud and 50% extractability of REEs. Including Sc with other REEs and
Y increases the value to $11.6 billion. Considering REEs as oxides (Pr+Nd+Tb+Dy) for permanent
magnet results in $3.6 billion. Additional coproducts include iron oxides to increase the economic
returns. These red mud beds are within a few meters of sea level and the economic benefits from
extracting the REEs could offset remediation costs for these sites.
Evaluation of heavy mineral sands in Alabama resulted in low levels of REEs, similar to
concentrations in the Upper Continental Crust (UCC). However, very limited sampling of
concentrates from the hydraulic fracturing sand industry in Texas show some concentrations after
spiral and “gold” table mechanical sorting exceeding 1% (> 10,000 ppm). Zeolite also represents a
 8

potential source of REEs. Graphite can also be recovered from historically produced mines and
prospects in Texas.
Regional infrastructure, industries, and businesses were evaluated to assess linkages with the
CORE-CM supply chains, including extraction, processing, and manufacturing of high value CM
products and nonfuel carbon-based products. Existing infrastructure, industries, and businesses in
the Gulf Coast Basin were characterized and results were integrated with novel technologies for
expanding activities to advance CORE-CM supply chain. The results of this analysis indicate that
the Gulf Coast Basin is highly suitable for development of CM and REE technologies because of a
strong history of coal, oil, and gas production, expanding processing industries to support CM and
REE refining, and a variety of industries that would use the output from CM and REE processing.
The Gulf Coast also benefits from a long coastline with large ports to support international trade
and some processing industries are locating along the coast, allowing them to import materials to
support CM and REE processing.
Technology assessment, development, and field testing focused on the current state of the art
and possible future technological options for extraction, refining, processing, and manufacturing
of REE and CM components from relevant CORE-CM-based resources. The Gulf Coast CORE-CM
team includes members from the Williston Basin CORE-CM team to facilitate knowledge transfer
from processing similar lignites in North Dakota to the Gulf Coast. In addition, members from the
Wyoming CORE-CM team will transfer knowledge related to processing coal ash from the Powder
River Basin coals that were burned in the Gulf Coast. The Gulf Coast CORE-CM team is working
closely with processing industries, such as ElementUSA and others, to support processing of
different feedstocks for CMs and other carbon products.
Technology Innovation Center (TIC) planning emphasized different TICs for various feedstocks,
including coal, coal ash, red mud, heavy mineral sands, graphite, and zeolite. The Gulf Coast team
is collaborating with other CORE-CM teams to leverage the work that they have done on lignite
processing (North Dakota) and coal ash processing (Wyoming).
Outreach is critical for development of the CM and REE industry within the basin. Cooperation
throughout federal and state governments, state agencies, industry, and resource end users is
vital for the industry to form and grow. We connected with these groups in various ways,
including an advisory board, online presentations, Geological Society of America topical sessions,
annual symposia, and a website.
The following report is a summary of the work conducted and throughout refers to a series of
appendices which contain more thorough and specific information about each section.

Introduction
The University of Texas Bureau of Economic Geology (UT-BEG) is leading the Gulf Coast Carbon
Ore, Rare Earth, and Critical Minerals (CORE-CM) Initiative to assess the potential for using coal,
coal byproducts, produced water, red mud from bauxite, heavy mineral placer deposits, zeolite,
and graphite, within the Gulf Coast to produce CMs, REEs, and nonfuel carbon-based products
(CBPs). The location of the Gulf Coast basin is shown relative to the other coal basins throughout
the United States (US) (Fig. 1). This project is the first phase of a long-term program that will set
the foundation for future work by conducting reconnaissance assessment of the resources.

Figure 1. Map showing the coalfields of the conterminous United States.

The project included seven tasks.
Task 1.0 “Project Management and Planning” focused on project management and planning to
ensure that all remaining tasks were completed on time and within budget and conform to U.S.
Department of Energy (DOE) reporting requirements.
Task 2.0 “Basinal Assessment of CORE-CM Resources” emphasized basinal assessment of CORE-
CM resources, including updating coal resource mapping and quantification of REE and CM levels
in coal and coal ash.
Task 3.0 “Basinal Strategies for Reuse of Waste Streams” includes various waste streams, such as
coal ash from power plants, and produced water associated with oil and gas production. In 2024
the work was expanded to include red mud from alumina (bauxite) processing waste, heavy
 10

mineral sands, zeolite, and graphite.
Task 4.0, “Basinal Strategies for Infrastructure, Industries and Business” is designed to support the
development of CMs, REEs, and nonfuel CBPs in the Gulf Coast. Examples of businesses include
coal companies, such as North American Coal Company, North American Construction Group Ltd
(NACG), ElementUSA, and various oil companies that provided produced water samples.
Task 5.0 “Technology Assessment, Development and Field Testing”. Our analysis of technologies
and field testing focused on technologies being developed and tested on lignites in the Williston
Basin (North Dakota). Preliminary analyses suggest that lignites from the Gulf Coast Basin behave
similarly to those in the Williston Basin in terms of CM and REE mode of occurrence and
extractability and the Gulf Coast CORE-CM program will benefit from the lab, bench, and pilot
testing conducted in North Dakota.
Task 6.0 “Technology Innovation Center” involved discussions about approaches to developing
Technology Innovation Centers based on a variety of feedstocks in the Gulf Coast Basin. We had
discussions with various industry groups and also with the University of North Dakota, because
similar coal resources (lignite) are found in North Dakota.
Task 7.0 “Stakeholder Outreach and Education” was addressed by holding stakeholder meetings,
conducting and posting webinars, incorporating materials into relevant university courses, and
planning outreach and education, and deploying outreach materials.

Chapter 1, Task 1: Project Management and Planning
1.1 Background
The following provides answers to some of the questions listed in Task 1.1 Phase I of the Gulf
Coast CORE-CM project.
How the technology relies on limited resources such as coal, biomass, freshwater, land, and/or
low-carbon energy: Indicate the relationship between the amount of resources used and the
amount of product formed
Different unconventional resources were evaluated during Phase I of this project that will require
different technologies for CM and REE extraction and processing. The feedstocks being considered
can be subdivided into two primary categories:
 1) feedstocks from raw resources, including coal and
 2) feedstocks based on waste streams including coal ash in power plants, produced water
 from oil and gas production in red mud from alumina processing, and zeolite and
 graphite mine waste.
Raw Resources (Coal)
As detailed in Task 2, coal resources are abundant in the Gulf Coast, with an estimated 83 Gt of
coal available in the upper 300 ft (~ 100 m) (Appendix 1). There are five active coal mines
operating in the Gulf Coast basin, three in Texas, one in Louisiana and one in Mississippi. Coal
mining relies on land, water, and energy resources. Coal mining in the Gulf Coast is open pit that
includes land disturbance from vegetation, topsoil, and overburden removal. The sites are
reclaimed after mining is completed. Reclamation includes removal of facilities and equipment,
restoring overburden and topsoil, and revegetating the land. Additional inputs for coal mining
include electricity, fuel for trucking materials, and water for extraction, dust suppression, and
washing during processing. Machinery for excavation and trucking require mostly diesel for
operations.
CM and REEs could be extracted from the active coal mines in the Gulf Coast, which have large
coal resources. Use of existing mines would not cause any further degradation of land and water
resources. If existing mines are used, then no new mines would be required to be permitted or
additional land and water resources would be used. Studies in N Dakota indicate that the coal
after CM and REE extraction is of higher quality than the original coal (Mann et al., 2022). Our
proposed technologies will focus on active coal mines and will not result in increased resources
being used. Extraction of CMs and REEs would provide an additional revenue stream to existing
mining operators.
Waste Streams (Coal Ash, Produced Water, Red Mud, and Heavy Mineral Sands)
Critical minerals can also be recovered from coal ash disposed near power plants. Many of the
power plants in the Gulf Coast are located near the mouths of coal mines (e.g., San Miguel coal
mine and related power plant). In addition, waste coal can be considered for CM and REE
extraction, and generally includes coal, soil, and rock and can either be stockpiled or burned. Fly
ash and bottom ash represent residue after burning coal in a power plant. Coal ash can be used
onsite or sold or can be disposed of in surface impoundments or landfills (Reedy et al., 2024).
Many power plants in the Gulf Coast are located close to water bodies because water is required

 12

for cooling the power plants. Therefore, these water bodies are vulnerable to contamination from
flooding of impoundments. In addition, leaching of materials from the impoundments can
potentially contaminate underlying aquifers. Coal ash and related coal byproducts could be
beneficially reused for CM and REE extraction and to provide other carbon products and turning
waste to resources creating a circular economy. Using these waste stream would not require any
additional resource extraction, greatly reducing use of limited natural resources although some
resources would be required to process CMs from these waste streams.
Additional waste streams include produced water from oil and gas production that contains CMs,
including lithium, red mud from alumina processing, heavy mineral sands from hydraulic frac sand
operations in Texas, and zeolite and graphite historical mines. These waste streams could be used
to source CMs and REEs with various types of physical separation and beneficiation. Red muds
from alumina processing also represent an excellent source of CMs and REEs that could offset
remediation costs because these sites are very close to the coast and vulnerable to storm surge
and sea level rise.
If coal is used as a feedstock, where will it be mined and what are the associated near-term and
legacy environmental impacts of the coal mining, including methane leakage.
Environmental impacts of coal mining
Previous studies have documented the environmental and human health impacts of coal mining.
Below is a summary of impacts occurring from current coal mining operations and legacy impacts
from past mining.
Land disturbance and ecosystem impacts
Development of new surface coal mines has substantial impacts on the environment, including
reclassification of land from agricultural or other uses. Land clearing of vegetation and removal of
overburden is required to access the coal seam below. These processes will impact the people
who currently live and work near the proposed mine, and also the vegetation and animals.
Land-use impacts can also include subsidence, sedimentation, and erosion (Finkelman et al.,
2021). However, land reclamation after the coal mine has ceased operations can restore the
ecosystems by replacing overburden, soil, and vegetation. As part of the Surface Mining Control
and Reclamation Act of 1977, the Office of Surface Mining Reclamation and Enforcement was
established to regulate mine land reclamation and ensure public and environmental protection
from hazards and impacts of surface coal mining.
Air quality impacts
Coal mining negatively impacts air quality through many different processes. Coal dust during
extraction includes crystalline silica that can cause black lung disease through inhalation. Methane
gas can also be released during mining. Additionally, machinery used for excavation and
transportation generate emissions, contributing to air pollution. Coal waste piles and coal in
mines can spontaneously combust resulting in uncontrolled fires and emitting pollutants. Recent
regulations have greatly reduced harmful effects of coal mining on air quality. For example, the
U.S. Department of Labor updated the Mine Safety and Health Administration rules to further
reduce health hazards from coal mining, reducing exposure levels to coal dust, requires dust
sampling and periodic health examinations for coal miners and updated the standards for
respiratory protection.

 13

Surface water and groundwater impacts
Water resources, including surface water and groundwater, can be degraded from coal mining.
Water is required to extract coal and wash it and transport it, which can result in contamination of
water resources. Surface water flows and groundwater levels can be negatively impacted by coal
extraction (Finkelman et al., 2021). Runoff from coal waste can contaminate surface water and
groundwater, increasing salinity resulting in water being unsuitable for drinking. Gulf Coast coal
mines are surface mines and acid mine drainage (AMD) is not widespread in these mines. There
was some AMD in Gibbons Creek mine. AMD results from reactions between pyrite in coal with
water and air that increases the acidity of runoff resulting in the water being toxic for plants,
animals and human consumption (Keating, 2021). Federal, state, and local regulations have been
developed to protect water supplies. These regulations include isolating mine waste storage from
water resources, managing runoff at mines, and water treatment.
Impacts on people
Coal mining and power plants can also negatively affect the health of communities who live and
work in the vicinity of coal mines and related operations. Land-use change linked to coal mining
can reduce property values and scenic viewsheds (Keating et al., 2021). Mine workers are
subjected to many health and safety hazards including exposure to coal dust contributing to
pulmonary diseases such as “black lung disease”. Many other health concerns have been linked to
exposure to particles related to coal such as heart disease, low birthweights, infant mortality, and
reduced life expectancy (Finkelman et al., 2021; Keating et al., 2021). However, regulations have
been developed to reduce mine workers’ exposure and related health and safety hazards.
If coal waste is being remediated, indicate the relationship between the amount of coal waste
used versus the amount of product formed.
Because the phase of development of CORE-CM in the Gulf Coast is in the early stages, it is
difficult to estimate the ratio of coal waste used to critical mineral production. Concentrations of
REEs in coal are generally less than 1,000 ppm (0.1%), indicating that 99.9% of coal will remain
after REE extraction in most cases. The extractability of REEs is also a factor and preliminary
results suggest high levels of extractability (63 – 93%) in lignites in the Gulf Coast, similar to
extractability from lignites in N Dakota. However, the Gulf Coast CORE-CM team is evaluating
approaches that would develop coproducts in addition to CMs to maximize use of coal stock by
generating carbon products and critical minerals from the same location. This approach should
minimize waste. Therefore, there are projections for less jobs in mining, but more jobs in
processing coal feedstocks.

Proposed Waste Management Strategy
The proposed waste management strategies for the Gulf Coast CORE-CM project are to maximize use of all
waste streams to minimize any residual waste that would need to be managed. The team is prioritizing
technologies for commercialization that use waste streams for CM and REE extraction. As discussed
previously, several promising waste streams are being examined including any waste from coal mines and
power plants, produced water from oil and gas, red mud from alumina processing, and heavy mineral

 14

sands from frac sand operations.
During Phase I of the project, the team contacted various companies, including N American Coal
Co., ElementUSA, and Blue Line to discuss use of mine waste and red mud for CM and REE
extraction. These companies and others are extremely interested in evaluating the potential use
of various waste streams for CM and REE extraction.
Remediation of legacy environmental impacts
The proposed technologies should remediate legacy environmental impacts linked to coal mining,
power plants, oil and gas production, red mud disposal pits, frac sand operations, and historical
zeolite mines by using these waste streams to extract CMs and REEs. The USGS has promoted
“waste to resources” or “trash to treasure” to remediate waste streams while developing CM
resources.
Remediation of legacy environmental impacts of coal
Waste generation related to coal mining is estimated to be ~40% (i.e., for each ton of coal 0.4 tons
of waste is generated, including coal wash rejects, tailings, and waste rock). Previous studies
estimated that there are over 4.4 billion tons of coal waste at storage sites within the U.S. (U.S.
DOE, 2024). These waste streams can cause significant environmental impacts including increased
acidity and heavy metals in surface water and groundwater (US DOE, 2024). A report by the
Environmental Integrity Project and Earthjustice indicates that coal ash in ponds and landfills
associated with power plants may be leaching toxic materials such as arsenic and lead into
underlying aquifers (EarthJustice, 2022). However, CMs such as Mn, Ni, and Co could be extracted,
precluding leaching them into water supplies (U.S. DOE, 2024).
Remediation of legacy environmental impacts of aluminum processing
The United States produced ~ 4.1 million tons of aluminum in 2023, with primary production of ~
0.75 million metric tons (Statistica, 2025). For every ton of aluminum produced from bauxite,
nearly 2.5 to 4 tons of red mud is generated (NSF, 2018). Red mud is caustic and fine grained,
making it hazardous and difficult to manage. The Center for Resource Recovery and Recycling
(CR3), an Industry-University Cooperative Research Center funded by the National Science
Foundation (NSF), has developed a metallurgical process to extract iron oxide and REEs from red
mud (NSF, 2018). Red mud pits on the Gulf Coast are located near the coast and are highly
vulnerable to storm surge during hurricanes.
We will evaluate various technologies to remediate red mud and generate CMs and REEs.
ElementUSA is currently evaluating various approaches to use red mud to generate various
products for beneficial use. As discussed previously, the technologies proposed in the Gulf Coast
CORE-CM project aim to maximize the use of red mud. Use of red mud could offset the costs of
remediation of these pits near the coast.

Chapter 2, Task 2: Basinal Assessment of CORE-CM Resources
2.1 Coal Resources
2.1a Summary
The coal resources were quantified to evaluate in-place coal as a potential source of rare earth
elements (REEs) and critical minerals (CMs). Details related to coal resources in the Gulf Coast
Basin are provided in Warwick et al., 2025 (Appendix 1). This study presents the results of a
regional analysis of the U.S. Gulf Coast lignite and bituminous coal resources that might be
available as potential sources of REEs and CMs. The resource analysis used kriging methods to
develop maps of cumulative coal thickness throughout the region using data from 31,181 drill
holes and other data points. The estimated total coal resource in the Gulf Coast is about 83 Gt in
the upper 90 m (~ 300 ft) of the subsurface. Texas accounted for 40 percent (32 Gt) of the total
resource, followed by Mississippi (24%, 20 Gt), Louisiana (14 %, 12 Gt), Tennessee (10 %, 8.5 Gt),
and Arkansas (6 %, 5.1 Gt). The remaining states each accounted for less than 5 percent of the
total resource. Mining of these coals for CMs and REEs could provide additional co-benefits such
as activated carbon and other uses because power generation using coal has sharply decreased in
past decades.
2.1b Methods
This regional coal resource analysis used kriging methods to develop an isopleth map of
cumulative coal thickness throughout the Gulf Coast region using all available data. The
Geostatistical Analyst software package of Esri ArcGIS Desktop version 10.7.1 was used to spatially
interpolate and estimate cumulative coal thicknesses using ordinary kriging methods. Polygon
areas were hand-drawn around the various clusters of drill holes across the region and adjusted to
minimize edge-effect kriging artifacts near the margins of the polygon areas. The total modeled
area inside all polygons was about 132,000 km2 (~51,000 mi2) divided into three model regions:
east, north, and west.
Data points in this study represent the total cumulative thickness of all coal beds between the
ground surface and the lesser of either total drill-hole depth or about 90 m (~300 ft). Data for
typical drill holes include the thicknesses and top depths for all coal beds encountered. Local
conditions vary and operators generally drill no deeper than necessary to characterize coal
resources based on local knowledge or information from nearby drill holes. We assume that the
drill-hole data represent all coal resources to a depth of 90 m (300 ft).
For each model region, estimates of cumulative total coal volumes were generated for six depths
where the top of the coal was encountered above that depth, including 15, 30, 45, 60, 75, and 90
m (50, 100, 150, 200, 250, and 300 ft) using a 1 km (0.6 mi) grid spacing. A minimum coal
thickness of 0.6 m (2 ft) was used in the analysis and the thickness numbers were not rounded.
Coal thickness of 0.6 m (2 ft) was selected because it represents the minimum coal thickness that
may be surface minable within ~90 m (300 ft) depth from surface in the Gulf Coast. The coal
resources were estimated for a 1 km (0.6 mi) grid spacing and were summarized for each depth
interval by county area and converted to total coal mass using a density of 1.29 g/cm 3 for lignite
and 1.32 g/cm3 for bituminous coal. All coal mass values were totaled, rounded to two significant
figures, and reported in million metric tons units (1 short ton = 2,000 lb = 0.9072 metric ton).

Results show that the estimated total coal resource in the Gulf Coast is about 83 Gt in the upper
90 m (~300 ft) based on data from 31,181 drill holes and other data points (Fig. 2). Texas
accounted for 40 percent (32 Gt) of the total, followed by Mississippi (24 %, 20 Gt), Louisiana (14

2.1d Conclusions
This regional study of the U.S. Gulf Coast provides an initial analysis the coal resources that might
be available as potential sources of REEs and CMs. The resource analysis used kriging methods to
develop maps of cumulative coal thickness throughout the region using data from 31,181 drill
holes and other data points. The estimated total coal resource in the Gulf Coast is about 83 Gt in
the upper 90 m (~300 ft) of the subsurface. Texas accounted for 40 percent of the total, followed
by Mississippi (24 %), Louisiana (14 %), Tennessee (10 %), and Arkansas (6 %). A comparison
between previous Gulf Coast coal resource studies and the results of this study is difficult because
each study used different data sources, assessment methodologies, overburden depths, and
qualifying coal thicknesses. The Gulf Coast basin has significant lignite resources at shallow depths
(within 90 m) of the subsurface that might serve as potential sources of REEs and CMs. Future coal
resource and reserve studies could be focused on areas within the Gulf Coast regions with
elevated REEs and other CM concentrations.

2.2 Rare Earth Element Potential in Coals in the Gulf Coast
2.2a Summary
Critical mineral (CM) and rare earth element (REE) concentrations were quantified in coal (lignite)
in the US Gulf Coast region. Details of CM and REE levels are described in Scanlon et al., 2025
(Appendix 2). Concentrations of CMs and REEs were measured in 118 samples from outcrops and
14 mines in the Gulf Coast. Results show that total REE+yttrium (REY) concentrations (dry coal
basis) are comparable to those of the upper continental crust (UCC) with localized hot spots, such
as the Texas Gibbons Creek mine (REY≤~2,860 ppm). REY to UCC ratios for Gulf Coast coal samples
range from 2.2 to 307 (median ratio 11). REY extractability from lignites is high (median: 63– 93%)
using environmentally benign weak acid. The estimated value of REY oxides for the coal resources
is $262 billion, based on 83 Gt of coal in upper 90 m and 70% extractability of REYs. Considering
REEs that are most important for permanent magnets (Pr+Nd+Tb+Dy as oxides) results in an
estimated $16 billion. This reconnaissance study shows the potential for REY production from coal
on the Gulf Coast; however, carbon coproducts would likely be required for socioeconomic
viability.
2.2b Methods
Critical mineral (CM) compositional data, including REEs, were collated for 118 samples that were
taken from outcrops, coal mines, and public databases. Most samples (n=63) were collected by
the USGS in the 1990s and 2000s, archived at USGS headquarters in Reston, Virginia (U.S.), and
made available for compositional analysis for this study. The USGS samples represent 13 coal
mines, with 10 mines in Texas, two mines in Louisiana (Dolet Hills and Oxbow), and one mine in
Mississippi (Red Hills); and coal outcrops in an abandoned mine in Arkansas (Camden) and two
clay pits in Tennessee and Kentucky (Swaim and Sims) (Fig. 3). In addition to the archived USGS
samples, new samples were collected from the active San Miguel mine in Texas (n=6) and from
coal outcrops in Alabama (n=12) and Arkansas (n=3). The analytical data collected for this study
were complemented by publicly available data (n=34) from Zygarlicke et al. (2019) and accessed
through the U.S. DOE Data eXchange (EDX) database.
Sample analyses of CMs and REEs were conducted by Standard Laboratories, Inc. (Freeburg,
Illinois, U.S.). All samples were analyzed for REE + Y + Sc and 23 other CMs as well as uranium.
Quantitative elemental analysis was performed following ASTM (2021) D6357 using Method B for
Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass Spectrometry (ICP-MS) determination. All REE data are reported
on a dry coal basis.
The extractability of REEs from coal was evaluated in five samples using general sequential
extraction procedures. One sample was selected from the Gibbons Creek archive because of high
REE concentrations and four samples from San Miguel mine, which were collected during this
study. Extractions were conducted at the University of North Dakota, Institute of Energy Studies.
Sequential extraction was conducted using water initially, then ammonium acetate to quantify ion
exchangeable REE concentrations, and a weak acid (0.5 molar HCl). The kinetics were rapid,
generally less than 2 to 3 h to reach equilibrium. REE concentrations in the residues were analyzed
at Standard Laboratories using the same procedure as for the coal analyses.

Concentrations of REYs in the Gulf Coast coals vary from ~18 to 2862 ppm on a dry coal basis
(n=118 samples) (Fig. 4). These concentrations suggest that the coal represents a low-grade ore as

dry coal basis, mostly restricted to the Gibbons Creek mine (eight samples) and single samples
from Alabama and Arkansas. The Gibbons Creek samples have elevated REY concentrations, likely
because of the influence of leached volcanic ash (Hower et al., 2023).

Extractability of REYs from different source materials is an important factor for REY development
potential. REYs from Gulf Coast lignites were readily extractable using environmentally benign,
weak acid (0.5 molar HCl) with extraction percentages for individual REEs ranging from 53 to 96%.
No systematic variation in extractability of individual REEs or Y or Sc was found. REEs + Y and Sc
were generally not water soluble and percentages that were extractable using ion exchange in
ammonium acetate were mostly ≤6%. Most of the REEs were extracted using weak acid leaching.
Extraction of ash during this process was generally low, mostly ~30%. The extraction rates are
similar to extraction rates of REEs reported for lignites in the Williston Basin in North Dakota
(Theaker et al., 2024) and are interpreted to reflect REE occurrence in organic rather than
inorganic phases in the lignites.

The economic potential of REE resources is often evaluated based on the ratio of heavy REEs
(HREEs) to light REEs (LREEs) because HREEs are considered more valuable for various applications
and HREE-rich deposits are rarer overall. In the Gulf Coast coal samples studied here, HREEs are
less abundant than LREEs (median ratio 0.4). Some studies report HREE/LREE ratios after
normalizing the data to UCC; however, dividing by the UCC, which has an HREE/LREE ratio of 0.3,

 23

artificially increases the median HREE/LREE ratio by ~6 times; in this study, for example, from 0.42
to 2.7 in the Gulf Coast samples which skews the data towards a theoretical economic value that
is not representative of the quantitative elemental abundances. Moreover, some LREEs, such as
Nd and Pr, are now considered among the most critical REEs for clean energy applications which
further emphasizes that HREE/LREE ratios are not necessarily useful measures for REE resource
evaluations.
The high cost of mining and coal beneficiation for REE production suggests that REE production
would be most feasible in active mines where many of these costs would be absorbed by current
production. Because REE levels are in the ppm range, if coal was produced for REE production
alone, > 99% of the coal would remain after REE extraction and preliminary studies of lignite in the
Williston Basin (North Dakota) have shown that the quality of lignites improves with leaching
using weak acids for REE recovery (Mann et al., 2022). However, owing to the current prices of
REEs, Y, Sc, and the price estimates for the samples studied here, an economic feasibility of REY
extraction would require additional uses for coal byproducts. In addition to selling the remaining
lignite, some companies use coal and coal byproducts for building materials, such as fire-proof
roof tiles that are 2 to 4 times stronger than ceramic tiles. Alternatively, production of activated
carbon and/or humic acid from coal has been proposed to make REE extraction economically
viable (Mann et al., 2022). Although coal resources in the upper 90 m in the Gulf Coast are
plentiful, only five out of 29 coal mines operating since 1983 are currently active, and the San
Miguel mine is slated to close by 2027. Coal in the Gulf Coast is mined in open pits and the closed
mines are in various stages of remediation; only mines in the first phase of reclamation (i.e.,
covered with ~ 1 m of soil) would be considered accessible for REE production.
2.2d Conclusions
The Gulf Coast Basin has large coal (87 Gt) resources that could be suitable for extraction of REEs.
Analysis of 118 samples from outcrops and coal mine samples in the region shows variable
concentrations of total REE + Y (REY) on a dry coal basis (18 to 2,860 ppm) with local hotspots
(e.g., Gibbons Creek mine, Texas). The mean REY value for all samples is similar to that in the
upper continental crust, with REY to UCC ratios up to 17 in Gibbons Creek mine. Extractability of
REEs from the coal is high (median: n 63 to 93%) using environmentally benign weak acid, similar
to extractability from lignites in North Dakota. The value of REY oxides from coal in the Gulf Coast
is estimated to be $262 billion based on 83 Gt of coal in the upper 90 m and ~70% extractability.
The value of the REEs that contribute to permanent magnets is $216 billion for Pr+Nd+Tb+Dy
oxides. Development of coproducts, such as activated carbon and/or humic acid, could enhance
the profitability of CM and REE extraction. This reconnaissance study provides an initial analysis of
the potential for developing REEs and other CMs from coal in the Gulf Coast. Future studies could
expand sampling and analysis and examine the potential for developing additional products to
improve economic viability.

3.1 Coal Ash in the Contiguous United States
3.1a Summary
Data on spatiotemporal variability in coal ash resources and disposition throughout the US were
compiled from various federal databases and rare earth element (REE) levels in ash were compiled
from the literature and are described in Reedy et al., 2024. The following is excerpted from that
paper and the paper is included in Appendix 3. The analysis was conducted for the entire
contiguous US (CONUS) to provide context for the data in the Gulf Coast region. Results show that
~ 52 Gt of coal were produced in the US (1950–2021). Power plants account for most of the coal
use, particularly since 1980. Coal ash (5.3 Gt) represents a mean of 10% of coal by weight, ranging
from 6% for subbituminous to 14% for lignite. About 70% of coal ash is potentially accessible for
REE extraction (1985–2021) and was disposed in landfills and ponds with the remaining coal ash
used onsite or sold. Median values of total REEs are much higher in ashes derived from the
Appalachian Basin (median 431 mg/kg) than in the Illinois (282 mg/kg) or Powder River basins
(264 mg/kg). Considering the market value of rare earth oxides, potentially accessible ash
volumes, and percent REE extraction (30% Appalachian and Illinois Basins; 70% Powder River
Basin) results in an estimated $8.4 billion value for REEs in the CONUS.
3.1b Methods:
This study is based primarily on data from two main sources: the United States Geological Survey
(USGS) and the US Energy Information Administration (EIA) (Fig. 5). The EIA data include archived
information originally collected by the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC). Results of
this study were compared in part with data from the American Coal Ash Association (ACAA, 2015,
2021). Concentrations of REEs in coal ash were compiled from the literature.
Ash content represents the non-combustible portion of coal that is generated upon combustion,
collectively termed coal combustion byproducts, that is expressed as a weight percent of the coal.
We estimated ash content based on reported values for monthly coal deliveries to power plants
recorded on FERC Form 423 during 1972-2001, combined data from FERC and EIA Form 423
during 2002-2007, and EIA Form 923 during 2008-2021.

3.1c Results
In 2021, the US had an estimated 11.2 Gt of technologically and economically recoverable coal
reserves (EIA, 2021). Coal production varied over time in the US (Fig. 6a). Between 1912 and 1960,
coal production averaged 0.48 Gt/yr (range: 0.31 – 0.61 Gt). After 1960, annual production
increased steadily, peaking in 2008 at 1.06 Gt, and then declined afterwards to 0.52 Gt by 2021. A
total of 78 Gt of coal was produced in the US during 1800-2021, with about 70% produced since
1950 (52 Gt). Production is dominated by bituminous and subbituminous rank coals (Fig. 6a). At
the peak of US coal production in 2008, the rank distribution consisted of 47% bituminous, 46%
subbituminous, 6.5% lignite, and 0.1% anthracite (Fig. 6a) (EIA, 2021). During 1972-2021, 31% of
total production was from the Appalachian Basin, 14% from the Illinois Basin, and 46% from
Western basins combined with the Powder River Basin, with increased contributions (8%) from
the Gulf Coast and Ft Union basins, mostly lignite. Coal production in the Gulf Coast totaled 1.7 Gt
(1972 – 2021), representing ~ 5% of total US coal production during this period (32 Gt).
Consumption by the power sector steadily increased, reaching 81% by 1980, and averaging 92%
after 1998 (range: 91-94%) (EIA, 2022).
Total reported ash disposition during 1985-2021 was 2.5 Gt, plateauing in the 2000s and
decreasing substantially since 2010 (Fig. 6b). Texas ranked number 1 in terms of total ash (303
million tonnes). About 69% (1.9 Gt) of all ash generated in the US during 1985-2021 is potentially
accessible for evaluation as an REE resource, having been disposed of in landfills (33%), ponds
(20%), and offsite disposal facilities (13%), or placed in storage (2%). The remaining 31% of ash
(0.86 Gt) was either sold (22%) or otherwise used for some purpose (9%). An estimated 426
million tonnes of ash was delivered to the Gulf Coast, representing 15% of US deliveries and 27%
of the ash was derived from mines in the Gulf Coast states (Reedy et al., 2024).

3.1d Conclusions
This study focused on spatiotemporal variability in coal ash generated at power plants as a source
of REEs. Coal production doubled from 1950s (0.45 Gt/yr) to late 2000s (1.04 Gt/yr) but reduced
to half within the past decade (0.52 Gt 2021). Most (79%) of the US coal consumption has been
for power generation (1950-2021). Coal accounted for about 50% of power generation from 1950-
2005 but decreased to 20% by 2021. A total of 52 Gt of coal was produced (1950-2021) and
associated ash represents about 10% of coal production (5.8 Gt). Burning coal in power plants
concentrates REEs by factors of 4 to 10, enhancing the value of ash as a source of REEs. An
estimated 69% of ash is considered potentially recoverable (1985-2021) as it was stored in
landfills and ponds on and offsite whereas the remaining 31% was sold and used by other
industries. The Powder River Basin in Wyoming accounted for about 44% of US coal production
and almost 27% of associated ash since 2000. Most coal that is produced from a specific basin is
also consumed within that region with the major exception of the Powder River Basin which
exports most of its coal to other states. Using mean REE concentrations in coal ash derived from
major coal basins (Appalachian, Illinois, and Powder River Basin, 85% of US production 1950-

 28

2021), the potentially recoverable ash content (68% of total), varying extraction rates (30% for
Appalachia and Illinois basins and 70% for Powder River Basin), and 2020 REO market prices
resulted in an estimated potential value of REEs in ash of about $8.4 billion. Including yttrium and
scandium increases the estimated value to $97 billion. In addition to enhancing energy security in
the US through development of REE resources, the economic value of producing these REEs could
help offset the costs of remediation of unlined ash landfills or ponds in environmentally
vulnerable areas. The potential for REE resource development from coal ash should be evaluated
globally in nations where coal ash is available.

There are many tradeoffs when considering coal from mines versus coal ash from power plants as
a source of REEs. The median concentration of REY in coal ash (354 ppm, dry coal basis) is ~4
times greater than that in dry coal (median=109 ppm) (Fig. 7) because REEs are nonvolatile and
thus concentrated in the ash after combustion. However, the extractability of REEs is much higher
for coal (63 to 93%) relative to coal combustion ash and can be achieved through rather simple
and environmentally benign sequential extraction with weak acid leaching (Mann et al., 2022).
Therefore, REE extractability may partially counterbalance REE concentration differences between
coal and ash and profitability considerations will need to be made on a case-by-case basis.
Extraction of REEs from coal ash could be used to offset costs of thermal power plant operation
and help to improve management of coal ash impoundments and reduce adverse environmental
impacts. However, electricity generation from coal has been steadily decreasing since ~2000 and
is being replaced by natural gas and renewables, primarily wind and more recently solar power.
There is also concern about water contamination from leaking ash landfills or ponds because
many power plants are located adjacent to water bodies to provide cooling for thermoelectric
power generation. Monitoring is required adjacent to coal ash disposal sites and Ashtracker has
been compiling data on groundwater contamination related to ash disposal. Many sites have
documented groundwater contamination related to coal ash on the Gulf Coast. If those sites need
to be remediated, extraction of REEs from coal ash could help offset remediation costs. The USGS

3.3 Produced Water and Related Lithium Concentrations
3.3a Summary
The potential for Li production was assessed from oilfield waters in the U.S. Gulf Coast region.
Data on Li concentrations were obtained from ~2450 formation water samples from states along
the greater Gulf Coast (NM, TX, OK, LA, AR, and MS). The geochemical analyses spanned >70
years, and include data include the USGS database (v2.3), recent data previously published by the
authors, analyses from recently collected samples and from archived samples, and data from U.S.
Bureau of Mines reports.
Aqueous Li distribution is lognormal, typical of trace elements, with background levels in the 0–20
mg/L range. High concentrations (≥80 mg/L) are restricted to some intervals at certain locations:
Jurassic Smackover Formation, a well-known brine deposit in Arkansas (300+ mg/L Li), but also
along the Gulf Coast in Texas, Louisiana, and Mississippi (several samples ≥100 mg/L Li); and
Cretaceous Edwards Formation of South Texas (several samples ≥100 mg/L Li). Genetic models for
Li enrichment are lacking, but high Li seems to be related to the noted fact that Li concentrations
increase with depth and with total dissolved solids. Other common characteristics of high-Li brines
in the Gulf Coast are high K, Ca, and B, which reflect water-rock interactions, presence of major
faults, and association with higher permeability intervals that can spread Li-bearing fluids. Host
rocks for high-dissolved Li brines commonly are carbonates, possibly because of their limited
sorption opportunities. Close connection to an igneous basement or to a depositional
environment that supported accumulation of surface continental brines does not seem to be
required.
3.3b Methods
The dataset used in the study comprises three main sources for a total of 2455 analyses (Fig. 8):
(1) the USGS produced water database (v2.3) (75 %); (2) produced water samples analyzed by UT-
BEG team (24 %); and (3) archived U.S. Bureau of Mines (USBM) samples (2 %) and, used in a
more qualitative way, data extracted from USBM reports. More details are available in Darvari et
al., (2024) (Appendix 4).
3.3c Results
Results show that Li concentrations are variable with most samples showing low concentrations.
The median Li concentration is 9 mg/L, and an average of 29 mg/L. Three areas show high Li
values (≥300 mg/L): Smackover samples in AR, TX, LA, and MS, Deep Edwards in South Texas, and,
possibly, Anadarko Basin in the Texas Panhandle (Fig. 9a). Lithium concentrations generally
increase with Total Dissolved Solids (TDS), similar to that of most other elements. The relationship
between Li concentrations and TDS is shown in Fig. 5a and with depth in Fig. 9b.
The depth plot (Fig. 9b) emphasizes the depth range of the high-Li areas. Anadarko high-Li
samples are restricted to the vicinity of the shallow-buried Amarillo Uplift. Smackover samples are
taken in the Upper Gulf Coast where the basement is relatively shallow whereas Edwards samples
are derived from Gulf Coast sections located further downdip, where the basin deepens.
Smackover samples from TX, LA, and MS are taken at greater depth than AR samples. A few
isolated high-Li samples may or may not be real as discussed below. For example, the two

elevated samples in Schleicher (83 mg/L) and Montague (221 mg/L) counties (SE Permian Basin
and NW Fort Worth Basin, respectively), taken from Pennsylvania.

Economic mineral accumulations revolve around three essential processes: mobilization at the
source, transport, and accumulation/ concentration in a host reservoir. The source for Li in the
study area seems to be felsic basement rocks rather than remobilization of Li from enriched
sedimentary facies. Unfortunately, testing this hypothesis is difficult because the nature of the
basement rocks is poorly known at most locations. Transport occurs through permeable
pathways, such as faults and more permeable units. If not self-sourced (possibly some section of
the Anadarko Basin), Li will follow the deep hydrological flow system. Any genetic hypothesis
must incorporate the flow history of the basin. Flow dynamics in the subsurface falls between two
poles: permeable intervals, hosting typical oil reservoirs, and aquitards, where flow is limited and
slow, and typically acting as reservoir seals. This dichotomy also corresponds to the two major
rock types supporting oil accumulations (most samples come from oil and gas wells): historical
conventional and active unconventional. Favorable conventional formations have relatively high
porosity in addition to high permeability. A suitable permeability is needed to receive high Li
brines and high porosity allows for substantial resources in terms of stored Li (AR Smackover
porosity in the 10–15 % range). In the study, high Li samples were mostly associated with
carbonates where sorption opportunities are more limited than in clastics.
3.3d Conclusions
Geochemical measurements were obtained from ~2450 produced water samples across the Gulf
Coast to assess the potential for commercial Li production from oilfield brines. High TDS, K, B, and
Ca seem to be favorable characteristics, at least in the Gulf Coast Basin. Many samples (~90 %)
show background levels (<40 mg/L Li), whereas only 6 % of the samples have >80 mg/L Li. The
dataset is not necessarily representative of all geological units in the study area, but high-Li
samples are confined to particular strata at specific locations: Jurassic Smackover in the northern
Gulf Coast, Cretaceous Deep Edwards in South Texas, and mostly Granite Wash and dolomite of
Pennsylvanian age in the Texas Panhandle (to be confirmed). These high-dissolved Li host units
 34

are permeable and located near faults, but their extents and formation waters are poorly
characterized, and additional information is needed to develop genetic hypotheses. In addition,
many sampling gaps exist as resident water from many intervals has not been analyzed for Li and
other elements. Understanding the entire geochemical context is important because recovering
multiple elements (Br, I, REEs, other metals) could be advantageous from an economic standpoint.

3.4 Red Mud from Alumina Processing
3.4a Summary
Alumina processing of bauxite has been conducted at several sites in the Gulf Coast, including
Copano and Point Comfort in Texas and Gramercy and Burnside in Louisiana. Aluminum was
generated using the Bayer process. The waste product from the Bayer process, commonly
referred to as bauxite residue, or “red mud”, is the focus of this study. These sites are close to the
coast within a few meters of sea level and are vulnerable to storm surge and sea level rise. The
red mud provides a potential source of REEs and CMs.
Results of analysis of REEs and CMs in 16 samples from the Copano site, 9 samples from the Point
Comfort site, and one sample from the Gramercy site shows variable levels of REEs and CMs.
Median REY values for the Copano site (~ 2000 ppm) were much higher than those from the Point
Comfort site (73 ppm). The one sample from Gramercy is even higher than that from the Copano
site (median REY, ~ 4000 ppm).
Median REY concentrations in the Copano site are 12 times greater than those in the Upper
Continental Crust (UCC) and REY concentration at the Gramercy Site is 24 times greater than UCC
but median REY concentration at the Point Comfort site is only 2 times greater than UCC. Our
analysis focuses on the Copano samples. Y is the most concentrated REE with a median value 24
times that of UCC. The REEs that are important for permanent magnets (Pr, Nd, Tb, and Dy) are
also much higher than UCC, with a median sum 14 times that of UCC.
Many other CMs are also high in red mud. Median Al is 10,000 times higher than UCC in the
Copano samples which is expected. Median concentrations of some other CMs are also much
higher than those in the UCC, including Mg, P, Ti, V, Cr, Mn, Ni, Zn, and Zi.
To estimate the potential value of REYs from red mud, we converted the REY values to REOs and
used the value of REOs and estimated volume of red mud at the Copano site (120 million tonnes).
Assuming extractability of the REYs of 50% resulted in REY oxide value of $4.5 billion, REYSc of
$11.6 billion and permanent magnet oxide value (Pr+Nd+Tb+Dy) of $3.6 billion.
3.4b Background
The following provides background information related to bauxite and red mud in the Gulf Coast
and summarizes previous work conducted on the mineral composition and preliminary analyses of
red mud at the Texas Copano site (Kyle et al., 2025).
Production and Natural Occurrence
In the Gulf Coast Basin, alumina production occurred in Alabama and Texas with bauxite ore
occurrences in Alabama. The source of Alabama's bauxite ore is primarily constrained to the
Eufaula and Richards Crossroads (Bollen et al., 2024). The Eufaula district, situated in Barbour and
Henry Counties, has historically been the most productive, with ~ 400,000 tonnes mined through
1943. Mining began in 1927 and continued intermittently, with significant production during
World War II. The bauxite from Eufaula was primarily sent to Eufaula for shipment and
refinement, with some ore processed locally in Abbeville. The Mount Andrew district is notable
for its bauxitic clay and ilmenite deposits. The Richards Crossroads district, located near Baker Hill,
has seen limited production but is known for its high-iron bauxite.
Texas production of alumina from foreign-sourced bauxites occurred from 1953 until 2016 with
refinery locations in the middle Texas coast at a site in Copano near Corpus Christi and Point

 36

Comfort and (Fig. 10). Total processed bauxite is estimated to be ~326 million tonnes, producing
an estimated 163 million tonnes of bauxite residue at these Texas sites (US Bureau of Mines and
USGS Mineral Yearbooks).
Physical Character of Bauxite Residue
The Copano bauxite residue or red mud consists of distinctly red, fine-grained sediments with
sufficient moisture to maintain an in-situ plastic consistency. High resolution X-ray computed
tomography (XCT) was used to gain a better understanding of the CBR components. The red mud
consists of: (1) natural “resistate” mineral grains that remained unaffected by the Bayer Process,
(2) synthetic minerals created during the Bayer reactions, (3) unreacted NaOH reagent, and (4) an
aqueous fluid in pores possibly affected by surface fluid infiltration or mineral reactions over 20+
years. The petrography of Copano samples were evaluated using X-ray computed tomography,
and X-ray EDS elemental composite map of resistate grains and chemical compounds in Fe-Al
hydroxide matrix, and X-ray EDS map of zircon and quartz grains in residue matrix (Fig. 11).
Chemical Composition of Bauxite Residue
A summary of Copano bauxite residue chemistry from this study is compared with published data
for Jamaican red mud (Wagh & Pinnock, 1987). Major and trace element chemistry was assessed.
These results were compared with an archival Jamaican bauxite sample, as well as with bauxite
residue from Jamaican storage sites.
The major chemical components of Jamaican bauxites (Wagh & Pinnock, 1987; Lalor, 1995) are
representative of lateritic soils developed from an Fe-rich volcanic protolith, as interpreted for the
origin of Jamaica bauxite (Comer, 1974). Al2O3 is the dominant component (>40%), as required for
a commercial Al source, along with a low SiO2 (~1%) content. Iron is the second highest metal
component (~20% Fe2O3); more than 25% of the sample is LOI, presumably largely related to
hydrous Al and Fe oxides. TiO2 is the range of 2-5% due to various Ti minerals; P2O5 approaches
1%.
Mineral Composition
Powdered Copano red mud samples and of the archival Jamaica bauxite were analyzed by X-ray
diffraction, producing quantitative modal mineralogy. The bauxite sample results confirmed the
major mineral assemblage for Jamaican bauxite defined by other studies (e.g., Wagh & Pinnock,
1987). The predicted result of the Bayer process is apparent, cf. reduction of the alumina content
minerals and an increase of the resistate components.
SEM reconnaissance of Copano red mud generated a better understanding of textural and
component features (Fig. 11). Residual Al-Fe hydroxides, as well as resistate minerals quartz,
zircon, and titanium (±Fe) oxides are present. REE-bearing phases were detected in association
with Ba-rich grains, suggesting relative enrichment of the heavy REE which affects economic
potential. Further work remains to characterize the REE occurrences (e.g., Vind et al., 2018).
3.4c Methods
This investigation is concerned primarily with the Copano Enterprise LLC bauxite residue storage
site in Aransas and San Patricio counties, Texas, that received waste from the Sherwin plant ~12
km south (American Conservative Values ETF, ACVF, 2022, 2023) that processed Jamaican bauxite.
Four bauxite residue impoundments, termed Beds 1 through 4, totaling ~3,100 acres (1,255
hectares), were developed on an 11,000-acre (4,450-hectare) tract (Fig. 10). Bed 1 began to

 37

receive bauxite residue in 1968, Bed 2 in 1972, and Bed 3 in 1978 (TCEQ, 2020). Limited analyses
of Point Comfort samples are also considered.
A total of 26 samples were analyzed for REEs and critical minerals (CMs), 17 samples from the
Copano site and nine from the Point Comfort site. The sample locations are shown in Fig. 10.
These analyses were conducted at Standard Labs with the exception of one sample from the
Copano site that was analyzed in a previous study (Kyle et al., 2025).
3.4d Results
Total REE + yttrium (REY) concentrations were much higher in the Copano site (median REY: 1988
ppm) relative to the Point Comfort site (median: 54) (Fig. 12). All of the Copano samples were high
(REY range: 922 – 3330 ppm) and the Point Comfort samples were all low (REY: 269 – 436 ppm).
Four REEs are of substantial interest for permanent magnets (Pr+Nd+Tb+Dy) with median sum of
415 ppm in Copano samples relative to 73 for Point Comfort samples. The differences in REY
concentrations between the two sites likely reflect differences in concentrations in the source
material, which is predominantly Jamaican bauxite for Copano site and Brazil for Point Comfort
site.
Comparison of REY concentrations with those in the Upper Continental Crust (UCC) results in a
median ratio of 12 for the Copano samples relative to a ratio of 2 for the Point Comfort samples.
The following description focuses on the Copano samples (Fig. 13). Each of the REEs are much
more concentrated than the UCC, with medians ranging from 8 (Ce) to 24 (Y). The REEs that are
important for permanent magnets (Pr, Nd, Tb, and Dy) are also much higher than UCC, with a
median sum 14 times that of UCC.
Many other CMs are also high in red mud. Median Al is 10,000 times higher than UCC in the
Copano samples (Fig. 14). Median concentrations of some other CMs are also much higher than
those in the UCC, including Mg, P, Ti, V, Cr, Mn, Ni, Zn, and Zi.
Potential Value of Rare Earth Elements in Red Mud
The value of the REEs at the Copano site was estimated by converting REE values to Rare Earth
Oxides (REOs) and multiplying the estimated tonnage of red mud at the site by the value of each
REO. The depth of each bed varies by bed (Bed 1, ~ 12 m, Bed 2, ~ 5 m, Bed 3, 3.5 m, and Bed 4 ~
0.5 m). The US Geological Survey estimated the tonnage of red mud from the volume of alumina
produced in Texas at ~ 163 million tonnes. If we assume ~ 120 million tonnes at the Copano site
and extractability of REEs of 50%, then the value of the median REYs as oxides is $4.5 billion,
median REYSc as oxides is $11.6 billion, and median Pr+Nd+Tb+Dy for permanent magnets as
oxides is ~ $3.6 billion (Table 4).
3.4e Conclusions
Waste generated from aluminum processing of bauxite in the Gulf Coast, including Copano and
Point Comfort in Texas and Gramercy and Burnside in Louisiana provide potential feedstocks for
REE and CM extraction. Red mud generated from the Bayer process contains concentrated REEs,
at a minimum related to the removal of Al from the bauxite. These Gulf Coast sites are vulnerable
to storm surge and sea level rise; which suggests that the value of the REEs could offset site
remediation costs.

3.5 Heavy Mineral Concentrates from Sand Mining Industry
Throughout the Gulf Coast Basin, heavy mineral sands have accumulated in coastal and near-
shore environments as a result of long-term, sedimentary processes. Limited analysis of heavy
mineral sands was conducted in Alabama and Texas.
Heavy mineral sand resources in the Alabama East Gulf Coastal Plain include Gulf coastal beaches
and lithostratigraphic units of the Tuscaloosa Group and its Coker Formation and Gordo
Formation; Ripley Formation and its Cusseta Sand Member; Providence Sand; Tallahatta
Formation and its Meridian Sand Member; and Lisbon Formation (Bollen et al., 2024). Estimated
heavy mineral abundances from thin sections of beach sand samples range from 36 to 84 percent.
The thin section analysis revealed heavy minerals including magnetite, ilmenite, rutile, kyanite,
staurolite, epidote, tourmaline, and zircon. Analyses of 12 heavy mineral sand samples from these
geologic units show REY values ranging from 36 to 755 ppm (median 115 ppm) with only one
sample exceeding 300 ppm. All samples except one have REY levels less than the UCC.
In Texas, heavy mineral sands are primarily sourced from metamorphic and igneous rocks,
contributing to the state’s heavy mineral placer deposits. The repeated cycle of source rock
erosion, transport, and deposition have been ongoing for over 1 billion years, before eventual
deposition along the flanks of the Llano Uplift in central Texas sandstone formations and along the
Texas Gulf Coastal Plain sediments in the form of Cenozoic beach placer sands along regressing
shorelines to their current location. These heavy mineral sands are often enriched in Ti, REY, Th,
and uranium (U) (Van Gosen et al., 2017). The highest concentrations of zircon, monazite,
magnetite, rutile, ilmenite, and other heavy minerals in beach sand samples typically coincide
with the highest REE, Ti, and V concentrations. The elevated REEs and Zr with minor U are
indicative of zircon as, at least, a partial source for REEs in the beach sand samples. Ti and V are
predominantly sourced from Fe-Ti oxides, such rutile, magnetite, and ilmenite. Mineral
identification from petrographic analysis identified sparse heavy minerals, including kyanite,
tourmaline, staurolite, magnetite, and ilmenite. Previous studies of south Texas heavy mineral
localities identified mostly magnetite and ilmenite, between 50-87%. Zircon ranges from 4-23%,
leucoxene ranged from 2-14%, tourmaline up to about 4%, with minor amounts of staurolite,
kyanite, spinel, corun-dum, garnet, rutile, limonite, biotite, apatite, titanite, monazite, and
fluorite.
Limited sampling (4 samples) of heavy mineral separates was conducted from hydraulic fracturing
sand mine operations around the flanks of the Llano Uplift in central Texas, mainly from the
Hickory sandstone (Fig. 15). These samples had undergone physical processing to concentrate the
heavy minerals at the site. Heavy minerals composed of ~ 2% of the mined material and consisted
mainly of magnetite, ilmenite, zircon, monazite, and titanite. The control sample that was passed
through a 100-mesh resulted in low levels of REEs (Median REY 82 ppm). However, one of the
density-separated samples had very high concentrations of REEs, exceeding the analytical process
(> 1000 ppm for many REEs, including Pr and Nd and Dy. These preliminary results suggest that
physical processing could concentrate the REEs within the heavy mineral sands. Mining operations
in Texas have ~ 178 million tons of reserves, and ~4 million tons of heavy mineral concentrate.

3.7 Graphite
Graphite is found in different regions of the Gulf Coast. Graphite is common in the Packsaddle
schist around the flanks of the Precambrian Llano Uplift in central Texas, particularly in Llano and
Burnet counties (Fig. 19). This schist series was originally a sedimentary series composed mostly
of argillaceous, calcareous, arenaceous, and carbonaceous sediment mixtures, with the
carbonaceous material metamorphosing into graphite during the formation of Proterozoic
bedrock and intrusion of 1.082 Ga Town Mountain granite. The graphite content of these rocks
varies in amount, correlating with the carbonaceous material in the original sediments (Fig. 20).
The schist bands carrying graphite vary in width from a fraction of a meter to ~50 m and can be
traced for a significant distance, often intermittently, for several km. The graphite occurs in a
variety of schist compositions, but typically in schists with high silica content. The flakes or crystals
are well distributed and of unequal size. Crystals are elongated parallel to schistosity. Crystal size
of the average material from ten sites is reported to be predominantly medium or small. Large
crystals (+30 mesh) are less common and occur only in quartz sills or dikes, in pegmatite dikes,
augen structures or intrusive stringers of varying composition. Hand specimens generally contain
quartz, graphite, muscovite, feldspar, and a host of minor constituents such as pyrite, actinolite,
and vesuvianite. Ores contain varying quantities of graphite from a trace to 20 percent.
Samples of graphite-bearing Packsaddle schist from the Southwestern Graphite Mine show
graphite comprising up to ~12% of prospective ore-body delineated zones. Samples from mine
tailings, consisting of about 2 million tons of remaining material, contain up to ~3.5% graphite (see
BSE images in Figure 20, for example). These tailings contain 2-18% graphitic clay, 20-35% quartz,
5-8% K-feldspar, 14-36% plagioclase, 2-12% biotite, and accessory muscovite, amphibole,
clinochlore, pyroxene, garnet, rutile, ilmenite, leucoxene, apatite, xenotime, monazite and zircon.
While most of the mined flake graphite from whole rock material was >80 mesh, tailings contain
primarily flake sizes greater than 50 mesh (see Figure 21).

4.4 Gap Analysis and Challenges
Much of the extraction, processing, refining, and manufacturing technology that could be used for
CORE-CM materials from nontraditional sources remains uncommercialized. As a result, the
economics associated with implementing these critical mineral technologies and directly
measuring their impact on regional economy is speculative. Identifying strategies for projected
CORE-CM opportunities, implementation challenges, and information gaps is still critical to
establishing the necessary infrastructure for production and refinement of critical minerals and
 57

rare earths from nontraditional resources.
Most of the technical challenges to establishing a domestic supply chain for CORE-CM materials
lies with the establishment of regional processing and extraction facilities. The economic barriers
to entry are related to critical mineral extraction techniques, lack of expertise in intermediate and
end product supply chain links, cost of production, and uncertainty in the reliability of
domestically produced critical minerals and rare earths to be competitive with their foreign
counterparts.
Many of the processing and extraction technologies for CORE-CM products are still in
development and economic evaluation phases. While some technologies are moving into pilot
scale phases, there are several viable options being considered and will eventually need economic
considerations before large-scale adoption. The uncertainty of which of these technologies will
reach commercialization and be able to absorb the associated costs of integration will largely be
mitigated by whole scale extraction of primary, secondary, tertiary etc. products that maximize
economic potential of feedstocks, and/or include tangible socio-environmental benefits, like
waste stream feedstocks.
Uncertainty with market stability, unknown costs, sustainable feedstocks, funding opportunities,
and development of more economical technologies are significant business risks. Producing
products from coal ore and extracting critical minerals and rare earths from waste streams is new
to most businesses, and a change from standard operations. If the upfront capital costs or
operating and maintenance costs are too high, businesses may forego the opportunity or delay
development and wait for government intervention to ensure some level of profitability and
limited risk. Because those costs are not yet known in most cases, potential CORE-CM market
entrants may be deterred from early investment. Market manipulation, price setting by foreign
entities - like China, is probably the largest risk and greatest barrier to entry for businesses. There
is a real risk that even with initially promising benefits, a new entrant into the market may not be
sufficiently efficient and economical to remain competitive without subsidy or a government set
price floor.
4.5 Strategies for Infrastructure, Industries, and Business Assessment
The existing infrastructure, industries, and businesses built up around coal and industrial mineral
mining in the Gulf Coast basin have the necessary connections for the development of critical
mineral technologies. These industries already have specialized laborers in the mining,
transportation, and construction industries that can be utilized in CORE-CM related initiatives. The
Gulf Coast region may have a comparative advantage over other areas, particularly the supporting
industry for mining, quarrying and oil and gas, could be the most economical and efficient path
towards extracting, processing, refining, and manufacturing CORE-CM materials. From a business
perspective, the tax structure and regulatory framework in Texas is well-suited for investment and
new development in the region.
Coal mines have been established in the region for over a century, where novel CORE-CM
technologies that utilize coal and coal waste streams may have fewer barriers to entering the
market than those focusing on critical mineral extraction and refinement from other feedstocks.
The feedstock is already being exploited for energy generation, and mines have demonstrated an
excess capacity to serve new customers as new energy generation comes online and coal energy
declines. Trucking and rail companies provide transportation services for the coal industry and can

 58

facilitate new industry needs.
One of the most pressing questions for developing initial commercialization plans is which critical
minerals and technologies should be focused on. While the costs associated with integrating novel
technologies into the CORE-CM supply chain remain largely unknown, diminishing domestic
reliance on foreign critical minerals and rare earths is a high priority for the CORE-CM initiative.
Therefore, looking at which critical minerals are most valuable and abundant, identifying waste
streams with the least requirements for further refinement and the economics of processing and
extraction will determine commercialization ranking. Traditionally mined critical minerals and
existing processing and manufacturing plants, including interregional options, merit some
consideration as these may prove to be more economical options for Gulf Coast Basin resources in
developing a CORE-CM supply chain.
Data on domestic consumption, domestic production, imports, exports, and primary uses of each
critical mineral can be evaluated from the last 20 years of commodity statistics and information
from the USGS National Minerals Information Center. Only those materials which have promising
concentrations in the Gulf Coast basin are considered. The intent of evaluating historic data and
material characterized through the CORE-CM project is to inform decisions regarding which
critical minerals and technology to prioritize when multiple options are available.
4.6 Discussion
The CORE-CM project work provides a foundation for future research. We have identified the
strengths and weaknesses of the infrastructure, industries, and businesses that have been built up
around the mining industry in the Gulf Coast basin. Data was collected that will help develop a
framework for which technology innovation centers can be located, scaled and optimized.
Evaluation and selection of new technologies will be easier as more information related to mineral
criticality, technology readiness level, availability of feedstock, cost, and ease of extraction is
released. In the next phase, we intend on broadening the target industries and feedstocks for
expanding the area and scope of the geospatial data. Businesses in the manufacturing sector will
be of particular interest, and geospatial and economic data will grow to encompass trends in
education and workforce transition. Economic data, industry employment and wages will be
updated to visualize how they have changed over time and how they compare with other regions.
Interregional cooperation will also be prominently featured in future phases, leveraging each
region’s comparative advantages for mining, extracting, refining, and manufacturing intermediate
and end products. Natural resource endowments for each region, availability of feedstock,
infrastructure, and governmental structure will be compared and ranked. With greater access to
critical mineral commodity prices and cost of commercialization data, goals for technology
innovation centers will be established to evaluate competitiveness in a global market.
4.7 Conclusions
The conclusions from analyzing industry, resource, geospatial, and economic data suggest that the
Gulf Coast basin is well-suited to take on the task of integrating novel CORE-CM technologies into
the supply chain and expanding existing infrastructure, industries, and businesses. The production
capacity and supporting industries for coal and industrial mineral mines provides a solid
framework for critical mineral development in the basin. Waste streams from mined or processed
resources, such as coal waste or alumina processing waste, are promising feedstocks for critical
mineral extraction. The tax structure, lax regulatory framework and well-developed

 59

transportation infrastructure are solid advantages for development of a regional critical mineral
industry.
The CORE-CM initiative is still developing, and businesses have concerns regarding government
regulations, cost of production, demand for domestically produced critical minerals, and
government incentives to mitigate the risk of sustainable production against foreign competition.
The abundance of skilled labor but needing to re-train the workforce for critical mineral
specialized tasks will need to be considered. Overall, the advantages in the region outweigh the
prospective challenges, as long as the market is stable and a price floor is established to make U.S.
business ventures profitable.

identification of any CORE-CM-related technologies, both conventional and novel, requires an

manufacturing, and specific to the Gulf Coast basin.
Technologies related to extraction and processing of CMs and REEs from coal leveraged previous

5.3 Findings and Recommendations
Preliminary analysis of the extractability of REEs from Gulf Coast lignites indicates that they
behave similarly to lignites in the Williston Basin. Results show high extractability with weak,
environmentally benign acids with low environmental impacts. The extractability analyses were
conducted at the Univ. of North Dakota using the same equipment and chemicals as applied to
their lignites. Therefore, the Gulf Coast CORE-CM project will benefit from the lab-scale, bench-
scale, and pilot-scale testing that has been conducted in North Dakota (Mann et al., 2022). The
facility in North Dakota is designed for 1,000 lbs.hr of lignite feedstock to produce sufficient REEs
for an electric vehicle (EV) and generate a concentrate which includes a mixture of all REEs/CMs.
The Gulf Coast CORE-CM project will also benefit from the Techno-economic analysis conducted in
North Dakota that evaluated various byproducts, including humic acids, building materials, and
battery materials.
The Gulf Coast CORE-CM team will collaborate with the Powder River Basin CORE-CM team on use
of coal ash for REEs and CMs (Bagdonas et al., 2024). These technologies and economic analysis
will apply to power plants that use PRB coals for power generation on the Gulf Coast.
Technologies for extracting REEs and CMs from red mud will be based on collaboration with ElementUSA
and other relevant companies who are currently using red mud as a feedstock for REEs and CMs.
ElementUSA has established an REE and CM extraction technology incubator in Austin Texas and is
collaborating with the Jackson School of Geosciences at the University of Texas. We will benefit from their
research. They are also planning student internships to facilitate knowledge exchange.
Preliminary results of REE levels in heavy mineral sands from frac sand facilities in Texas show promise in
having high levels of REEs after some density concentration steps at these facilities. We will coordinate
with these companies to further develop these technologies.
We will continue to work with companies on zeolite and graphite to develop these resources.
We are working closely with industry and with the Texas Produced Water Consortium, which is involved
with treatment of produced water, to assess the potential for Li extraction from produced water. We are
also working with various groups in the potential for Li extraction from the Smackover and deep Edwards
sources in the Gulf Coast.

6.2 Background
The main objective for developing the Technology Innovation Centers (TIC’s) plan for the Gulf Coast
CORE-CM project is to develop initial plans that could be implemented to advance technology
innovation, economic growth, and job creation related to development of supply chains for critical
minerals and carbon ore. The analysis focused on identifying various technology solutions and new
research required to test these technologies with various potential feedstocks to achieve sustainable
goals with respect to CORE-CM supply chains within the context of regional supply chain demand.
We also discussed opportunities with other CORE-CM basinal projects to help meet U.S. domestic
supply goals. Important aspects include resource assessment technologies, advanced mining,
extraction and separation technologies specific to the different feedstocks, co-production and
processing of carbon ore and related waste feedstocks that contain REEs and CMs, and considering
existing infrastructure and businesses across the Gulf Coast Basin. Additional factors include
availability of a suitable workforce and training and working with regional community colleges to
develop suitable training programs.
The TIC plan focuses on basinal strengths by emphasizing basinal resources which show the most
promise for regional advantage, basinal infrastructure, and current industry that can be utilized for
TIC development, and inclusion of waste streams. Combining all of these aspects should enhance
training, and technology development and commercialization within the Gulf Coast Basin. The plan
emphasizes technologies and processes that minimize adverse impacts to the environment and
consider community health and well-being involved in the development and implementation of TICs.
As a result, the expectation is that development of new CM and REE resources, if economically
viable in an U.S. domestic market, will greatly improve the security of CM and REE domestic supply,
develop new portions of supply chains within the Gulf Coast Basin and maximize use of existing
supply chain components.
The main drivers for conducting TIC activities include:
i. Expansion of local economic and job opportunities to enhance CORE-CM supply within the
 Gulf Coast Basin.

 investment designed to achieve CORE-CM supply chain goals.

6.5 CORE-CM Feedstock Opportunities
There are a variety of potential feedstocks available in the Gulf Coast Basin. Primary sources include
coal (lignite) whereas secondary sources include waste streams from coal-fired power plants (coal
ash), oil and gas production (produced water), frac sand operations (heavy mineral sands), and red
mud from alumina processing, and graphite and zeolite mines.
Ranking various feedstocks depends on various drivers, including (1) concentrations and
extractability, (2) techno-economic viability, and (3) abundance to support long-term production.
Feedstock values reflect the relationship between supply and demand which can be highly volatile.
Projected demand for REEs used for permanent magnets, including praseodymium, neodymium,
terbium, and dysprosium, is projected to increase in demand substantially to support clean energy
technologies, including wind turbines and electric vehicles. In addition, domestic supplies for these
REEs are limited. Various technologies to extract and process REEs are currently at different
technology readiness levels (TRLs). The TICs will focus on the most promising technologies,
emphasizing feedstocks that are readily available, adapt to changing criticality levels over time, and

 18

determine threshold pricing for economic viability. The highest priorities will be assigned to the most
promising feedstocks that have already been refined and include CMs with high priority ranking. In
addition, emphasis will be placed on supply chains with minimal environmental impacts that include
byproducts to minimize waste generation.
The clean energy transition is a major driver of CM and REE demand, particularly magnets and
batteries. Demand for energy storage solutions is increasing and graphite is becoming more and
more important to address intermittency linked to renewable energy sources (e.g., wind and solar).
Demand for REEs used in batteries is also increasing rapidly for electric vehicles (EVs) and utility-
scale energy storage industry and is projected to continue to grow.
6.6 Current Technology Innovation Center Priorities
There is a wide range of potential technology options for processing CMs and REEs that should be
considered in TIC developing in the Gulf Coast region. TICs should be broad and comprehensive and
not select winners in terms of technologies too early. Gulf Coast TICs can benefit from advances in
mining developed in Wyoming, N Dakota and other regions. The USGS has been applying remote
sensing to map coal ash feedstocks in the Gulf Coast.
One potential TIC option is collaboration with the CORE-CM team in the Williston Basin and applying
the extraction and mixed REO technology that they have developed for lignite. Preliminary analysis
of lignites from the Gulf Coast suggest that they are similar to those in the Williston Basin; therefore,
the lab, bench and pilot testing that has been developed at the Univ. of North Dakota should apply
to the Gulf Coast lignites. In addition, the CORE-CM team in the Williston Basin has been working
with Rare Earth Salts in Nebraska to process the oxides produced in North Dakota and develop pure
metal output. We have also discussed this option with Rare Earth Salts to process output from the
Gulf Coast Basin. Rare Earth Salts is a rare earth element separation company that uses non-solvent
extraction technologies.
Another TIC option would result from collaboration with the University of Wyoming CORE-CM team
to process ash from power plants in the Gulf Coast. This TIC would focus on ash derived from
burning Wyoming coals in power plants in the Gulf Coast basin. The Gulf Coast team includes
members from the Dept. of Petroleum engineering working on extraction of REEs and CMs from coal

Stakeholder outreach and education was challenging because of the limited information on critical
mineral (CM) and rare earth element (REE) levels in different potential sources in the Gulf Coast

feedstocks and developing information to share with various stakeholders and educators. The lack of

various potential stakeholders. We focused on outreach with relevant businesses and industries as
part of the CORE-CM project in the Gulf Coast Basin. We conducted outreach efforts in collaboration
with our project partners, particularly the Geological Survey of Alabama.

many aspects important to stakeholder outreach from these meetings, including best practices.

7.5 Potential Collaborations
During Phase I of the CORE-CM project, the team emphasized networking with other CORE-CM
groups and included those groups in the project team: Wyoming (Davin Bagdonas) and N Dakota
(Nolan Theaker). There was a lot of overlap with these teams because both N Dakota and the Gulf
Coast produce lignite and N Dakota was much more advanced in their analyses of REEs in lignite and
processing approaches. Therefore, we benefited substantially from this collaboration. The Gulf Coast
also imports a lot of coal from Wyoming and the data generated from WY on REEs from coal ash was
very helpful for our program.
Early outreach efforts focused on providing an understanding of all aspects of the CORE-CM supply
chain through virtual online presentations that were recorded and shared with a large group of
stakeholders. The Advisory Committee also helped the team understand what would be required to
develop a successful CORE-CM program.
We are continuing to expand our network with businesses, including industries with potential
feedstocks (coal companies, power plants, frac sand companies, alumina companies, etc.).
We will increase our work with regulatory agencies, including the Railroad Commission of Texas
(RCT) and TCEQ because there are many additional regulations related to management of produced
water and beneficial uses of treated produced water from oil and gas production.
The team and the Gulf Coast have strong connections with the oil and gas industry and that
facilitated interactions with industry on produced water and potential CMs to offset management of
produced water. We also benefited by leveraging consortia at the Bureau of Economic Geology.
We will also increase our connections with the Texas Produced Water Consortium and New Mexico
Produced Water Research Consortium as they bridge the gap between industry and regulatory
agencies, including TCEQ and EPA, and we can learn a lot from their work.
The CORE-CM team included many individuals from academia and each of the team members
interacted with students in their universities. Having Dr. Peter Warwick on the team also connected
us with the USGS and related programs, such as Earth MRI. We will also work with community
colleges in critical regions where there is proposed development of CM and REEs.

Chapter 8: Phase 1 Gaps and Future Work
8.1 Gaps from Phase 1
8.1a Characterization of Critical Minerals and Rare Earth Elements in Gulf Coast Coals and Coal Ash
A number of gaps were identified during Phase 1 of the CORE-CM project. During Phase 1 coal
samples were primarily derived from archived samples at USGS headquarters in Reston Virginia.
These samples were collected from coal mines that were open during the 1970s and 1980s.
Additional detailed sampling was conducted in San Miguel Mine in Texas during Phase 1. However,
one of the gaps identified during Phase 1 was the limited number of samples available for CM/REE
analysis in the Gulf Coast Basin (~ 118 samples). The number and location of samples should be
expanded to more fully characterize the CM/REE levels in the Gulf Coast region in the future.
One of the major gaps during Phase 1 of the CORE-CM project was obtaining samples of coal ash for
analysis of CM and REEs. A total of 24 power plant facilities located in Texas and Louisiana were
short-listed out of the ERCOT database to contact them about sampling coal ash. The EPA Coal
Combustion Rule - CCR - regulates the handling, storage, and disposal of coal combustion residuals;
however, sharing any chemical data was a sensitive issue. We provided a list of chemical
constituents that we wished to sample and discussed various options with some of the power plant
staff. We also provided confidentiality language to ensure that any cooperating facility would be
comfortable allowing us to analyze their ash samples. After initial research as to the status of the
facilities, many were found to have closed and/or are in the process of converting them to natural
gas. We identified a total of 13 active facilities. Because of the sensitivity of the chemical data, we
did not receive permission to sample most of the power plant facilities even though we were willing
to sign confidentiality agreements. Phase 2 of CORE-CM program expands the study area beyond the
Gulf Coast Basin and new team members from Alabama have access to major power plant
companies to sample ash resources.
Another gap in Phase 1 was limited analysis of the extractability of REEs from coal and coal ash. We
relied on similarities between REE extractability from a limited number of lignite samples from the
Gulf Coast with REE extractability from lignite in the Williston Basin in North Dakota to strengthen
the argument that REEs from lignites are readily extractable. We will expand the number of samples
that will be tested for extractability in Phase 2 and also expand analysis of REE and CM extractability
to coal ash samples from the Gulf Coast also.
8.1b Characterization of Rare Earth Elements in Produced Water
Limited characterization of lithium in produced water in the Gulf Coast and Permian basin regions
was conducted during Phase 1 and results described in Darvari et al., (2024). However, produced
water has not been analyzed for REEs or CMs. Phase 2 will address this gap and new instrumentation
has been purchased at Texas Tech Univ. and the Univ. of Texas at Austin to analyze produced water
from the Permian Basin and other potential sources of produced water and brines within the Gulf
Coast Basin.
8.2 Future Work in Phase 2 of CORE-CM
Future work will focus on the Gulf Coast and Permian Basin, extending across the states of Alabama (AL),
Mississippi (MS), Louisiana (LA), Texas (TX) and southern New Mexico (NM) (Fig. 24). The Gulf Coast and
Permian Basin provide an ideal region for expanding on CORE-CM Phase 1 and enhancing economic and
workforce development.

NM, TX, LA, MS, and AL.

8.2a Characterization of CM/REEs from Coal, Coal Ash Derivatives, and Coal Mine Waste Materials
Future work during Phase 2 will build on the previous assessments of coal and ash resources and related CMs
and REEs by filling gaps identified during Phase 1 and expanding the work throughout the Gulf coast region
(Fig. 25). State-of-the-art approaches will be applied to modeling the geology of coals in the region. Previous
work included development of regional isopleth maps of cumulative coal thickness along with overburden
thickness using data from 31,000 boreholes in the Gulf Coast Basin. Phase II will involve updating these maps
and expansion beyond the Gulf Coast Basin across Region 5 area, including coals in north-central TX, eastern
NM, and the Black Warrior Basin and other southern Appalachian coals in AL. In addition, detailed 3D geologic
modeling of CM/REE resources will be conducted in hotspots that were identified in Phase I (e.g., Gibbons
Creek and San Miguel Mine in TX). More detailed assessment of coal ash resources will be conducted,
expanding on the ash resource assessment conducted in Phase 1 (Reedy et al., 2024).
Future work will involve increased sampling of active coal mines in the Gulf Coast region for CMs and REEs.
Many samples will be collected of coal ash at power plants to expand on the limited sampling conducted
during Phase 1. Connections with power plant operators will be facilitated by working with the Electrical
Power Resource Institute and building on CM/REE data from ash in power plants in Alabama (Donahoe et al.,
2023; Tew and Donahoe, 2023). Coal ash samples will be analyzed for CMs and REEs.
In addition, Phase 2 will include evaluation of CM/REE extractability from coal and coal ash, leveraging lessons
learned from previous experience with North Dakota lignites and limited analyses of Gulf Coast lignites. We
will also leverage previous experience of CM/REE extraction from coal ash (Gerardo et al., 2022, 2023). We
will conduct detailed evaluation of CM/REE mode of occurrence in coal and coal ash. Future work will include
lab- and bench-scale pilot plants, learning from experience with lignite in N Dakota and ashes in the Powder
River Basin in WY. Output from these tests will feed data into a techno-economic assessment (TEA).
Connections with industry partners will be expanded, including REE/CM processing industries. Technology
Innovation Center (TIC) planning will be continued, technology gaps identified, and stakeholder outreach and
education expanded. Many of these elements initiated in Phase 1 of CORE-CM will be continued through
Phase 2, with completion of the regional resources and expansion of industry liaisons.

8.2b Characterization of CMs/REEs in Other Nonfuel Mine and Mineral Processing Material, Drill
 Cuttings, Drilled Core, Sedimentary Rocks
Project participants have identified nonfuel mine and mineral processing material, drill cuttings, drilled core
and sedimentary rocks with CM/REE potential. The region has more than 20 industrial sand mines that
produce hydraulic fracturing sand for the petroleum industry (Fig. 26). These mines separate heavy minerals
during washing and sorting and have potential for CM/REE development. The coalition will analyze regional
frac sand mine waste piles to assess the potential of these heavy sands as a CM/REE feedstock. Future work
will determine mineral constituents, mineral chemistry and potential ore resources. Similarly, bauxite residue
waste generated during alumina processing has potential for CM/REE development. Future work will engage
with industry partners (Alcoa, ElementUSA) to sample bauxite processing residue (red mud) at sites, such as
Point Comfort (TX), and Grammercy site (LA) across the region, and will characterize and assess the potential
of these materials for CM and REE extraction.
Sedimentary units in the region have the potential to host CMs and REEs and will be evaluated in future work.
West TX and New Mexico already have areas identified for historic ore production and recently recognized as
having elevated CM/REE concentrations. There are also strata-bound copper deposits (red bed copper
deposits) and copper, tungsten, tin prospects associated with volcanism, and potential stratiform cobalt
associated with historic copper mining in New Mexico. Potash deposits are known to occur in northwest
Texas and eastern New Mexico. These deposits will be evaluated and characterized for CM/REE potential and
assessed as a resource.
Texas core facilities alone house more than 2 million boxes of drill core that will be evaluated from CM/REE
potential. We will also identify and characterize formations that host subsurface brines and have the
potential for CM industry development through infrastructure and economic analysis. We will also evaluate
the potential for repurposing of fossil-fuel infrastructure to promote the energy transition. Future work will
perform comprehensive geochemical analysis of selected drilled cores and cuttings and sedimentary rocks
from across the region. The data will be compared and evaluated with brine chemistry and inorganic
compositions from oil to better understand the CM distribution and evolution at the scale of formation, basin,
and regional level.
Future work will explore the potential for CM/REE extraction from sampled drill core and cuttings using a
synthetic enzyme technology originally developed for hydrolysis of lithium aluminosilicates from oil shale. The
technology expands the capability to add value to waste streams and repurpose boreholes drilled for oil and
gas production for in situ extraction of CMs. Lab-scale testing will include high throughput activity and
reaction kinetics at a microscale (300 μL) to adapt reaction parameters to the new substrate. The lab-scale

demonstration will allow determination of CM extraction efficiency and inform the TEA and scale-up viability
of the technology for CM recovery from shales cuttings and other geologic material feedstocks.

8.3 OVERALL CONCLUSIONS and RECOMMENDATIONS
The work conducted in this first phase only begins the understanding of the potential of REE and CM
resources in the Williston Basin. More data throughout the basin must be collected in the form of
sample cores, drilling logs, and waste streams to better identify locations of high concentrations of
these resources. Work performed to date indicates potential sites suitable for mining and extraction
of REEs and CMs within the basin.
The infrastructure in the Williston Basin is very suitable for the creation of an industry to remove,
extract, and concentrate REEs and CMs with the ability to generate new jobs. The extent of the
supply chain that can be sustained within the basin needs further study. With much of the
technology for this industry lying outside of the United States, further work is needed to improve
domestic extraction techniques for REEs and CMs.
There are many resources that can be utilized in the Williston Basin to develop a TIC. Accompanying
the center, the development of educational pathways is also needed to train the workforce in the
technological skills required. Continued outreach is mandatory to continue to educate the public,
state legislators and departments, and industry on the potential of this new industry in the basin.
