HOW TO DO IT

Frey procedure with distal pancreatectomy: How to do it

Abstract
Although the Frey procedure effectively treats chronic pancreatitis, late relapse can occur in the pancreatic tail. This paper
details the essential steps of combining the Frey procedure with distal pancreatectomy (Frey + DP) to address this issue.
The key surgical steps include distal pancreatectomy, longitudinal opening of the main pancreatic duct, pancreatic head
coring, and longitudinal pancreaticojejunostomy. Comparing the outcomes of nine Frey + DP and 13 Frey-alone patients
(2016–2024), Frey + DP had significantly longer operative times and higher estimated blood loss. However, the postoperative hospital stay, mortality, 1-year pain recurrence, and new-onset diabetes were comparable between the two groups.
Although more invasive, Frey + DP definitively treats concurrent severe tail disease, mitigating the risk of late relapse
and requiring difficult reoperations. Frey + DP is a safe and effective approach for patients with chronic pancreatitis with
inflammatory lesions in both the pancreatic head and tail.
Keywords Frey procedure · Distal pancreatectomy · Chronic pancreatitis

Introduction
The Frey procedure, which involves coring out the pancreatic head and performing a longitudinal pancreaticojejunostomy, is a well-established and effective surgical technique
for managing chronic pancreatitis [1]. It offers significant
pain relief by decompressing the complex branch ducts in
the pancreatic head, which are often implicated in the persistent pain associated with this condition [2, 3].
However, a challenge identified in long-term follow-up
is the potential for relapse of inflammation in the pancreatic
tail in some patients [4]. To overcome this limitation, the
combination of the Frey procedure with distal pancreatectomy (DP) has proven to be a highly effective strategy [4,
5]. This combined approach offers the dual benefit of treating any existing inflammation in the pancreatic tail while
preventing its future occurrence.

In this “how-to” paper, we meticulously detail the steps
involved in performing the Frey procedure in conjunction
with DP, including practical strategies for managing technically challenging situations. Our aim was to leverage
our extensive experience to provide practical, hands-on
guidance for surgeons considering this integrated surgical
approach.

Methods
Surgical indications
The Frey procedure is suitable for most patients with chronic
pancreatitis, excluding those with suspected malignancy,
normal duct pancreatitis (e.g., autoimmune pancreatitis),
or disease confined to the pancreatic tail. Careful preoperative evaluation is essential to differentiate dilated ducts
from intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasms (IPMNs)
and pseudocysts from mucinous cystic neoplasms, as these
lesions can coexist with chronic pancreatitis. Most pseudocysts associated with chronic pancreatitis can be managed
using the Frey procedure alone. The Frey + DP procedure is indicated for cases with severe inflammation and

calcification or a symptomatic cyst in the pancreatic tail, in
addition to pancreatic head lesions and main pancreatic duct
dilatation.
The decision to perform Frey + DP rather than Frey-alone
was based on specific radiologic and clinical criteria regarding “tail involvement” (Supplementary Fig. 1). Frey + DP
is indicated when patients exhibit one or more of the following in the pancreatic tail: (1) dense calcification burden
or calculi strictly localized to or heavily involving the tail;
(2) complete ductal obstruction or stricture in the tail that
prevents adequate drainage via a standard longitudinal incision; or (3) symptomatic pseudocyst in the tail that cannot
be adequately drained internally.
Furthermore, it is important to distinguish between
planned and unplanned Frey + DP resections. Frey + DP is
typically selected as a primary strategy based on the aforementioned preoperative imaging findings. However, it
can also be performed as an intraoperative extension of a
planned Frey procedure when severe fibrosis and inflammation are encountered, thus making adequate opening and
drainage of the tail duct technically unfeasible or unsafe.

● Distal Pancreatectomy (DP): When Frey + DP is performed as a primary procedure, the pancreatic tail is
typically resected first. This may involve standard DP
or spleen-preserving DP, depending on the status of the
splenic vessels and the surgeon’s discretion (Fig. 1A).
In cases of severe chronic pancreatitis, marked inflammatory changes can obscure the anatomical planes,
making the identification of the splenic artery and vein
extremely difficult. In such technically challenging situations, a subadventitial dissection plane or a distal-toproximal retrograde approach should be considered to
facilitate posterior dissection of the graft. If the splenic
vessels are completely encased and inseparable from the
fibrotic pancreas, concomitant splenectomy is highly
recommended to ensure safety and avoid uncontrollable
hemorrhaging.
● Main Pancreatic Duct Opening: Following the DP, the
main pancreatic duct was opened longitudinally from
the pancreatic stump in the tail. This is typically performed using electrocautery, carefully guided by rightangle forceps inserted into the duct to prevent thermal
injury to the posterior pancreatic capsule (Fig. 2A).

Patients and analysis
Patients who underwent the Frey procedure, including Frey
+ DP, for chronic pancreatitis at our institution between
November 2016 and November 2024 were identified using
our medical information database. This analysis specifically
focused on cases from November 2016 onwards, as previous data from our institution up to 2016 were analyzed by
Sato et al. [5].
The perioperative outcomes, including patient characteristics, operative time, estimated blood loss, postoperative
hospital stay, postoperative pancreatic fistula (POPF), and
mortality, were compared between the Frey procedure and
Frey + DP groups. Clinically relevant POPF was defined
as grade B or higher according to the ISGPS classification
[6]. Additionally, the 1-year pain recurrence rate (evaluated
based on clinical judgment indicating the need for escalation of analgesic therapy or hospital readmission for pain)
and the rate of new-onset diabetes mellitus were investigated. For long-term observational outcomes beyond one
year postoperatively, only cases that could be followed up
in outpatient clinics were included in the analysis.
Numerical data are presented as medians (interquartile
range [IQR]). All statistical analyses were performed using
R statistical software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Wilcoxon’s rank test was applied for
continuous variables, and Fisher’s exact test was used for
categorical data. The present clinical analysis was performed
in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration

Results
This analysis included 22 patients: 9 underwent the
Frey + DP procedure, and 13 underwent the Frey procedure
alone (Table 1).
In the Frey + DP group, the median operative time was
393 min (IQR: 317–417 min), and the median estimated
blood loss was 649 mL (IQR: 365–889 mL). Compared
to the Frey-alone group (median operative time: 315 min;
median estimated blood loss: 309 mL), the Frey + DP group
had significantly longer operative times (p = 0.01) and notably higher estimated blood loss (p = 0.05).
All patients achieved pain control at the time of hospital
discharge in both groups (p = 1). The median postoperative
hospital stay was 18 days (IQR: 15–22 days), which showed
no significant difference compared to the Frey-alone group
(median: 15 days; p = 0.42). No operative mortality was
observed in either group (p = 1).
The clinically relevant POPF rate was 11% (1/9) in the
Frey + DP group and 38% (5/13) in the Frey-alone group
(p = 0.33). The 1-year pain recurrence rate was 12% (1/8) in
the Frey + DP group and 9% (1/11) in the Frey-alone group
(p = 1). The new-onset diabetes mellitus (DM) rate was 25%
(1/4) in the Frey + DP group and 40% (2/5) in the Frey-alone
group (p = 1).

Discussion
The Frey procedure, which is based on the principle of
decompressing the pancreatic head, is widely recognized as
a key intervention for “controlling inflammation” in chronic
pancreatitis [4]. Unlike the older Partington and Rochelle
procedure [7], the Frey procedure effectively decompresses
the pancreatic head, thereby addressing the intractable pain
[8]. Compared with the more invasive pancreaticoduodenectomy, the Frey procedure offers a safe, straightforward,
and reliable surgical approach [2]. Studies have also demonstrated that the Frey and Beger procedures offer comparable
efficacy [3, 9], allowing surgeons to select a technique based
on their preference and expertise.
A significant challenge in our long-term follow-up was
the relapse of inflammation, which typically occurs in the
pancreatic tail and often presents as an inflammatory pseudocyst [4]. While frequently observed in patients who
resumed alcohol consumption, this relapse also affected
those with hereditary and idiopathic pancreatitis with no
history of alcohol use. These findings underscore the limitations of conventional surgical strategies in diverse patient
cohorts [4]. Therefore, we propose considering Frey + DP
with or without splenectomy as a primary surgical option
for patients with inflammatory lesions in the pancreatic tail.
Previously, our group reported that while the Frey + DP procedure tended to have slightly longer operative times and
greater blood loss than the Frey procedure alone, there was
no significant difference in therapeutic efficacy [5]. Consistent with our previous report, the current analysis also demonstrated that the Frey + DP group had significantly longer
operative times and higher estimated blood loss than the
Frey-alone group, but no major differences were observed
in the overall surgical outcomes.
Although Frey + DP entails increased operative invasiveness, its true benefit must be evaluated in the context of
long-term management of the disease. The primary advantage of Frey + DP lies in the definitive eradication of severe
concurrent tail disease. By completely resecting the heavily fibrotic or cyst-burdened tail, Frey + DP mitigates the
substantial risk of long-term relapse, such as recurrent tail
pseudocysts, isolated tail duct strictures, or persistent localized pain. Such relapses following a Frey-alone procedure
would necessitate a technically demanding reoperation in a
previously dissected, “hostile” abdomen. Because our shortterm recovery (hospital stay, POPF rate) and 1-year clinical
outcomes (pain control, new-onset DM) were comparable
to those of the Frey procedure alone, we believe that the
theoretical long-term benefits of preventing tail recurrence
outweigh the increased operative invasiveness, provided
that the procedure is applied using strict decision criteria.
Despite these theoretical benefits, the precise indications

for Frey + DP require careful consideration. We recently
encountered a rare case of idiopathic chronic pancreatitis
requiring a distal pancreatectomy for a delayed tail lesion
exacerbation after an initial Frey procedure, even though no
tail lesions were present at the time of the initial surgery.
Such cases highlight that the indications and the balance of
advantages and disadvantages of Frey + DP remain a subject for ongoing debate and require further accumulation of
long-term data.
To preserve the endocrine and exocrine functions of the
remnant pancreas, resection of the pancreatic tail should be
kept to a minimum. The extent of resection varies depending on the location of the pancreatic tail lesion in Frey + DP
procedures.
In conclusion, the combined Frey and DP or spleen-preserving DP procedures are safe and effective approaches.
We recommend considering Frey + DP for patients presenting with inflammatory lesions in both the pancreatic head
and tail, based on clear radiologic and clinical parameters.
