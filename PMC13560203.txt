The efficacy of a PERMA-based intervention on PTSD symptoms and well-being in newly qualified operating room nurses: a quasi-experimental study
Authors: Linhe Yang, Shun Zhou, Xiaoya Zhang, Xiuli Zhu, Lihua Bai, Yunying Li, Zhenyu Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNewly qualified operating room nurses suffer from common workplace verbal violence and occupational stress, increasing their risk of Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD). However, evidence regarding the efficacy of the PERMA model (Positive Emotion, Engagement, Relationships, Meaning, Achievement) in reducing PTSD symptoms in this cohort remains limited.MethodsThis preliminary, quasi-experimental study included two phases: a cross-sectional survey of 178 operating room nurses to assess probable PTSD prevalence, followed by an 8-week PERMA-based intervention for 27 nurses who screened positive for probable PTSD. The PTSD Checklist-Civilian Version (PCL-C) and Campbell Index of Well-being (IWB) were measured at baseline, post-intervention, and 3-month follow-up.ResultsThe prevalence of probable PTSD was 19.0%. All nurses in the probable PTSD group reported verbal violence exposure; specific occupational fears were significant independent predictors of probable PTSD. A dose-response relationship existed between stressor count and probable PTSD risk (OR = 7.426, 95% CI: 3.704–14.890). The intervention was associated with a significant reduction in PCL-C scores (p = 0.007), while IWB scores showed no significant change.ConclusionsNewly qualified operating room nurses are exposed to probable PTSD risk, closely linked to verbal violence and occupational stressors. A PERMA-based intervention was associated with a reduction in their PTSD symptoms, highlighting the need to incorporate targeted psychological support and anti-workplace violence policies to protect this workforce. As a preliminary study, these findings should be confirmed in a larger, definitive randomized controlled trial.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12912-026-05158-x.

Introduction

A growing body of evidence underscores concerning prevalence rates of mental health disorders among nursing professionals, including Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD). This debilitating condition severely undermines professional functioning and demands timely intervention [1–3]. Existing research, though, has predominantly focused on emergency medical staff, overlooking operating room nurses, who work in environments with comparable psychological risk [4, 5].

The operating theatre is a high-risk, enclosed environment characterised by a strict hierarchy and precise teamwork [6]. Within this setting, nurses contend with substantial occupational demands: adherence to strict aseptic protocols, maintenance of prolonged static postures, and vigilant monitoring of surgical procedures [7]. These stressors are frequently amplified during complex procedures—such as endoscopic and abdominal surgeries [8].

Exacerbating these inherent pressures, the operating room is a well-documented high-risk setting for workplace psychological violence and bullying [9, 10]. Studies conducted in mainland China report prevalence rates of such violence ranging from 7.8% to 92.1%, a wide range that reflects differences in study designs, settings, and definitions of violence, with early-career nurses consistently recognized as a particularly vulnerable subgroup [11–15]. Newly qualified nurses, with fewer than two years of experience, are especially susceptible because they simultaneously juggle extensive training, frequent departmental rotations, and additional work-related pressures, and they present with a high burnout rate [16]. The work environment has been shown to be a critical determinant of mental health outcomes in nursing units, which further justifies a targeted focus on operating room nurses [17, 18]. This confluence of occupational stressors and systemic vulnerabilities-most notably exposure to workplace violence-creates a pathogenic context for PTSD, generating an urgent need for targeted mental health support.

The PERMA model-a framework developed by Martin Seligman in 2011-defines five core components of psychological well-being: Positive Emotion, Engagement, Relationships, Meaning, and Achievement. The model has been validated in the Chinese context: recent psychometric studies of the Chinese PERMA-Profiler reported a Cronbach’s alpha ranging from 0.79 to 0.91 for the overall scale, and confirmed measurement invariance across gender and age groups [19, 20]. This model, focused on cultivating positive psychological resources, offers potential to mitigate the negative affect and disengagement inherent to PTSD, and is increasingly used to improve mental health outcomes in clinical populations [21–26]. Conceptually, the PERMA framework can be situated within contemporary trauma recovery models. Specifically, the Positive Emotion and Engagement components are thought to counteract the emotional numbing and constricted affect that characterize PTSD by broadening the affective repertoire and rebuilding a sense of agency. The Relationships component directly addresses the social withdrawal and avoidance that maintain post-traumatic symptomatology, while Meaning and Achievement help restore the disrupted sense of self-efficacy and future orientation that are central to post-traumatic growth. In this way, PERMA does not target traumatic memories directly, as exposure-based therapies do, but rather strengthens the psychological capital that supports natural recovery processes [27]. Despite this promise, current literature highlights a critical gap in studies-the lack of application of the PERMA model to support the mental health of nursing personnel.

To address this critical gap, we designed this study with two primary objectives: (1) to determine the prevalence of probable PTSD and its association with workplace verbal violence and occupational stressors among newly qualified operating room nurses, and (2) to assess the efficacy of a novel, PERMA-informed psychological intervention in reducing their PTSD symptoms and improving subjective well-being.

Materials and methods

Study design and subjects

This quasi-experimental study employed a two-stage design and received approval from the Ethics Committee of Guangyuan Central Hospital (Approval No: GYZXLL2023039). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

The initial phase involved a cross-sectional survey conducted between January 2025 and March 2025 to assess PTSD symptoms and subjective well-being among nurses from ten hospitals. All 34 nurses who screened positive for probable PTSD were invited to participate in the intervention phase; 27 provided informed consent and completed the full intervention, whereas 7 declined, citing scheduling conflicts or personal reasons. The 27 consenting nurses then took part in a voluntary, informed-consent, 8-week PERMA-based intervention program delivered between April 2025 and June 2025. Outcomes were evaluated at baseline (pre-intervention), immediately post-intervention, and at a 3-month follow-up; a CONSORT-style participant flow diagram illustrating recruitment, enrollment, allocation, and retention is provided in Fig. 1.

Eligibility criteria included: (1) being a registered nurse (including those in standardized training programs) with over three months and no more than two years of operating theatre experience, consistent with the definition of a newly qualified operating room nurse; (2) an absence of severe physical illnesses or mental disorders other than PTSD; (3) fluency in reading and understanding Mandarin Chinese, which was the language of all study materials; (4) access to a smartphone or computer with internet connectivity for completing the online survey and attending the online intervention sessions; and (5) provision of informed consent. Exclusion and withdrawal criteria were: (1) an absence from operating theatre duties exceeding two weeks during the study period; (2) concurrent participation in other psychological intervention studies; or (3) the occurrence of severe adverse reactions necessitating urgent medical referral.

The sample size for the cross-sectional phase was determined using the formula for prevalence studies: n = Z² × p(1-p) / e², with Z = 1.96, e = 0.05, derived from an estimated PTSD prevalence in prior literature [9]. This calculation yielded a minimum sample of 139 participants.

The questionnaire

The survey instrument consisted of three sections. The first collected sociodemographic and occupational baseline data. The subsequent two sections assessed post-traumatic stress and psychological well-being using the Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder Checklist-Civilian Version (PCL-C) and the Campbell Index of Well-Being (IWB), respectively. Nurses were recruited through the nursing departments of the ten participating hospitals, which distributed an online survey link via departmental WeChat work groups. The survey was administered online using the Wenjuanxing platform (Changsha Ranxing Information Technology Co., Ltd.), and all materials were presented in Mandarin Chinese. To ensure data integrity, responses completed in less than five minutes were invalidated, and prompts notified participants of any omitted items. Digital informed consent was secured before survey access.

The PCL-C

The Posttraumatic Stress Disorder Checklist-Civilian Version (PCL-C) is a 17-item self-report measure that assesses three core symptom clusters: re-experiencing (Cluster B), avoidance (Cluster C), and hyperarousal (Cluster D), originally developed by Weathers et al. in 1993 [28], respondents rate each item on a five-point severity scale from 1 (“no reaction”) to 5 (“extremely severe reaction”). For the Chinese version, a total score threshold of 38 points is considered optimal for screening probable PTSD, demonstrating high diagnostic efficacy [29]. The PCL-C was used because, at the time the study was designed, it had a well-validated Chinese-language version with established cutoff scores for this population, whereas the Chinese PCL-5 was still undergoing psychometric evaluation. It is important to emphasize that the PCL-C is a self-report screening instrument used for identifying probable PTSD and monitoring symptom severity; it is not a diagnostic tool. A definitive diagnosis of PTSD requires a structured clinical interview such as the Clinician-Administered PTSD Scale (CAPS). Accordingly, throughout this manuscript, the term “probable PTSD” is used to describe participants who met the screening threshold.

The IWB

Subjective well-being was measured using the Campbell Index of Well-being (IWB). This scale contains nine items across two dimensions: the General Emotional Index (weighted 1.0) and the Life Satisfaction Index (weighted 1.1). Its application was supported by a study of 279 Chinese nurses, which reported a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.78 and sound internal consistency [30].

The PERMA-based intervention

The 8-week PERMA-based intervention was developed through a comprehensive literature review and refined via a two-round electronic Delphi process involving a panel of five experts (see Appendix 1 for details). The research team, which included a senior nurse practitioner, a nationally certified Level II psychological counsellor, an operating theatre nurse, and two postgraduate nursing students, delivered the intervention. All members completed standardized training and a competency assessment to guarantee protocol fidelity. Sessions were held online for one hour each week in a small-group format, with 8–10 nurses per group, to encourage peer support and shared learning. To monitor intervention fidelity, all sessions were conducted following a standardized facilitator manual, and the lead facilitator completed a session-by-session adherence checklist after each meeting. In addition, one randomly selected session per group was audio-recorded (with participant consent) and independently reviewed by a second trained rater, who confirmed that the core content and exercises for each weekly theme were delivered as specified. Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp). Descriptive statistics characterized the demographic data. Independent samples t-tests and chi-square tests facilitated group comparisons. Logistic regression examined the relationship between stressors and probable PTSD incidence. Because these experiences (e.g., fear of surgical scenes, work unpredictability) may not meet the strict DSM-5 Criterion A definition of a potentially traumatic event, we refer to them throughout as occupational stressors rather than traumatic stressors. For the intervention phase, a one-way repeated-measures ANOVA analyzed changes in PCL-C and IWB scores across the three time points. Post-hoc comparisons were performed using the Least Significant Difference (LSD) method. Effect sizes were reported using Cohen’s d and related odds ratio (OR). A P-value < 0.05 (two-tailed) denotes statistical significance.

Results

Prevalence and correlates of PTSD

A total of 178 questionnaires were included in the analysis. Probable PTSD was defined as a PCL-C total score at or above the validated Chinese cutoff of 38. Using this threshold, the prevalence of probable PTSD was 19.0% (34/178). While most demographic and occupational characteristics were similar between groups, the probable PTSD group reported a significantly higher prevalence of verbal violence (100% vs. 81.25%, p = 0.006). This group also exhibited greater fears associated with surgical scenes (26.47% vs. 2.08%, p < 0.001), committing medical errors (23.53% vs. 6.94%, p = 0.004), exposure to tissues/fluids (17.65% vs. 3.47%, p = 0.002), and work unpredictability (29.41% vs. 5.56%, p < 0.001). The distribution of stressor counts also differed significantly (p < 0.001) (Table 1), with the probable PTSD group endorsing a greater number of concurrent stressors (Table 2).

PCL-C scores differentiate probable PTSD and non-PTSD groups, while well-being does not

As shown in Table 3, the probable PTSD group (n = 34, defined by a PCL-C total score > = 38) demonstrated significantly higher PCL-C total scores than the non-PTSD group (n = 144) (42.82 +/- 3.23 vs. 26.98 +/- 3.91, p < 0.001), with a very large effect size (Cohen’s d = -4.18). This significant difference (all p < 0.001) was consistent across all symptom clusters, with particularly large effects for the avoidance (d = -3.49) and re-experiencing (d = -2.32) subscales, and a moderate effect for hyperarousal (d = -0.85). In contrast, there was no statistically significant difference in well-being between the groups, as measured by the IWB (7.41 +/- 1.32 vs. 7.56 +/- 1.33, p = 0.558).

Positive factors associated with PTSD occurrence

The forest plot (Fig. 2) indicates the logistic regression analysis, which identified fears associated with surgical scenes (OR = 21.564, 95% CI: 4.689–99.167), committing medical errors (OR = 6.936, 95% CI: 2.042–23.558), exposure to tissues/fluids (OR = 6.986, 95% CI: 1.333–36.613), and fear of the unpredictability of work (OR = 12.814, 95% CI: 3.660-44.866) as significant independent predictors of probable PTSD incidence. Further, the more exposure to stressors, the higher the risk of probable PTSD (OR = 7.426, 95% CI: 3.704–14.890).

Efficacy of the PERMA-based intervention

Twenty-seven nurses with probable PTSD completed the intervention and all subsequent assessments; none of them were lost to follow-up. A one-way repeated-measures ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of time on the Total PCL-C score (F = 5.290, p = 0.007, ηp² = 0.169). Post-hoc comparisons (LSD) confirmed that scores decreased significantly from baseline (Mean+/-SD: 42.81+/-3.23) to post-intervention (40.22+/-2.89, p = 0.003, Cohen’s d = 0.86). A slight increase in the PCL-C score was noted at the 3-month follow-up (41.04+/-2.85) compared to the post-intervention assessment, though this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.210). Crucially, the follow-up score remained significantly lower than the baseline (p = 0.018, Cohen’s d = 0.58). No significant changes were observed for the PCL-C subscales or the IWB total score (Table 4).

Discussion

This two-phase, quasi-experimental pilot study yielded two principal findings regarding newly qualified operating room nurses. First, it identified a concerningly high prevalence of probable PTSD (19.0%) in this cohort, strongly associated with exposure to workplace violence and specific occupational fears. Second, the PERMA model-based intervention was associated with a significant reduction in probable PTSD symptoms, with benefits partially sustained over three months. However, as a preliminary feasibility study without a randomized control group, the present findings should be interpreted as suggestive rather than definitive; their primary value lies in providing the effect size estimates and protocol refinement needed to inform a larger, more definitive randomized controlled trial.

The high prevalence of probable PTSD highlights a significant mental health burden among these nurses, even exceeding those departments commonly regarded as high-pressure for PTSD [1, 2]. Our findings confirm workplace violence and specific fears as pathogenic contributors. The logistic regression identifies potent independent predictors: fear of surgical scenes (OR = 21.564), work unpredictability (OR = 12.814), exposure to tissues/fluids (OR = 6.986), and committing medical errors (OR = 6.936). Verbal violence was nearly universal in this sample, reported by all nurses in the probable PTSD group. This 100% prevalence produced quasi-complete separation, precluding reliable estimation in the multivariate logistic regression model. Nevertheless, the pronounced dose-response relationship for cumulative stressor exposure (OR = 7.426, 95% CI: 3.704–14.890) indicates that verbal violence substantially amplifies PTSD risk when it co-occurs with other occupational stressors. It is also worth noting that the mandatory two-year rotation programme exacerbates this situation by preventing newly qualified nurses from avoiding triggering environments, potentially perpetuating trauma [16]. This dynamic parallels findings from Aqtam and colleagues [31], who documented how nursing students exposed to chronic political violence during clinical training relied on cultivated resilience to sustain their professional functioning; the present study suggests that the chronic “occupational violence” of the operating room may exact a comparable psychological toll on early-career nurses and warrants similarly targeted resilience-building efforts.

An unexpected finding was the stark discrepancy between probable PTSD symptoms and subjective well-being, unlike the work of Tian et al. [32] and Li & Shen [30]. While the PCL-C scores differentiated the two groups with large effect sizes, there was no significant difference in their IWB scores. This indicates that the debilitating symptoms of re-experiencing, avoidance, and hyperarousal can exist alongside a self-reported sense of well-being that is comparable to that of unaffected peers. This distinction suggests that general well-being measures may lack the sensitivity to capture the specific distress in those with modest probable PTSD symptoms. It further implies that new nurses may normalize their psychological distress, maintaining a sense of well-being while internally grappling with significant trauma symptoms. This finding carries direct implications for institutional support: validated PTSD screening instruments such as the PCL-C are more effective than general well-being surveys at identifying nurses in urgent need of psychological intervention. Sole reliance on well-being assessments risks missing those with concealed symptoms. We recommend that hospitals supplement periodic well-being surveys with targeted PTSD screening, particularly following workplace violence or other significant occupational stressors.

The positive results of the PERMA-based intervention represent a promising step forward. The statistically significant reduction in total PCL-C scores (F(2, 52) = 5.290, p = 0.007) suggests that a program focused on building positive psychological resources can help mitigate core PTSD symptomatology. The effect sizes observed from baseline to post-intervention (Cohen’s d = 0.86) and to the 3-month follow-up (d = 0.58) are clinically meaningful, indicating a reduction in symptom severity that would be perceptible to the nurses themselves [5]. The slight, non-significant rebound at follow-up is typical of many psychological interventions and highlights the chronic nature of PTSD, pointing to the need for long-term care for those at potential risk.

Traditional trauma therapies typically center on direct processing of traumatic memories. In contrast, the PERMA model tends to operate by strengthening psychological capital-enhancing positive emotions, reinforcing a sense of professional meaning, and building supportive relationships-in turn indirectly lessening both the salience and debilitating effects of trauma symptoms. This strengthening of psychological capital was operationalized through specific weekly exercises detailed in Appendix 1: for example, the Cognitive Reframing exercise in Week 2 (Positive Emotion) was designed to counter negative affect by helping nurses re-interpret stressful operating room events, while the Goal Setting exercise in Week 6 (Achievement) was designed to rebuild self-efficacy by guiding nurses to set and accomplish concrete, professionally meaningful goals. By cultivating engagement and feelings of achievement, the intervention also helps counteract the emotional numbing and avoidance-key hallmarks of PTSD-supporting nurses in re-engaging with their work with heightened agency and resilience [6, 7]. Notably, while probable PTSD symptoms declined, well-being (IWB) scores showed no significant improvement. This reinforces that the intervention targeted the specific pathology of PTSD, rather than broadly boosting a general sense of happiness-a state shaped by multiple external factors.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, as a preliminary pilot study without a randomized control group, causal inferences remain tentative; PCL-C score reductions should be interpreted as associated with, rather than attributable to, the intervention. Second, the modest sample size (n = 27) constrained statistical power, likely accounting for the non-significant changes in PCL-C subscales (Clusters B, C, and D) and the IWB total score; confirmation in larger trials is warranted. Third, self-selection bias may have occurred, as 7 of 34 eligible nurses declined participation; volunteers may have been more motivated or receptive to psychological intervention. Fourth, because PTSD symptoms were self-reported via the PCL-C during concurrent intervention enrollment, social desirability bias may have led to under-reporting at post-intervention and follow-up. Fifth, conceptual overlap between the PERMA “Positive Emotion” component and the IWB may partly explain the null well-being finding, or alternatively, suggest that the IWB lacks sensitivity to intervention-specific changes. Finally, generalizability is limited by the single-region, ten-hospital sampling frame and the use of the PCL-C rather than a structured diagnostic interview.

Conclusion

This study confirms the profound psychological toll exacted on new graduates in operating room nursing, firmly associating probable PTSD with workplace violence and specific occupational fears. The PERMA model provides a novel and promising framework for intervention, with the present preliminary findings suggesting significant potential to alleviate PTSD symptoms. To protect this vital component of the healthcare workforce, institutions should implement dual strategies: integrating brief, group-based PERMA modules into transition-to-practice programs for newly qualified operating room nurses, and enacting unwavering, systemic measures to eradicate the scourge of workplace violence. A larger, definitive randomized controlled trial is warranted to confirm these preliminary findings.
