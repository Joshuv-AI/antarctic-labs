Determinants of electron transport at asymmetric metal/molecule/metal contacts
Authors: Guirong Su, Rulin Feng, Yonghao Zhao, Igor Ying Zhang, Wei Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSingle–molecule electronic devices offer the ultimate pathway to transcend post–Moore scaling limits. However, developing molecular diodes remains paralyzed by trial–and–error, as classical transport models fail at complex hybrid interfaces involving both strong covalent and weak non–covalent interactions. Here, we establish a unified theoretical model enabling precise quantitative prediction of electron transport across highly asymmetric metal/molecule/metal contacts. By screening 144 diverse molecular junctions—including classic donor–acceptor architectures—we uncover the microscopic mechanisms driving charge rectification: asymmetric anchoring groups induce pronounced electronic polarization, while dynamic adsorption–state changes dictate spatial coupling. This interplay is fundamentally governed by a deep synergy between interfacial bond dipoles and effective tunneling widths. We derive a composite descriptor bridging quantum tunneling and thermal excitation, achieving >93% predictive accuracies across monostable and bistable regimes. Guided by this, we identify optimal configurations yielding an intrinsic rectification ratio of 19.25 at 0.12 V, drastically outperforming conventional D–π–A systems.Subject terms: Molecular electronics, Scaling laws

Introduction

Engineering molecular structures for specific functionalities is a central objective of molecular electronics, with single–molecule rectification being a particularly attractive device function1–3. These molecular diodes—relying on asymmetric electron transmission across electrode/molecule/electrode junctions—offer a promising route to bypass CMOS scaling limits by effectively mitigating short-channel effects and suppressing tunneling leakage through ultrathin oxides4,5. The first example was introduced in 1974 with Aviram and Ratner’s (AR) proposal of the σ–bridged donor–acceptor (D–σ–A) diode6. Inspired by this initial model, research over the past 50 years has shifted toward π–conjugated (D–π–A) systems, which offer superior synthetic feasibility and enhanced charge transfer through strong intramolecular coupling (Type I rectifiers in Fig. 1a)7–10. Despite these advancements, most monostable molecular diodes exhibit modest rectification ratios (RRs) due to the persistent reverse tunneling leakage4 caused by the strong coupling between donor and acceptor moieties5. To circumvent this, decoupling strategies have been proposed to isolate transport pathways, the most effective of which is a novel rectification paradigm leveraging the conductance disparity within molecular switches11–13. These molecules exhibit characteristic adsorption bistability, enabling reversible switching between weakly coupled physisorbed and strongly coupled chemisorbed states under pulsed scanning tunneling microscopy (STM) bias14,15 (Type II rectifiers in Fig. 1b). Unlike monostable systems, where transport directions are coupled, this dynamic mechanism decouples the transport pathways, effectively suppressing reverse leakage to yield superior RRs. Consequently, establishing a robust, predictive structure–property relationship for these newly developed bistable molecular rectifiers is essential for their future design and applications.

Central to establishing these relationships is the modulation of the asymmetric electronic structure, which serves as the primary mechanism for facilitating the asymmetric energy level shifting of molecular orbitals. For Type I rectifiers, electronic polarizability is typically induced by engineering the intrinsic dipole moments or introducing asymmetric molecular configurations16–21. In contrast, for Type II rectifiers, experimental efforts focus on modulating interfacial behavior through asymmetric contacts, such as varying electrode compositions or tuning STM tip positions22–25. However, as the chemical and structural design space expands, exploring this landscape for both paradigms via conventional trial-and-error methods has become increasingly intractable26,27. A more rational strategy involves identifying robust physical descriptors that correlate strongly with electron transport properties28–30, enabling predictive structure-property mapping for the efficient screening of high-RR configurations. Conventionally, for strongly bonded systems in both Type I and II rectifiers, the interfacial Schottky barrier (ΦB)31,32 serves as the primary metric for estimating current flow (I) based on classical Schottky theory. Furthermore, for strongly coupled Type II interfaces (e.g., Ni/CoPc/Au), descriptors derived from the Fermi’s golden rule—specifically the product of the local density of states—have proven effective33,34. Yet, establishing a quantitative, generalizable link between RR and microscopic interfacial parameters remains a fundamental challenge for complex hybrid interfaces. This difficulty stems from the intricate interplay among molecular contact geometries, interfacial dipole formation, and electronic hybridization—particularly when strong covalent bonds and weak non-covalent interactions intimately coexist35,36. To date, a universal descriptor capable of unifying these distinct transport regimes across diverse asymmetric junctions remains conspicuously absent.

Here, we confront this challenge by systematically investigating a comprehensive library of 144 electrode/molecule/electrode configurations—encompassing over 1000 data points—that spans both monostable Type I and bistable Type II rectifier systems across a diverse set of metal electrodes (Fig. 1c). Our analysis identifies two decisive physical parameters that govern charge rectification: the interfacial bond dipole (ΦBD) and the effective tunneling width (w), whose magnitudes are dictated by the competitive interplay of covalent and non-covalent interactions. Distilling these insights, we introduce a unified descriptor, |ΔT|, which quantifies transport asymmetry by explicitly bridging the contributions of thermal excitation and interfacial tunneling. For monostable Type I rectifiers, |ΔT| scales with the barrier disparity between the right and left interfaces (ΔT = w2RΦBD–R − w2LΦBD–L). Conversely, for bistable Type II rectifiers, it captures the state–dependent barrier modulation between physisorbed and chemisorbed configurations at the right interface (ΔT = w2cΦBDc − w2pΦBDp). Notably, this descriptor achieves striking predictive accuracies exceeding 93% for the covalent-dominated interfaces of Type I rectifiers, and surpassing 94% for the hybrid interfaces of Type II rectifiers (even with varied tip sites). These results significantly outperform conventional models rooted in classical Schottky theory or Fermi’s golden rule. Crucially, the optimal configuration identified by our model exhibits an RR of up to 19.25 at 0.12 V, drastically outperforming previously reported values for conventional D–π–A-based molecular rectifiers. Furthermore, the proposed descriptor maintains robust predictive power even in systems with asymmetric electrode materials, establishing it as a generalizable design principle for engineering next–generation single–molecule electronic components.

Results

Type I: monostable rectifiers

To elucidate the determinants of electron transport at metal/organic/metal interfaces, we first examine the relationship between the structure and transport properties of monostable Type I rectifiers, characterized by an intrinsic donor–acceptor (D–A) molecular structure bridging two electrodes. We construct three representative molecular models based on a dipyrimidinyl–diphenyl backbone37,38 (Fig. 2a–c)—a prototypical platform for investigating intrinsic molecular diodes. By strategically varying the arrangement of isocyanide (–NC) and thiol (–SH) anchoring groups at the molecular termini (denoted as M1, M2, and M3), we systematically engineer the asymmetric interfacial coupling induced by different anchoring configurations.

Junction geometries are optimized using density functional theory (DFT) with a linear combination of atomic orbitals (LCAO) basis set. Electronic transport calculations are subsequently performed within the DFT–NEGF framework under open–boundary conditions (Supplementary Fig. 1). Computational details are provided in the Methods section. Key geometric parameters, including dihedral angles and terminal atom–electrode bond lengths, are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. Structural analysis reveals that while the molecular backbone exhibits a slight deviation from strict planarity, the first phenyl ring and the two pyrimidinyl rings maintain a nearly coplanar alignment. This internal rigidity ensures that electronic conjugation across the backbone remains robust against perturbations from anchoring groups or contact configurations. Crucially, the calculated C–Au bond lengths are consistently shorter than the S–Au bond lengths, providing a distinct structural signature of the asymmetric interfacial binding strengths.

We systematically probe the transport responses of the M1, M2, and M3 junctions across six different metal electrodes. For the M1 junction, the asymmetric anchoring—comprising an isocyanide group at the diphenyl terminus and a thiol group at the dipyrimidinyl terminus—induces a distinctive interfacial electronic polarity. This structural asymmetry manifests as a significant rectification effect, as observed in Fig. 2d (top). Notably, the polarity of this rectification is electrode–dependent, as quantified by the RR distributions in Fig. 2d (bottom). This phenomenon is driven by work function differences that realign the frontier molecular orbitals (HOMO/LUMO) relative to the Fermi level39.

Building upon the M1 configuration, we theoretically construct the M2 diode by swapping the positions of the two anchoring groups. As illustrated in Fig. 2e (top), the Au electrode system exhibits a distinct reversal in its rectification direction, with the current magnitude in the positive bias region remarkably increasing by nearly an order of magnitude. Notably, the RR for the Au electrode system is substantially enhanced (Fig. 2e, bottom), reaching a peak value of 15.3 at 1.4 V, which is comparable to the maximum values recently reported for D–π–A systems39. This further validates that the performance of dipyrimidinyl–diphenyl diodes can be significantly modulated through the strategic selection of anchoring groups.

In contrast, for the M3 junction (featuring symmetric thiol anchoring), the current predominantly flows from the dipyrimidinyl block toward the diphenyl block under positive bias (Fig. 2f, top). Due to the inherent structural symmetry of the junction, the I–V curves exhibit only marginal asymmetry, yielding RR values across all electrode systems that are significantly lower than those of the M1 and M2 configurations (Fig. 2f, bottom). These calculated trends align with previous experimental and theoretical observations37–39, providing a robust data foundation for the development of electronic transport descriptors for asymmetric molecular junctions.

Type II: bistable rectifiers

To assess the generality and predictive power of our descriptor |ΔT|, we extend our analysis to typical Type II rectifiers, where the molecule can reversibly switch between physisorbed and chemisorbed configurations under an applied bias. These systems embody a more intricate interfacial landscape, with coexisting weak and strong coupling regimes. By applying voltage pulses via an STM tip (Fig. 4a), the molecular adsorption state can be reversibly toggled. Inspired by the experimentally demonstrated switching behavior of thiophene–based molecules on metal surfaces15,43, we select trans–ADS on Cu(111) as a model system to explore the impact of interfacial asymmetry on the transport properties of bistable Schottky junctions. Binding energy calculations (Supplementary Fig. 2) reveal two energetically favorable adsorption states: a physisorbed state and a chemisorbed state, as shown in Fig. 4b. Simulated STM images capture their distinct topographic signatures: the physisorbed trans–ADS appears rod–like, while the chemisorbed configuration exhibits a dumbbell shape, consistent with experimental imaging of trans–ADT on Cu(111). The calculated electronic structural differences between the two states ultimately lead to distinct interfacial contact barriers (Supplementary Fig. 3). These findings confirm structural bistability and provide a suitable platform to investigate the interplay between adsorption geometry and charge transport in single–molecule junctions.

To quantitatively assess the influence of adsorption geometry on electron transport, we perform DFT–NEGF calculations for a symmetric Cu/trans–ADS/Cu junction. To simulate the constant–height mode typically employed in STM–based switching experiments15,43, the tip–substrate separation is held constant, with the overall junction geometry remaining fixed for both configurations (Supplementary Fig. 4). This ensures that transport differences arise exclusively from variations in the adsorption state. As shown in Fig. 4c, the transition from physisorption to chemisorption increases the tip–molecule distance, which weakens the coupling and lowers conductance. This confirms that the increased interfacial distance and the elevated Schottky barrier are intrinsically coupled, both contributing to the observed suppression of leakage current in the chemisorbed state. These findings demonstrate that interfacial bistability offers a viable strategy for enhancing rectification behavior within a single molecular device.

In this framework, the rectification process is established through a specific operational sequence: (i) a positive voltage pulse is applied to switch the molecule from the chemisorbed to the physisorbed state; (ii) a forward bias voltage (Va) is applied, and the system outputs the current characteristic of the physisorbed state (Iphys); (iii) a negative voltage pulse is applied to switch the molecule back from the physisorbed to the chemisorbed state; and (iv) a reverse bias voltage (V−a) is applied, and the system outputs the current characteristic of the chemisorbed state (Ichem). Consequently, the RR is defined by the current disparity between these two distinct conformational states:

Hence, the rectification is a result of the non–volatile switching between the on and off states.

Accordingly, the capability to independently and accurately predict the transport currents for both adsorption regimes is a critical prerequisite for the rational design and performance forecasting of such bistable molecular interfaces. In the following, we first examine the fundamental structural and transport characteristics of five representative monostable organic molecules, including benzene (Ben), naphthalene (Nc), anthracene (Ac), tetracene (Tc) and pentacene (Pc) on six noble and transition metal surfaces—specifically the (111) surfaces of Ag, Cu, Au, Rh, Ir, and Pt. Adsorption geometries are predicted using the PBE+vdWsurf–R method, which has been proven accurate for both physisorption and chemisorption regimes44,45. In this approach, the renormalized screened vdW parameters are determined fully ab initio by combining the Lifshitz–Zaremba–Kohn (LZK) theory with the random phase approximation (RPA) and a Drude description of the substrate dielectric response, thereby explicitly incorporating the collective electronic screening effects of the metal. The calculated molecular adsorption sites (Supplementary Fig. 5a), adsorption energies, and adsorption heights (Supplementary Fig. 5b, c) show excellent agreement with previously reported experimental and theoretical data46–48, validating the reliability of our approach. These distinct interfacial electronic properties (Supplementary Fig. 6) naturally lead to pronounced disparities in electron transport behavior.

The calculated I–V characteristics for these representative molecules across various electrodes are shown in Supplementary Fig. 7a, b. Based on this database, we examine the efficacy of our proposed composite electronic descriptor, defined as T = w2ΦB in Eq. 6, which accounts for the synergistic effects of both quantum tunneling and thermal excitation. The individual contributions of w and ΦB are visualized in Supplementary Fig. 8. As shown in Fig. 4d, this descriptor exhibits a strong exponential correlation with the computed current under a bias voltage of 0.12 V, in excellent agreement with the analytical model. Remarkably, its predictive power surpasses that of the local interfacial barrier, ΦB, extracted from the controlling right–hand molecule/electrode interface12 (Fig. 4e), achieving a correlation coefficient exceeding 0.93.

We next systematically analyzed the key physical parameters derived from Fermi’s golden rule in combination with the modified Bardeen approximation—an established framework that has successfully captured the influence of STM tip position on transport in Ni/cobalt phthalocyanine (CoPc)/Au junctions, which are structurally analogous to our system33. Within this formalism, the transport current is expected to scale with the product of the local DOS at the Fermi level of the molecule and that of the adjacent electrode (Supplementary Fig. 9). We thus hypothesize that this DOS product could serve as a quantitative descriptor for current. However, as shown in Fig. 4f, the correlation between the DOS product and the calculated current varies significantly across different systems, with coefficients of determination spanning from 0.52 to 0.93. These results indicate that the DOS product alone lacks the universality needed to accurately capture charge transport across structurally and electronically diverse metal/organic interfaces.

The limitations of the aforementioned descriptors stem from their formulation specifically for strongly bound regimes, which reduces their efficacy for hybrid interfaces where multiple interaction types coexist. To further elucidate these diverse electronic couplings, we visualize the interfacial interactions of these systems with the independent gradient model based on Hirshfeld partition (IGMH)49 analyses, and plotted sign(λ2) colored dg isosurface maps (Fig. 4g). The same coloring scheme is adopted for all subsequent IGMH maps. As illustrated in the plots, chemisorbed systems clearly display dg isosurfaces with green and red color in the central area, which clearly reveals electronic interactions featuring the coexistence of both strong and weak bonds. Whereas the physisorbed interfaces reveals an IGMH dg isosurface with completely green color, indicating fairly weak vdW forces. These findings are in excellent agreement with the interfacial adsorption energies calculated using high–precision hybrid DFT50,51 (Supplementary Fig. 10). Consequently, it remains uncertain whether descriptors tailored for Type I rectifiers can be directly extrapolated to this bistable regime, where vdW dispersion forces often play a decisive role in determining binding structures and energetics.

To further investigate the structural sensitivity of rectification, we systematically evaluate the influence of STM tip positioning on the RR. A total of 24 distinct tip contact sites are considered (Fig. 5a, b; side views in Supplementary Fig. 11), with the tip–substrate distance held constant to isolate the effect of contact asymmetry—an established approach in molecular conductance studies. The calculated RR values (Supplementary Fig. 12) exhibit pronounced spatial dependence, ranging from 1.55 at site c2 to 19.25 at site t1. Notably, the RR at site t1 is nearly threefold higher than that at the commonly employed c0 site, highlighting the critical role of local contact geometry in modulating electron transport at bistable interfaces.

As shown in Fig. 5d, the rectification ratio increases exponentially with |ΔT|, in excellent agreement with Eq. 9. Notably, this descriptor yields a predictive accuracy of 94%. These results highlight |ΔT| as a robust and physically grounded descriptor that integrates both interface dipole effects and tunneling characteristics, offering a reliable metric for assessing rectification in bistable Schottky junctions with asymmetric contacts.

To account for the bias dependence of the rectification ratio, we evaluate the predictive power of the proposed descriptor across different applied biases (Supplementary Fig. 14). At a low bias of 0.04 V, the descriptor predicts the RR with a R2 of 0.83. As the bias increases to 0.08 V, the predictive accuracy significantly improves to R2 = 0.95. Crucially, at 0.12 V (the benchmark bias employed in this study), the correlation remains highly stable (R2 = 0.93), and the fitting curve nearly overlaps with the 0.08 V result. This convergence indicates that at a bias of 0.12 V, the system has entered a stable tunneling regime, establishing the RR as a robust and representative metric for the molecule’s intrinsic rectification performance.

To disentangle the individual contributions of the tunneling width and interfacial bond dipole barrier to charge rectification, we evaluate their respective influences on the RR. For data points near the midpoint of the |ΔT| curve in Fig. 5d, the difference in ΦBD between the physisorbed and chemisorbed states is negligible (Supplementary Table 3). Under these conditions, the impact of ΦBD can be reasonably excluded, allowing us to isolate the effect of the tunneling width. As shown in Fig. 5e and summarized in Supplementary Table 4, the RR exhibits a strong linear dependence on the difference in w2, confirming that the tunneling width is the dominant factor governing rectification in this regime.

In contrast, within the exponential growth region of the ΔT curve (Fig. 5f), the variation in w2 becomes relatively small, while the RR displays a pronounced exponential dependence on the difference in bond dipole barriers (ΔΦBD = ΦBDp − ΦBDc) (Supplementary Table 5). Although ΔΦBD provides a useful qualitative indicator for identifying tip positions that promote higher rectification, its predictive power is limited when considered alone. Collectively, these findings underscore the cooperative role of the interfacial bond dipole and tunneling width in determining rectification performance. Neither parameter alone sufficiently captures the observed variations in RR; rather, their combined effect—encapsulated by the composite descriptor |ΔT|—is essential for a comprehensive understanding of electron transport in bistable metal/organic junctions.

Based on these findings, one might expect a larger tunneling width (indicative of weaker coupling) for physical adsorption. However, Supplementary Table 3 reveals that wp is smaller than wc, which is seemingly counterintuitive. This arises because the RR is not solely a function of w, but is also crucially governed by the interfacial bond dipole—another key parameter within the descriptor’s physical framework. Generally, a larger tunneling distance would lead to a weaker interfacial dipole. Our results highlight a critical trade-off: for instance, for the system with the electrode at the t1 site, despite wp being smaller than wc, the distinct electronic hybridization in the chemisorbed state induces an interfacial bond dipole six times larger than its physisorbed counterpart. This dominant dipole contribution effectively reshapes the tunneling barrier, ultimately resulting in superior rectification performance.

We next evaluate the interfacial barrier height difference between the physisorbed and chemisorbed states (ΔΦB) as a potential descriptor for rectification in bistable metal/organic junctions (Supplementary Table 6). As shown in Fig. 5g, the RR exhibits an exponential dependence on ΔΦB. However, the moderate correlation (R2 = 0.69) indicates inherent limitations. While systems with large ΔΦB values follow a clear exponential trend, those with smaller ΔΦB deviate substantially. This discrepancy arises from the dominant role of quantum tunneling at chemisorbed interfaces, where weak tip–molecule coupling diminishes the influence of barrier height alone, leading to an unreliable prediction of the RR. These results suggest that ΔΦB, though physically intuitive, is insufficient as a standalone descriptor for capturing rectification in systems where tunneling effects are significant.

Guided by Fermi’s golden rule and the modified Bardeen approximation, the tunneling current is expected to scale with the product of the local DOS at the Fermi level of the molecule and the adjacent electrode (Supplementary Fig. 9). Based on this model, the RR in the bistable junction should reflect the relative change in this DOS product (ρ = ρmρt) between the physisorbed and chemisorbed states. We compute the site-resolved DOS values across all STM tip positions for both adsorption configurations (Supplementary Fig. 15 and Supplementary Table 7). However, as shown in Fig. 5h, the resulting RR values exhibit no meaningful correlation with the variations in ρ.

Further analysis reveals that the limited predictive power of these DOS–based descriptors primarily stems from the nature of the chemisorbed interface. In this configuration, the STM tip resides at a relatively large distance from the molecule, resulting in weak tip–molecule interfacial coupling. Consequently, variations in tip position induce only minor perturbations in the DOS of both the molecule and the electrode, thereby reducing the sensitivity of the rectification ratio to structural changes. In contrast, systems, such as Ni/CoPc/Au, where strong coupling exists between the Ni electrode and the Co center of the molecule, exhibit a highly responsive interface—small displacements of the electrode lead to substantial changes in the DOS and, in turn, the rectification behavior. This comparison highlights the intrinsic limitations of relying solely on the Fermi’s golden rule to describe charge transport in bistable junctions, where both strongly and weakly coupled interfaces coexist and collectively influence the electronic response.

We also compare the maximum RR of our bistable Type II rectifiers with typical π–conjugated asymmetric structures based on donor–acceptor interactions (Fig. 5i). Our computed RRs for Type I systems show excellent agreement with both experimental and theoretical values reported in the literature. Moreover, the results indicate that while high RR values can be achieved by employing different materials or environmental stimuli (e.g., solvents and humidity), the intrinsic RR of conventional single-molecule diodes remains fundamentally limited. As summarized in Supplementary Table 8, asymmetric molecular structures based on π–conjugated systems typically exhibit RR values ranging from only 1 to 16. In comparison, our proposed bistable Type II rectifiers exhibit an intrinsic RR of up to 19.25 at 0.12 V by switching between the physisorbed and chemisorbed configurations. This underscores the great potential of introducing adsorption bistability for the future design of molecular diodes. Building on this foundation, further increases in the RR can be achieved via external modulations, such as electrode gating, potentially enabling performance comparable to conventional diodes and paving the way for the integration of molecular devices in future logic computing.

We systematically explore the impact of electrode asymmetry on the rectification behavior of the bistable trans–ADS/Cu(111) system by introducing a series of dissimilar electrode materials, including Ag, Au, and Pt (Supplementary Fig. 16). The corresponding structural models are shown in Supplementary Fig. 17. For each asymmetric junction, we evaluate the bond dipoles and tunneling widths associated with the respective electrode combinations. As shown in Fig. 6a, the RR exhibits a strong exponential dependence on our proposed composite descriptor, yielding an excellent correlation (R2 = 0.96). This result is consistent with the above analysis of asymmetric tip sites and aligns well with the theoretical predictions of Landauer’s formalism. To further assess the influence of electrode composition, we examine the rectification under varying bias voltages. As shown in Fig. 6b, the RR follows a clear monotonic trend: RRCu > RRAg > RRAu > RRPt. This behavior arises from a more rapid increase in the transmission for the chemisorbed configuration relative to the physisorbed state (Supplementary Fig. 18). These findings highlight the pivotal role of electrode asymmetry in modulating rectification and confirm the generality and robustness of our descriptor across a wide range of material combinations and operational conditions.

To further clarify the mechanism underlying the material dependence of rectification, we independently assess the contributions of the interfacial bond dipole and tunneling width in systems with asymmetric electrode materials. As shown in Fig. 6c, junctions incorporating Au and Pt electrodes exhibit a pronounced increase in tunneling width, while the change in the interfacial bond dipole between the physisorbed and chemisorbed states remains relatively small. This imbalance leads to a significant reduction in the composite descriptor difference, w2pΦBDp − w2cΦBDc, resulting in a marked decrease in the RR. The origin of this suppression lies in the enhanced interfacial coupling induced by the Au and Pt electrodes under chemisorption, which reduces the contrast in dipole strength between the two adsorption states (Supplementary Table 9). By contrast, the system employing an Ag electrode displays a less pronounced change in tunneling width, yet retains a moderate dipole variation relative to the symmetric baseline. Collectively, these observations identify the diminished bond dipole contrast between physisorbed and chemisorbed configurations as the principal factor limiting rectification in junctions with asymmetric electrode materials. Interfacial dipole asymmetry thus emerges as a key determinant of rectification performance in bistable metal/organic interfaces.

Finally, we conduct a comprehensive assessment of how asymmetric electrode positions and material compositions influence rectification behavior in bistable junctions (Supplementary Tables 10–12). As shown in Fig. 6d–f, for all examined metal electrodes, the RR reaches its maximum when the tip is positioned at the t1 site—substantially outperforming the commonly employed c0 site in experimental setups. Remarkably, the engineered Schottky interfaces presented here exhibit RRs that compare favorably with those reported for conventional nanoscale Schottky diode benchmarks52,53, underscoring their promise for next–generation molecular electronics. Moreover, across the Ag, Au, and Pt systems, our proposed descriptor consistently shows a strong exponential correlation with the RR, with R2 surpassing 0.91. This agreement with results from the Cu/trans–ADS/Cu system confirms both the robustness and broad applicability of the descriptor across a diverse range of electrode materials.

Discussion

Using vdW–inclusive DFT calculations, we systematically explore the impact of asymmetric contacts—encompassing both electrode positions and material compositions—on the rectification behavior of two typical rectifiers. The performance of Type I rectifiers with an intrinsic D–A molecular structure can be significantly improved through the strategic selection of asymmetric anchoring groups. The bistable Type II rectifier system exhibits two distinct adsorption states, each with markedly different electronic properties. The operational switching cycle combines a high–current physisorbed state under forward bias with a low–leakage chemisorbed state under reverse bias, enabling intrinsic rectification within a single molecular junction. We uncover a strong exponential dependence of the RR on a composite electronic descriptor, |ΔT|, which captures the interplay between interfacial tunneling width and bond dipole. Notably, this descriptor achieves an impressive predictive correlation (R2 > 0.93) for the covalent–dominated interfaces of Type I rectifiers, and R2 > 0.94 for the hybrid interfaces of Type II rectifiers with the tip at varied sites. This correlation is in excellent agreement with predictions from Landauer transport theory. Moreover, the intrinsic rectification performance at the optimal contact site identified by our descriptor exceeds that of previously reported π–conjugated systems. These findings provide not only a fundamental understanding of structure–transport relationships in bistable interfaces but also a robust, physically motivated framework for the rational design and rapid screening of high–efficiency molecular diodes.

Methods

Metal/molecule/metal interfaces structural optimizations

In the present study, we utilized two DFT codes: the all–electron Fritz–Haber–Institute ab initio molecular simulations (FHI–aims) and the plane–wave–based Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP). The FHI–aims code was employed for calculations of the structural optimizations, the molecular electron affinities and the electronic density differences. The exchange–correlation functional was expressed with the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) generalized gradient approximation (GGA)54. The PBE + vdWsurf–R method was adopted to describe the van der Waals (vdW) interactions between the molecule and the metal substrate. While the vdWsurf framework is well established55,56, recently we have developed a fully first–principles strategy for evaluating the screened vdW parameters within this scheme, which substantially enhances its generality and predictive power. In this approach, the Lifshitz–Zaremba–Kohn (LZK) theory is combined with the RPA and the Drude model to compute the frequency–dependent dielectric function of metal substrates. This enables a fully ab initio determination of the screened vdW parameters, explicitly accounting for the collective electronic screening effects within the solid. Importantly, this strategy eliminates the reliance on experimental dielectric functions for metallic systems. This method demonstrates excellent agreement with experimental results in predicting energetics and structures of both physisorbed and chemisorbed systems44,45. For our calculations, we used the tight settings, including the standard numerical atom–centered orbital basis sets tier 2 for hydrogen (H) and carbon (C), and the tier 1 for sulfur (S), oxygen (O), selenium (Se), and copper (Cu). The convergence thresholds were set to 10−2 eV/Å for the final forces in structural relaxations, 10−5 electrons per unit volume for the charge density, and 10−6 eV for the total energy of the system. To model the Cu(111) surface, we constructed four–layer slabs with a (8 × 4) surface unit cell, which has been shown to be large enough to yield converged results12. Each slab was separated by a 30 Å vacuum. During geometry optimizations, we constrained the bottom metal layer while allowing the molecule and atoms in the three uppermost metal layers to relax. The k–points were sampled using 2 × 5 × 1 Monkhorst–Pack mesh for slab calculations. The construction of computational models and post–processing analyses were performed using the dispersion–inclusive surface chemistry optimizer, an in–house developed, open–source Python framework57.

Electrostatic potential and interfacial dipole calculations

The VASP code was employed for calculations of the work functions, molecular dipole, and bond dipole potentials. To achieve this, a dipole correction was applied along the direction perpendicular to the metal surface. Leveraging the optimized structures, we calculated the electron potential energy of trans–ADS on Cu(111) with the PBE functional. A cutoff energy of 450 eV was used for the plane–wave basis set. A Monkhorst–Pack grid with 2 × 5 × 1 k–point sampling was used. A convergence criterion of 10−4 eV/atom was adopted for the total energy calculations. The charge density difference of the physisorbed and chemisorbed systems after molecular adsorption were obtained by Δρ(z) = ρsys(z) − ρsub(z) − ρmole(z), where ρsys(z) is the charge density of the adsorption system, while ρsub(z) and ρmole(z) represent the charge densities of the substrate and the isolated molecule, respectively. The interfacial dipole can be obtained by solving Poisson’s equation58.

High–level energy corrections and IGMH analyses

High–precision corrections for periodic boundary condition (PBC) calculations are achieved using the XO–PBC scheme59. This approach involves determining a high–level (H) energy correction by evaluating an isolated cluster derived from the complete metal–adsorbate interface, then incorporating this correction into the initial low–level (L) PBC calculation result. The final energy is calculated as △EXO−PBCad=△EPBC@Lad+(△ECluster@Had−△ECluster@Lad). All PBC calculations used periodic slab models with the GGA PBE functional and D3BJ dispersion correction in VASP60. For cluster calculations, two–layer models of 22, 30, and 49 metal atoms were built for benzene, naphthalene, and ADS adsorption, respectively. The study uses the XYGJ–OS double hybrid functional51 together with the def2–SVP basis set and its associated pseudopotentials61 as the high–level method. For analyzing cluster models, electronic interactions were investigated using the independent gradient model based on the Hirshfeld partition of molecular density (IGMH)49, with B3LYP–D3BJ62 densities serving as the input for IGMH calculations. The REST program package63 was employed for the energy correction, and the IGMH was conducted with the Multiwfn program64. The figures were rendered with isosurface value of 1.0 using the VMD program. Matplotlib was used to draw the adsorption–energy bar plot.

DFT–NEGF quantum transport simulations

The asymmetric molecular junctions of M1, M2, and M3 with the arrangements of the two electrodes were optimized in the Atomistix Toolkit (ATK) software, based on DFT combined with the non–equilibrium Green’s function method65. The computational models were organized into three regions: left electrode, right electrode, and central region, which encompassed the relaxed slab model. During the optimization, the anchoring atoms were allowed to fully relax along the transport direction. To simulate the constant–height mode typically employed in STM–based switching experiments of trans–ADS, we fixed the electrode/molecule interface while maintaining the STM tip at a constant height above the metal substrate. In this setup, the STM tip is positioned at a relatively large distance from the molecule, especially for the chemisorbed state, thereby exerting a negligible perturbation on the structural integrity of the adsorbed species. Consequently, we kept the overall junction configuration fixed during the transport simulations. The model was further used to calculate the electronic transport properties, including the I–V curves, transmission spectrum and density of states of systems with asymmetric tip materials and sites. For the transport calculations, we employed the PBE functional with a double-ζ polarized LCAO basis set. A total of 261 k–points were used in the transport direction, and a real–space mesh cutoff of 100 Ha was applied to ensure accuracy and convergence. The total energies and input files (in .py format), which include the atomic coordinates of all asymmetric structures employed for electron transport calculations, are presented in the Supplementary Methods. The visualization of these asymmetric structures can be realized by importing the .py files into the ATK software.
