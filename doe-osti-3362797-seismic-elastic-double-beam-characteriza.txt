Executive summary

Site characterization for underground injection and storage of gigatonne-scale CO₂ requires
reliable and cost-effective methods to detect and characterize faults and fractures and to assess
their stress state and fault activation potential. This is critical, as wastewater injection and
disposal have been shown to activate faults and induce earthquakes, and CO₂ leakage remains a
key concern for long-term storage.
 In this project, we developed seismic methods to detect and characterize large-scale
sedimentary and crystalline basement faults and associated small-scale fractures below
conventional seismic imaging resolution using multicomponent (9C) surface seismic data.
Machine learning was used to automatically interpret large-scale faults, providing key
information for estimating the maximum magnitude of potential induced earthquakes. High-
fidelity imaging was achieved by exploiting redundancy across multiple elastic wave modes,
where independent images from different modes and frequencies cross-validate each other. We
also used our nonlinear signal comparison (NLSC) method for ground roll removal, improving
data quality in complex near-surface conditions.
 The methods were validated using field data acquired in central Montana. Results show that
basement faults extend into the sedimentary section and that small-scale fractures are widespread
above the basement. The inferred stress orientation is consistent with regional stress data, and the
estimated maximum induced earthquake magnitude is small (Mw ~2.3).The developed workflow
provides a practical approach for fault and fracture characterization and for assessing induced
seismicity and leakage risk. It is directly applicable to CO₂ storage site selection and to other
subsurface systems.

1 Introduction

This project is in response to Area of Interest 1a—Fault Detection, Characterization, and Hazard
Assessment under DOE FOA DE-FOA-0002401 “Emerging CO₂ Storage Technologies:
Optimizing Performance Through Minimization of Seismicity Risks and Monitoring Caprock
Integrity”.
 Site characterization for underground injection and storage of gigatonne-scale CO2 requires
reliable and cost-effective methods to detect and characterize faults and fractures to determine
their stress state and activation potential. It is known that injection of a large quantity of
wastewater has activated faults in the crystalline basement and induced pervasive earthquakes in
Oklahoma and Texas (Walsh and Zoback, 2015; Quinones et al., 2019). Noncontainment of
injected carbon dioxide has been reported at permitted CCS sites in recent regulatory actions (U.
S. Environmental Protection Agency, 2024).
1.1 Technical objectives
The proposed work emphasizes novel seismic method and algorithm development rather than
field operations. The primary objective is to produce new cost-effective, and self-validating
fault/fracture detection and characterization algorithms using multicomponent surface seismic
data. The final deliverable includes a scalable seismic algorithm for subsurface characterization
of faults and fractures, designed to support gigatonne-scale CO₂ storage scenarios to improve
risk assessment and decision-making for safe and secure long-term storage. The developed
algorithm is also useful in other geological settings such as geothermal and unconventional
energies. Using the scaling law between the earthquake magnitude and fault area, we can
estimate the maximum earthquake magnitude based on the detected faults.

1.2 Technical roadmap

Specifically, we aim to develop new methods to detect/directly image large-scale and previously
unidentified sedimentary and crystalline basement faults and to directly characterize small-scale
fractures (Figure 1a).
 To characterize small-scale fractures below conventional seismic imaging resolution, we
propose a novel elastic double-beam (eDB), which uses seismic wave diffraction pattern
observed on the surface to characterize subsurface fractures. Small-scale fractures can be fluid
flow (or CO2 leakage) pathways,
 To image large-scale faults, we use an elastic reverse time migration (RTM) and machine
learning. Faults are often interpreted by geologists from seismic images indirectly. To avoid
human subjective biases, we use a machine learning approach.

 7

 In particular, we will conduct both a synthetic test and a multicomponent 9-C field seismic
dataset acquired in central Montana to validate our methods. In decision making, reliability of
the available information is paramount.
 This project is divided into a phase 1 and phase 2. In phase 1, we mainly concentrated on
synthetic model and datasets; in phase 2, we worked on field data and synthesis.

Figure 1. Technical roadmap. (a) Our work targets include: [1] imaging basement faults and fracture

2 Fault and fracture imaging and detection and synthetic tests

2.1 Background on fracture and fault imaging

 8

Information on both faults and fractures is critical in understanding potential seismicity,
subsurface stress field and potential fluid flow pathways (Kang et al., 2011; Nick et al., 2011;
Kang et al., 2016). Therefore, cost-effective methods for reliable detection of faults/factures
remotely are crucial in GCS. Here we distinguish faults and fractures. Faults are generally
interpreted in time or depth migrated seismic images based on offsets in seismic images across
the interpreted faults. Faults have much larger spatial scales than the seismic wavelength and are
usually associated with fault surface slip (throw). Previously, fault interpretation was carried out
by human interpreters in the oil industry. Recently, there were many progresses in using machine
learning methods to detect large-scale faults (e.g., Wu and Fomel, 2018; Gao et al., 2021a; Gao
et al., 2021b). If one can estimate the fault area and measure the cumulative throw based on the
image offset, one can put a bound on the maximum moment magnitude of a potential earthquake,
natural or induced.
 On the other hand, fractures (or joints) appear in clusters (Gale et al., 2007) and they are
smaller than the seismic wavelength in space. In contrast with faults, small-scale fractures may
not cause visible offsets on seismic images. Burns et al. (2007) showed that the standard
common-mid-point stacked trace over a fractured reservoir is the same as if there were no
fractures if the source-receiver line is perpendicular to fracture planes.
 Conventional fracture characterization was in fact indirectly inferred based on the
assumption that aligned fractures can cause seismic anisotropy (e.g., Thomsen, 1995). The
symmetry types of the anisotropy can be the horizontally transverse isotropy (HTI) caused by a
set of vertical fractures, or the orthorhombic owing to two vertical fracture sets orthogonal to
each other, or the monoclinic because of two fracture sets that are at an angle not 90 degrees. Of
course, the fractured layer can also be tiled, which adds more parameters and challenges to
fracture characterization. There are two broad methodologies for anisotropy characterization:
AVO/AVAZ (amplitude variation with offset and azimuth for the reflected waves (Rüger and
Tsvankin, 1997; e.g., Lynn et al., 1999; Perez et al., 1999; Thomsen, 1999; DeVault et al., 2002;
Stewart et al., 2002; 2003; Vasconcelos and Grechka, 2007; Far et al., 2014) and the shear-wave
splitting (Crampin, 1985; Tatham et al., 1992; e.g., Vetri et al., 2003; Long, 2013; Verdon and
Wustefeld, 2013). These anisotropy-related methods are based on the effective medium theory
(EMT), which implicitly assumes that the distribution of fractures is spatially uniform and has
one spatial scale (i.e., the distance between neighboring fractures) (e.g., David et al., 1990). If
there is some randomness in the distribution of natural fractures (e.g., azimuth or spacing),
spatial clusters can emerge (Fang et al., 2017), and the fracture system can exhibit multiple
spatial scales (e.g., distance between neighboring fractures, size of the fracture clusters, distance
between clusters, etc.). These multiple scales can produce multiple scattering and interference.
As a result, the EMT-based methods can produce erroneous results for randomly distributed
fractures (Fang et al., 2017). Another challenge in the application of the EMT if the fractured
layer is thin. In this case, there would be not enough shear wave splitting effect or AVO effect to
be observed.
 There are also other methods for detecting fractures. The Scattering Index (SI) methodology
(e.g., Willis et al., 2006; Fang et al., 2013a; Fang et al., 2013b) is a data-based method and it

 9

used the azimuthal differences in the stacked data. SI can yield information on fracture
orientation. Diffraction imaging (Fomel et al., 2007; Landa et al., 2008; Landa et al., 2011; e.g.,
Klokov and Fomel, 2012; Landa, 2012; Landa et al., 2013; Schoepp et al., 2015; Protasov et al.,
2016; Silvestrov et al., 2016) tries to migrate fracture-diffracted signals to form a subsurface
image. Challenges exist for this method because the amplitudes of the diffracted signals are
much weaker compared with those of specular reflections from layer interfaces, isolation of the
fracture diffraction is a prerequisite. If a fracture behaves as a diffractor, the non-specular
imaging filtered in the local image matrix can be directly used to identify scattering/diffraction
features in the subsurface (Zhu and Wu, 2010). However, these scattering/diffraction-based
imaging/migration methods can only provide locations of focused scattering energy instead of
the local fracture spacing and fracture orientation. The image of scattering waves needs further
interpretation to obtain the detailed spatial distribution of fractures and their connections via such
as ant-tracking (e.g., Pedersen et al., 2002; Zhu and Wu, 2010; Aarre et al., 2012).

2.2 Elastic double-beam (eDB) method for imaging discrete fractures

The acoustic double-beam (DB) method developed by Zheng et al. (2013) aims to characterize
fractures in a fractured reservoir using the interference pattern of multiple-scattering (in the
horizontal directions) waves from fractures. In (Zheng et al., 2013), they synthesize a group of
point sources into a directional source beam shot into the subsurface fracture targets. A fracture
target is a box that may contain multiple fractures. The source beam interacts with the fractures
locally and produces a scattered beam propagating to surface receivers. The scattering direction of
the receiver beam depends on the orientation and density of the fractures.

Figure 2. Basic idea of the double-beam using beam scattering. (a) model representation using “boxes.”
Green and red lines are fracture planes. (b) source beam scattering by fractures to create seismic
interference pattern. (c) P-to-P, P-to-Sin, P-to-Santi beam scattering geometry.

 The eDB fracture characterization is based on scattering and the basic physical idea can be
understood in Figure 2. We partition the geological layer as “boxes” (Figure 2a). We then shoot a
beam from the surface to bombard a subsurface “box.” The beam is multiply scattered by the
fractures in the box, generating a seismic wave interference pattern observed on the surface. The

 10

incident P-wave source beam is scattered as P, anti-plane polarized Santi (parallel to fracture
strike), in-plane polarized Sin (perpendicular to fracture strike). Our goal is to take the observed
three DB interference patterns (i.e., P-to-P, P-Santi, P-Sin) to characterize discrete fractures in
the box.
 The outputs of eDB are the fracture parameters as a function of space: fracture orientation,
density, and fracture compliance (Schoenberg, 1980). Because of the use of a source beam and a
receiver beam, the method is called the double-beam method (DB for short). Both beams are
focused Gaussian beams toward the fracture target. Hu and Zheng (2018) extended the original
DB into a dipping layer case. Hu et al. (2018) also extended it to the case of multiple sets of
irregularly distributed random fractures and elastic case(Hu et al., 2021). DB can successfully
and correctly characterize fractures in terms of fracture parameters: fracture orientation, fracture
spacing and compliance, even for multiple coexisting fractures with random spacing and varying
compliances in a non-flat dipping reservoir layer. The DB interference pattern (Figure 2b) is a
function of the frequency, the fracture plane orientation (the polar angle), and the averaging
spacing between fractures in the box (i.e., the radius). Large amplitude “red spots” in the DB
pattern indicate true orientation and spacing. Multiple spots show conjugate fracture sets in the
same box, trending in different orientations and having different spacings. For eDB, we can
obtain three DB patterns (Figure 2c) because of P-to-P and P-to-S scattering. Decomposition of
the vector S wave into anti-plane and in-plane components is a systematic manner of analyzing S
waves.
2.3 eDB features
 Specifically, eDB has the following unique innovative capabilities for fracture
characterization:
 1) The orientation, density, and fracture compliance (Schoenberg, 1980) for multiple sets of
 conjugate fractures as a function of space;
 2) If there are no fractures, eDB will output ‘no fracture.’ This detection capability is
 important for GCS injection.
 3) eDB is frequency independent. Results from different frequencies should be the same and
 can cross validate one another.
 4) eDB is based on elastic waves, and P-to-P, P-to-Sin, and P-to-Santi should produce the
 same fracture parameters, another cross-validation feature.
 5) The fracture compliance is related to the fracture saturation property, gas or liquid filled.
 A large compliance is also found to be related to fluid flow property.
 6) The performance of eDB is not affected by layer reflections, multiples, or surface waves.
 7) eDB can also characterize fractures in thin layers, which overcomes one challenge in the
 anisotropy-based method.
 8) No interpretation step is needed. eDB directly outputs the parameters of interest, which
 overcomes the additional work in SI and diffraction imaging.

2.6 Enhancing fault images using elastic deconvolution reverse time migration

Subsurface imaging using elastic migration of multicomponent data is challenging because of
difficulty in properly handling of shear-wave polarizations in complex media. Our vector elastic
deconvolutional reverse-time migration method can properly handle shear-wave polarizations
and enhance the image resolution. The method employs an imaging condition based on a dual
wavefield decomposition strategy, including a directional wavefield decomposition using Hilbert
transform and a compressional/shear (P/S) vector wavefield decomposition using the low-rank
method, to accurately image subsurface structures in isotropic and anisotropic elastic media. The
elastic deconvolutional imaging condition is equivalent to seeking an optimal space-time Wiener
filter between the upgoing and downgoing complex vector elastic wavefields.
 We use synthetic multicomponent surface seismic data for vertical sources on the surface of
the SEG/EAGE overthrust model to demonstrate the improved imaging capability of the vector
elastic deconvolutional reverse-time migration method. Figure 5 shows the P-wave velocity
model and its smoothed model of the SEG/EAGE overthrust model containing several overthrust
faults. The shear-wave velocity Vs model has the same spatial distribution with a ratio of
Vp/Vs=1.5 where Vp is the P-wave velocity. Figure 6 displays migration imaging results
obtained using the vector elastic reverse-time migration method developed by Gao and Huang

(2019). Figure 7 shows much improved migration images obtained using LANL’s recently
developed vector elastic deconvolutional reverse-time migration method. The images in Figure 7
show the overthrust faults with much higher resolution and image quality than those in Figure 6,
making it possible for reliable fault detection.

2.7 Fault detection using machine learning

We used LANL’s nested residual U-shaped convolutional neural network to automatically detect
faults on 3D seismic migration images including PP, SP, PS, and SS images. The objectives are
to improve the ability to cost-effectively and reliably detect faults during site characterization,
and to detect previously unidentified faults in the area, particularly in the basement.
 Fault detection on 3D seismic images by a seismic interpreter is challenging and time-
consuming, taking usually more than 10 days. Human picking of faults is sometimes impossible
for complex 3D fault systems. Ant tracking of the Schlumberger Petrel package is the industry
widely-used fault detection technique. It is more efficient than human fault picking and takes a
few days for a large-scale 3D image. In addition, ant-tracking can be more accurate compared
with manual picking (Silva et al., 2005). However, it is an attribute-based approach and could
potentially introduce uncertainty in the results for noisy images.
 Human picking and semi-human-intervened fault detection approaches are being replaced by
fully automatic methods with the development of machine learning. Machine learning-based
fault detection can greatly improve the computational efficiency and reliability of automatic fault
detection, reducing the computational time to hours for large-scale 3D seismic images. The
current state-of-the-art automatic fault detection based on machine learning is based on
Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) (Wu et al., 2019). The method is based on a simplified

 15

version of the U-Net developed initially for image segmentation. U-Net is a special type of CNN
that contains an encoding branch and a decoding branch. Wu et al. (2019) demonstrated that the
U-Net-based approach provides superior detection accuracy and significantly improved
computational efficiency compared with conventional attribute-based approaches.

LANL has recently developed a novel fault detection method using a nested residual U-shaped
convolutional neural network, called NRU for short. Each of the encoders and decoders in NRU

particularly on noisy seismic images.
 Figure 8 demonstrates the fault detection capability of NRU on a 3D noisy synthetic seismic

3 Field data in Montana

3.1 Seismic acquisition and tectonics

We used 9-component (9C) seismic data acquired by Vecta Oil & Gas using 3-component
sources and 3-component receivers in Central Montana for this research. The proprietary 9C 3D
seismic dataset was acquired in 2011 using shear-wave sources and recorded all modes,
including the PS and SP modes. Vecta carried out the seismic survey over an area of ~17 square
miles in the Central Montana Trough, a Laramide feature bounded by pre-existing basement
faults—some with surface exposure—reactivated in an oblique-slip and strike-slip sense.
 CO2 could be stored in any number of formations above the basement, including the
Cambrian, Amsden oil reservoir itself, and the Cat Creek in the Cretaceous. All these units are
regionally extensive, so storage potential is very high. The trapping structure is several square
miles in area.
 En-echelon basement fault sets are observed inside the 9C 3D data and likely contribute to
natural fracturing in the basement and overlying sedimentary overburden. Several wells drilled
inside the 9C 3D seismic data contain dipole sonic logs where shear-wave splitting diagnostic of
natural fracturing is observed in the Cretaceous and Paleozoic overburden above the basement,
the top of which occurs at a depth of around 3 km. No wells penetrate basement inside the
seismic survey area. The presence of natural fracturing in the overburden is further demonstrated
by lost-circulation zones encountered in the Cretaceous in most wells in the field, as well as the
production of fresh water derived from deeper zones in the main oil reservoir in the field. Zones
of moderate to high shear-wave splitting are observed at some locations in the sedimentary

 17

overburden on the shear modes of the survey, and it is likely that the natural fracturing seen in
the wells and seismic extends to the basement interval as well.
 It is crucial to reliably detect and characterize faults and fractures and assess seismic hazards
for the deployment of large-scale geologic carbon storage in this area. Usually, seismic migrated
images are formed by reflected waves. Land seismic also contains ground rolls or surface waves
which propagate along the Earth’s surface. These waves are usually considered as noise.

3.2 Ground roll removal of field data

The vibroseis sources of the field data generated strong ground rolls. Removing them is a critical
step before any further processing. We applied the proprietary algorithm of University of
Houston group. The algorithm is based on a novel nonlinear signal comparison (NLSC)
technique to precisely measure surface wave dispersion. We then accurately predict the surface
waveform and subtract it from data (Zheng and Hu, 2017; Hu et al., 2019; Hu and Zheng, 2019;
Zheng and Hu, 2021; Zheng and Hu, 2024). In the NLSC prediction, the input is the whole field
data including both reflections and surface waves and the output is only the surface waves.
Conventionally, ground roll removal was done by f-k or two-station crosscorrelation. However,
these types of measurement resolution depends on large receiver spread which may cover
heterogeneous media with significant lateral variations.

 18

the horizontal distance is 2500m. The vertical axis is time (unit masked out; not shown).

 NLSC can deliver high resolution dispersion measurement using close receivers, under which
the subsurface is likely to be locally layered. NLSC has the following nice properties:
 • NLSC can surgically remove surface waves and keep intact the useful reflections.
 • It can work with a wide range of complex geological terranes.
 • It can handle irregularly distributed seismic sources and receivers.
 • It can work with multicomponent seismic data.

4 Machine learning fault detection

During our project, we improved the machine learning fault detection work. Now we call our
new algorithm FaultNeXt.

4.1 FaultNeXt machine learning architecture

Fault detection is a crucial component of site characterization for exploration and monitoring
programs. Faults can act as traps for hydrocarbon reservoirs or instead as passageways for
geothermal fluids or supercritical CO2 to migrate. Although proper structural models of the
subsurface are commonly necessary, expert fault interpretation is expensive and time consuming.
To overcome these problems, many authors have turned to convolutional neural networks (CNN)
(Lecun et al., 1998), and in particular variants of U-Net (Ronneberger et al., 2015). For example,
the paper by Wu et al. (2019) used a simple 3D U-Net. Recently, transformers have shown state-
of-the-art (SOTA) results on image tasks and improvements such as the shifted window (swin)
transformer (Liu et al., 2021) are shown to be very versatile tools. Since transformers lack the
implicit bias that the limited receptive field of CNNs exhibit, they can learn long-range spatial
relationships, but it comes at a cost: they require more training data, and thus longer training
times (Dosovitskiy, 2020). The already high demand for labeled data (synthetic or not) means
that it may not be feasible to simply continue to scale transformers. We follow the reasoning
outlined in Woo et al. (2023) and Roy et al. (2023) which utilizes the strengths from both CNNs
and transformers. We first discuss the synthetic data generation, then the architecture of
FaultNeXt, and end by showing results on field seismic migration images.
 Due to the lack of expertly labeled fault volumes available, we follow the approach by
Wu et al. (2019); Wu et al. (2020) and others by using synthetic data to train FaultNeXt. First,
we create a vector by sampling a random Gaussian distribution and copy that vector to all (x, y)
coordinates. Then, we generate a 2D array of fractal noise with which we vertically shear the

The FaultNeXt architecture follows the methods of Roy et al. (2023) and is shown in Figure 13.
Modern conv-nets described in Todi et al. (2023); Woo et al. (2023) use transformer inspired
components such as feature map complexity reducing stem blocks, inverted bottle necks, and
depth-wise convolutions. These components allow higher image throughput without sacrificing,
or even increasing, model performance. Other features include the replacement of ReLU
activation function with the more recent GELU (Hendrycks, 2016), and modified expansion
ratios from standard conv-nets. Additionally, the CNNs retain the inductive bias that
transformers lack to more quickly converge to a solution. The input to the network is a 2D or 3D
seismic migration image, and the output is a 2D or 3D probability cube. The probability cube
ranges from 0 to 1 and represents the estimated probability that the pixel belongs to the
foreground class (a fault) as opposed to the background class (not a fault). Training consists of

 22

inputting a synthetic seismic data square or cube and comparing the output with the ground truth.
The performance of the network is measured by the soft Dice loss function (Jadon, 2020), which
we look to minimize over the course of training. FaultNeXt is trained with Pytorch (Paszke et al.,
2017) for 40 epochs using Adam optimizer (Kingma, 2014) and an exponential learning rate
scheduler.

6 Small-scale fracture characterization using eDB and stress

Using the 9C field data, we run our double-beam method for small-scale fracture
characterization. The advantage of using multicomponent elastic waves is that we will use elastic
DB results of P-P and P-S scattering modes to cross-validate among one another, because they
should all yield the same consistent results of fracture parameters. The DB method is target
oriented. We can place the fracture target at any location. For each target at x, y, z location, we
perform DB fracture analysis. The computation cost linearly scales with the number of fracture
targets. In principle, one can place fracture targets everywhere in the subsurface.
 In the following, we show the DB fracture detection results at a depth ~2100 m,
corresponding to the top of the Madison group (Figure 20). The locations of fracture targets are
shown in Figure 21. Field observations, including production of water from deeper zones,
suggest that natural fracture systems are active and may connect different formations above the
top of the Madison Group. Therefore, fault and fracture connectivity is a key factor controlling
fluid flow.
 At each target, we perform DB analysis using both P-P data and P-S data at different
frequencies. Figure 22 shows such an example. A MATLAB GUI has been developed to pick a
fracture orientation. We will pick a fracture orientation only when we see consistency among all
the DB plots.

7 Conclusions

In this project, we developed seismic methods to detect and characterize faults and fractures for
CO₂ storage applications using multicomponent (9C) surface seismic data. Synthetic tests and
field data analysis show that both large-scale basement faults and small-scale fractures below
conventional seismic resolution can be reliably identified.
 We also demonstrated our nonlinear signal comparison (NLSC) method for ground roll
removal. The method predicts and removes surface waves directly from the field data while
preserving reflection signals. Compared with conventional f–k filtering, NLSC provides higher
resolution and performs well in complex near-surface conditions, improving the quality of
subsequent imaging and interpretation.
 Our machine learning (FaultNeXt) enables efficient detection of large-scale faults from
seismic images. Application to field data in central Montana shows that basement faults extend
into the sedimentary section and that small-scale fractures are present above the basement. The
maximum fault patch area in Figure 19 is ~20,000 m²; therefore, the estimated maximum
induced earthquake magnitude is Mw ~2.3 for a pre-existing basement fault.

 32

 The elastic double-beam (eDB) method directly provides fracture orientation and average
spacing. Results from different elastic wave modes (P-P and P-S) are consistent, providing cross-
validation of the detected fracture parameters and improving confidence in the interpretation.
The inferred SHmax orientation (~NE–SW) at ~2100 m depth (top of the Madison Group) is
consistent with regional stress data, indicating that the detected fracture system could be
controlled by the present-day stress field.
 These results indicate that, although multiple formations provide sufficient storage capacity,
the main uncertainty for CO₂ storage in this area is containment. Faults and fractures may act as
migration pathways and need to be carefully evaluated for safe and long-term storage.

8 Broader applications

The methods developed in this project are applicable beyond CO₂ storage. The NLSC method
improves seismic data quality in the presence of strong surface-wave noise and can be applied to
land seismic surveys in complex near-surface conditions.
 The eDB method provides direct fracture characterization, including orientation, spacing, and
compliance, and can be applied to subsurface systems where fracture-controlled flow is
important, such as hydrocarbon reservoirs, geothermal systems, and groundwater aquifers.
 Machine learning–based fault detection (FaultNeXt) can be used for structural interpretation
of large 3D seismic datasets and for identifying previously unmapped faults. The workflow can
be applied to both sedimentary and crystalline basement settings.
 The combined workflow can also be used to assess induced seismicity by identifying fault
systems and estimating potential earthquake magnitudes. This is relevant for wastewater
injection, geothermal stimulation, and subsurface energy storage.
 These methods provide a scalable approach for characterizing fault and fracture systems and
for evaluating fluid flow and mechanical stability in a wide range of subsurface applications.
