Novel Insights
into the Acute and Sublethal Toxicity
of Layered Double Hydroxide Nanoparticles in Spodoptera
frugiperda Larvae

André Lopes Ferreira, Kelly Cristina Gonçalves, Sofia Cristina Picotte, Raqueli Viana, Ricardo Antonio Polanczyk, Marcelo Bispo de Jesus

Abstract

Nanotechnology is increasingly integrated into agriculture,
yet
some nanomaterials are investigated primarily as delivery platforms,
nanofertilizers, controlled-release pesticides, antimicrobials, or
oligonucleotide delivery systems, while their intrinsic biological
activity remains largely unexplored. Layered double hydroxide (LDH)
transition metal containing are widely assumed to be biologically
inert despite their tunable metal composition, high surface reactivity,
and potential to interact directly with biological systems. Here,
we evaluated the acute and sublethal toxicity of LDH nanoparticles
containing transition metals (Al–Ni, Al–Co, or Al–Cu)
on Spodoptera frugiperda larvae. Laboratory
bioassays were performed by incorporating nanoparticles into the larval
artificial diet to quantify dose–mortality relationships, estimate
LC50 values, and assess impacts on larval and pupal development.
All LDH formulations exhibited dose-dependent toxicity, with Al–Cu
LDH showing the highest potency and the lowest LC50 (19.1–37.3
mg mL–1). Furthermore, a significant reduction in
pupation rate and larval weight was observed, suggesting relevant
sublethal effects. These findings provide evidence that metallic LDHs
possess bioactivity against an agriculturally important pest, challenging
the assumption of LDH inertness and revealing their potential as dual-function
materials that combine inherent biological effects with delivery capabilities.
This work establishes a new foundation for evaluating LDHs within
pest-management frameworks and underscores the need to assess selectivity,
environmental fate, and impacts on nontarget organisms before field
deployment.

Introduction

The increase in the world population continues
to intensify the
demand for food, reinforcing the need for agricultural practices that
ensure both food security and production sustainability. However,
crop yields are constrained by multiple factors, including abiotic
stresses such as drought, low temperatures, salinity, and soil waterlogging.
Biotic pressures exerted by pathogenic microorganisms (bacteria, viruses,
fungi, and parasites), weeds, and insect pests further exacerbate
these limitations. These environmental
and biological stressors trigger complex physiological responses in
plants, compromising their growth, metabolism, and development. Among
these biotic stressors, insect pests constitute one of the most pervasive
and economically damaging threats to global agricultural productivity.

Arthropods account for substantial annual losses, estimated at
18–20% of total production, which represents an economic impact
of approximately USD 470 billion, driving
extensive reliance on chemical control strategies. Among these pests,
the fall armyworm (Spodoptera frugiperda) stands out as one of the most destructive agricultural pests due
to its exceptional polyphagy, capable of feeding on over 350 plant
species, including major crops such as maize, rice, wheat, and sorghum. Moreover, S. frugiperda exhibits rapid development, high dispersal capacity, and a remarkable
capacity for resistance evolution. S. frugiperda is particularly devastating in tropical and subtropical regions;
for example, annual losses exceed USD 9 billion across sub-Saharan
Africa and around USD 400 million in Brazil because under severe infestation,
maize productivity can decrease by 50% to 80%.
−

 Given its biology
and widespread resistance to conventional insecticides, S. frugiperda has become a central driver of pesticide
use and an urgent priority for the development of more efficient,
selective, and sustainable technologies.

In contemporary agriculture,
the use of agrochemicals, including
fertilizers and pesticides remains critical for ensuring crop productivity,
although their intensive and recurrent use has raised serious concerns.
Residues in food, soil contamination, and water pollution pose risks
to human and animal health, underscoring the need for safer and more
environmentally responsible alternatives.
,
 Many
pest species exhibit high reproductive rates, remarkable adaptive
capacity, and develop resistance to pesticides, progressively reducing
the effectiveness of current management strategies.
,
 Moreover,
the recurrent use of broad-spectrum insecticides can disrupt ecological
balance, harm beneficial organisms, and promote pest resurgence. Climatic
conditions also play a crucial role, as variations in temperature
and humidity directly influence pest distribution, reproduction, survival,
and infestation intensity. In light of these multiple obstacles, it
becomes evident that innovative, selective, and sustainable approaches
are essential to achieving more efficient and resilient agricultural
pest management.

In response to
these challenges, nanomaterials have emerged as
a promising class of tools capable of enabling more selective, efficient,
and sustainable pest-management strategies. Among engineered nanomaterials Layered
Double Hydroxides (LDHs) containing transition metal stand out due
to their positively charged surfaces, tunable metal composition, and
high anion-exchange capacity, properties that strongly influence their
interactions with biological systems.
,
 These attributes
position LDHs as a compelling candidate for agricultural delivery
platforms, yet their intrinsic biological activity has rarely been
investigated, and LDHs are still broadly assumed to be biologically
inert.

We therefore hypothesized that LDHs, particularly those
containing
transition metals, could induce detectable acute or developmental
effects in S. frugiperda. Testing this
hypothesis would not only clarify the intrinsic biological activity
of these materials but also determine whether LDHs could serve as
inherently bioactive platforms for future delivery strategies in pest
management. To test this hypothesis, we followed a logical experimental
framework that integrates (i.) acute toxicity assays
for determination of dose–mortality relationships and LC50 values, (ii.) sublethal developmental assessments
to quantify impacts on larval growth, pupal formation, and metamorphosis,
and, (iii.) comparative analysis among LDH compositions
(Al–Ni, Al–Co, Al–Cu) to understand how metal
composition influences acute and sublethal responses. By combining
these complementary approaches, this study aims to determine not only
whether LDHs can affect survival and development, but also whether
their effects are sufficient to justify consideration of LDHs as stable,
biocompatible, and potentially materials for pest management.

Materials and Methods

Synthesis and Characterization of Nanoparticles

Layered
double hydroxides (LDHs) were synthesized by the coprecipitation method.
Saline solutions (10 mL) containing M2+ (3.0 mmol L–1) and M3+ (1.0 mmol L–1) were slowly added to 40
mL of NaOH (0.15 mol L-1), previously deaerated with N2, under vigorous
stirring for 20 min in an inert atmosphere. The resulting precipitate
was separated by centrifugation, washed, and subjected to hydrothermal
treatment at 100 °C for 16 h. The formed crystals were redispersed
in 50 mL of deionized water, yielding a homogeneous, stable suspension.
Dynamic light scattering (DLS) and zeta potential analyses were performed
using a ZetaSizer Nano ZS 90 (Malvern, UK) at 25 °C and a 90°
angle, using standardized suspensions at 0.1 mg mL–1. A more comprehensive physicochemical characterization of these
formulations (including transmission electron microscopy (TEM), X-ray
diffraction (XRD), Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR),
and long-term colloidal stability analyses) has been reported in our
complementary study.

Rearing of S. frugiperda

The colony was maintained at the Laboratory of Microbial Control
of Pest Arthropods, FCAV/UNESP. Adults were kept in plastic cages
(35 cm × 30 cm) and fed with a 10% honey solution. Larvae were
fed an artificial diet composed of common bean (Phaseolus
vulgaris), wheat germ, and soy protein, prepared in the laboratory. Eggs were collected
daily. After hatching, larvae were placed individually in plastic
containers (7 cm diameter; 3 cm height) and fed the artificial diet
until pupation. Pupae were sexed and transferred to cages until adult
emergence. Insects were reared in a climate-controlled chamber at
25 ± 2 °C, 60–70% relative humidity, and a 16:8 h
(light:dark) photoperiod.

Lethal Concentration (LC50) Bioassays

Preliminary
screening assays were conducted to determine the range of concentrations
to be used in the experiments. The selected concentrations were Al–Ni
(8–100 mg mL–1), Al–Co (3–100
mg mL–1), Al–Cu (1–100 mg mL–1), and an untreated control. Each treatment consisted of in 100 insects
per treatment.

Mortality Bioassays

Nanoparticles were diluted in sterile
deionized water, and 80 μL of the suspension was uniformly applied
to the surface of the diet contained in transparent cylindrical plastic
containers (Ø = 2.5 cm; volume = 1.5 cm3). Sterile
deionized water was used as the control. After application, the diets
were allowed to dry completely, and one second-instar larva was then
placed in each container (n = 100).

Sublethal Effects on S. frugiperda Larval Development

To evaluate the effects of each nanoparticle
treatment on larval development, surviving larvae were weighed from
day 12 onward, at two-day intervals, using an analytical balance,
until pupation, when the pupae were also weighed. The measurements
of the larvae and pupae were then compared with the control group
to identify possible developmental differences.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed
using GraphPad Prism version 8.0.2 (GraphPad Software, San Diego,
CA, USA). Mortality data were analyzed by nonlinear regression using
a four-parameter logistic model after logarithmic transformation of
the concentrations to estimate the median lethal concentration (LC50) and the corresponding 95% confidence intervals. Larval
and pupal weight data are presented as mean ± standard deviation
(SD). Differences among treatments were analyzed independently at
each assessment point using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA),
followed by a multiple-comparison test. Pupation rates were expressed
as percentages and analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA)
followed by a multiple-comparison test. Statistical significance was
established at p < 0.05.

Results and Discussion

Acute Toxicity and Concentration-Dependent Mortality

Understanding the direct biological effects of LDH nanoparticles
on Spodoptera frugiperda is central
to evaluating their potential as novel pest-management tools. S. frugiperda is an exceptionally damaging and resilient
lepidopteran pest, with well-documented resistance to multiple classes
of conventional insecticides and Bt crops, which makes the discovery
of new modes of action particularly valuable.
,,
 All three formulations were previously confirmed
to exhibit the characteristic lamellar structure, hexagonal platelet
morphology, and FTIR spectral signatures of LDH materials. The hydrodynamic diameters and zeta potentials
measured in the present study are comparable to those reported in
the complementary study within expected batch-to-batch variability:
266.8 nm and +41.49 mV (Al–Ni), 223.8 nm and +47.0 mV (Al–Co),
and 241.3 nm and +46.0 mV (Al–Cu). Given this context, assessing
mortality following exposure to metallic LDHs provides critical insight
into whether these nanomaterials can exert biologically meaningful
pressure on a species that is notoriously difficult to suppress. The
use of S. frugiperda, a species that
is widely distributed, agriculturally significant, and easily maintained
under laboratory conditions, enables controlled and reproducible evaluation
of these potential effects. The results obtained from the acute toxicity
assays () demonstrated
that Layered Double Hydroxide (LDH) nanoparticles containing transition
metals (Al–Ni, Al–Co, and Al–Cu) caused larval
mortality in S. frugiperda in a clearly
concentration-dependent manner.

Al–Ni LDH nanomaterial exhibited progressive
mortality with
increasing concentration, reaching 92% at 100 mg mL–1, with an LC50 of 58.12 mg mL–1 (53.7
to 64.9 mg mL–1; dark gray line in ). The ingestion of nickel
nanoparticles may result in their accumulation in the gastrointestinal
tract, causing histological alterations,
physiological dysfunctions, and imbalance of the gut microbiota. Furthermore, internalized particles can induce
cellular and tissue damage either through direct contact with organelles
or through the release of Ni2+ ions following dissolution,
acting as an intracellular nickel delivery system capable of causing
ionoregulatory, oxidative, or respiratory stress associated with dissolved
Ni toxicity.
,

Al–Co nanoparticles
exhibited a marked increase in mortality
even at lower concentrations, with 40% mortality observed at 10 mg
mL–1, reaching 100% mortality at 50 mg mL–1. The estimated LC50 was 40.81 mg mL–1 (36.8–46.3 mg mL–1; red line in ), indicating higher
relative toxicity compared to the nickel-based material. One hypothesis
for this difference may be related to the higher redox potential of
cobalt, which can induce oxidative stress through the generation of
reactive oxygen species (ROS), affecting larval metabolism and compromising
epithelial tissues.
,

Al–Cu nanoparticle
formulation provided the lowest LC50 of 21.05 mg mL–1 (95% CI: 19.1 to 37.3
mg mL–1), indicating greater toxicity even at lower
concentrations (green line in ) when compared with the other nanoparticle formulations
(Al–Ni and Al–Co). Despite the nonlinear initial response
observed at lower doses, mortality increased sharply above 20 mg mL–1. One possible explanation for the elevated toxicity
can be attributed to the partial degradation of nanoparticles in the
larval intestinal tract and the consequent release of Cu2+ ions, known to interact with essential proteins and enzymes, leading
to denaturation and homeostatic imbalance in the larvae. Moreover,
metallic nanoparticles can inhibit substrate migration across cell
membranes, disrupt basic metabolic processes in electron transfer
systems, and impair respiration in insect pests.
,
 Although our study did not aim to fully elucidate the mechanisms
triggered by nanoparticle exposure, the literature provides some insights,
for instance, NPs can easily bind to and disrupt biological molecules
such as DNA, lipids, and proteins, potentially causing histological
damage and malformations.
,

Taken together,
the results demonstrate that all tested LDH formulations
exert dose-dependent toxicity against S. frugiperda, with copper-containing LDHs producing the most pronounced effects,
followed by cobalt and nickel. By quantifying mortality after 10 days
of exposure, this study provides evidence that LDH nanoparticles can
directly compromise the survival of a major agricultural pest, positioning
metallic LDHs as candidates for futures studies on insect-control
technologies. The concentration range evaluated in this study was
selected to establish dose–response relationships and determine
LC50 values under controlled laboratory conditions. Therefore,
the tested concentrations should be interpreted as part of an initial
toxicological screening rather than a direct simulation of agricultural
application rates. Although the highest concentrations may exceed
those expected under field conditions, they provide important information
regarding the biological activity and toxicological profile of transition-metal-containing
LDHs.

Evaluation of Sublethal Effects

Acute mortality provides
an essential indication of the biological activity of LDH nanoparticles
against S. frugiperda, yet effective
pest-management technologies also depend on their capacity to disrupt
key physiological processes. Because growth suppression and delayed
development are critical determinants of population dynamics in this
resilient pest, we evaluated whether LDHs impose additional sublethal
effects beyond lethality. Assessing changes in larval body mass thus
offers a sensitive measure of metabolic and energetic disruption,
further revealing the extent to which these nanomaterials can compromise
the performance and fitness of S. frugiperda. Exposure of S. frugiperda larvae
to different compositions of Layered Double Hydroxides (Al–Ni,
Al–Co, and Al–Cu) produced distinct patterns of sublethal
growth inhibition that varied with nanoparticle composition, concentration,
and time of exposure (A–D).

Al–Ni LDHs (A) led to a marked reduction in larval mean
weight, particularly
at 8 mg mL–1, where the average weight decreased
from approximately 354 mg in the control to values close to 150–144
mg on days 20 and 22 of evaluation. This inhibitory effect on larval
growth was significant (p < 0.05) and persisted
over time; which could be attributed to the gradual release of Ni2+ from the matrix interfering with essential metabolic processes,
reducing nutrient assimilation and biomass accumulation. Higher concentrations
(10–75 mg mL–1) did not further enhance the
inhibitory effect during the initial weighing days; however, concentrations
above 10 mg mL–1 ultimately resulted in complete
larval mortality, indicating that the growth effects observed at lower
concentrations represent an early manifestation of toxicity.

Al–Co LDHs (B) exhibited the most sublethal effects among the tested formulations.
Significant reductions in larval weight were observed from 3 mg mL–1 (p < 0.05), with mean values
frequently below 200 mg even in the early days of exposure. This trend
persisted at intermediate concentrations (5–30 mg mL–1), indicating that small amounts of Al–Co LDH are sufficient
to induce severe physiological stress. Cobalt is known to participate
in redox reactions capable of generating reactive oxygen species (ROS),
leading to lipid peroxidation and mitochondrial damage.
,
 The resulting metabolic disruption likely impairs energy allocation
and growth, contributing to the rapid decline in body mass.

Al–Cu LDHs (C) displayed a nonlinear response. At low concentrations (3
mg mL–1), a significant increase in body weight
was observed compared to the control (p < 0.05),
reaching mean values close to 500 mg. However, higher concentrations
(10–20 mg mL–1) resulted in a significant
mass reduction, with values ranging between 200 and 300 mg. This reversal
in response is consistent with the dose-dependent toxicity of Cu2+, potentially reflecting increased ionic release at higher
concentrations and the resulting disruption of cellular homeostasis.

Although Al–Cu LDH exhibited the highest acute toxicity,
reflected by lower LC50 values and increased mortality,
Al–Co LDH frequently produced more consistent sublethal effects
on larval growth and development. These findings suggest that acute
toxicity and developmental impairment may not necessarily follow the
same toxicity pattern among transition metal containing LDHs indicating
that different formulations may affect distinct physiological processes.

The reductions in larval mass were consistent with the morphological
alterations observed in D. Larvae exposed to 10 mg mL–1 of any LDH
formulation showed reduced body size and darker pigmentation compared
to the control, changes commonly associated with physiological stress,
impaired development, and metal accumulation.
−

 These morphological
effects indicate not only growth retardation, but also physiological
alterations associated with intoxication processes and, possibly,
metal accumulation. Overall, the results suggest that LDHs containing
transition metals (Ni, Co, and Cu) distinctly modulate the larval
growth of S. frugiperda. Among them,
Al–Co exhibited the highest inhibitory potential, followed
by Al–Ni and Al–Cu. The pronounced weight suppression
suggests that multiple toxicity pathways may be triggered, including
metal-ion release and the affinity of each metal for biomolecules
in the digestive tract. Taken together, these findings demonstrate
that LDH nanoparticles exert not only acute toxicity but also pronounced
sublethal impairments on larval development, strengthening the case
for their evaluation as innovative pest-control materials.

Evaluation of Sublethal Effects on Pupal Weight and Pupation
Rate

Building upon the pronounced effects of LDH nanoparticles
on larval growth, we next evaluated whether these materials also disrupt
later developmental transitions in S. frugiperda. For formulations containing Al–Ni (A), the mean pupal weight ranged from approximately
210 to 240 mg at concentrations between 8 and 75 mg mL–1, values close to the control (211 ± 20 mg). This indicates
that individuals able to complete metamorphosis did not exhibit severe
impairment in pupal development; however, the pupation rate was severely
impaired. The control group exhibited about 75% pupation, whereas
larvae exposed to 8 mg mL–1 of Al–Ni showed
a pupation rate close to 12%, representing an 84% reduction, and at
higher concentrations, the rate remained below 30% (D). These findings imply that
Al–Ni primarily disrupt the larval-to-pupal transition, possibly
through metabolic or hormonal disturbances that affected the larvae
development, rather than affecting the morphology of pupae that failed
to develop successfully, as previously reported in the literature.

Al–Co exposure resulted in a distinct response.
Pupal weight
ranged from 220 to 180 mg at 3 and 5 mg mL–1, without
difference from the control. Remarkably, the pupation rate dropped
sharply, decreasing from approximately 75% in control to below 20%
at concentrations higher than 5 mg mL–1. Above 10
mg mL–1, pupae were virtually absent. These results
indicate a high sensitivity of S. frugiperda to nanoparticles containing cobalt, suggesting that small variations
in concentration are sufficient to affect physiological processes
critical for metamorphosis.

The copper-containing formulation
(Al–Cu) produced a gradual
response. Although the mean pupal weight remained stable, ranging
from 180 to 245 mg at concentrations between 1 and 100 mg mL–1, the pupation rate exhibited a more gradual dose-dependent response.
A progressive reduction was observed, from 75% in the control to approximately
66% at 5 mg mL–1, with values dropping below 10%
only above 20 mg mL–1. This difference in response
suggests that, although copper is known to be a potentially more toxic
metal, its release dynamics within the LDH matrix may modulate ionic
bioavailability and delay deleterious effects on the organism.

Taken together, the results indicate that
metallic LDHs significantly
impair metamorphosis in S. frugiperda, predominantly by reducing pupation success even when the pupae
that do form maintain normal weight. Because successful pupation is
essential for adult emergence and reproduction, such disruptions can
dramatically reduce population viability. The distinct toxicity profiles
observed among the three LDH compositions highlight the importance
of metal identity, ionic solubility, and release kinetics in shaping
biological outcomes. Overall, the data reinforces that LDH nanoparticles
can meaningfully disrupt key developmental transitions in S. frugiperda, supporting their potential as innovative
components in pest-management strategies while underscoring the need
to evaluate environmental selectivity and effects on nontarget species.

The high development costs and limited durability of classical
pest management strategies, such as chemical control and Bt crops,
highlight the need for innovative approaches with multiple modes of
action. Such strategies ideally should offer greater efficacy and
longer-lasting performance, primarily by slowing the evolution of
resistance, which directly influences the adoption and sustainability
of control measures. In addition, it is increasingly important to
reconsider the concept of “efficacy,” as the nonlethal
effects of control tactics can significantly influence pest population
dynamics and their impact on ecosystem services. In this context,
our findings show that layered double hydroxide (LDH) nanoparticles
containing transition metals exert pronounced toxic effects on S. frugiperda larvae. This species is a major agricultural
pest with a wide host range, feeding on more than three hundred plant
species, including economically important crops such as maize, rice,
wheat, and sorghum. Without effective control measures, S. frugiperda infestations can severely reduce crop
productivity, damage entire production areas, and lead to yield losses
of up to 73%. Toxicity was dependent on nanoparticle composition,
with Al–Cu LDH showing the highest potency (LD50: 19.1–37.3 mg mL–1), surpassing both Al–Ni
and Al–Co formulations. Beyond acute mortality, important sublethal
effects were observed, including reductions in larval and pupal weight,
extended developmental time, and decreased pupation rates effects
that intensified with increasing concentrations.

The biological
activity observed in the present study also highlights
the importance of evaluating potential effects on nontarget organisms.
While LDHs have frequently been proposed as safe and biocompatible
delivery systems for agricultural applications, the toxicity observed
against S. frugiperda indicates that
transition-metal-containing LDHs may not be biologically inert under
all exposure conditions. Consequently, potential impacts on pollinators,
natural enemies, parasitoids, soil invertebrates, and aquatic organisms
should be carefully assessed. Comprehensive ecotoxicological studies
will therefore be essential to determine the environmental safety
profile of these materials and support their responsible development
for agricultural applications.

This study has several limitations
that should be considered when
interpreting the results. First, although the observed effects are
consistent with known metal-associated toxicity pathways, the underlying
mechanisms were not directly investigated. In particular, parameters
such as metal ion release, oxidative stress, or tissue-level alterations
were not measured, and therefore mechanistic interpretations remain
inferential. Furthermore, future experiments involving equivalent
concentrations of soluble metal salts as controls (Ni2+, Co2+, and Cu2+), dissolution studies, and
comparisons with metal-free LDHs would be important, as they would
provide valuable mechanistic information. Such studies should include
a direct assessment of nanoparticle internalization by cells, metal
ion release kinetics, and oxidative stress biomarkers such as reactive
oxygen species (ROS) production, lipid peroxidation, antioxidant enzyme
activity, and DNA damage. Together, these approaches would allow for
a more precise distinction between effects stemming from the lamellar
structure of LDHs and those associated with metal ion release, contributing
to a more comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms underlying
the observed toxicity.

Second, the environmental relevance of
these findings remains to
be established. The potential impact of LDH nanoparticles on nontarget
organisms and broader ecological systems was not assessed and requires
dedicated evaluation. Third, the exposure model used in this study,
based on nanoparticle incorporation into an artificial diet, does
not fully replicate environmental exposure scenarios. As such, the
extent to which these results translate to field conditions should
be interpreted with caution. Future studies integrating environmentally
relevant exposure routes and ecological assessments will be important
to further evaluate the applicability of these materials.

Collectively,
our findings demonstrate that metallic LDHs exert
both acute and sublethal effects on S. frugiperda, decreasing survival, impairing development, and disrupting metamorphosis.
These outcomes indicate that transition-metal-containing LDH formulations
cannot be considered biologically inert under the conditions tested,
exhibiting significant acute and sublethal effects on S. frugiperda. However, the relative contributions
of the LDH matrix, metal composition, and metal-ion release remain
to be determined. This discovery advances the current understanding
of LDH–insect interactions and introduces a new conceptual
dimension in which LDHs can be viewed as dual-function platforms,
combining delivery capacity with inherent biological activity. From
an applied perspective, our results position LDHs as candidates for
studies on pest-control technologies, leveraging tunable metal composition
to enhance potency and specificity. At the same time, the intrinsic
activity we report underscores the need to evaluate selectivity, environmental
fate, and potential impacts on nontarget and beneficial species before
LDHs can be responsibly advanced toward field deployment.
