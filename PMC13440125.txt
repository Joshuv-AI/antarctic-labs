Real-world outcomes of definitive chemoradiotherapy for bladder cancer: a multi-institutional retrospective study in Japan
Authors: Daichi Sugahara, Keiko Nemoto Murofushi, Hideomi Yamashita, Etsuyo Ogo, Koyo Kikuchi, Anneyuko I Saito, Takafumi Komiyama, Nobuki Imano, Ataru Igarashi, Sayaka Ohno, Hiroshi Onishi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveWe aimed to evaluate real-world clinical outcomes and prognostic factors of definitive chemoradiotherapy (CRT) for bladder cancer in Japan.MethodsPatients with bladder cancer treated with definitive CRT between 2010 and 2023 at seven Japanese institutions were retrospectively analyzed. Overall survival (OS) was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, whereas cause-specific survival (CSS) and bladder preservation were analyzed using competing risk methods. Univariable analyses were performed using the log-rank and Gray’s tests, and multivariable analyses were performed using Cox and Fine-Gray models with Bayesian Information Criterion-based stepwise selection.ResultsA total of 175 patients were included. Maximal transurethral resection of the bladder tumor (TUR-Bt) was performed in 55% of patients, and 49% were deemed ineligible for radical cystectomy (RC). The 5-year OS, CSS, and bladder preservation rate were 52% (95% CI, 42–60%), 73% (95% CI, 66–80%), and 69% (95% CI, 61–77%), respectively. In multivariable analyses, poorer performance status remained associated with worse OS (P = .003), whereas advanced clinical T stage was associated with worse CSS (P = .018) and maximal TUR-Bt was associated with improved bladder preservation (P = .024). Although patients ineligible for RC demonstrated inferior OS (P < .001) and CSS (P = .007) in univariable analyses, bladder preservation was comparable to those treated by patient preference.ConclusionsThis multi-institutional retrospective study demonstrates acceptable real-world outcomes of definitive CRT for bladder cancer in Japan. Patient-related factors influenced OS, whereas tumor burden and local tumor control were associated with CSS and bladder preservation.Keywords: urinary bladder neoplasms, Chemoradiotherapy, organ preservation

Introduction

Bladder cancer, particularly muscle-invasive bladder cancer, is associated with a poor prognosis. In Japan, radical cystectomy (RC) is recommended as the standard treatment for muscle-invasive bladder cancer [1]. Definitive chemoradiotherapy (CRT) represents a safe and effective bladder-sparing treatment approach and is used in clinical practice, most commonly for patients who wish to preserve their bladder or who are deemed ineligible for RC [2–6]. Although direct prospective comparisons between CRT and RC have been challenging, several studies have suggested that under appropriate patient selection, CRT may provide oncologic outcomes comparable to those of RC [7, 8]. Accordingly, several clinical practice guidelines recommend definitive CRT as a treatment option for muscle invasive bladder cancer alongside RC [9]. Despite these recommendations, CRT is not widely performed in Japan. Even at institutions where CRT is available, the number of eligible patients remains limited. Furthermore, in the absence of a widely standardized CRT protocol for bladder cancer, treatment strategies vary across institutions. As a result, cases are dispersed among multiple centers, making large-scale prospective studies difficult and leading to a limited number of published reports, even in retrospective settings [10].

In real-world clinical practice, the population undergoing CRT is inherently heterogeneous [11]. The reasons for selecting CRT range from patient preference for bladder preservation to oncologic considerations such as locally advanced or technically unresectable disease, as well as patient-related factors including advanced age, comorbidities, and poor performance status. Pre-radiotherapy maximal transurethral resection of bladder tumor (TUR-Bt) is widely regarded as a standard component of bladder-preserving therapy when feasible [12]. In routine clinical practice, however, maximal TUR-Bt may not be feasible in all patients [11]. To address these issues, we conducted a multicenter retrospective study to evaluate real-world treatment outcomes and identify prognostic factors in patients with bladder cancer treated with definitive CRT in Japan.

Materials and methods

Study design and data source

This was a multi-institutional retrospective study conducted at seven Japanese clinical centers under a specific funding program of the Japanese Society for Radiation Oncology. Eligible cases were identified through institutional medical records, and a retrospective chart review was performed to collect clinical data on patient characteristics, treatment details, survival outcomes, and treatment-related toxicities.

Patients

Patients with bladder cancer who underwent definitive CRT between 2010 and 2023 at the participating institutions were eligible for inclusion. In this study, definitive CRT was defined as radiotherapy delivered with curative intent, consisting of a total dose of ≥50 Gy combined with chemotherapy administered before, during, or after radiotherapy.

Patients who received palliative radiotherapy were excluded. The treatment intent (definitive vs palliative) was determined based on clinical records at each institution.

To reflect real-world clinical practice, broad inclusion criteria were adopted. Patients with any clinical T and N stage were included if definitive CRT was selected in clinical practice. Clinical T and N stages were determined according to the TNM classification (8th edition) based on available imaging, cystoscopic findings, and pathological reports at each institution. Tumor grade was recorded as reported at each participating institution (G1, G2, G3, or unknown) and was dichotomized as G3 versus non-G3 for analysis, given potential variations in grading systems. Patients with distant metastases at diagnosis were excluded. Regarding the reason for selecting CRT, patients were classified as undergoing CRT by patient preference or as ineligible for RC. Ineligibility was determined based on comprehensive clinical judgment at each institution, taking into account tumor extent, comorbidities, advanced age, performance status, and other patient-related factors.

Treatment details

Radiotherapy was delivered with curative intent as definitive CRT, with all patients receiving a total dose of ≥50 Gy according to institutional protocols. Radiation fields included the bladder alone or the bladder with prophylactic pelvic lymph nodes, at the discretion of each institution. Chemotherapy was administered in the pre-radiotherapy, concurrent, and/or post-radiotherapy setting. Both systemic and intra-arterial chemotherapy approaches were used, with specific regimens selected according to institutional practice. Prior to CRT, TUR-Bt was performed according to institutional practice, including decisions regarding its indication and extent. Maximal TUR-Bt was defined as visibly complete tumor resection based on operative and clinical records. Post-treatment surveillance generally consisted of imaging studies, cystoscopic evaluation, and urine examinations at intervals of ~3–6 months, following institutional protocols.

Outcomes and statistical analysis

Overall survival (OS), cause-specific survival (CSS), and bladder preservation rate (BPR) were evaluated as time-to-event outcomes. OS was defined as the time from the start of radiotherapy to death from any cause. CSS was defined as the time to death attributable to bladder cancer, with deaths from other causes treated as competing events.

Bladder preservation was defined as freedom from local recurrence (intravesical recurrence or local progression), irrespective of whether salvage treatments such as RC were performed. Time to event was calculated from the start of radiotherapy to the first documented local recurrence. Cystectomy was not considered an event unless preceded by documented local recurrence, and patients with residual disease at the first post-treatment evaluation were considered to have experienced an event. Deaths without prior local recurrence were treated as competing events. Local recurrence was assessed based on cystoscopic findings and imaging studies according to institutional practice.

Analyses were performed in the overall cohort and stratified by maximal TUR-Bt status and the reason for selecting CRT, as these variables were considered to reflect local tumor control and patient selection in real-world clinical practice. OS was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and group comparisons were performed using the log-rank test. For CSS and BPR, cumulative incidence functions were estimated using the Aalen-Johansen method, and event-free probabilities were plotted. Group comparisons were performed using Gray’s test. Univariable analyses were conducted using the log-rank test for OS and Gray’s test for CSS and BPR for clinically relevant patient-related, tumor-related, and treatment-related factors. Multivariable analyses were performed using Cox proportional hazards models for OS and Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard models for CSS and BPR, with stepwise variable selection based on the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). All candidate variables defined a priori were entered into the initial multivariable models, and only variables retained in the final BIC-selected models are reported. The reason for selecting CRT was not included in multivariable analyses because it represents the outcome of a clinical decision-making process integrating multiple patient-related factors, rather than an independent baseline covariate. Patterns of recurrence, including local, regional, and distant recurrence, were summarized descriptively based on available clinical and imaging data at each institution and are presented in tabular form. Treatment-related adverse events were graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events, version 5.0 and grade ≥3 treatment-related adverse events were collected and presented in tabular form. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P value <.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using R software, version 4.5.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 175 patients were included in the analysis. Patient characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The median follow-up period calculated from the initiation of radiotherapy, was 25.2 months. The median age at the time of treatment was 77 years. All patients had an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 0–2. Most patients had muscle-invasive disease, with 94% presenting with clinical T2–4 tumors. A small proportion of patients (6%) had clinical T1 or less disease. This subgroup included patients with high-risk features such as nodal involvement, T1 high-grade disease, and variant histology (e.g. small cell carcinoma). Most patients (85%) had no clinical lymph node involvement at diagnosis. Maximal TUR-Bt was performed in 55% of patients. Regarding the reason for selecting CRT, 90 patients (51%) underwent CRT by patient preference, whereas 85 patients (49%) were deemed ineligible for RC.

Treatment characteristics

Radiotherapy was delivered using three-dimensional conformal radiotherapy or intensity-modulated radiotherapy, with target volumes varying across institutions and including the bladder alone or the bladder with elective pelvic lymph node irradiation. The prescribed total radiation dose ranged from 50 to 68.4 Gy, and most patients received doses of 60 Gy or higher. Chemotherapy was administered using systemic and/or intra-arterial approaches according to institutional practice. The majority of patients received platinum-based chemotherapy, and a subset were treated with immune checkpoint inhibitors. Survival

At the time of analysis, 71 patients (41%) had died, 82 (47%) were alive at the last follow-up, and 22 (13%) were lost to follow-up. The median follow-up duration among surviving patients was 35.1 months.

The 2- and 5-year OS rates for the entire cohort were 69% (95% CI, 61–76%) and 52% (95% CI, 42–60%), respectively (Fig. 1). The corresponding CSS rates were 82% (95% CI, 75–87%) at 2 years and 73% (95% CI, 66–80%) at 5 years. BPR at 2 and 5 years were 74% (95% CI, 67–81%) and 69% (95% CI, 61–77%), respectively. Patients who underwent maximal TUR-Bt demonstrated significantly better OS (log-rank P = .017), as well as CSS and BPR (Gray’s test P = .014 and .027, respectively), compared with those who did not (Fig. 2, Supplementary Table S3). When stratified by reason for CRT, patients treated by preference showed significantly better OS and CSS than patients ineligible for RC (log-rank P < .001 and Gray’s test P = .007, respectively), whereas BPR did not differ significantly between the groups (Gray’s test P = .956) (Supplementary Fig. S1, Supplementary Table S3). Recurrence at any site was observed in 76 patients (43%). Salvage RC was performed in five patients due to local recurrence (Supplementary Table S4). Univariable analyses were performed using the log-rank test for OS and Gray’s test for CSS and BPR for clinically relevant patient-, tumor-, and treatment-related factors (Tables 3–5). In multivariable analyses with stepwise variable selection based on the BIC, only a limited number of variables were retained in the final models. For OS, an ECOG performance status of 1–2 (vs 0) was retained in the final BIC-selected Cox model and was associated with worse outcomes (HR, 2.0; 95% CI, 1.3–3.3; P = .003). For CSS, sex and clinical T stage were retained in the final Fine-Gray model. Male sex (vs female) was associated with improved outcomes (sHR, 0.4; 95% CI, 0.2–0.9; P = .019), whereas clinical T3–4 disease (vs Tis–T2) was associated with worse outcomes (sHR, 2.3; 95% CI, 1.2–4.5; P = .018). For bladder preservation, maximal TUR-Bt was retained as the only factor in the final Fine-Gray model and was associated with improved outcomes (sHR, 0.5; 95% CI, 0.3–0.9; P = .024).

Toxicity

Nine patients experienced grade 3 adverse events and four experienced grade 4 adverse events; no grade 5 adverse events were observed (Supplementary Table S5). Treatment-related toxicities included gastrointestinal, genitourinary, hematologic, and other events attributable to radiotherapy or chemotherapy. Six patients experienced acute adverse events (within 90 days after completion of radiotherapy), whereas seven experienced late adverse events.

Discussion

In this multi-institutional retrospective study, we evaluated real-world outcomes of definitive CRT for bladder cancer in Japan. The major findings of this study were as follows. First, definitive CRT achieved favorable long-term outcomes, with both 5-year CSS and BPR of ~70% in an unselected, heterogeneous patient population. Second, clinical T stage was associated with cancer-specific survival, whereas maximal TUR-Bt was the most important factor associated with bladder preservation. OS was primarily influenced by baseline patient condition, as reflected by ECOG performance status. Third, although patients deemed ineligible for RC demonstrated worse OS and CSS compared with those treated by preference, BPR was comparable between the two groups, highlighting the potential role of CRT as a bladder-sparing option even in frail patients. Collectively, these findings underscore the distinct contributions of patient-related factors, tumor burden, and local tumor control to survival and bladder preservation in real-world CRT practice.

In a Japanese multi-institutional retrospective study published approximately one decade ago, the reported 2- and 5-year OS rates among patients treated with definitive CRT were ~75% and 65%, respectively [10]. In the present study, the corresponding OS rates were 69% and 52%, which were somewhat lower and may reflect the older age or heterogeneity of our cohort. In large pooled analyses of prospective Radiation Therapy Oncology Group trials conducted outside Japan, the 5-year OS was ~57% despite a younger median patient age, and the OS observed in the present study was broadly comparable [3]. In addition, the 5-year CSS in the present study was 73%, which is consistent with bladder cancer-specific survival outcomes reported in these pooled analyses. Regarding bladder preservation, the 5-year BPR in the present study was 69%. Previous studies of bladder-preserving therapy have generally reported 5-year bladder preservation-related outcomes in the range of ~40%–60% [3, 10]. Although the extent of TUR-Bt and patient selection criteria varied across studies, the BPR observed in the present study appears broadly consistent with previously reported outcomes in heterogeneous patient populations. Taken together, these comparisons indicate that real-world CRT outcomes in Japan remain comparable to those reported in more selected cohorts, despite differences in patient age and clinical heterogeneity. This may be partly explained by patient selection in routine clinical practice, whereby individuals likely to benefit from CRT-based bladder-preserving therapy are appropriately identified despite clinical heterogeneity. In addition, improvements in treatment techniques and supportive care over time may also have contributed to maintaining these outcomes.

Pre-radiotherapy maximal TUR-Bt is widely recognized as a standard component of bladder-preserving therapy and has been associated with improved local control in prior studies, and is therefore recommended in major clinical guidelines [3, 5, 12, 13]. However, in real-world clinical practice, achieving maximal TUR-Bt may be difficult in some patients because of frailty, advanced local tumor extent, or other practical considerations. In this heterogeneous real-world cohort, maximal TUR-Bt was the factor most strongly associated with bladder preservation. These findings do not directly imply a causal effect of maximal TUR-Bt itself but further support its clinical relevance for bladder preservation, even in real-world settings where such procedures may not always be feasible.

When interpreting the results across different survival endpoints, a clear separation emerged between factors associated with OS and those related to CSS and BPR. OS was primarily influenced by baseline patient condition, as reflected by ECOG performance status, whereas CSS and BPR were associated with tumor- and treatment-related factors, namely clinical T stage and maximal TUR-Bt, consistent with previous reports identifying these factors as important prognostic determinants in bladder-preserving therapy [13]. This divergence is clinically intuitive, particularly in an elderly population with substantial comorbidities, in whom non-cancer-related mortality substantially contributes to OS. In contrast, outcomes directly related to bladder cancer control and organ preservation appear to depend predominantly on the extent of tumor burden and the quality of local tumor management. This should not be interpreted as indicating that other clinical factors are unimportant, but rather that substantial correlations among baseline variables led to the retention of a limited number of representative factors for each endpoint. Taken together, these findings suggest that while patient-related factors largely determine OS, tumor burden and local tumor control remain central to achieving CSS and successful bladder preservation in real-world CRT practice. Although sex was retained as a significant factor for CSS in the multivariable analysis, its clinical relevance is unclear and may reflect residual confounding or unmeasured factors; therefore, it should be interpreted with caution.

The classification of ineligibility for RC was used to reflect real-world patient selection rather than as an independent prognostic variable. Patients deemed ineligible for RC demonstrated poorer OS and CSS compared with those treated with CRT by patient preference, which is consistent with their older age, poorer performance status, and more advanced disease at baseline. These findings are therefore not unexpected and likely reflect underlying patient- and tumor-related factors rather than treatment-related effects. Notably, BPR was comparable between patients ineligible for RC and those treated by preference. This finding suggests that, even in frail or elderly patients for whom RC is not feasible, definitive CRT may allow maintenance of bladder integrity in a substantial proportion of cases, thereby representing a clinically meaningful outcome in real-world practice.

This study has several limitations inherent to its retrospective, multi-institutional design. First, treatment strategies varied across institutions, reflecting real-world practice in Japan, including radiotherapy techniques, dose target volumes, and chemotherapy approaches. In particular, chemotherapy administration varied across institutions and was largely determined by institutional practice patterns, resulting in heterogeneous treatment approaches. Second, follow-up schedules and surveillance methods were not standardized and differed across institutions and individual patients, which may have affected the detection of recurrence and adverse events. In addition, the retrospective nature of data collection and variability in follow-up may have limited the completeness of information on late toxicities and salvage treatments, particularly in elderly patients or those with limited follow-up. Clinical staging was based on a combination of imaging, cystoscopic findings, and pathological information, and the availability of pathological confirmation varied across patients. Consequently, clinical T stage was determined based on the best available information at each institution, which may have introduced some heterogeneity in staging. Furthermore, the assessment of maximal TUR-Bt was based on retrospective review of operative and clinical records, and the definition of visibly complete resection may have varied across institutions, potentially introducing inter-institutional variability. Also, several known prognostic factors, including hydronephrosis, tumor size and multiplicity, were not included in the present analysis due to incomplete and non-uniform documentation across institutions. Finally, although efforts were made to include all eligible cases, residual confounding and selection bias cannot be fully excluded.

Conclusion

In this multi-institutional retrospective study, definitive CRT achieved acceptable long-term survival and bladder preservation outcomes in a heterogeneous real-world cohort of patients with bladder cancer in Japan. OS was primarily influenced by patient-related factors, whereas CSS and bladder preservation were associated with tumor- and pre-treatment-related factors. These findings highlight the importance of adequate local tumor control and tumor burden in bladder-preserving therapy, even in routine clinical practice. In patients ineligible for RC, preservation of the bladder itself may represent a clinically meaningful treatment outcome. Further prospective studies or well-designed registry-based analyses are warranted to refine patient selection and optimize bladder-preserving treatment strategies.
