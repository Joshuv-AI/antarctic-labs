Beyond Menopausal Hormone Therapy: Exercise as a Dual-Target Strategy for Sleep Disturbance and Urinary Incontinence in Menopausal Women
Authors: Seok Kyo Seo
Published: 2026

Sleep disorders and urinary incontinence are among the most bothersome yet poorly treated symptoms that appear during the menopausal transition; these two symptoms are quietly interconnected, and a decrease in estrogen is the underlying cause of both [1,2]. Menopausal hormone therapy is the most effective treatment for vasomotor symptoms and helps improve sleep, but it is not a suitable or sufficient treatment for all menopausal symptoms [3,4,5].

In this issue, Martins et al. [6] present the results of a timely systematic review and meta-analysis evaluating the effects of physical exercise on sleep quality and urinary incontinence in menopausal women.

The clinical message is clear and practical. Regarding sleep, exercise was more effective than various forms of intervention; mind-body practices such as yoga and Pilates showed effects similar to conventional aerobic and strength training, with the greatest effects observed in studies conducted three or more times a week. For urinary incontinence, while the combined estimate did not reach a statistically significant level, pelvic floor strengthening exercises demonstrated a distinct effect. This reiterates that the specificity of stimulation is more important than the frequency of exercise. The greatest strength of this study is likely that it not only confirms the benefits of exercise but also demonstrates that necessary exercise prescriptions vary depending on menopausal symptoms. The core message of this study is that personalized treatment tailored to symptoms is needed, rather than a general recommendation to “increase exercise volume.” This difference holds significant clinical implications, as the trend in menopausal management is increasingly emphasizing individualized treatment rather than a uniform approach.

Another significant contribution of this paper lies in its conceptual aspects. Sleep disorders and urinary incontinence are not merely similar symptoms; they interact to amplify distress and degrade quality of life. Nocturia, urgency, sleep fragmentation, embarrassment, low body confidence, and social isolation can form a vicious cycle. By addressing these findings comprehensively, Martins et al. [6] suggest viewing the complex patterns of menopausal symptoms from a holistic perspective, rather than focusing solely on individual symptoms. This represents a significant step forward. Future menopausal management will depend not merely on symptom relief, but on integrated strategies that recognize how these symptoms manifest together.

In summary, Martins et al. [6] present a thoughtful and clinically applicable comprehensive analysis of a topic that deserves more attention. Their research supports the view that exercise is not merely a slogan for health promotion, but a meaningful therapeutic tool in menopausal management. More importantly, the study conveys a more specific message: appropriate exercise during menopause can improve sleep quality, and targeted exercise can help effectively alleviate symptoms of urinary incontinence. This is a beneficial and empowering message for both medical professionals and patients.
