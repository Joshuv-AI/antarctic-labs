Compensated Splitting For Generalized Lyapunov Equations
Hongjia Chen, Ren-Cang Li

Abstract
The generalized Lyapunov equation has a natural splitting that leads to the standard fixed-point iteration (sFPI) for its numerical solution. sFPI is convergent, for an arbitrarily given initial guess, if and only if the spectral radius of the associated linear operator is smaller than 1 1 . This means that sFPI may diverge if the spectral radius is 1 1 or bigger. In this paper, we propose a compensated splitting scheme that aims to reduce the spectral radius so that the resulting compensated fixed-point iteration (cFPI) has a better convergence property, namely, cFPI may still converge even if sFPI does not or cFPI converges faster than sFPI does when the latter is also convergent. Numerical results are presented to demonstrate the superiority of cFPI to sFPI. Keywords: generalized Lyapunov equation, compensated splitting, fixed-point iteration, spectral radius Mathematics Subject Classification 65F45, 65F08, 15A06, 15A24

Introduction

Our focus in this paper is the numerical solution to the generalized Lyapunov equation

AX+XA^{\HH}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}N_{j}XN_{j}^{\HH}+BB^{\HH}=0,

(1.1)

where the matrices
A,N_{j}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
and
B\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times\ell}
with usually
\ell\ll n
.
Such an equation often arises
from the model order reduction of bilinear control systems and linear parameter-varying systems,
the stability analysis of linear stochastic differential
equations, as well as the linearization of stochastic continuous-time
algebraic Riccati equations
[
2
,
3
,
5
,
6
,
10
]
.
The constant term in (
1.1
) takes such a special form
BB^{\HH}
because of
the sources of the generalized Lyapunov equation in applications.
As far as the development in this paper is concerned, the constant term can be replaced with any square matrix
C
.
Imposing that
C
is Hermitian will generally imply that the solution
X
is Hermitian, too.
In most existing literature, all matrices in (
1.1
) are assumed to be real, but we let them be complex for generality
and that all matrices are real is simply a special case.

Theoretically,
(
1.1
) is an
n^{2}
-by-
n^{2}
linear system of equations and can be turn into the familiar standard form of a linear
system of equations:

\mathscr{A}\vex(X)\equiv\Big[I_{n}\otimes A+\bar{A}\otimes I_{n}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\bar{N}_{j}\otimes N_{j}\Big]\vex(X)=-\vex(BB^{\HH}),

(1.2)

where
\otimes
is the matrix Kronecker product,
\bar{A}
and
\bar{N}_{j}
denote the entrywise complex conjugate of
A
and
N_{j}
, and
\vex(\cdot)
vectorizes a matrix into a column vector by stacking up the columns of the matrix. It follows from this standard form that if

\mathscr{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{n^{2}\times n^{2}}
is nonsingular then (
1.1
) has a unique solution
and the solution
X
is Hermitian, but it is not clear if
X
is definite or not.
Another implication of (
1.2
) is the temptation to solve the original
(
1.1
) via (
1.2
). Unfortunately, any direct solver such as variants of Gaussian elimination
and is not feasible unless
n
is very small (a few tens) because of its
O(n^{4})
storage and
O(n^{6})
computational
complexity
[
7
,
8
]
.
Usual iterative methods for linear systems
[
7
,
9
,
15
]
are straightforwardly applicable without
the need to form
\mathscr{A}
explicitly.

The generalized Lyapunov equation (
1.1
) has received increasing attention over the last decades.
The simplest numerical method is through the fixed-point technique, and it is based on splitting the linear terms in
X
in (
1.1
) naturally into
\mathscr{M}(X)=\mathscr{N}(X)-BB^{\HH}
, where

\mathscr{M}(X):=AX+XA^{\HH},\quad\mathscr{N}(X)=-\sum_{j=1}^{m}N_{j}XN_{j}^{\HH}.

(1.3)

Both
\mathscr{M}(\cdot)
and
\mathscr{N}(\cdot)
are linear operators
from
\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
to itself, and in particular,
\mathscr{N}(X)\preceq 0
(negative semidefinite) for any
X\succeq 0

(positive semidefinite). When
\mathscr{M}
is invertible, such a splitting yields the following standard fixed-point
iteration (sFPI): given
X_{0}
, for
k=1,2,\ldots
solve

\mathscr{M}(X_{k})\equiv AX_{k}+X_{k}A^{\HH}=\mathscr{N}(X_{k-1})-BB^{\HH}

(1.4)

for
X_{k}
until convergence. Suppose that
\mathscr{M}
is invertible.
A sufficient condition for the convergence of sFPI (
1.4
) is

\rho(\mathscr{M}^{-1}\mathscr{N})<1,

(1.5)

where
\rho(\mathscr{M}^{-1}\mathscr{N})
is the spectral radius of
\mathscr{M}^{-1}\mathscr{N}
as a linear operator in

\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
. The condition implies that necessarily

\mathscr{M}-\mathscr{N}=\mathscr{M}\,(\Id-\mathscr{M}^{-1}\mathscr{N})

is invertible, where
\Id
denotes the identity operator in
\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
.
But,
if
\rho(\mathscr{M}^{-1}\mathscr{N})\geq 1
, then the scheme is in general divergent.
Our goal in this paper is to rectify
\rho(\mathscr{M}^{-1}\mathscr{N})\geq 1
to yield a new iterative scheme that
may still converge. Roughly speaking,
\rho(\mathscr{M}^{-1}\mathscr{N})\geq 1
is caused by the
\mathscr{N}
-part being relative too “heavy”.
Hence in order to overcome the violation, we have to take out a portion of the
\mathscr{N}
-part to compensate
the
\mathscr{M}
-part. For that purpose, we propose to somehow decompose the
\mathscr{N}
-part as

-\mathscr{N}(X)=EX+XE^{\HH}-\widetilde{\mathscr{N}}(X)

(1.6)

in such a way that
\widetilde{\mathscr{N}}(\cdot)
is made relatively less “heavy” than before and then compensate

\mathscr{M}(X)
with
EX+XE^{\HH}
, leading to

\mathscr{M}(X)-\mathscr{N}(X)=\underbrace{[\mathscr{M}(X)+EX+XE^{\HH}]}_{=:\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}(X)}-\widetilde{\mathscr{N}}(X),

(1.7)

which we will call a
compensated splitting
.
As a result, we can establish the following compensated fixed-point
iteration (cFPI): given
X_{0}
, for
k=1,2,\ldots
solve

\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}(X_{k})\equiv(A+E)X_{k}+X_{k}(A+E)^{\HH}=\widetilde{\mathscr{N}}(X_{k-1})-BB^{\HH}

(1.8)

for
X_{k}
until convergence.
An ideal compensated splitting (
1.7
) should minimize
\rho(\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}^{-1}\widetilde{\mathscr{N}}\,)
subject to

\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}
being invertible. Conceivably, seeking such an ideal compensated splitting is utterly impractical.
Instead, we seek somehow to “minimize”

\widetilde{\mathscr{N}}(\cdot)
optimally in certain sense.
In the end, we come up with an optimal
E
that takes a
very elegant form

E=\frac{1}{n(1+\mu)}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\tr(\bar{N}_{j})\left[N_{j}-\frac{1}{2n}\tr(N_{j})\cdot I_{n}\right],

(1.9)

where
\tr(\cdot)
takes the trace of a matrix, and
\mu\geq 0
is a regularizing parameter to control the magnitude of
E
.
How it is derived will be detailed in
section

2
.

FPI-type methods are simple to design and implement.
Beyond such methods as we have discussed so far, not surprisingly, modern iterative techniques
for linear systems (see, e.g.,
[
7
,
8
,
9
,
15
]
)
have seen their extensions to the generalized Lyapunov equation (
1.1
), including,
for example, the bilinear ADI method
[
2
]
,
the greedy low-rank methods
[
12
]
,
Krylov subspace-type methods
[
11
]
,
and exploitations of the fixed-point iteration
[
16
]
,
a residual-based generalized rational-Krylov-type subspace method
[
4
]
,
and a method based on the Sherman–Morrison–Woodbury formula

[
5
]
.
However, in this paper we will be focusing solely on rescuing sFPI from its divergence or making it converge faster.
Any potential combination of the key idea of this paper with the state-of-the-art subspace-based projection techniques
will be investigated in forthcoming studies.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows.
In
section

2
, a compensated splitting
is proposed and its main result is (
1.9
).
Numerical experiments are reported in
section

3
, demonstrating
the new method’s superiority to sFPI. Finally, we draw conclusion and make comments in

.

.

\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and
\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
denote the sets of real
and complex
m\times n
matrices, respectively;

\mathbb{R}^{n}=\mathbb{R}^{n\times 1}
and
\mathbb{C}^{n}=\mathbb{C}^{n\times 1}
, and

,

.

2
Compensated Splitting

As we discussed in section
1
, an ideal compensated splitting (
1.7
)
should make
\rho(\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}^{-1}\widetilde{\mathscr{N}}\,)
as small as possible. Unfortunately, that is not numerically feasible, and
so we will have to be less ambitious.
We start by making
\widetilde{\mathscr{N}}(X)
as insignificant as possible over all
X\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
and
seek
E\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
by

\min_{E}\,\max_{\|\vex(X)\|_{2}\leq 1}\Bigl\|\sum_{j=1}^{m}N_{j}XN_{j}^{\HH}-(EX+XE^{\HH})\Bigr\|_{\F}^{2}+\mu\|EX+XE^{\HH}\|_{\F}^{2},

(2.1)

where
\mu\|EX+XE^{\HH}\|_{\F}^{2}
can be regarded as a regularization term on
E
to keep it from being too “big”, and
\mu\geq 0

is the regularizing parameter that has to be chosen properly.
Notice that

\max_{\|\vex(X)\|_{2}\leq 1}\Bigl\|\Bigl(I_{n}\otimes E+\bar{E}\otimes I_{n}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\bar{N}_{j}\otimes N_{j}\Bigr)\vex(X)\Bigr\|_{2}^{2}+\mu\|(I_{n}\otimes E+\bar{E}\otimes I_{n})\vex(X)\|_{2}^{2}\\
\leq\Bigl\|I_{n}\otimes E+\bar{E}\otimes I_{n}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\bar{N}_{j}\otimes N_{j}\Bigr\|_{2}^{2}+\mu\|I_{n}\otimes E+\bar{E}\otimes I_{n}\|_{2}^{2},

(2.2)

which involves the sum of two spectral norms of two
n^{2}\times n^{2}
matrices, and conceivably
minimizing the expression in (
2.2
) over
E
is a difficult thing to do, if at all possible.
Instead, we relax (
2.2
) to

\min_{E}\Bigl\{f(E):=\Bigl\|I_{n}\otimes E+\bar{E}\otimes I_{n}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\bar{N}_{j}\otimes N_{j}\Bigr\|_{\F}^{2}+\mu\|I_{n}\otimes E+\bar{E}\otimes I_{n}\|_{\F}^{2}\Bigr\}.

(2.3)

It can be seen that the objective
f(E)
in (
2.1
)
is a non-negative quadratic function in the entries of
E
and it is convex.
Hence any stationary point of (
2.3
) is a global minimum point. Our main result in this section is stated in the following theorem, where
\tr(\cdot)
takes the trace of a square matrix.

Theorem 2.1
.

An optimal
E
to (
2.3
) is given by

E=\frac{1}{n(1+\mu)}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\tr(\bar{N}_{j})\left[N_{j}-\frac{1}{2n}\tr(N_{j})\cdot I_{n}\right].

(2.4)

Moreover, the minimizer is unique for the real case, and for the complex case this optimal
E
is the one with the smallest
Frobenius norm.

We will defer the proof of this theorem to subsections
2.1
and
2.2
below. A couple of
immediately questions are:
1) what is the invertibility of the operator
\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}
?
2) does
E
in (
2.4
) always decrease the spectral radius in question? The answers to both questions are negative,
as our later numerical experiments in
section

3
suggest. The following identity may provide some understanding as to
when
E
in (
2.4
) will work better.

\tr(E)=\frac{1}{2n(1+\mu)}\sum_{j=1}^{m}|\tr(N_{j})|^{2}\geq 0.

(2.5)

Suppose that the magnitudes of all
N_{j}
are small and then so is that of

E
in (
2.4
), and thus
E
can be considered a perturbation.
Since
\tr(A+E)=\tr(A)+\tr(E)\geq\tr(A)
,
E
will move some of the eigenvalues rightward.
Now if the eigenvalues of
A
lie in the left-half plane, then collectively these eigenvalues will be moved rightward towards
0
,
making
\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}
“less invertible” than
\mathscr{M}
. In such a case there is a possibility that any gain in “minimizing”
\widetilde{\mathscr{N}}
may get offset by
worsening in
\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}
along the way. On the other hand, if the eigenvalues of
A
lie in the right-half plane, then collectively
the eigenvalues of
A
will also be moved rightward but further away from
0
, making
\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}
even “more invertible”. In such a situation, determining
E
by (
2.3
) has an implicit effect of serving the purpose of what an ideal compensated splitting (
1.7
) should achieve.
This quantitative explanation is actually reflected in our numerical experiments in
section

3
.

E
in

, we create

) and numerically the fixed-point

In the next two subsections, we will establish (

In theory, the real case is a special one of the complex case when all involved matrices are real matrices.

Consider

\displaystyle f(E)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{n}\Bigl\|E+E_{(s,s)}I_{n}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}(N_{j})_{(s,s)}N_{j}\Bigr\|_{\F}^{2}+\mu\sum_{s=1}^{n}\Bigl\|E+E_{(s,s)}I_{n}\Bigr\|_{\F}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s,t=1\\

(2.7)

We start by finding the stationary points of (

). To that end, it suffices to set the partial derivatives of

0

We have,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl[2E_{(s,s)}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\big[(N_{j})_{(s,s)}\big]^{2}\Bigr]2+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1,\,i\neq s}^{n}\Bigl[E_{(i,i)}+E_{(s,s)}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}(N_{j})_{(s,s)}(N_{j})_{(i,i)}\Bigr]

\displaystyle\quad+\mu\frac{1}{2}\Bigl[2E_{(s,s)}\Bigr]2+\mu\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1,\,i\neq s}^{n}\Bigl[E_{(i,i)}+E_{(s,s)}\Bigr]

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1,\,i\neq s}^{n}\Bigl[E_{(s,s)}+E_{(i,i)}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}(N_{j})_{(i,i)}(N_{j})_{(s,s)}\Bigr]+\mu\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1,\,i\neq s}^{n}\Bigl[E_{(s,s)}+E_{(i,i)}\Bigr]

\displaystyle=(n+1)(1+\mu)E_{(s,s)}+(1+\mu)\sum_{i=1,\,i\neq s}^{n}E_{(i,i)}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}(N_{j})_{(s,s)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(N_{j})_{(i,i)}

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial f(E)}{\partial E_{(s,t)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial E_{(s,t)}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Bigl\|E+E_{(i,i)}I_{n}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}(N_{j})_{(i,i)}N_{j}\Bigr\|_{\F}^{2}+\mu\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial E_{(s,t)}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Bigl\|E+E_{(i,i)}I_{n}\Bigr\|_{\F}^{2}

2.2
Optimal
E
for the Complex Case

Consider the case when
E

\displaystyle f(E)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{n}\Bigl\|E+\bar{E}_{(s,s)}I_{n}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\bar{N}_{j})_{(s,s)}N_{j}\Bigr\|_{\F}^{2}+\mu\sum_{s=1}^{n}\Bigl\|E+\bar{E}_{(s,s)}I_{n}\Bigr\|_{\F}^{2}

To find the optimal
E
to (
2.3
), we will find its stationary points determined by setting
the partial derivatives of

with respect to the real and imaginary parts of each entry of

.
Write

E_{(s,t)}=E_{(s,t)}^{{\rm re}}+\iota E_{(s,t)}^{{\rm im}},\quad\bar{E}_{(s,t)}=E_{(s,t)}^{{\rm re}}-\iota E_{(s,t)}^{{\rm im}},

\iota

.
It is known that

\frac{\partial f(E)}{\partial E_{(s,t)}^{{\rm re}}}=\frac{\partial f(E)}{\partial E_{(s,t)}}+\frac{\partial f(E)}{\partial\bar{E}_{(s,t)}},\quad\frac{1}{\iota}\cdot\frac{\partial f(E)}{\partial E_{(s,t)}^{{\rm im}}}=\frac{\partial f(E)}{\partial E_{(s,t)}}-\frac{\partial f(E)}{\partial\bar{E}_{(s,t)}},

(2.12)

in which
E

\bar{E}

For the diagonal entries, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f(E)}{\partial E_{(s,s)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial E_{(s,s)}}\Bigl\|E+\bar{E}_{(s,s)}I_{n}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\bar{N}_{j})_{(s,s)}N_{j}\Bigr\|_{\F}^{2}+\mu\frac{\partial}{\partial E_{(s,s)}}\Bigl\|E+\bar{E}_{(s,s)}I_{n}\Bigr\|_{\F}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\partial}{\partial E_{(s,s)}}\sum_{i=1,\,i\neq s}^{n}\Bigl\|E+\bar{E}_{(i,i)}I_{n}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\bar{N}_{j})_{(i,i)}N_{j}\Bigr\|_{\F}^{2}+\mu\frac{\partial}{\partial E_{(s,s)}}\sum_{i=1,\,i\neq s}^{n}\Bigl\|E+\bar{E}_{(i,i)}I_{n}\Bigr\|_{\F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial E_{(s,s)}}\Bigl|E_{(s,s)}+\bar{E}_{(s,s)}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\bar{N}_{j})_{(s,s)}(N_{j})_{(s,s)}\Bigr|^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+\frac{\partial}{\partial E_{(s,s)}}\sum_{i=1,\,i\neq s}^{n}\Bigl|E_{(i,i)}+\bar{E}_{(s,s)}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\bar{N}_{j})_{(s,s)}(N_{j})_{(i,i)}\Bigr|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\mu\frac{\partial}{\partial E_{(s,s)}}\Bigl|E_{(s,s)}+\bar{E}_{(s,s)}\Bigr|^{2}+\mu\frac{\partial}{\partial E_{(s,s)}}\sum_{i=1,\,i\neq s}^{n}\Bigl|E_{(i,i)}+\bar{E}_{(s,s)}\Bigr|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\partial}{\partial E_{(s,s)}}\sum_{i=1,\,i\neq s}^{n}\Bigl|E_{(s,s)}+\bar{E}_{(i,i)}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\bar{N}_{j})_{(i,i)}(N_{j})_{(s,s)}\Bigr|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\mu\frac{\partial}{\partial E_{(s,s)}}\sum_{i=1,\,i\neq s}^{n}\Bigl|E_{(s,s)}+\bar{E}_{(i,i)}\Bigr|^{2}

\displaystyle=2\Bigl[E_{(s,s)}+\bar{E}_{(s,s)}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\bar{N}_{j})_{(s,s)}(N_{j})_{(s,s)}\Bigr]

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+\sum_{i=1,\,i\neq s}^{n}\Bigl[E_{(i,i)}+\bar{E}_{(s,s)}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\bar{N}_{j})_{(s,s)}(N_{j})_{(i,i)}\Bigr]

\displaystyle\quad+\mu 2\Bigl[E_{(s,s)}+\bar{E}_{(s,s)}\Bigr]+\mu\sum_{i=1,\,i\neq s}^{n}\Bigl[E_{(i,i)}+\bar{E}_{(s,s)}\Bigr]

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i=1,\,i\neq s}^{n}\Bigl[\bar{E}_{(s,s)}+E_{(i,i)}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}(N_{j})_{(i,i)}(\bar{N}_{j})_{(s,s)}\Bigr]+\mu\sum_{i=1,\,i\neq s}^{n}\Bigl[\bar{E}_{(s,s)}+E_{(i,i)}\Bigr]

\displaystyle=2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Bigl[(1+\mu)\bigl(\bar{E}_{(s,s)}+E_{(i,i)}\bigr)-\sum_{j=1}^{m}(N_{j})_{(i,i)}(\bar{N}_{j})_{(s,s)}\Bigr]

\displaystyle=2n(1+\mu)\bar{E}_{(s,s)}+2(1+\mu)\sum_{i=1}^{n}E_{(i,i)}-2\sum_{j=1}^{m}\tr(N_{j})\,(\bar{N}_{j})_{(s,s)},

(2.13a)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f(E)}{\partial\bar{E}_{(s,s)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{E}_{(s,s)}}\Bigl\|E+\bar{E}_{(s,s)}I_{n}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\bar{N}_{j})_{(s,s)}N_{j}\Bigr\|_{\F}^{2}+\mu\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{E}_{(s,s)}}\Bigl\|E+\bar{E}_{(s,s)}I_{n}\Bigr\|_{\F}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\partial}{\partial E_{(s,s)}}\sum_{i=1,\,i\neq s}^{n}\Bigl\|E+\bar{E}_{(i,i)}I_{n}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\bar{N}_{j})_{(i,i)}N_{j}\Bigr\|_{\F}^{2}+\mu\frac{\partial}{\partial E_{(s,s)}}\sum_{i=1,\,i\neq s}^{n}\Bigl\|E+\bar{E}_{(i,i)}I_{n}\Bigr\|_{\F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{E}_{(s,s)}}\Bigl|E_{(s,s)}+\bar{E}_{(s,s)}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\bar{N}_{j})_{(s,s)}(N_{j})_{(s,s)}\Bigr|^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{E}_{(s,s)}}\sum_{i=1,\,i\neq s}^{n}\Bigl|E_{(i,i)}+\bar{E}_{(s,s)}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\bar{N}_{j})_{(s,s)}(N_{j})_{(i,i)}\Bigr|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\mu\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{E}_{(s,s)}}\Bigl|E_{(s,s)}+\bar{E}_{(s,s)}\Bigr|^{2}+\mu\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{E}_{(s,s)}}\sum_{i=1,\,i\neq s}^{n}\Bigl|E_{(i,i)}+\bar{E}_{(s,s)}\Bigr|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{E}_{(s,s)}}\sum_{i=1,\,i\neq s}^{n}\Bigl|\bar{E}_{(s,s)}+E_{(i,i)}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}(N_{j})_{(i,i)}(\bar{N}_{j})_{(s,s)}\Bigr|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\mu\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{E}_{(s,s)}}\sum_{i=1,\,i\neq s}^{n}\Bigl|\bar{E}_{(s,s)}+E_{(i,i)}\Bigr|^{2}

\displaystyle=2\Bigl[E_{(s,s)}+\bar{E}_{(s,s)}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\bar{N}_{j})_{(s,s)}(N_{j})_{(s,s)}\Bigr]

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+\sum_{i=1,\,i\neq s}^{n}\Bigl[\bar{E}_{(i,i)}+E_{(s,s)}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}(N_{j})_{(s,s)}(\bar{N}_{j})_{(i,i)}\Bigr]

\displaystyle\quad+\mu 2\Bigl[E_{(s,s)}+\bar{E}_{(s,s)}\Bigr]+\mu\sum_{i=1,\,i\neq s}^{n}\Bigl[\bar{E}_{(i,i)}+E_{(s,s)}\Bigr]

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i=1,\,i\neq s}^{n}\Bigl[E_{(s,s)}+\bar{E}_{(i,i)}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\bar{N}_{j})_{(i,i)}(N_{j})_{(s,s)}\Bigr]+\mu\sum_{i=1,\,i\neq s}^{n}\Bigl[E_{(s,s)}+\bar{E}_{(i,i)}\Bigr]

\displaystyle=2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Bigl[(1+\mu)\bigl(E_{(s,s)}+\bar{E}_{(i,i)}\bigr)-\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\bar{N}_{j})_{(i,i)}(N_{j})_{(s,s)}\Bigr]

\displaystyle=2n(1+\mu)E_{(s,s)}+2(1+\mu)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bar{E}_{(i,i)}-2\sum_{j=1}^{m}\tr(\bar{N}_{j})\,(N_{j})_{(s,s)}.

(2.13b)

According to (
2.12
) and upon using (
2.13
), the first order optimality condition on the diagonal entries of
E

is given by, for
1\leq s\leq n
,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial f(E)}{\partial E_{(s,s)}^{{\rm re}}}=\frac{\partial f(E)}{\partial E_{(s,s)}}+\frac{\partial f(E)}{\partial\bar{E}_{(s,s)}}

\displaystyle=2n(1+\mu)\big[E_{(s,s)}+\bar{E}_{(s,s)}\big]+2(1+\mu)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big[E_{(i,i)}+\bar{E}_{(i,i)}\big]-4\sum_{j=1}^{m}\Re\big(\tr(\bar{N}_{j})\,(N_{j})_{(s,s)}\big)

\displaystyle=4n(1+\mu)E_{(s,s)}^{{\rm re}}+4(1+\mu)\sum_{i=1}^{n}E_{(i,i)}^{{\rm re}}-4\sum_{j=1}^{m}\Re\big(\tr(\bar{N}_{j})\,(N_{j})_{(s,s)}\big),

(2.14a)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\iota}\cdot\frac{\partial f(E)}{\partial E_{(s,s)}^{{\rm im}}}=\frac{\partial f(E)}{\partial E_{(s,s)}}-\frac{\partial f(E)}{\partial\bar{E}_{(s,s)}}

\displaystyle=-2n(1+\mu)\big[E_{(s,s)}-\bar{E}_{(s,s)}\big]+2(1+\mu)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big[E_{(i,i)}-\bar{E}_{(i,i)}\big]+2\iota\sum_{j=1}^{m}\Im\big(\tr(\bar{N}_{j})\,(N_{j})_{(s,s)}\big)

\displaystyle=-2n(1+\mu)\iota E_{(s,s)}^{{\rm im}}+2(1+\mu)\iota\sum_{i=1}^{n}E_{(i,i)}^{{\rm im}}+2\iota\sum_{j=1}^{m}\Im\big(\tr(\bar{N}_{j})\,(N_{j})_{(s,s)}\big),

(2.14b)

where
\Re(\cdot)
and
\Im(\cdot)
take the real and imaginary parts of a complex number, respectively.

First we solve (
2.14a
) for
1\leq s\leq n
. Summing up (
2.14a
) over
1\leq s\leq n
,
we get, after simplifications,

\displaystyle 8n(1+\mu)\sum_{i=1}^{n}E_{(i,i)}^{{\rm re}}=4\sum_{j=1}^{m}\tr(\bar{N}_{j})\tr(N_{j})

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}E_{(i,i)}^{{\rm re}}=\frac{1}{2n(1+\mu)}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\tr(\bar{N}_{j})\tr(N_{j}).

(2.15)

Plug (
2.15
) into (
2.14a
) to get, for
s=1,2,\ldots,n
,

E_{(s,s)}^{{\rm re}}=\frac{1}{n(1+\mu)}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{m}\Re\big((N_{j})_{(s,s)}\tr(\bar{N}_{j})\big)-\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\tr(\bar{N}_{j})\tr(N_{j})\right].

(2.16)

Next, we solve (
2.14b
) for
1\leq s\leq n
, which can be simplified to

-nE_{(s,s)}^{{\rm im}}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}E_{(i,i)}^{{\rm im}}=-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\Im\big(\tr(\bar{N}_{j})\,(N_{j})_{(s,s)}\big)\quad\mbox{for $1\leq s\leq n$},

(2.17)

or, compactly,

\begin{bmatrix}n-1&-1&\cdots&-1\\
-1&n-1&\cdots&-1\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
-1&-1&\cdots&n-1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}E_{(1,1)}^{{\rm im}}\\
E_{(2,2)}^{{\rm im}}\\
\vdots\\
E_{(n,n)}^{{\rm im}}\end{bmatrix}=\frac{1}{1+\mu}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\begin{bmatrix}\Im\big((N_{j})_{(1,1)}\tr(\bar{N}_{j})\big)\\
\Im\big((N_{j})_{(2,2)}\tr(\bar{N}_{j})\big)\\
\vdots\\
\Im\big((N_{j})_{(n,n)}\tr(\bar{N}_{j})\big)\end{bmatrix}:=\boldsymbol{b},

(2.18)

whose coefficient matrix has rank
n-1
and can be compactly expressed as

C=n\Big(I_{n}-\frac{1}{n}\boldsymbol{e}\boldsymbol{e}^{\T}\Big),

where
\boldsymbol{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the vector of all ones. We make a couple of observations:

1)

the linear system (
2.18
) is consistent because
the range of
C
is the orthogonal complement of
{\rm span}\{\boldsymbol{e}\}
and
\boldsymbol{b}
is in the orthogonal complement:

(1+\mu)\boldsymbol{e}^{\T}\boldsymbol{b}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Im\big((N_{j})_{(i,i)}\tr(\bar{N}_{j})\big)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\Im\big(\tr(N_{j})\tr(\bar{N}_{j})\big)=0;

2)

Since that
I_{n}-(1/n)\boldsymbol{e}\boldsymbol{e}^{\T}
is an orthogonal projection matrix, its Moore-Penrose
pseudo-inverse
[
8
, p.290]
is itself and hence the Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse of
C
is given by

C^{\dagger}=\frac{1}{n}\Big(I_{n}-\frac{1}{n}\boldsymbol{e}\boldsymbol{e}^{\T}\Big)
, yielding the minimum norm solution to (
2.18
):

C^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{b}=\frac{1}{n}\boldsymbol{b},

i.e.,

E_{(s,s)}^{{\rm im}}=\frac{1}{n(1+\mu)}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\Im\big((N_{j})_{(s,s)}\tr(\bar{N}_{j})\big).

(2.19)

Putting (
2.16
) and (
2.19
) together gives

E_{(s,s)}=\frac{1}{n(1+\mu)}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{m}(N_{j})_{(s,s)}\tr(\bar{N}_{j})-\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\tr(\bar{N}_{j})\tr(N_{j})\right].

(2.20)

Now, we consider the off-diagonal entries of
E
and
\bar{E}
.
For
s\neq t
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f(E)}{\partial E_{(s,t)}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial}{\partial E_{(s,t)}}\Bigl|E_{(s,t)}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\bar{N}_{j})_{(i,i)}(N_{j})_{(s,t)}\Bigr|^{2}+\mu\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial}{\partial E_{(s,t)}}\Bigl|E_{(s,t)}\Bigr|^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial}{\partial E_{(s,t)}}\Bigl|\bar{E}_{(s,t)}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\bar{N}_{j})_{(s,t)}(N_{j})_{(i,i)}\Bigr|^{2}+\mu\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial}{\partial E_{(s,t)}}\Bigl|\bar{E}_{(s,t)}\Bigr|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Bigl(\bar{E}_{(s,t)}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}(N_{j})_{(i,i)}(\bar{N}_{j})_{(s,t)}\Bigr)+\mu\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bar{E}_{(s,t)}

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Bigl(\bar{E}_{(s,t)}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\bar{N}_{j})_{(s,t)}(N_{j})_{(i,i)}\Bigr)+\mu\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bar{E}_{(s,t)}

\displaystyle=2n(1+\mu)\bar{E}_{(s,t)}-2\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\bar{N}_{j})_{(s,t)}\tr(N_{j}),

(2.21a)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f(E)}{\partial\bar{E}_{(s,t)}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{E}_{(s,t)}}\Bigl|E_{(s,t)}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\bar{N}_{j})_{(i,i)}(N_{j})_{(s,t)}\Bigr|^{2}+\mu\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{E}_{(s,t)}}\Bigl|E_{(s,t)}\Bigr|^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{E}_{(s,t)}}\Bigl|\bar{E}_{(s,t)}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\bar{N}_{j})_{(s,t)}(N_{j})_{(i,i)}\Bigr|^{2}+\mu\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{E}_{(s,t)}}\Bigl|\bar{E}_{(s,t)}\Bigr|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Bigl(E_{(s,t)}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\bar{N}_{j})_{(i,i)}(N_{j})_{(s,t)}\Bigr)+\mu\sum_{i=1}^{n}E_{(s,t)}

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Bigl(E_{(s,t)}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}(N_{j})_{(s,t)}(\bar{N}_{j})_{(i,i)}\Bigr)+\mu\sum_{i=1}^{n}E_{(s,t)}

\displaystyle=2n(1+\mu)E_{(s,t)}-2\sum_{j=1}^{m}(N_{j})_{(s,t)}\tr(\bar{N}_{j}).

(2.21b)

According to (
2.12
) and upon using (
2.21
), the first order optimality
condition on the off-diagonal entries of
E
is given by,
for
1\leq s,\,t\leq n
and
s\neq t
,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial f(E)}{\partial E_{(s,t)}^{{\rm re}}}=\frac{\partial f(E)}{\partial E_{(s,t)}}+\frac{\partial f(E)}{\partial\bar{E}_{(s,t)}}

\displaystyle=2n(1+\mu)\big[\bar{E}_{(s,t)}+E_{(s,t)}\big]-2\sum_{j=1}^{m}\Bigl((\bar{N}_{j})_{(s,t)}\tr(N_{j})+(N_{j})_{(s,t)}\tr(\bar{N}_{j})\Bigr)

\displaystyle=4n(1+\mu)E_{(s,t)}^{{\rm re}}-4\sum_{j=1}^{m}\Re\big((\bar{N}_{j})_{(s,t)}\tr(N_{j})\big),

(2.22a)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\iota}\cdot\frac{\partial f(E)}{\partial E_{(s,t)}^{{\rm im}}}=\frac{\partial f(E)}{\partial E_{(s,t)}}-\frac{\partial f(E)}{\partial\bar{E}_{(s,t)}}

\displaystyle=2n(1+\mu)\big[\bar{E}_{(s,t)}-E_{(s,t)}\big]-2\sum_{j=1}^{m}\Bigl((\bar{N}_{j})_{(s,t)}\tr(N_{j})-(N_{j})_{(s,t)}\tr(\bar{N}_{j})\Bigr)

\displaystyle=-4n(1+\mu)\iota E_{(s,t)}^{{\rm im}}-4\iota\sum_{j=1}^{m}\Im\big((\bar{N}_{j})_{(s,t)}\tr(N_{j})\big).

(2.22b)

Equations in (
2.22
) can be readily solved to yield

E_{(s,t)}^{{\rm re}}=\frac{1}{n(1+\mu)}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\Re\big((\bar{N}_{j})_{(s,t)}\tr(N_{j})\big),\quad E_{(s,t)}^{{\rm im}}=-\frac{1}{n(1+\mu)}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\Im\big((\bar{N}_{j})_{(s,t)}\tr(N_{j})\big).

Putting them together gives

E_{(s,t)}=\frac{1}{n(1+\mu)}\sum_{j=1}^{m}(N_{j})_{(s,t)}\tr(\bar{N}_{j}).

(2.23)

In summary, all
E_{(s,s)}^{{\rm re}}
and
E_{(s,t)}
for
s\neq t
are uniquely determined by the first order optimality condition
but
E_{(s,s)}^{{\rm im}}
for
1\leq s\leq n
are not as their determining system (
2.18
)
has infinitely many solution. We take a particular one with the minimal Frobenius given by (
2.19
).
Finally, putting (
2.20
) and (
2.23
) together,
we get (
2.4
).

4
Concluding Remarks

The generalized Lyapunov equation

AX+XA^{\HH}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}N_{j}XN_{j}^{\HH}+BB^{\HH}=0

arises frequently in applications.
Such an equation is naturally split into
\mathscr{M}(X)=\mathscr{N}(X)-BB^{\HH}
where
\mathscr{M}(X)=AX+XA^{\HH}
and

\mathscr{N}(X)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}N_{j}XN_{j}^{\HH}
, and it has been done frequently to yield the commonly used
standard fixed-pointed iteration (sFPI) for solving the equation.
The iteration converges
if the spectral radius
\rho(\mathscr{M}^{-1}\mathscr{N})<1
but otherwise diverges in general.
In this paper, we propose
a compensated splitting technique to tackle situations where
\rho(\mathscr{M}^{-1}\mathscr{N})\geq 1

or
\rho(\mathscr{M}^{-1}\mathscr{N})<1
but too close to
1
leading very slow convergence.
The basic idea is to
compensate
\mathscr{M}(X)
by projecting
\mathscr{N}(X)
into an operator in the form
EX+XE^{\HH}
to merge with

\mathscr{M}(X)
, yielding the so-called compensated splitting
\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}(X)=\widetilde{\mathscr{N}}(X)-BB^{\HH}
for which there is a
corresponding fixed-point iteration called the compensated fixed-pointed iteration (cFPI). Ideally,
we should minimize
\rho(\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}^{-1}\widetilde{\mathscr{N}}\,)
over matrix
E
, but that is not practical. Instead
we determine
E
by (
2.3
).
It is shown that (
2.3
) has a unique global minimizer in the real case and a unique global minimizer
with the smallest Frobenius norm in the complex case. Compactly the optimal
E
is given by

E=\frac{1}{n(1+\mu)}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\tr(\bar{N}_{j})\left[N_{j}-\frac{1}{2n}\tr(N_{j})\cdot I_{n}\right],

where
\mu\geq 0
is a regularizing parameter to control the magnitude of
E
. At this point, we have little idea on
how to determine a good
\mu
, and in fact in our reported numerical experiments in
section

3
, we simply take

\mu=0
as the default.

Unfortunately, our current (
2.3
) to determine
E
does not involve the
\mathscr{M}
-part in any way. Quantitatively,
we have analyse the interaction between this optimal
E
and the
\mathscr{M}
-part. Our numerical experiments
appear to behave consistently with our analysis, while
demonstrating superiority of cFPI to sFPI.

In our numerical experiments, we limit the problem sizes, i.e.,
n
, modest so that
involved Lyapunov equations in the middle of computations can be solved by the Bartels-Stewart method
[
1
]

with ease on everyday personal computers. For that reason,
our purpose in this paper is two-fold: one is to demonstrate our compensated splitting can
rescue the natural splitting, the commonly used one (
1.3
) when the resulting
sFPI (
1.4
) diverges, and the other one is to show that the compensated splitting
can speed up cFPI when it indeed converges. It is expected the key idea in this paper can be combined with
more sophisticated subspace-based projection iterative methods, e.g.,
[
4
,
16
]
,
to yield more efficient methods for large scale generalized Lyapunov equations.
Such investigations will be forthcoming.

A similar looking to the generalized Lyapunov equation is the generalized Sylvester equation

AX+XB^{\HH}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}N_{j}XM_{j}^{\HH}+C=0.

It arises from, for example, discretizing certain partial differential equations
[
14
]
and perhaps other places, and it too
possesses a natural splitting that leads to a natural fixed-point iteration, similarly to the discussions
we had in
section

1
for the generalized Lyapunov equation. Due to high similarity, our compensated splitting
in
section

2
can be straightforwardly attempted for potential improvement. We will leave the detail to another
paper.
