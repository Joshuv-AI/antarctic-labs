Abstract
This project advanced understanding of aerosol–cloud interactions in extratropical cyclones
using observations from the Atmospheric Radiation Measurement (ARM) Eastern North At-
lantic (ENA) observatory and ACE-ENA field campaigns, along with satellite datasets and
numerical modeling.
 We have quantified the susceptibility of frontal clouds to aerosols, showing that this suscep-
tibility is similar to that in non-frontal clouds despite substantial (factor 2) differences in droplet
concentrations between these cloud types, and also significant differences in cloud albedo. A
key finding was that Aitken-mode (here, 60-100 nm diameter) aerosols play a disproportion-
ately important role in droplet activation in frontal clouds, and concentrations of these particles
are frequently strongly biased in climate models. Activation of aerosols depends strongly
on updraft speeds, and via collaboration with Argonne National Laboratory we contributed to
quantifying these better with the ARM radar wind profiler at the ENA site.
 Our studies in the North Atlantic have analogs in the Southern Ocean, and to draw these
out we examined data from the MARCUS and SOCRATES field campaigns. We identified
new particle formation within extratropical cyclones as a potentially important source of cloud
condensation nuclei in these pristine marine environments.
 By combining ARM observations with perturbed parameter ensemble simulations, we demon-
strated that surface observations can effectively constrain aerosol–cloud adjustments and re-
duce uncertainty in simulated cloud liquid water path responses by approximately 15%, yield-
ing stronger constraints on historical aerosol cooling effects. The work further established
precipitation processes as a dominant control on aerosol–cloud interactions and Earth system
predictability.

Introduction
In this Atmospheric System Research project, we aimed to understand aerosol-cloud inter-
actions in extratropical cyclones using data from the Atmospheric Radiation Measurement
(ARM) Eastern North Atlantic (ENA) fixed site at Graciosa Island, and the Aerosol Character-
ization Experiment-ENA (ACE-ENA) field campaigns. Our examination of in-situ and remote
sensing ARM observations was complemented by analysis of geostationary satellite datasets,
reanalysis, and simulations using weather and climate models. Our overall goal was to bet-
ter understand the processes by which aerosols affect clouds and precipitation near marine
extratropical cyclone fronts and how aerosols are transported and processed by cyclones.
 Mid-latitude cyclones are important. Cyclone systems overall account for close to a third
of mid-latitude ocean area, so clouds in these systems account for a sizable fraction of global
cloud radiative effects. While the majority of clouds in cyclone systems are either low-level
boundary layer clouds which have been extensively studied in the past, or are ice-phase and
therefore difficult to characterize quantitatively with remote sensing methods, liquid frontal
clouds associated with storms frequently also extend over substantial swathes of the ocean.
The radiative effects of these frontal clouds have not been studied extensively before. All
clouds within cyclone systems have uncertain interactions with aerosols. In this project we
focused on studying and comparing frontal and non-frontal cloud responses to aerosol in the
North Atlantic. We also considered aerosol-cloud interactions in the Southern Ocean.
 The fronts of mid-latitude cyclones frequently pass over the ENA observatory, so the ARM
datasets are a critical resource for our project.
 In the project, we proposed to identify fronts and frontal volumes, classify them, and char-
acterize the frontal structure. We then proposed to perform a study of aerosol activation in
mid-latitude cyclones and consider the importance of mid-latitude cyclones for North Atlantic
aerosol budgets. A key part of our proposal was a closure study of aerosol activation us-
ing ARM datasets, in which we would understand the relationship between the aerosol size
distribution and the cloud droplet number concentration in liquid cyclone clouds.
 We also proposed to start investigating how cyclones affect aerosol budgets in both the
North Atlantic and Southern Ocean. In both regions, cyclones are critical to the transport of
aerosol and to creating favorable conditions for aerosol number concentrations to be replen-
ished either via emissions of sea spray or new particle formation. The aerosols so created act
as cloud condensation nuclei in low clouds and cyclones alike.

Major findings

1 Surface observations can provide effective constraints
 on simulated aerosol-cloud interactions
The adjustment of cloud liquid water path (LWP) to enhanced cloud droplet concentration (Nd )
and associated radiative forcing is uncertain in sign. One reason underlining this uncertainty is
that while we understand individual physical processes (e.g. size dependent cloud top entrain-
ment), it is difficult to disentangle the causality between different processes and the observed
state of the system. To better understand how observations constrain aerosol-cloud adjust-

ments, we combined Nd , precipitation (P), and LWP surface observations from ENA with the
output from a perturbed parameter ensemble (PPE) hosted in the Community Atmosphere
Model version 6 (CAM6). As shown in Mikkelsen et al. (2025b), this allows for causal interpre-
tation of observed covariability between Nd and LWP. It is found that this observed covariability
is dominated by coalescence scavenging and is not strongly related to aerosol–cloud adjust-
ments. Critically, observed covaraiability between LWP and Nd has been used in the past to
argue for the dominance of cloud thinning adjustments. This analysis underlines that this is
conflating correlation with causation.
 In addition to revealing how processes combine to produce the observed state, obser-
vations of precipitation and cloud from ENA constrain the range of possible LWP-mediated
aerosol–cloud adjustments relative to the prior from the PPE by 15% (see Figure 1), resulting
in a global value that is confidently positive (a historical cooling) ranging from 2.1 to 6.9 gm−2 .
This work complements constraints on ∆LW P for the same PPE using satellite data from the
Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectrometer (MODIS) established by Song et al. (2024), and
there is potential for further studies to explore combining the constraints from the surface and
from satellite observations to tighten the overall constraint.
 Following on the success of the methodology in Mikkelsen et al. (2025b), we decided to
leverage recent advances in retrievals of Nd combined with a PPE developed in E3SMv3. This
allowed us to build on the approach linking observable state, process, and global constraint in
Mikkelsen et al. (2025b) to anthropogenic perturbations in Nd . ARM observatories allow simul-
taneous measurement of CCN and Nd, which allows unique insight into Nd perturbations. This
work is published in Jones et al. (2026) as well as being featured in the University of Wyoming
magazine (https://issuu.com/uwyo/docs/uwyo\_magazine\_winter\_2026). Based on
this approach, we were able to develop a framework oriented around basic process under-
standing of aerosol activiation that linked observed mean states to the anthropogenic per-
turbation to Mikkelsen et al. (2025b). We found that observations from ASI, SGP, and ENA
provided a substantially tighter constraint on the anthropogenic contribution to Nd than previ-
ous studies.

 Aligned with these efforts, we were able to leverage efforts with other funding focused
on airborne measurements to complement work in Jones et al. (2026) and Mikkelsen et al.
(2025b). Leveraging the same PPE used in Mikkelsen et al. (2025b), we simulated the sam-
pling conducted during the Southern Ocean Clouds, Radiation, Aerosol Transport Experimen-
tal Study (SOCRATES) field campaign. This provided insight into how approaches using a
fixed ARM site mapped to a moving airborne platform and translated to remote, pristine envi-
ronments. The results of this analysis are published in Song et al. (2025b).

2 Precipitation exerts a strong control on aerosol-cloud in-
 teractions
In Mikkelsen et al. (2025b), we identified precipitation processes as being both an important
confounder to observing aerosol-cloud adjustments as well as critical in setting the regime.
One major limitation of Mikkelsen et al. (2025b) was that we were not able to isolate virga
as a sink term of cloud droplets. To tackle this, we developed a new observations frame-
work leveraging KaZR reflectivity, micropulse lidar-derived Nd , and microwave LWP. When we
didn’t account for cloud base precipitation rate, a negative correlation between Nd and LWP
appeared, consistent with previous literature. Once we conditioned on cloud base reflectiv-
ity (effectively rain rate, as we show in the article), a positive correlation between Nd and
LWP appeared for all regimes except heavily precipitating (consistent with frontal passages)
and non-precipitating. Updating previous assessments of aerosol-cloud cooling with our new
observational insight revealed a substantially stronger cooling and constrained Earth system
predictability. The resulting paper is under review with a preprint available (Mikkelsen et al.,
2026).
 In parallel, questions raised in Mikkelsen et al. (2025b) led us to consider how precipitation
processes set Earth system predictability. We published this in a series of papers: Song et al.
(2024) and Song et al. (2025c). We found that precipitation processes, broadly considered
in terms of precipitation efficiency, set the mean state cloud amount, which in turn sets the
radiative susceptibility of the Earth system to aerosol-cloud interactions. Simultaneously, pre-
cipitation processes underline precipitation suppression and enhanced precipitation efficiency
hobbles precipitation suppression. Together, these effects result in a net buffering of the ra-
diative effect of aerosol-cloud adjustments (Song et al., 2024). We carried this result on to
considering how clouds respond to changes in sea surface temperature (Song et al., 2025c).
We believe that this series of papers underline how critical microphysical cloud and precipita-
tion processes as observed by ARM instruments are for setting Earth system predictability.

 as non-frontal cloud albedo

project which will form the core of a PhD thesis and is to be submitted for publication immi-
nently, we quantify aerosol–cloud interactions in frontal clouds observed at the ENA facility and
compared them to non-frontal clouds. We first use surface and satellite measurements to char-

tify albedo susceptibility. We focus on liquid, non-precipitating and drizzling frontal clouds over
the North Atlantic (2017–2018), identified using manual classification of satellite imagery and
filtered with radar to exclude ice and heavy precipitation. Aerosol number concentrations are
measured at the surface and used as a proxy for cloud-base aerosol, supported by the good
correlation of simulated aerosol at the surface and at cloud base in cold fronts (R2 = 0.82). We
used regional simulations previously evaluated in the Amazon rainforest by Wang et al. (2025).
We are therefore able to perform the activation closure we proposed, comparing simultaneous
measurements of aerosol size distribution with remotely sensed droplet concentration. We
compared several techniques to extract droplet concentrations, taking advantage of lidar and
microwave radiometer data at the ENA site that is only intermittently available from satellites.
 Interestingly, median droplet concentrations in our sample are much higher in cold front
clouds (140 cm−3 ), compared to non-frontal clouds (57 cm−3 ). We quantify albedo susceptibil-
ity using a multiple linear regression accounting for the Twomey effect and for liquid water path
adjustments, broadly following an earlier study by Varble et al (Atmos. Chem. Phys. 2023).
The trends in albedo as a function of droplet concentration and cloud liquid water path across
the three cloud types are shown in Figure 2.
 Despite the radically different droplet concentrations, the key result is that albedo suscep-
tibility to aerosol is similar across cloud types. The susceptibility of albedo α to the number
concentration of aerosols greater than 60 nm in diameter N60 , dα/dlnN60 , is 0.020 for cold
fronts, 0.024 for warm fronts, and 0.027 for non-frontal clouds, with much higher uncertainties
in warm front clouds. Frontal clouds have stronger droplet responses to aerosol, but this is
offset by their higher baseline albedo, which reduces sensitivity. The higher uncertainties in
warm front clouds are associated with the unsurprisingly poor correlation between surface and
cloud base aerosol concentrations in warm front clouds.

4 Aitken-mode aerosols are important for activation in frontal
 clouds
A central finding of the same study is the enhanced importance of small (60-100 nm diameter)
aerosols in frontal clouds due to stronger dynamical forcing. In these environments, higher su-
persaturations allow activation of smaller particles, reducing activation diameters by 20–40 nm
relative to non-frontal clouds. This shift substantially increases the pool of particles able to
form droplets, such that 50% of cloud droplets originate from aerosols smaller than 100 nm,
compared to 30% in non-frontal clouds. The global climate model we tested struggles to repre-
sent this process, underestimating the both the number concentration of 60-100 nm diameter
particles and the fraction of them that activate, leading to an overall 40% low bias in droplet
number concentrations in cold frontal clouds. The underestimation in Aitken-mode aerosol
number concentration in the mid-latitude boundary layer is confirmed by evaluations against
ATom aircraft measurements in a complementary study (see next section) by He et al. (2026).
We have good reason to suspect that this result would generalize to other CMIP6-era climate
models.

5 New particle formation associated with extratropical cy-
 clones could be an important source of CCN in pristine
 regions
Cyclones affect aerosol budgets primarily by converting moisture to precipitation, which re-
moves particles. They also lead to strong winds which can cause stronger emissions of
sea spray. In the North Atlantic, the dominant spatial trend in simulated (and observed)
accumulation-mode aerosol number concentration is a decrease with latitude. However, scav-
enging should mean a higher concentration before the passage of a cold front, in the lower-
right quadrant of a cyclone, compared to the lower-left quadrant which comes after the cold
front. This is not consistently observed, though we do see evidence for scavenging in the lower
concentrations observed in older cyclones compared to younger cyclones (Figure 3).

 Since aerosol budgets in climate models in the Southern Ocean, which is strongly impacted
by extratropical cyclones, are very uncertain, we also performed an evaluation of simulated
aerosol number concentration against data from the Measurements of Aerosols, Radiation,
and Clouds over the Southern Ocean (MARCUS) ship cruise. We find large underestimates,
consistent with other studies. While we are not yet sure of the origins of these underestimates,
this motivated a promising new research direction, which was not supported directly by this
ASR project but should in future help address ASR goals. Supported by an NSF RAPID
grant, the CMU team contributed aerosol forecasts and mission science to the HALO-south
aircraft field campaign in the Southern Ocean in September 2025. Similar studies of aerosol
interactions with extratropical cyclones in the Southern Ocean will follow our studies at ENA
using the aircraft data. We were able to forecast new particle formation within extratropical
cyclones in the free troposphere using an almost identical model to that evaluated by He et al.
(2026), and subsequently sample the same particle formation events with the HALO aircraft.
We now need to address the question of how the particles formed mix into the boundary layer
and why the model can perform so well at high altitude while underpredicting aerosols where
they are needed to seed droplets at cloud base.

6 Turbulence that affects aerosol activation can be mea-
 sured by an appropriately calibrated radar wind profiler
Turbulence in the marine boundary layer is critical for aerosol activation and marine cloud
properties as it leads to variability in vertical velocity, a key input to aerosol activation param-
eterizations. At the University of Wyoming, a project started under the SGCSR program and
continued under this award quantified the ability of the radar wind profiler (RWP) to measure
this important quantity. The RWP wind, precipitation long pulse and precipitation short pulse
modes were calibrated using the surface laser disdrometer as described in a paper in re-
view (Mikkelsen et al., 2025a). Over the 10 years of data analyzed here, the RWP wind mode
calibration constant, sensitivity, and dynamic range fluctuated between -84 to -44 dB, -15 to 9
dBZ, and 12 to 21 dBZ, respectively. The clear-air RWP echoes were used to derive the struc-
ture function of the refractive index and dissipation rates of turbulent kinetic energy. Both of
these quantities decreased from the surface upwards, with higher values in the winter months
and lower values in the summer months, consistent with previous studies. At CMU, aerosol ac-
tivation parameterizations that use turbulent updrafts were refined by Ghosh et al. (2025) and a
further study in progress aims to use ARM data to produce a resolution-independent parame-
terization of updraft speeds for aerosol activation using the RWP at the SGP site together with
data from the large eddy simulation (LES) ARM Symbiotic Simulation and Observation Activity
(LASSO) and the Aerosol Characterization Experiment-ENA (ACE-ENA) field campaign.
