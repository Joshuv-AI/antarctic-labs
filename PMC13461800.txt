Type-II-CD20-targeted therapy for CD38 antibody-refractory antibody-mediated rejection after kidney transplantation: a case report
Authors: Jonathan de Fallois, Sebastian Baatz, Bastian M. Krüger, Lisa-M. Dilz, Andreas Boldt, Julia Beck, Kirsten Bornemann-Kolatzki, Friederike Petzold, Christoph Daniel, Kerstin Amann, Jan Kowald
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
With the advent of CD38 antibody therapy, an effective treatment option for antibody-mediated rejection (ABMR) appears achievable. However, a subset of patients remains refractory. We report a kidney transplant recipient with persistent microvascular inflammation in kidney allograft biopsy after six months of CD38 antibody treatment, necessitating rescue therapy. Persistent detection of peripheral CD38-negative class-switched memory B cells prompted additional deep B-cell depletion using a humanized, glycoengineered, IgG1 monoclonal antibody targeting the type-II epitope of CD20. Follow-up kidney biopsy demonstrated histological resolution of microvascular inflammation, while normalization of donor-derived cell-free DNA was consistent with remission of ABMR. In this case of CD38 antibody-refractory ABMR, adjunctive type-II-CD20 antibody therapy had a favorable response. This highlights the potential contribution of additional effector cells to refractory ABMR, such as CD38-negative class-switched memory B cells. These findings suggest a potential role for individualized, multimodal B-cell–directed immunomodulatory strategies that may improve outcomes in selected patients with treatment-refractory ABMR, thus contributing to sustained allograft survival.

Introduction

Recent advances in targeted immunotherapy have begun to challenge the paradigm that Antibody-mediated rejection (ABMR) is an often progressive and unmodifiable fate, and also a major cause of allograft failure after kidney transplantation (KTx) (1, 2).

Depletion of CD38-expressing immune cell populations, particularly natural killer cells (NKC) and plasma cells (PC), is emerging as a promising therapeutic target (3). CD38-directed therapy has demonstrated encouraging efficacy in terms of stabilization of eGFR, histological activity, and molecular ABMR activity scores in a phase II trial and several case reports, suggesting that effective control of ABMR may be achievable (4–12). However, in the phase II trial evaluating the anti-CD38 monoclonal antibody felzartamab, complete histologic resolution of ABMR was not achieved in approximately 18% of treated patients (5). In a currently recruiting phase III study, the efficacy and safety of felzartamab will be further evaluated [NCT06685757]. A retrospective study including patients treated with daratumumab for chronic active ABMR showed improvement of microvascular inflammation (MVI) scores in 13 of 16 patients, while a subset of patients remained refractory (12). The mechanisms underlying incomplete therapeutic response remain poorly understood. Identification of immune pathways contributing to treatment failure is crucial for developing effective rescue therapies.

Here, we report a case of CD38 antibody–refractory ABMR successfully treated with additional deep B-cell depletion using a humanized, glycoengineered, IgG1 monoclonal antibody targeting the type-II epitope of CD20.

Case presentation

We report on a 38-year-old man with chronic kidney failure due to IgA nephropathy who underwent deceased-donor KTx after five years of hemodialysis. Patient characteristics during follow-up displays Supplementary Table 1.

Written informed consent for publication of clinical data and images was obtained. Immunological assessment revealed three human leukocyte antigen (HLA) mismatches 1-2-0–0 for HLA-A, -B, -DR, -DQ, absence of anti-HLA-antibodies, and no preformed donor-specific antibodies (DSA). Immunosuppression was induced with basiliximab and maintained with prednisolone, tacrolimus and mycophenolate mofetil. Kidney allograft function remained stable during the first two years after transplantation and, apart from diet-managed post-transplant diabetes mellitus, no relevant complications occurred.

A sudden increase of creatinine and the resulting decline in estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) (calculated by the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equation) from 84 to 54 ml/min/1.73m2 (creatinine 97 to 138 µmol/L) accompanied by increasing proteinuria and hematuria prompted an allograft biopsy demonstrating active ABMR (Banff-score 2022: g2 ptc2 cg0) (Figure 1). Four de-novo DSA against HLA-A3, HLA-B27, HLA-B44, and HLA-Cw7 were detected. At this point, the patient was in good general condition, had no edema, and office blood pressure was within the high-normal range. Ultrasounds of the allograft kidney showed normal morphology and size, and no signs of tumor, no nephrolithiasis, and no urinary obstruction as well as adequate perfusion. Immediate treatment with ten sessions of immunoadsorption, intravenous immunoglobulins, and a high-dose corticosteroid pulse resulted in insufficient clinical response (creatinine 188 µmol/L eGFR 37ml/min/1.73m2). In the control biopsy after two months persistent MVI (Banff-score 2022: g1 ptc2 cg1) (Figures 2A, B), ongoing detection of DSA against HLA-B27, HLA-B44, HLA-Cw7, and elevated donor-derived cell-free DNA (dd-cfDNA), indicated continuing allograft injury. CD38-directed therapy with daratumumab was therefore initiated (16 mg/kg intravenously weekly for four weeks, followed by 1800 mg subcutaneously monthly).

After six months of daratumumab therapy, kidney function showed only partial recovery (creatinine 138 µmol/L; eGFR 54 ml/min/1.73m2) and dd-cfDNA levels remained elevated. Consistent with these findings, the follow-up biopsy demonstrated progression of MVI and chronic microvascular injury (Banff-score 2022: g2 ptc3 cg3) (Figures 2C, D), as well as borderline rejection (i1, t2). This was accompanied by progressive interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy.

While mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) values of DSA against HLA-A3 and HLA-B27 decreased below the diagnostic thresholds, MFI values for HLA-B44 remained stable, whereas HLA-Cw7 increased. Peripheral blood immunophenotyping revealed persistent CD19-positive B cells (126/µL) with a high proportion of CD38-negative class-switched memory B cells (mBC) (29,5% of B cells), despite marked depletion of CD16/56-positive NKC (4/µL).

Due to ongoing inflammatory deterioration and progressive fibrotic remodeling, a rescue therapy with the humanized, glycoengineered, IgG1 monoclonal antibody targeting the type-II epitope of CD20 (obinutuzumab 1000 mg) was administered. Additionally, a high-dose corticosteroid pulse was administered to treat the borderline rejection.

Subsequently, dd-cfDNA levels normalized, MFI values of DSA against HLA-B44 and HLA-Cw7 modestly decreased (Supplementary Figure 1), and kidney function remained stable (creatinine 142 µmol/L; eGFR 52 ml/min/1,73m2). Three months after the CD20-directed rescue therapy with obinutuzumab, kidney biopsy demonstrated resolution of MVI and improvement in chronic microvascular injury, including transplant glomerulopathy (Banff-score 2022: g0 ptc0 cg0) (Figures 2E, F); without further progression of fibrotic remodeling (Figure 1; Supplementary Tables 1–4). Multiplex immunofluorescence staining of the biopsies showed that subsets of CD38- and CD16- positive cells decreased after daratumumab therapy, (Figures 3A–H), whereas two distinct subsets of CD38-negative, CD27-positive cells persisted, one being CD138-positive and the other CD138-negative. (Figure 3G). These subsets became less detectable only after obinutuzumab therapy (Figure 3G).

No infectious complications were observed during treatment or follow-up. After the first administration of daratumumab, the patient developed acute transient myopia, which resolved spontaneously within a few days.

Discussion

With the increasing use of CD38 antibody therapy for ABMR, treatment-refractory cases may be observed more frequently (5, 12). The mechanisms underlying incomplete therapeutic responses in some patients are currently unknown.

Recent data from CD38 antibody-based pretransplant desensitization protocols in highly HLA-sensitized patients demonstrated persistent circulation of CD38-negative class-switched mBC in non-responders. These cells retain the capability to switch and rapidly differentiate and proliferate into anti-HLA-antibody producing PC (13). Enthusiasm for conventional CD20 antibody treatment for addressing ABMR has markedly declined due to negative study results. However, there are still scarce data on whether type II anti-CD20–targeted therapy could achieve superior effects (14, 15).

In our patient, CD19-positive B cells, including a substantial proportion of CD38-negative class-switched mBC, persisted in the peripheral blood after CD38 antibody treatment (Supplementary Table 1). In the context of desensitization prior to KTx, patients with pretreatment CD38-negative class-switched mBC above 15.6% among all CD19-positive B cells were more likely to show suboptimal responding to CD38 antibody therapy (13). In comparison, the proportion of CD38-negative class-switched mBC in our patient after daratumumab treatment was 29.5%. Although based on a single case and in a different clinical setting, the proportion of CD38-negative class-switched mBC above the cutoff of 15.6% supports the hypothesis that these cells could be associated with a poorer response to CD38-directed therapy in the treatment of ABMR. One possible explanation involves rapid redifferentiation of CD38-negative class-switched mBC into plasmablasts and short-living PC, which are able to produce large quantities of high-affinity alloantibodies (16, 17). Upon re-exposure to alloantigens, they may rapidly generate large amounts of proinflammatory and complement-binding antibodies that sustain endothelial injury and thereby ongoing MVI. The increase in MFI values of DSA against HLA-Cw7 and the persistence of CD27-positive cells in the kidney biopsy despite CD38-targeted therapy reinforces this hypothesis. Based on these considerations, we opted for a treatment with the CD20 antibody obinutuzumab. Compared with conventional type-I anti-CD20 antibodies such as rituximab, obinutuzumab induces more profound depletion of peripheral and central naïve B cells, as well as mBC. In contrast to Rituximab, complement-dependent cytotoxicity of obinutuzumab is reduced, while B cell-depletion is predominantly mediated by direct antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity and phagocytosis (15, 17–19).

Another recent proof-of-concept desensitization trial combining belatacept and daratumumab showed that sequential modulation of B cell activation and PC depletion leads to a substantial reduction in anti-HLA antibodies, indicating synergistic effects (20). The sequential therapy of daratumumab and tocilizumab improved MVI and reduced DSA in late ABMR (21). These recent studies support the hypothesis that combination therapies exert additional effects on PC and B cell activation and differentiation. Therefore, combination therapies may provide additional benefits in selected patients.

The feasibility of type-II-CD20 targeting in the treatment of early ABMR has previously been described in two cases, treated in combination with C5 complement inhibitor (22). Obinutuzumab has also been successfully used in combination with a B cell maturation antigen-targeted T cell engager in a desensitization protocol (23). In this case, desensitization therapy with daratumumab was unsuccessful in reducing HLA antibody levels, and persistent memory B-cell reactivation was proposed as a potential explanation for the observed rebound. Although reported in a different clinical context, it supports the relevance of exploring resistance mechanisms and alternative B-cell-targeting strategies (23).

In contrast, our report illustrates the potential role of an adjunctive obinutuzumab therapy in chronic active ABMR refractory to CD38 antibody treatment. Overall, the therapy was well tolerated without severe complications. Acute transient myopia is a recognized adverse effect associated with daratumumab (24). The body mass index was already low before the onset of ABMR but remained stable throughout therapy.

Following CD20-directed therapy, DSA against HLA-Cw7 decreased but remained detectable, although MVI resolved. Persistent antibody production may predominantly be maintained by CD19-negative long-lived PC, which escaped CD20 antibody treatment. These cells do not necessarily trigger MVI (17, 18). In addition, a subset of these PC may have escaped CD38 antibody treatment, potentially due to residence in bone marrow niches, where they are shielded from immune-mediated depletion (25). A similar escape mechanism is observed in hematological malignancies, such as multiple myeloma (26). Mechanisms of resistance include downregulation of CD38 and upregulation of CD47, which blocks antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis (25).

Beyond antibody production, mBC contribute to immune activation through antigen presentation, cell-cell interactions, and cytokine production (16, 17). Eliminating of these mBC disrupts this proinflammatory immune network, thus enabling the cessation of MVI despite the persistent DSA (27). Supporting this concept, circulating donor‐reactive mBC were associated with ABMR occurrence even in the absence of detectable DSA (28). Direct measurement of cell populations is proposed as a complement to DSA determination and could provide a more sophisticated approach to detecting refractory or subclinical ABMR during CD38-directed therapy (28). Whether quantification of CD38-negative class-switched mBC may predict treatment response requires further studies.

NKC activation is recognized as a critical mediator of MVI in ABMR and can be triggered either antibody-mediated or antibody-independently, such as by missing self-recognition (5, 29). In our case, NKC were effectively depleted following the six-month course of CD38-directed therapy, in line with the known effects of daratumumab (5, 6).

These observations suggest that in CD38 antibody-refractory ABMR, mechanisms beyond NKC and PC may contribute to ongoing alloimmune activity, potentially involving incomplete depletion of other B-cell subsets, including CD38-negative class-switched mBC.

Whether a sequential approach starting primarily with type II-CD20 antibody therapy instead of CD38 antibody targeting would achieve a comparable or superior effect on histological resolution requires further investigation.

Temporal association of obinutuzumab administration and consistent findings of histology, circulating biomarkers (dd-cfDNA), and immunophenotyping strengthen the interpretation of the clinical case and make it possible to consider mechanistic aspects. However, these findings are based on the observation of a single case, and a clear causality cannot be established. Alternative explanations for the observed therapeutic response include delayed effects of prior CD38 antibody therapy, the corticosteroid pulse administered shortly before obinutuzumab, or natural fluctuation in ABMR activity. Similarly, trends in dd-cfDNA levels should be interpreted with caution, as this biomarker lacks specificity for ABMR (30). Other histopathological lesions, e.g. T-cell mediated rejection, may also influence dd-cfDNA levels. Interpretation of longitudinal dynamics using peripheral immunophenotyping data is limited as it has been assessed only before and after obinutuzumab treatment.

The immunofluorescence study illustrates the dynamic tissue infiltration of different immune cells. The reduced intensity of CD16- and CD38-positive subsets after daratumumab treatment may be associated with decreased infiltration by NKC and PC (Figures 3G, H). The persistence of a subset of CD38- negative, CD138-negative, CD27-positive cells after daratumumab treatment suggests ongoing presence of mBC, which decreased only after obinutuzumab (Figure 3G). However, it should be noted that the markers used are expressed by multiple cell types and therefore have limited specificity. In particular, CD56 staining would have strengthened the assessment of NKC infiltration; however the staining was unsuccessful (31, 32). While the immunofluorescence study supports the findings, it does not proof direct functional evidence that CD38-negative mBC actively maintain MVI.

Although transplant glomerulopathy has traditionally been considered largely irreversible, recent reports have raised the possibility that at least partial regression of chronic lesions may occur following effective CD38-directed therapy (33). Therefore, in our single case, regression of cg lesions cannot be entirely excluded. However, biopsy heterogeneity and sampling variability may also represent possible explanations for the variability observed in the MVI and cg score, as well as in the extent of fibrotic injury. This important limitation should be considered, as creatinine level did not return to baseline after treatment.

In summary, this case suggests that B-cell populations escaping CD38-directed therapy may contribute to treatment-refractory ABMR. Targeting these residual B-cell compartments using type II- CD20 antibody therapy may represent a promising rescue strategy. In the reported case of CD38 antibody-refractory ABMR, adjunctive type-II-CD20 antibody therapy was followed by histological improvement and stabilization of graft function.

Although limited by its descriptive nature and the absence of functional validation of the proposed mechanisms, this case provides valuable hypothesis-generating insights.

Further studies are needed to define the role of sequential or combination B-cell–directed therapies and to identify immunologic biomarkers that predict therapeutic response and guide individualized treatment approaches in ABMR.
