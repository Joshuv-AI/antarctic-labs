 HIGHLIGHTS

U.S. HYDROPOWER AND PUMPED STORAGE HYDROPOWER (PSH) CAPACITY AND
INVESTMENT TRENDS

At the end of 2025, the U.S. hydropower fleet (excluding PSH) contained 2,258 hydropower
plants with a total generating capacity of 80.55 GW.1 The fleet produced 23.1% of all renewable
electricity and 5.6% of total electricity in the United States in 2025.2 (Chapter 1)

 • The net capacity of the U.S. hydropower fleet increased by 2.1 GW from 2010 to 2025. This net
 change is a combination of 1.9 GW added to the existing fleet, 0.7 GW of capacity from new
 facilities (of which 94% of the capacity was from retrofits of non-powered dams (NPDs) and
 conduits), and 0.5 GW of capacity retirements. The three years since the previous edition of the
 report (2023–2025) accounted for 16% of the net capacity additions at the existing fleet, 0.4% of
 new project additions, and 46% of retired capacity in 2010–2025. A single project (the Klamath
 project) with three plants in California and one in Oregon accounts for 75% of the 217 MW of
 capacity retired in 2023–2025. (Section 1.1)

Forty-one PSH plants with a total nameplate generating capacity of 22.2 GW and an estimated
energy storage capacity of 553 GWh accounted for 33.5% of the nameplate generating capacity
and 82.1% of the energy storage capacity of utility-scale storage units in the United States.
(Chapter 1)

 • From 2010 to 2025, the net change in U.S. PSH nameplate generating capacity was an increase
 of 1.7 GW. From 2023 to 2025, 258 MW added through upgrades to existing facilities became
 operational and two PSH facilities in California retired (47.4 MW). (Section 1.1)

 • Batteries surpassed PSH in 2024 in terms of nameplate generating capacity. By the end of 2025,
 994 utility-scale batteries were in operation in the United States with total nameplate generating
 capacity of 43.6 GW (of which 97% are lithium-ion batteries) and energy storage capacity of 121
 GWh.

 • PSH continues to provide most (82%) of the energy storage capacity because of its longer storage
 durations (i.e., the number of hours it takes to deplete the energy stored in the unit if discharging it
 at its rated nameplate capacity). The median storage duration for U.S. utility-scale battery systems
 at their rated capacity is two hours. On the other hand, the estimated median storage duration of
 U.S. PSH plants at their rated capacity is ten hours. (Chapter 1 Overview)

The average annual investment in refurbishments and upgrades (R&U) to the existing U.S.
hydropower and PSH fleets in 2023–2025 was $646 million (24% lower than the 2010–2022
average in 2025 dollars). Eighty percent of the investment value corresponds to projects where the
turbine-generator units are the main scope item. (Section 1.2)

 • Hydropower assets in the Northwest, Midwest, and Southeast regions, large (>100 MW–500 MW)
 plants, and plants owned by non-federal public entities are the fleet segments that received the
 highest average R&U investment per kilowatt in 2010–2025. (Section 1.2)

HYDROPOWER AND PSH RELICENSE AND LICENSE SURRENDER TRENDS

At the end of 2025, 200 hydropower and PSH projects with a total capacity of 18.5 GW (18% of
U.S. installed capacity) were in the relicensing process. (Section 1.4.1)

  • The number of relicense issuances in 2025 (21) was the largest since 2005, helping to alleviate the
 backlog of relicense applications. (Section 1.4.2)

  • The median duration of the relicensing process, from submission of the notice of intent (NOI)
 to relicense until relicense issuance, for 165 relicenses issued from 2010 to 2025 was six years.
 Typical durations were longer for large (>100MW–500MW) projects (7.5 years) than other size
 categories. Relicensing duration was less variable and slightly shorter (5.9 years) for projects that
 used the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) Integrated Licensing Process (ILP)
 modality. (Section 1.4.3)

Forty projects totaling 65 MW were in the process of surrendering their licenses at the end of 2025.
(Section 1.4.1)

  • The annual average number of surrender applications submitted in 2023–2025 was eight (versus
    six from 2010 to 2022).

  • The median capacity of the 40 projects in the license surrender process is 0.8 MW. The Borel
 project in California (12 MW) is the one license surrender with capacity greater than 10 MW.
 Many of the projects whose licenses are being surrendered have not generated power for years
 often because they are in a state of disrepair due to wear and tear of plant components or damage
 by a natural hazard. (Section 1.4.1)

  • Median duration from surrender application submission to surrender issuance was one year.
 Durations were typically longer for projects in the Northwest (1.9 years) and Southwest (2.2 years)
 as well as those that involve dam removal (1.6 years). (Section 1.4.3)

U.S. HYDROPOWER AND PSH DEVELOPMENT PIPELINE

At the beginning of 2026, the U.S. hydropower development pipeline included 161 projects with
a combined capacity of 2.06 GW. Seventy-seven percent of these projects propose construction of
new facilities with a combined capacity of 1.4 GW (versus 109 proposed facilities with combined
capacity of 1.1 GW one year earlier). Six of them (14 MW) were under construction and 55 were
at the earliest stage of development in which feasibility is being assessed and attrition rate is very
high. Moreover, the owners of 33 operational hydropower facilities are planning or constructing
capacity additions totaling 669 MW. (Section 2.1)

  • Most capacity additions to existing facilities result from upgrades to existing turbine-generator
 units. The new 259-MW unit proposed at Dworshak Dam (a 465 MW U.S. Army Corps of
 Engineers (USACE) hydropower plant in Idaho) accounts for 90% of planned new capacity from
 unit additions to existing facilities. (Section 2.1)

  • For the 80 projects that obtained FERC original licenses from 2010 to 2025, the median licensing
 process durations were three years for conduits (seven licenses), four years for new stream-reach
 developments (NSD) (ten licenses), five years for NPDs (59 licenses), and six years for PSH
 (four licenses). Most conduit projects do not require a FERC license. Since the FERC qualifying
 conduit process was introduced in 2013, 175 conduit projects have used it to obtain development
 authorization. The median duration of that process is 0.1 years (Section 2.3).

 4

Seventy-four PSH projects (totaling 67 GW) were in the development pipeline at the beginning of
2026. Sixty-eight of them (totaling 59 GW) were pursuing the FERC permitting process for new
projects (versus 70 with total capacity of 61 GW one year earlier). Of those 68, four had already
received a FERC license. Three additional new projects under consideration do not require a
FERC license. Separately, owners of three existing PSH facilities plan to increase capacity by
uprating existing units or adding new units. (Section 2.2)

 • Almost 80% (58 of 74) of PSH projects are in the Western half of the country. The median capacity
 of U.S. PSH projects is 600 MW. Sixty projects propose closed-loop configurations in which the
 reservoirs are not continuously connected to a naturally flowing body of water. All the projects,
 whether greenfield developments or capacity additions, proposed by entities that already own and
 operate PSH capacity (Tennessee Valley Authority (TVA), Salt River Project, Duke Energy, and
 Pacific Gas & Electric) are among the 14 open-loop projects in the pipeline. (Section 2.2)

GLOBAL HYDROPOWER AND PSH TRENDS

In 2025, global hydropower capacity reached 1,296 GW.3 Additionally, the global PSH fleet had
a capacity of 160 GW. Together, hydropower and PSH represented 14% of global generation
capacity in 2025 and 15% of global electricity generated in 2024. (Section 3.1)

 • From 2016 to 2025, the average annual global growth rate was 1.4% for hydropower and 3.6% for
 PSH; total electricity generation capacity grew at an average of 5.2% per year. (Section 3.1)

 • Globally, PSH capacity in the development pipeline (628 GW) overtook hydropower capacity
 (431 GW) for the first time. Capacity under construction was 103 GW for hydropower (732 new
 facilities) and 172 GW (151 new facilities) for PSH. India has the most hydropower capacity
 under construction (14.3 GW) and China has the most PSH capacity under construction (149 GW).
      (Section 3.2)

U.S. HYDROPOWER REVENUE TRENDS

Amidst large price fluctuations in wholesale electricity markets in 2020–2024, federal hydropower
remained an affordable source of energy for the utilities that have access to it. (Section 4.1)

 • The average federal hydropower energy price in 2020–2024 ranged from $33.97/MWh in the
 Northwest to $44.64/MWh in the Southeast where federal hydropower is contracted as peaking
 power. (Section 4.1)

 • Due to drought conditions, average annual energy sold from the Western portion of the federal
 hydropower fleet in 2020–2024 was significantly lower than in the 2010s. For the rest of the federal
 fleet, annual energy sales in 2020–2024 were higher on average than in the 2010s. (Section 4.1)

Most U.S. hydropower owned by non-utilities (8% of electricity produced by the hydropower fleet
and 18% of electricity produced by the PSH fleet in 2025) is sold via power purchase agreements
(PPAs)—with utilities or other types of offtakers—or into organized wholesale markets managed
by an independent system operator (ISO) or a regional transmission organization (RTO).
(Section 4.2, Section 4.3)

 hydropower facilities selling energy into ISO/RTO markets ranged between $101/kW in ERCOT
 and MISO to $232/kW in NYISO. (Section 4.3)

U.S. HYDROPOWER AND PSH PERFORMANCE METRICS

In 2023–2025, average U.S. hydropower generation trended downwards (8% decrease relative to
the 2020–2022 average and 12% decrease relative to the 2010–2019 average). U.S. hydropower
generation is strongly influenced by hydrologic conditions in the Northwest region. (Section 5.3)

  • On average, the median capacity factor for U.S. hydropower plants in 2023–2025 (34.2%)
 decreased by one percentage point relative to 2020–2022 (35.5%). Average capacity factors (not
 adjusted by capacity) vary significantly across regions; they ranged from 26% in the Southeast to
    41% in the Midwest in 2023–2025. (Section 5.4)

  • In 2023–2025, hydropower imports from Canada decreased by 55% relative to the previous three
 years largely due to drought conditions. Two transmission lines starting operation in 2026 (with
 border crossings in Maine and New York and a combined capacity of 2,450 MW) strengthen U.S.
 capability to import Canadian hydropower. (Section 5.3)

U.S. PSH gross generation (i.e., without accounting for energy used in pumping) increased 12% in
2023–2025 relative to the 2020–2022 average and 11% relative to the 2010–2019 average, propelled
by higher output by the PSH fleets in the Northeast, Southeast, and Southwest. (Section 5.3)

  • Increases in average peak/off-peak price ratios in 2023–2025 relative to previous years can help
 explain the increase in PSH generation for facilities participating in several ISO/RTO markets.
    (Section 5.3)

In 2022–2024, the average availability factor for U.S. hydropower and PSH units was 78% for
small units (<= 10 MW), 83% for medium units (>10MW–100MW), and 79% for large units
(>100MW). (Section 5.5)

  • For large units, it has remained steady at ~80% since 2010. For small and medium units, it has
 trended slightly downwards (from 87% to 82% for medium units and from 84% to 75% for small
 units). (Section 5.5)

  • Hydropower availability factor in the Western Electricity Coordinating Council (WECC) has
 continued to be lower than in other North American Electric Reliability Corporation (NERC)
 regions in 2022–2024 (76% in WECC and greater than 80% in other NERC regional groupings).
    (Section 5.5)

 6

Hydropower and PSH units contribute to grid reliability by maintaining their highest availability
factors in the summer months (85% for hydropower units, 92% for PSH units) which is the peak
demand season in most U.S. markets.4 (Section 5.7)

 • Hydropower and PSH displayed consistent ability to increase generation (relative to average)
 during the top 100 net load hours in 2021–2025. The average generation increase was greater than
 20% in five of the ten balancing authorities (BAs) analyzed. BAs with high shares of peaking
 hydropower and PSH tend to show larger average increases. (Section 5.7)

U.S. HYDROPOWER AND PSH SUPPLY CHAIN

The U.S. hydropower supply chain includes 90 companies with domestic manufacturing locations
(147 manufacturing locations in 38 states).5 For large or heavy components, from castings
and forgings to turbine runners or generators, domestic manufacturing options are limited.
(Section 6.1)

 • The top ten states with the most manufacturing locations include the three states with the highest
 installed hydropower capacity (Washington, Oregon, and California) but also others like Texas,
 Ohio, and Illinois that have modest hydropower capacities but are large manufacturing hubs across
 industries. (Section 6.1)

 • In 2020–2025, an average of 15 turbine runners were installed in the United States per year.
 Twenty-seven of the 31 runners with capacities greater than 10 MW were supplied by five original
 equipment manufacturers (OEMs). The 58 turbine runners with capacities up to 10 MW installed in
 that same period were manufactured by at least 14 OEMs. (Section 6.2)

 • In the next decade, U.S. demand for hydropower plant components is expected to come primarily
 from modernization of the existing fleet which will require components for dozens of units greater
 than 100 MW—the ones with least domestic supply chain options. In contrast, for new facilities,
 the median capacity of proposed turbine-generator units is 2.4 MW. Of the 25 new units with
 capacities greater than 30 MW, all but one are for PSH projects. (Section 6.4)

In 2023–2025, U.S. exports of hydraulic turbines averaged $52 million and imports averaged $58
million. These values were 24% higher than the 2020–2022 average for exports and 31 % higher
for imports. However, the 2023–2025 average values remain significantly lower than in the 2010s
when both imports and exports averaged $77 million per year. (Section 6.3)

 • Eighty-seven percent of 2023–2025 turbine imports (by value) originated in Europe or the
 Americas. (Section 6.3)

 • The United States was the fifth largest exporter of hydraulic turbines globally in 2010–2024 (when
 taking all European Union (EU) countries as a single bloc). (Section 3.3)

POLICY DEVELOPMENTS

The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) is currently processing awards for hydropower projects
under three incentive programs, totaling $591 million, to modernize and expand the U.S.
hydropower fleet. (Chapter 7)

  • The two incentive programs directed towards capital improvements (Section 243 and Section 247)
 will support at least $1.8 billion in investments to improve efficiency, grid reliability, dam safety,
 and environmental performance of the hydropower fleet. (Chapter 7)

  • The third program (Section 242) is a production-based incentive available during the first ten years
 of operation to qualified facilities placed in service by the end of Fiscal Year 2027.

Hydropower and PSH facilities placed in service from 2025 to 2032 are eligible for tax credits.
(Chapter 7)

  • Hydropower facilities starting operation from 2025 to 2032 are eligible for the Clean Electricity
 Production Credit (Section 45Y) or Clean Electricity Investment Credit (Section 48E). New PSH
 facilities are also eligible for the Section 48E credit. Moreover, retrofitted hydropower facilities can
 be eligible for the Section 45Y or Section 48E credits if they meet the “80/20 rule.” Supply chain
 requirements affect both eligibility for and value of these credits. (Chapter 7)

In 2025–2026, FERC took steps to streamline multiple hydropower permitting processes, including
the issuance of preliminary permits, post-licensing activities involving maintenance and upgrades,
and license terminations. (Chapter 7)

The U.S. hydropower fleet includes baseload and peaking plants.
Mode of operation is an important attribute for understanding the role each hydropower plant plays
in the electric system. Within the U.S. hydropower fleet, 713 plants totaling 19.2 GW are run-of-
river plants that provide baseload power. These plants operate continuously for large stretches of time
(provided that adequate river inflow is available), have little to no storage capacity, and offer limited
flexibility to follow load. Their average capacity is 27 MW. At the other end of the spectrum of modes of
operation, 261 plants with combined capacity of 26.2 GW operate in peaking mode. These plants have
storage capacity and schedule their generation during periods of high demand and prices. Their average
capacity is 100 MW. PSH plants also are peaking plants and can either produce (in generation mode) or
consume (in pumping mode) power to balance supply and demand in the grid. They are the most flexible
hydropower assets. Many other hydropower plants have intermediate modes of operation between
peaking and run-of-river.

Research on hybrid configurations outlines multiple value propositions for different types of
hydropower plants; hydropower owners are starting to deploy these concepts.
Gorman et al. (2025) track operational and proposed hybrid power plant installations in the United
States.7 For run-of-river plants seeking to increase their flexibility and enhance their revenue, pairing
with a battery system can be an effective solution. Adding a battery to a run-of-river plant can increase
revenue by shifting a greater share of the energy generated to high-price hours and by enabling
participation in markets for grid services such as frequency regulation and black-start capability. As of
the end of 2024, eight hydropower-plus-battery hybrid projects were operational: three in Maine, two in
Alaska, two in Virginia, and one in New Hampshire. The eight hydropower plants that have been paired
with batteries range in capacities from 7.2 MW to 138 MW and operate predominantly in run-of-river
mode. The batteries added to these facilities ranged in capacity from 1 MW to 20 MW and had storage
durations of 1–2 hours. Three of these installations took place in 2023. One more is planned at a 6.7
MW hydropower plant in Massachusetts.

DOE has recently granted awards to two other hydropower hybridization projects involving the
installation of battery systems: one for the Forks of Butte hydropower facility (14.5 MW, run-of-
river plant in California) and another for Tuckertown (42 MW run-of-river plant in North Carolina).8
Even though most hydropower-battery hybrids in the United States to date have involved run-of-river
plants, value propositions from hybridization also exist for peaking plants (Durvasulu et al., 2024). For
example, a battery can reduce the number of starts and stops a peaking plant performs—by absorbing
energy during periods where the market does not need the hydropower output—reducing operations and
maintenance (O&M) costs and wear and tear of the turbine-generator units.9

Another hybrid hydropower configuration under investigation is floating photovoltaics (FPV), in which
solar panel arrays are installed on hydropower reservoirs. Rosenlieb et al. (2025) estimated a technical
potential of 861 GW–1042 GW for FPV on U.S. federal reservoirs. For hydropower plants, the benefits
of FPV installation include a reduction in evaporation of water stored in the reservoir and increases
in dissolved oxygen in the summer which can help comply with FERC license conditions. Gallego-
Calderon et al. (2026) find that the capital costs of FPV are generally not competitive with land-based
solar, but the business case varies geographically depending on solar irradiance and electricity prices.

With very fast deployment of short-duration battery storage in recent years, it becomes important to

At the end of 2025, the U.S. had 41 PSH plants with a combined nameplate generating capacity of 22.2
GW and 994 battery storage plants totaling 43.6 GW.10 Lithium-ion batteries accounted for 97% of the

Aside from its long-duration storage capabilities, PSH has some other important differences in lifecycle
performance with respect to batteries, such as longer operational life and better performance under
extreme temperatures. A PSH plant can operate for more than 100 years if properly maintained (e.g.,
Rocky River PSH has been in operation since 1928) while batteries have an expected lifetime of
approximately 20 years—influenced by the number and depth of charge/discharge cycles. Additionally,
ongoing research seeks to address some of the performance issues that batteries can experience under
extreme temperatures (Albeshr et al., 2023; Olis, 2021). Elevated temperatures accelerate capacity loss
and battery degradation over time. Cold temperatures can decrease energy storage capacity as well as
maximum charge and discharge rates. Approaches to mitigate these issues include the use of phase-
change materials and active temperature control systems.

The two panels in Figure 2 show where U.S. PSH and utility-scale battery storage are located and
when they were installed. More than 85% of U.S. PSH capacity started operation between 1960
and 1989. PSH was often developed to provide flexibility alongside baseload nuclear generation.
Five states (Pennsylvania, South Carolina, Georgia, Tennessee, and Michigan) are part of both the
top ten U.S. states by PSH capacity and by nuclear capacity. With its ability to quickly ramp output
up or down and either generate or consume energy, PSH can convert constant output from nuclear
plants into a daily generation profile that matches electricity demand more closely. In contrast, the
most common use of batteries is to convert the variable output from solar plants into a smoother
daily generation profile. Most batteries are in states with high penetration of solar generation.
Almost 40% of battery plants are part of hybrid solar-battery installations where batteries absorb
part of the solar output during late morning and early afternoon hours and release it in the evening
when net load (i.e., electricity demand minus solar and wind generation) and prices are higher.
Adding batteries to a solar project increases upfront capital costs, but it can improve project
economics by shifting generation to higher-price hours, reducing curtailment, increasing eligibility
for capacity revenues, and enabling participation in ancillary services markets.

1.1 NEW PROJECT DEVELOPMENT AND CAPACITY CHANGES (2010–2025)

U.S. hydropower fleet capacity increased by 2.1 GW from 2010 to 2025. This net change is a
combination of 1.9 GW added to the existing fleet, 0.7 GW of capacity from new projects, and 0.5
GW of capacity retirements. For PSH, net capacity additions to the existing fleet amounted to 1.7
GW from 2010 to 2025 all due to upgrades to plants already in operation in 2010.

Figure 3 shows the relative magnitudes (by capacity and number of plants) of the various activities
resulting in capacity changes, by region. Net capacity changes to the existing fleet (combination of
capacity additions and downrates) totaled 1.2 GW in the Northwest, 64 MW in the Southwest, 74 MW
in the Midwest, 259 MW in the Northeast, and 334 MW in the Southeast. Relative to their total installed
capacities in 2010, these changes represent increases of 3.4% for the Northwest (the region with the
most installed hydropower capacity), 3.1% for the Northeast, 2.3% for the Southeast, 1.3% for the
Midwest, and 0.4% for the Southwest.

Federal agencies own and operate almost 50% of the U.S. hydropower fleet, but the federal fleet
accounted for 22% of capacity additions. For investor-owned utilities, the shares of capacity additions
(18%) and installed capacity (19%) were very similar. The two categories of hydropower owners that
represent a higher share of capacity additions than installed capacity are publicly owned utilities (37% of
capacity additions versus 18% of installed capacity) and private non-utilities (21% of capacity additions
versus 8% of installed capacity).

Since 2010, 161 new facilities have been added to the U.S. hydropower fleet, with a combined capacity
of 712 MW. Their individual capacities ranged from less than 1 kW to 105 MW. These new facilities
include retrofits of 43 NPDs and 106 conduits with total capacities of 595 MW and 80 MW, respectively.
Conduit retrofits have predominantly taken place in the Southwest (50%) and Northwest (36%) because
these largely arid regions have a more developed water conveyance infrastructure network than the
rest of the country, built to support irrigation and connect major population centers with often distant
water sources. For NPDs, the Northeast accounts for 37% of projects and the Midwest for an additional
26%. The NPDs retrofitted in the Midwest had a larger average capacity (32 MW) than those in the
Northeast (4.6 MW). The remaining 12 new hydropower plants are NSDs, with a combined capacity of
36 MW, located at stream reaches without pre-existing dams. Eleven of the 12 NSD projects are in the
Northwest. The types and sizes of new projects added in 2010–2025 are similar to those of projects in
the development pipeline at the beginning of 2026 (see Chapter 2 for more details).

From 2010 to 2025, 139 hydropower plants with total capacity of 526 MW retired. Their capacities
ranged from 18 kW to 99 MW and their median capacity was 1 MW. The Northeast experienced the
highest number of retirements (26% of the U.S. total) and the Northwest had the most capacity retired
(36% of the U.S. total). Facilities that operate under a FERC license or exemption need to follow the
FERC license surrender process when they retire (see Section 1.4 for more details on the surrender
process and the attributes of plants that have retired and surrendered their authorization to generate
power in the past 15 years).

1.4 RELICENSES AND LICENSE SURRENDERS (2010–2025)

At the end of 2025, 200 hydropower and PSH projects (18.5 GW) accounting for 18% of U.S.
installed capacity were in the relicensing process. Forty projects totaling 65 MW were in the
process of surrendering their licenses.

1.4.1 Relicensing and License Surrender Pipeline

Relicensing pipeline
Forty-eight percent (47.8 GW) of U.S. hydropower capacity and 83% (18.4 GW) of U.S. PSH capacity
require FERC licenses to operate. FERC licenses need to be renewed periodically (license terms
are typically 30–50 years). The process that these projects follow to obtain a new license is called
relicensing. Project owners that decide not to pursue relicensing have two other options: applying for a
conversion from license to exemption (if eligible) or surrendering the license. For licensees that fail to
take any action and are in violation of their license conditions, FERC can initiate a license termination
proceeding. Both license surrenders and terminations result in projects no longer being authorized to
generate hydropower.

License surrender pipeline
Figure 8 also contains information about license surrenders. Licensees for 31 projects adding up to
53.4 MW have pending surrender license applications. Another nine licensees have submitted NOIs to
surrender their licenses, with combined capacity of 11.4 MW. The median capacity of the 40 projects

with capacity greater than 10 MW seeking license surrender. Its license is being surrendered because the
Borel Canal, required to operate the power plant, will be decommissioned as part of USACE’s Isabella
Dam modifications to meet current seismic standards.

The Northeast has the highest number of projects in the license surrender process (19 with a total
capacity of 11.8 MW) and the Southwest has the most capacity being surrendered (33 MW distributed
across 10 projects). Although license surrenders can be submitted at any point, FERC’s relicensing
schedule presents owners with a key decision point to evaluate whether continued operation of the
project is beneficial. Not only is relicensing a costly process but meeting the conditions of a new license
may require significant capital investments and changes in operations that, in some cases, render project
operation not profitable.

Along with the relicenses and surrenders, six projects in Western states (Oregon, Utah, Idaho, and
California) ranging in capacity from 0.115 MW to 5 MW are pursuing conversions from licenses to
exemptions. For projects that are eligible, converting from a license to an exemption is an attractive
option because exemptions are issued in perpetuity, reducing future regulatory burden.

 CHARACTERISTICS OF SMALL HYDROPOWER PLANTS WITH SURRENDERED
     LICENSES IN 2010–2025

 Ninety-six percent of license surrenders issued by FERC from 2010 to 2025 were for small
 hydropower plants (<= 10 MW). An analysis of the characteristics of small hydropower plants
 whose licenses have been recently surrendered can provide insights as to which other plants with
 active licenses may be considered for surrender in the future.

 • Almost 75% of the small hydropower plants with surrendered licenses had capacities below 1
 MW.

 • Five states (California, Massachusetts, Maine, North Carolina, and Washington) accounted for
 46% of the surrenders. Except for California, the share of surrenders these states represent is
 greater than their share of the U.S. small hydropower fleet.

 • Economic infeasibility is the most frequently stated reason for surrendering a small
 hydropower license. However, electricity price levels do not show a strong relationship with
 surrendered licenses in this dataset. States with higher end-use electricity price (average
 across all sectors) than the U.S. average accounted for 54% of surrenders. The top three
 states by number of surrenders (California, Massachusetts, and Maine) all rank among the ten
 states with highest electricity prices. Low electricity prices may increase likelihood of license
 surrender when the reason is economic infeasibility of continued operation of an active
 plant. However, other financial reasons cited for surrenders are independent from electricity
 price levels such as 1) the cost of restoring to operation a plant that has sustained damage
 (many plants surrendering their license have not generated any power for multiple years),
 2) expected costs of an upcoming relicensing process, 3) a bankruptcy filing by the plant
 owner.18

 • Most surrendered licenses were held by private non-utilities (62%) or publicly owned utilities
 such as municipalities (33%). Within these ownership categories, many of the plant owners
 surrendering their licenses only own one hydropower project and they may not have the
 expertise or resources necessary to optimize project operation or fund the capital investments
 needed to maintain it.

 • Surrendered projects span a wide range of ages. Although commissioning years are available
 for only 39 of the 103 surrendered projects, the partial data offer no evidence that the oldest
 projects are most likely to be surrendered. Commissioning years ranged from 1907 to 2014
 (median commissioning year of surrendered projects was 1983).

1.4.3 Relicensing and License Surrender Process Durations

The median duration of the relicensing process for 165 relicenses issued from 2010 to 2025 was
six years. Typical durations were slightly longer for Large (>100MW–500MW) than Small (<= 10
MW) projects. Relicensing duration was less variable and typically shorter for projects that used
the ILP versus other process modalities.

Relicensing process duration
The relicensing process has two stages. The first, pre-filing stage, extends from the time the project
owner submit a NOI and Pre Application Document (PAD) to the time they submit the Final License
Application (FLA). The second, post-filing stage, includes all the activities that take place from FLA
submission to relicense issuance. Notable milestones during post-filing include activities led by
FERC (review of the application and preparation of the environmental document for compliance with
the National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA)), consultation with state agencies regarding license
conditions over which they have jurisdiction (e.g., fish passage), and activities led by the licensee such
as obtaining a water quality certification from the relevant state agency or completing additional studies
if required by FERC.

For the 165 projects issued a relicense between 2010 and 2025, the median duration of the pre‑filing
stage is three years. Only 10% of projects have pre‑filing stages longer than 3.5 years. The narrow range
for the duration of the pre-filing stage is driven by FERC’s schedule since the NOI must be filed by 5
years before license expiration date and the license application must be filed by two years before the
expiration date. The median duration of the post-filing stage is 3.1 years (average 3.88 years) with a
range from 1.1 years to 18.9 years. The 10% of projects with the longest relicensing durations spent at
least 7.8 years in the post-filing stage.

FERC offers three licensing processes: ILP, Traditional Licensing Process (TLP), or Alternative
Licensing Process (ALP).19 The ILP became the default in 2005, but developers can request the use of
one of the other options when they submit the NOI to file a license application. The ILP follows a more
structured schedule than the other two. It also involves greater FERC engagement during the pre‑filing
stage to identify issues early and determine which studies are needed, helping avoid information gaps
that could otherwise cause delays during the post-filing stage.

The median duration of the relicensing process for the 73 licensees that used the default ILP process was
5.9 years, 6.2 years for the 89 licensees that selected the TLP, and 8.4 years for the three instances of
the ALP. The median duration of the post-filing stage is 2.7 years for the relicenses processed using the
ILP, 3.3 years for the TLP, and 4.7 years for the ALP. The median duration of the pre-filing stage is the
same across all three processes (3.0 years). Thus, for relicensing, the ILP meets its purpose of making
post-filing stage duration more predictable by imposing a stricter schedule and more FERC participation
in the pre-filing stage. Large and Very Large projects as well as projects owned and operated by investor-
owned utilities used the ILP more frequently than the other two processes.

issuance) among 77 surrenders issued from 2010 to 2025 was one year. Durations were typically
longer for projects in the Southwest (2.2 years) and Northwest (1.9 years) as well as those that
involve dam removal (1.6 years).

As part of a surrender application, the licensee typically must include 1) a decommissioning plan
describing whether the project facilities will be removed, modified, or left in place, 2) a report detailing

a schedule to complete the proposed decommissioning and mitigation actions, 4) copies of consultations
with relevant federal and state agencies. The decommissioning and mitigation actions listed in the
application are indicative of the complexity of the process and influence its duration. For example,
the shortest surrender process captured in Figure 12 took 63 days. It was an implied surrender for the

the surrender application. The application did not include a decommissioning plan and FERC determined
that no environmental analysis was needed because the plan was to leave the project facilities in place.

project facilities (including multiple diversion dams) and requiring environmental analysis by FERC.
Local opposition to removal of dams connected to this project, used for recreation, contributed to the
long process. FERC issued the license surrender in 2024.

pipeline and the proposed capacity from NPD projects surpasses 100 MW in seven states (Alabama,
Arkansas, Illinois, Kentucky, Louisiana, Pennsylvania, West Virginia). Federal agencies own 67 of the
dams being considered for NPD projects (66 by USACE and one by the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation
(BOR)); the remaining dams are owned by state agencies (nine) and by municipalities, electric utilities,
or private entities. Importantly, permitting processes vary depending on who owns the dam. For instance,
NPD projects on dams owned by USACE require a second federal permit (Section 408 permit from
USACE), in addition to a FERC license.

At the beginning of 2026, eight NPD projects—all of them located in Pennsylvania, West Virginia or
Ohio—had Section 408 permits under USACE review. Five of them are projects that already have a
FERC license and the other three have submitted their license applications, but they are pending a final
decision from FERC.

The Marseilles Lock & Dam (10.26 MW in Illinois) and Heidelberg (2.64 MW in Kentucky) NPD
projects are under construction. Thirty-three other NPDs, with a total capacity of 448 MW, already
have a federal authorization (FERC license, FERC exemption from licensing, or the U.S. BOR Lease
of Power Privilege (LOPP)) and six more (74 MW) have pending license applications under review by
FERC. The remaining 51 projects are at earlier development stages, during which feasibility is being
assessed and final license, exemption, or LOPP applications are being prepared.

Conduit projects
Twenty-seven of the projects shown in Figure 13 would add electricity generation equipment to existing
conduits.20 Their combined proposed capacity is 73 MW. The median proposed capacity at these sites
is 0.056 MW. Except for a project in Pennsylvania, all proposed conduit retrofit sites are in Western
states, including 14 in California. Two of the California projects account for 84% of proposed capacity
at all conduit sites in the development pipeline. Those two projects are being pursued by the Sites
Project Authority whose primary goal is building an off-stream reservoir to store excess water during
major storms and release it during dry periods. As water is released, it would pass through the proposed
hydropower turbines and generate electricity helping to recover some of the energy that will be needed
for pumping water into the reservoir.

Four conduit projects (all with capacities of 1 MW or less) are under construction: two in Colorado,
one in California, and one in Nevada. Of the remaining 23 conduit projects, 21 already have FERC
authorization and the other two have a pending FERC license or exemption in process.

Conduits are the only project category in the U.S. hydropower development pipeline for which most
developers are publicly-owned utilities. Municipalities or political subdivisions (e.g., irrigation districts)
own much of the U.S. conduit infrastructure and are the developers for 18 of the 27 conduit projects
shown in Figure 13. For NPDs and NSDs, most developers are private non-utilities.

projects that use an existing lower reservoir, environmental impacts of construction may be as low, if
not lower than closed-loop (DOE, 2020). All the projects, whether greenfield developments or capacity
additions, proposed by entities that already own and operate PSH capacity, including TVA, Salt River
Project, Duke Energy, and Pacific Gas & Electric are among the 14 open-loop projects in the pipeline.

of the permitting process. A preliminary permit grants the project developer priority for developing a
particular site during a four-year period. During that time, the developer conducts studies to assess the
technical and economic feasibility of the project and periodically communicates its progress to FERC.
The outcome from those studies informs the decision on whether to submit an authorization application
(FERC license or FERC exemption). Project attrition rate at the preliminary permit stage is very high.

For conduit retrofits, many projects can apply to FERC for qualifying conduit status and do not require a
FERC license or exemption.25 In that case, the first step is requesting qualifying conduit determination—a
step captured under the Authorization Applications category in Figure 16. FERC’s evaluation of whether
a conduit can receive “qualifying conduit” determination has a much quicker turnaround than the
authorization process for other types of projects. Figure 17 summarizes permitting timelines for projects

 CHAPTER 3. U.S. HYDROPOWER AND PSH IN THE GLOBAL CONTEXT

The United States has the fourth largest hydropower fleet and third largest PSH fleet (by individual
country) in the world. This chapter provides summary statistics for the global operational fleet and the
global development pipeline. It also discusses the evolution of the generation mix from 2016 to 2025
in the countries or regions with the largest hydropower fleets. Furthermore, it describes trends in global
hydraulic turbine trade.

For the United States, operational plant data are from ORNL EHA Plant Database 2026 and project
pipeline data are from U.S. Hydropower Development Pipeline Data, 2026. For the rest of the world,
plant-level data for operational facilities and project-level data for the development pipeline are from a
commercial database (GlobalData).27

3.1 DESCRIPTION OF EXISTING FLEETS

Global hydropower capacity (including capacity from hybrid plants that contain a mixture of
hydropower and PSH units) reached 1,296 GW in 2025. Additionally, the global PSH fleet had
a capacity of 160 GW. Together, hydropower and PSH represented 14% of global generation
capacity in 2025 and 15% of electricity generated in 2024. Over the past decade, the average
annual global growth rate was 1.4% for hydropower and 3.6% for PSH; total electricity
generation capacity grew at an average 5.2% per year.

The share of variable renewables (wind and solar) increased from 2016 to 2025 for all the countries/
regions in Figure 19 and this increase has implications for hydropower and PSH. First, high shares of
variable renewables may increase the flexibility demands on hydropower by requiring more ramping up
and down to maintain supply-demand balance in the grid as wind and solar output fluctuate throughout
the day. As of 2025, the variable renewable share in the global electricity generation capacity was 35%
worldwide. For the six countries/regions in Figure 19, it ranged from 15% in Canada to 47% in China
and 49% in the European Union. It was 28% in the United States. Although national aggregates are not
ideal because each of these countries or regions contain areas with much higher penetrations than the
average, U.S. hydropower owners and operators may gather valuable information from case studies

ROLE OF HYDROPOWER AND PSH DURING IBERIAN PENINSULA 2025 BLACKOUT

Shortly after 12:30 pm on April 28, 2025, the power grids of Spain and Portugal experienced a
total blackout. The factual report published by the European Network of Transmission System
Operators for Electricity (ENTSO-E) provides a detailed sequence of events that reveals the
important role played by hydropower and PSH in trying to prevent the collapse of the grid and in
the power restoration phase (ENTSO-E, 2025). The root cause of the event has been determined
as a “rapid rise in system voltage and loss of voltage control on a day with multiple concurrent
phenomena, accelerated by rapid generation output reductions and disconnections, leading to
voltage instability and cascading disconnections in Spain” (ENTSO-E, 2026). At the time the
event started, solar and wind plants were generating 63% of electricity in Spain and the number of
conventional units (>30 MW) synchronized to the Spanish network was the lowest for that hour
of the day up to that day in 2025. The event began as a net load increase at the distribution level,
following voltage oscillations earlier that morning. This increase was likely caused either by the
disconnection of small generators and/or by an actual rise in load. As grid frequency started to
drop, PSH units—which were operating in pumping mode at that time of day—were disconnected
to offset the lost generation. Nonetheless, more generators tripped offline, the interconnections
with Morocco and France tripped due to underfrequency, and the Spain and Portugal grids went
into total blackout. The restoration process involved using voltage from neighboring healthy parts
of the network (Morocco and France) and re-energization of plants with black start capabilities.34
Figure 22 compares the average hourly generation mix in Spain from April 1 to April 27, 2025
with the mix in April 28, before and after the outage. It illustrates that hydropower and PSH, along
with combined cycle gas plants, accounted for most of the output during the restoration phase. It
provides an example of the combination of hydropower attributes (black start capabilities, rapid
start-up times, dispatchability, spinning masses that contribute to stabilizing grid frequency) that
makes it especially valuable for grid resilience. In days with normal system conditions, solar, wind,
and nuclear all account for sizable portions of total generation in Spain. However, neither nuclear
nor variable renewables contributed to grid restoration throughout the afternoon and evening of
April 28. The system operator did not allow wind and solar plants to start reconnecting until the
grid was sufficiently stabilized (1:00 am on April 29). Similarly, nuclear plants have long and
complex start-up processes and could not be brought back in until the system was restored.

 CHAPTER 4. U.S. HYDROPOWER PRICES AND REVENUES
Multiple approaches are used to market the energy, capacity, and ancillary services provided by U.S>
hydropower and PSH plants.46 This chapter summarizes trends in energy and capacity revenues,
which are typically the two largest revenue streams for hydropower and PSH plants. Power marketing
administrations (PMAs) sell capacity and/or energy from the federal fleet, which accounts for almost 50%
of installed U.S. hydropower capacity, via long-term contracts signed largely with publicly owned utilities
(Section 4.1). Nonfederal fleet owners sell the energy output and other services from their plants through
a mixture of bilateral transactions (among plants owned by utilities), PPAs (Section 4.2), and participation
in wholesale markets managed by ISOs/RTOs (Section 4.3).

4.1 FEDERAL HYDROPOWER REVENUE

Amidst large price fluctuations in wholesale electricity markets in 2020–2024, federal hydropower
remained an affordable source of energy for the utilities that have access to it. The price advantage
(lower and more stable price levels) of federal hydropower versus wholesale electricity markets was
salient in recent years. Due to drought conditions, average annual energy sold from the Western
portion of the federal hydropower fleet in 2020–2024 was significantly lower than in the 2010s. For the
rest of the federal fleet, annual energy sales in 2020–2024 were higher on average than in the 2010s.

The PMAs are the federal agencies, within the U.S. DOE, in charge of marketing the electrical output from
the 132 federal hydropower plants owned and operated by the USACE, the U.S. BOR, or the International
Boundary and Water Commission (IBWC). Figure 30 shows the location of federal hydropower facilities
marketed by PMAs and the regions served by each of the four PMAs: Bonneville Power Administration
(BPA), Western Area Power Administration (WAPA), Southwestern Power Administration (SWPA), and
Southeastern Power Administration (SEPA).

By statute, PMAs market capacity and energy from the federal fleet at cost-based rates giving priority

rates charged to customers include adders that are applied in dry years when PMA must complement federal
hydropower generation with (typically more expensive) purchased power. In contrast, in years when the
federal hydropower fleet generates more than is needed to meet preference customer contract requirements,
PMAs sell the surplus power to their preference customers or in the wholesale market.

4.2 HYDROPOWER POWER PURCHASE AGREEMENTS

On average, the median energy price for hydropower PPAs in 2021–2024 ranged from $53/
MWh in the Southeast to $63/MWh in the Northwest. Selling energy through PPAs has typically
provided hydropower owners a more stable energy revenue in 2010–2024 than selling into the
wholesale market in the Northwest and Southeast. For hydropower PPAs in the Southwest,
Midwest, and Northeast, the median PPA price followed the wholesale price more closely. The
number of PPAs between hydropower owners and utilities that can be identified from mandatory
reporting by utilities to FERC has decreased by ~30% from 2010 to 2024.

Large utilities must report their purchased power volumes and expenditures (energy and capacity
components) as part of FERC Form 1. From that information, it is possible to compute the average
prices of purchased power. The capacity component of the price in a PPA is a fixed charge ($/kilowatt)
for making capacity available for generation over a certain period. The energy component is a variable
charge per kilowatt-hour generated.

For each transaction, FERC Form 1 contains information about the identity of the purchasing utility
and the entity that is selling the power. Only in cases where the name of the seller corresponds to a
specific hydropower plant or the seller only owns hydropower plants is it possible to classify a PPA as a
hydropower PPA. Based on those criteria, for purchased power transactions in 2010–2024, 617 unique
buyer-seller pairs were identified. Twenty-one percent of them reported transactions every year during
that period. The rest of PPAs either started or ended within that period. On average, the energy purchased
through transactions in the hydropower PPAs in this dataset accounted for 6% of U.S. hydropower
generation. Figure 32 compares the annual median price of hydropower PPAs to the median price across
all FERC Form 1 purchased power transactions.55 The left panel includes only the energy component of
the purchased power price and the right panel includes both the energy and capacity components.

The Northwest displays the most stable PPA price distributions with both the median and 10th and 90th
percentiles staying at similar levels in 2010–2024. Although the Northwest is also the region with the
highest share of long-term (five years or longer) PPAs (72%), the long-term fraction of PPAs is similar
for the Southwest (68%) and the Southeast (69%) whose median prices have been more variable over
time. Therefore, the stable media prices in the Northwest points to fixed price PPAs being more common
there than in other regions.

Most of the PPAs in the Southwest resulting in very low values for the 10th percentile of hydropower
PPA energy prices in that region ($5.99/MWh in 2014, $10.71/MWh in 2016, and $5.17/MWh in 2023)
are PPAs where the capacity component accounted for most of the revenue. When considering both the
capacity and energy components of prices, the 10th percentile of the price distribution for the Southwest
is not below $24/MWh in any year from 2010 to 2024.

The Northeast had been the only region where the 90th percentile energy price was greater than $150/
MWh in most years. The abrupt drop of the 90th percentile hydropower PPA energy price in the
Northeast to $92/MWh in 2024 can be traced back to renegotiation of a handful of long-term PPAs.
For those few PPAs (between Niagara Mohawk Power Corporation and multiple hydropower plants in
New York), the average energy prices had ranged from $150/MWh to $200/MWh (with zero capacity
payments) until 2023. In 2024, the structure of payments under those PPAs changed to average energy
prices in the $32/MWh–$34/MWh complemented with a capacity component of $2/MWh–$4/MWh.

The number of hydropower PPAs identifiable from the FERC Form 1 dataset has decreased over
time: from a maximum of 395 in 2012 to a minimum of 256 in 2024. The decrease is observable in
every region, but it is especially pronounced for the Southwest (from 74 PPAs in 2012 to 25 in 2024)
and Northeast (from 120 PPAs in 2011 to 59 in 2024) regions. This decrease suggests that either the
purchasing utilities are turning to alternative arrangements to procure the energy and capacity they
need or hydropower owners are opting for non-utility counterparties or direct participation in ISO/RTO
markets to market the energy and grid services from their facilities.

Because only utilities report to FERC Form 1, this dataset does not capture PPAs where the buyer is a
corporate entity. No public data are available to summarize price trends in the rapidly growing segment
of corporate PPAs. Corporate PPAs had initially focused on acquiring energy from new wind and solar
capacity. However, technology companies are starting to seek more baseload, dispatchable capacity like
that provided by hydropower to their portfolios in order to more closely match renewable energy use and
renewable energy generation in their daily operations. Recent key milestones for corporate PPAs where
the seller is a U.S. hydropower owner include:

4.3.2 Capacity Revenue

Capacity revenue can be a large share of total revenue for hydropower and PSH plants. It varies
substantially year to year depending on the capacity price, which reflects the surplus or deficit of
capacity to meet projected load in each region. Capacity revenue depends also on which fraction
of nameplate capacity can be shown to be consistently available, especially during hours with the
highest likelihood of supply shortages.

Resource adequacy is the process by which electricity planners ensure enough generation capacity is
available in the system to meet projected peak demand. That process is managed differently across the
seven ISO/RTOs. Four ISO/RTOs (MISO, ISO-NE, NYISO, and PJM) conduct capacity auctions. The
price at which the auction clears is the capacity payment that power plant owners will receive per unit
of accredited capacity. In CAISO and SPP, load-serving entities (i.e., utilities) must procure adequate
capacity to meet reliability requirements and do so primarily through their own generation resources and
bilateral trades. In Texas, ERCOT publishes multiple reports with information regarding projected load
and generation capacity. Market participants make investment decisions based on that information as
well as the expected revenues that could be achieved in that market.

5.2 O&M COSTS (2010–2024)

Hydropower O&M costs exhibit significant economies of scale (i.e., costs decrease on a per-kW
basis as project size increases). Average 2021–2024 hydropower O&M costs in 2024 dollars ranged
from $19/kW for very large plants (>500 MW) to $200/kW for small (<= 10 MW) hydropower
plants. These inflation-adjusted averages were lower than the 2010–2020 annual averages for all
size categories. For small hydropower plants, average wholesale electricity prices were lower than
O&M costs per kilowatt-hour for some combinations of regions and years suggesting it may be
challenging for them to operate profitably.

Figure 40 shows the trend in average O&M cost per kilowatt of capacity (in 2024 dollars) for utility-

relative to the annual 2010–2020 averages for all size categories: 7% decrease for small plants, 20%
decrease for medium plants, 33% decrease for large plants, and 12% decrease for very large plants. The
decrease could be partly due to increased operational efficiencies, but it also likely shows that O&M
budgets have not been keeping pace with inflation.

5.3 ENERGY GENERATION (2010–2025)

In 2023–2025, average U.S. hydropower generation trended downwards (8% decrease relative to
the 2020–2022 average and 12% decrease relative to the 2010–2019 average). U.S. hydropower
generation is strongly influenced by hydrologic conditions in the Northwest region. In contrast,
U.S. PSH generation increased during the most recent years (12% increase in 2023–2025 relative
to the 2020–2022 average, 11% relative to the 2010–2019 average), propelled by higher output by
the PSH fleets in the Northeast, Southeast, and Southwest.

The U.S. hydropower fleet generated an average of 245 TWh per year in 2023–2025. This value is 8%
lower than the 2020–2022 average and 12% lower than the average during the 2010s. Figure 43 shows
the trends in regional domestic hydropower generation and Canadian imports for 2010–2025. Except
for the Southwest—where generation in 2023–2025 was 41% higher than in 2020–2022—all other
series in Figure 43 decline from 2020–2022 to 2023–2025. For the Midwest, Northeast, and Southeast,
the decrease in 2023–2025 relative to the three previous years is larger than the decrease relative to the
2010s.

the snowpack too rapidly and affected volume of water available for the summer months. In California,
where two thirds of hydropower generation capacity in the Southwest region is located, snowpack in
2023 was the highest since the mid-1980s leading to a large increase in output from the hydropower
fleet. Severe drought conditions in the Pacific Northwest in 2024 kept hydropower generation levels
approximately flat relative to 2023. Meanwhile, generation in the Southwest decreased slightly in 2024
and 2025 without going back to the lower levels of the early 2020s. Nationally, hydropower generation

Recently completed and ongoing transmission line projects will strengthen the capabilities to import
Canadian hydropower in future years. In January 2026, the New England Clean Energy Connect

Northeast via Maine. A second transmission line (Champlain Hudson Power Express) crossing the
Canada-U.S. border carrying power from Hydro-Québec into New York was expected to start operating
in May 2026. The NYISO highlighted the importance of the Champlain Hudson Power Express (1,250
MW of transfer capability) to maintain adequate reliability margins in its system in the next five
years.64 Also in May 2026, the U.S. DOE issued a presidential permit authorizing Basin Electric Power
Cooperative to build two new transmission lines (650 MW of transfer capability) between North Dakota
and the Canadian border. When constructed, this transmission line will carry electricity produced by the
Manitoba Hydro fleet into the Midwest.

and Southeast, average capacity factor is lower and its range wider (from 20% to 40%). For the 2023–

three percentage points higher than for peaking facilities, but the difference has narrowed further from
2018 and was less than one percentage point in multiple years since then. If the 90th percentiles are
compared instead of the average, they are on average six percentage points higher for run-of-river plants

5.5 AVAILABILITY FACTORS (2010–2024)

In 2022–2024, the availability factor for U.S. hydropower units was 78% for small units, 83%
for medium units, and 79% for large units. For large units, it has remained steady at ~80%
since 2010. For small and medium units, it has trended slightly downwards (from 87% to 82%
for medium units and from 84% to 75% for small units). Availability factor has continued to be
lower in WECC than in other regions. Both hydropower and PSH units have their highest average
availability factor in the summer months (85% for hydropower units, 92% for PSH units).

For dispatchable electricity generation assets such as hydropower and PSH plants, availability factor
(i.e., the percentage of hours when its turbine-generator units are available to generate power) is an
important performance metric.71 When available, a hydropower unit may be synchronized to the grid
generating power (unit service hours), synchronized to the grid but not generating power (condensing
hours), or ready to synchronize to the grid if the system operator sends a dispatch order (reserve
shutdown hours). Outages that cause units to be unavailable can be either planned or forced. Data on the
fraction of hours that hydropower units spend in each of the available and unavailable operational modes
provides a rich picture of hydropower operations that is an important complement to the generation and
capacity factor data presented in Section 5.2 and Section 5.3.

Unlike generation data, submitted by all electricity generation plants with nameplate capacity greater than 1
megawatt to EIA at least annually and published along with plant details, data on availability factors or the
breakdown of available and unavailable hours into finer subcategories are much more limited. Quarterly
submissions on performance metrics to NERC were voluntary until 2011. They became mandatory for
nuclear, hydropower, and fossil fuel units with nameplate capacities >= 50 MW in 2012 and the mandatory

5.7 RELIABILITY AND FLEXIBILITY CONTRIBUTIONS OF HYDROPOWER AND PSH TO
THE ELECTRIC SYSTEM

As discussed in Chapter 1, the mode of operation of U.S. hydropower plants varies widely, from
baseload generation to peaking plants. In aggregate, the U.S. hydropower and PSH fleets contribute both
reliability and flexibility to the U.S. electric grid.

Reliability contribution of U.S. hydropower and PSH
With higher availability factors than the annual average during peak demand seasons,
hydropower and PSH demonstrate important contribution to grid reliability. In a set of ten U.S.
BAs, hydropower and PSH displayed consistent ability to increase generation (relative to average)
during the top 100 net load hours in 2021–2025. The average generation increase was greater than
20% in five of the ten BAs. BAs with high shares of peaking hydropower and PSH tend to show
larger average increases.

To ensure that generation capacity is adequate to meet expected load forecasts, system operators need
to translate nameplate capacity values into expected capacity available during the hours of the year
when demand is expected to peak. The metric that summarizes the contribution of a given generating
resource during peak hours is its effective load carrying capability (ELCC). ELCC is increasingly used
in resource adequacy planning and to allocate capacity revenue in those ISO/RTOs that have capacity
markets.

to increase its generation during the highest net load hours relative to the average for that year-season-
hour combination. The average response was greater than 20% for five of the ten BAs; it ranged from
7% in WACM/WALC to 62% in ERCOT.

The generation responses summarized in Figure 52 combine reliability and flexibility attributes of each
of the fleets. For a hydropower unit to increase its output at specific hours, it needs to be available and
operating at less than its nameplate capacity. Fleets with a high share of hydropower plants that operate
in peaking mode (as well as PSH) are likely to be able to increase their production the most relative
to average values because those types of plants tend to operate at lower capacity factors than run-of-
river plants during normal system conditions. For instance, 60% of hydropower in TVA operates in
peaking mode and the TVA BA also has a large PSH facility (Raccoon Mountain, 1.7 GW). The average
generation of the TVA fleet was 59% higher in the top 100 net load hours than the average for the
same year, season, and hour of the day. In contrast, BPA has a very large fleet, but it operates more as a
baseload resource under average conditions. Less than half (44%) of the BPA fleet operates in peaking
or intermediate peaking mode and PSH capacity is very small (0.3 GW).78 The average hydropower
generation during the top 100 net load hours in the BPA BA was 17% higher than the average for the
same year, season, and hour of the day. In the Southwest, WAPA markets hydropower from Hoover Dam
(in WALC) and Glen Canyon (in WACM)—large plants that operate in intermediate peaking mode—
among other hydropower plants. Even though this region of the country has experienced severe drought
conditions during 2021–2025, these facilities still managed to increase their output during top net load
hours in response to customer demand. The average increase in generation by the fleet in the combined
WACM/WALC BA over the 100 top net load hours was 7%. In the ISONE BA, three PSH plants with
combined capacity of 1.9 GW are a key factor for the large average increase in hydropower generation
during top net load hours (58%). The BA with the largest PSH fleet is PJM but its average increase in
hydropower generation during top net load hours is among the lowest in Figure 52 (18%). One factor
explaining this result is that PSH plants in the PJM BA operate at a higher capacity factor than those in
ISONE or TVA. If their utilization on a typical day is higher, they have less room to respond during top
net load hours.

When calculating generation volume changes (instead of percentage changes) during the top net load
hours, the largest average increase is for the BPA BA (1,403 MW) followed by TVA (1,370 MW) and
CAISO (1,113 MW). ERCOT has the smallest hydropower capacity installed out of the ten BAs shown
in Figure 52 (20 plants with combined capacity of 562 MW). Although hydropower generation in
ERCOT during some of the top net load hours was more than 200% larger than average, it is not a very
large generation increase by volume. The largest increase by the ERCOT fleet was 243 MW and the
average change was 55 MW.

 CHAPTER 6. TRENDS IN U.S. HYDROPOWER SUPPLY CHAIN

This chapter describes the manufacturing capabilities of the U.S. hydropower supply chain and identifies
components for which domestic procurement options are very limited (Section 6.1). It summarizes
recent turbine runner installation activity (Section 6.2) and trends in U.S. hydraulic turbine imports
and exports (Section 6.3). Finally, it offers estimates of potential turbine and generator unit demand for
modernization of existing facilities and construction of new facilities over the next decade (Section 6.4).

6.1 U.S. HYDROPOWER SUPPLY CHAIN MANUFACTURING CAPABILITIES

The U.S. hydropower supply chain includes 90 companies with domestic manufacturing locations
(147 manufacturing locations in 38 states). For large or heavy components, from castings and
forgings to turbine runners or generators, domestic manufacturing options are limited.

Disruptions to manufacturing and logistics during the COVID-19 pandemic in the early 2020s
underscored the need for improved data to better characterize the U.S. hydropower supply chain. First,
project developers and facility owners lack a comprehensive, up-to-date database of U.S. hydropower
manufacturing capabilities to inform procurement decisions. Second, greater visibility into the expected
demand for major plant components would benefit OEMs in evaluating capacity expansion and
manufacturing reshoring decisions. This section summarizes DOE activities that help address data needs
identified in recent, in-depth assessments of the hydropower supply chain (Putsche et al., 2024; Uría-
Martínez et al., 2022).

Manufacturing capabilities is a topic ORNL sought to address by developing a dataset of companies
with domestic manufacturing facilities for production of the following hydropower plant components:
castings/forgings, turbines, governors, bearings, generators, exciters, gates, penstocks, valves, trashracks,
cranes/hoists, switchgears, and transformers. Figure 54 summarizes the information in the dataset.

6.2 U.S. HYDROPOWER AND PSH TURBINE INSTALLATIONS

On average, 15 turbine runners were installed in the United States per year in 2020–2025. This
number is approximately half the 2010–2019 average and the drop is larger for turbine runners
in units with capacities greater than 10 MW (16 per year in the 2010s versus five per year in
2020–2025). Of the 31 units with capacities greater than 10 MW installed in 2020–2025, 27 were
supplied by five OEMs. The 58 units with capacities up to 10 MW installed in that same period
were manufactured by at least 14 OEMs.

Figure 55 summarizes the trend in annual number of turbine runner installations in 2010–2025. Relative to
the version of the plot in the 2023 edition of this report, it contains revisions to 2020–2022 data and three
new years of data (2023–2025). Figure 55 shows a drop in installations in the 2020s. The annual average
number of turbines installed in 2020–2025 (15) was almost 50% lower than for 2010–2019 (29). Average
annual capacity of those new runners was 1.2 GW in the 2010s versus 0.3 GW in 2020–2025. Since the
decrease in capacity is larger than the decrease in number of units, the reduction in activity has been more
concentrated in runners for larger turbine-generator units (an average of five turbine runners with capacities
greater than 10 MW installed per year in 2020–2025 versus 16 in 2010–2019).

The weak installation activity levels in 2023–2025 follow with a lag the decrease in R&U investment
in 2020–2022 discussed in the 2023 edition of this report. That drop resulted, at least partly, from the
supply chain challenges that arose during the COVID-19 pandemic. However, the drop observed in
Figure 55 may also partly be a result of incomplete reporting. Hydropower plant owners are increasingly
treating information about procurement decisions as strategic and signing confidentiality agreements
with OEMs requiring them not to share project-level information.

The data in Figure 55 confirm that the number of OEMs procuring larger turbine runners is much lower
than for smaller units. Of the 31 units with capacities greater than 10 MW installed in 2020–2025, 27
were supplied by one of the five individually-listed OEMs: American Hydro, Andritz, GE Vernova,
Toshiba, and Voith.82 For the 58 units with capacities up to 10 MW installed in that same period, nine
were supplied by one of the five OEMs, and the rest were manufactured by at least nine other OEMs or
its manufacturer was unknown.

6.4 U.S. HYDROPOWER SUPPLY CHAIN: POTENTIAL DEMAND FOR KEY COMPONENTS

In the next decade, most of the demand for U.S. hydropower plant components is expected
to come from modernization of the existing fleet. If all modernization projects with planned
completion dates in 2026–2035 move forward, they will require procurement for turbine-generator
components for 313 units. The median capacity of these units is 45MW and 48 of them have
capacities greater than 100 MW. Additionally, if all the new projects that have authorization
applications (to FERC or U.S. BOR) under review, or already issued, move forward to
construction in the next decade, 178 new turbine-generator units would need to be procured. The
median capacity of these units is 2.4 MW. Of the 25 new units with planned capacities greater than
30 MW, all but one are for PSH projects.

In 2025–2026, FERC has taken steps to streamline multiple hydropower permitting processes,
including the issuance of preliminary permits, post-licensing activities involving maintenance and
upgrades, and license terminations.

In July 2025, FERC issued two NEPA categorical exclusions (CEs), previously established and used
by the U.S. BOR, for hydropower-related activities.87 CEs describe types of actions that do not cause
significant effect on the human environment and therefore are excluded from the NEPA requirement
to prepare an environmental document.88 The Fiscal Responsibility Act of 2023 codified the practice
of one federal agency (FERC in this case) adopting the CEs of another agency (U.S. BOR in this case)
after consultation and analysis to determine that the types of actions to which the CEs would be applied
are similar to those which the original agency intended. FERC consulted with U.S. BOR and analyzed
40 actions over the past five years that would be covered by the CEs and found that none resulted
in significant effects on the human environment.

The two CEs cover the following types of actions:

 • “Maintenance, rehabilitation, and replacement of existing facilities which may involve a minor
 change in size, location, and/or operation”

 • “Minor safety of dam construction activities where the work is confined to the dam, abutment
 areas, or appurtenant features, and where no major change in reservoir or downstream operation is
 anticipated as a result of construction activities”

In October 2025, at the request of DOE, FERC issued a notice inviting comments to a proposed notice
of proposed rulemaking (NOPR) that would codify in FERC’s regulations that opposition from third
parties such as federal land managers and Tribes is not a basis to deny an application to a preliminary
permit.89 If the NOPR becomes a rule, it would reverse the policy established by FERC in 2024 to not
issue preliminary permits on Tribal lands if the relevant Tribes are opposed. The National Hydropower
Association, which is the U.S. hydropower industry trade association, expressed support for the NOPR
arguing that a preliminary permit should only be denied if there is a permanent legal barrier to licensing
the project. Tribes and conservation groups submitted dozens of comments opposing the NOPR as
dismissive of Tribal sovereignty and ultimately wasteful of FERC’s and tribal resources because projects
in Tribal land opposed by the relevant Tribes would have no realistic pathway to development.

In November 2025, FERC issued a Notice of Inquiry seeking stakeholder input on how to streamline
approval for post-licensing activities (e.g., maintenance, repairs, upgrades) in hydropower projects.90
It includes questions on 1) how to streamline approval for minor post-licensing activities that do not
require a license amendment 2) whether a blanket authorization for post-licensing activities currently
requiring amendments would be a good option and, if adopted, which activities it should cover. The
NHA strongly recommended the adoption of a blanket authorization program and requested FERC
to limit its review to actions involving “substantial alteration or addition not in conformity with the
approved plans”. Several commenters recommended FERC to better define what “substantial” and
“minor” alterations are. Multiple conservation groups opposed the use of blanket authorizations and
argued that the classification of post-licensing activities as “substantial” or not should be based on their
impacts on project operations, environmental conditions, or public uses.

The second order issued in February 2026 adopted five existing CEs from TVA.92 They became effective

CEs. None of the 60 actions identified as being covered by the CEs were found to have significant
impacts on the human environment. The five CEs cover the following types of actions:

 • TVA 16: Construction of new transmission line infrastructure, substations or interconnection
 facilities within limits for length (10 miles), new rights-of-way (125 acres), disturbed acreage (10
 acres), and new access roads (1 mile)

 • TVA 17: Routine modification, repair, and maintenance of, and minor upgrade or/and addition to,
 existing transmission infrastructure

 • TVA 36: Routine operation, repair or in-kind replacement, and maintenance actions for existing
 buildings, infrastructure systems, facility grounds, public use areas, recreation sites, and operating
 equipment. The actions covered by this CE must not result in changes in the project design
 capacity, function, or operation

 • TVA 37: Upgrades, uprates, and other actions that alter existing buildings, infrastructure systems,

 actions have limits to disturbed acreage (10 acres). For modifications to water intake and outflow
 structures, covered actions must keep intake velocities and volumes and water effluent quality and
 volumes within existing permit limits

 • TVA 38: Construction of new buildings and associated infrastructure within limits to disturbed
 acreage (10 acres of land not previously disturbed by human activity or 25 acres of previously
 disturbed land)

The newly established or proposed NEPA CEs for hydropower-related activities that do not cause
significant effects in the human environment translate into time savings for projects seeking
authorization for activities that fall under the CE. Additionally, by allowing FERC to focus its efforts
on those permitting activities that may have significant effects on the environment, these CEs may
shorten the time to complete those processes for the projects that need them. For the CEs adopted from
U.S. BOR and TVA, if there are extraordinary circumstances by which an action falling under these
categories may have significant impact on the human environment, FERC can decide not to use the CE
and require NEPA review.

 GLOSSARY

Availability factor – Percentage of hours in a year in which a hydropower unit is not offline due to a
planned or forced outage and is, therefore, available to operate

Capacity addition – This category, as shown in Figure 3, Figure 13, Figure 14, and Figure 15 includes
additions of new turbine-generator units to existing hydropower projects as well as upgrades to existing
turbine-generator units that result in an increase in unit nameplate capacity

Capacity factor – Ratio, typically expressed as percentage, between actual annual generation and
maximum possible annual generation if the hydropower unit generates continuously at its nameplate
capacity

Conduit retrofit – Hydropower project where electricity generation capability is added to an existing
conduit (“any tunnel, canal, pipeline, aqueduct, flume, ditch, or similar manmade water conveyance that
is operated for the distribution of water for agricultural, municipal, or industrial consumption and not
primarily for the generation of electricity” 18 CFR 4.30)

Large (project or unit) – Project or unit with a nameplate capacity greater than 100 MW and less than
or equal to 500 MW

Medium (project or unit) – Project or unit with a nameplate capacity greater than 10 MW and less than
or equal to 100 MW

Micro (project or unit) – Project or unit with a nameplate capacity of less than or equal to 0.1 MW

Plant downrate – Downward adjustment to the reported (to EIA Form 860) nameplate capacity of
existing turbine-generator units or situations where a plant owner decides to retire some of its turbine-
generator units but continues to operate the rest

New stream-reach development – Hydropower project where hydropower generation capability is
added to previously undeveloped waterways

Non-powered dam retrofit – Hydropower project where hydropower generation capability is added to
an existing dam previously used solely for other purposes (e.g., flood control, navigation)

Plant refurbishment – Project that involves modifications in turbine-generator units or other
hydropower plant components to extend the life of the facility and improve its performance but do not
result in increased generating capacity or increased energy output

Small (project or unit) – Project or unit with a nameplate capacity greater than 0.1 MW and less than or
equal to 10 MW

Unit upgrade – A hydropower unit is upgraded when some or all of its components are modified in
a way that results in increased generating capacity and/or increased energy output through improved
efficiency

Very Large (project or unit) – Project or unit with a nameplate capacity greater than 500 MW
