Racial outcomes in patients with diabetic cardiomyopathy treated with an aldose reductase inhibitor: the ARISE-HF trial
Authors: Jose L. Batista, Yuxi Liu, Javed Butler, Stefano Del Prato, Justin A Ezekowitz, Carolyn S P Lam, Thomas H Marwick, Julio Rosenstock, W H Wilson Tang, Riccardo Perfetti, Alessia Urbinati, Faiez Zannad, Onyedika Ilonze, James L Januzzi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRacial and ethnic differences in diabetic cardiomyopathy (DbCM) exist, with black and Hispanic participants showing poorer health status. It remains unclear whether these differences affect the natural progression of the disease. We aimed to evaluate disease progression in individuals with DbCM, as well as racial differences in the response to AT-001.MethodsA total of 625 participants with DbCM were randomised to either placebo or AT-001 and followed for 15 months. The primary outcome was change in peak oxygen uptake (peak VO2) and secondary outcomes included Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire (KCCQ) and Physical Activity Scale for the Elderly scores. Analyses were stratified by race and ethnicity (black, Hispanic, white).ResultsBlack and Hispanic participants who received placebo experienced greater declines in peak VO2 (−0.74 and −1.67 mL/kg/min, respectively) compared with white participants (−0.23 mL/kg/min, p=0.005). AT-001 demonstrated a non-statistically significant trend towards slower declines in peak VO2 in black and Hispanic participants (−0.31 and −0.62 mL/kg/min, p=0.29, respectively). Black participants who received placebo had the largest declines in most KCCQ scores.ConclusionBlack and Hispanic participants with DbCM experienced faster disease progression, with black participants showing the most pronounced functional and quality of life declines. The effect of AT-001 on peak VO2 changes was not statistically significant with similar effects between racial and ethnic groups (NCT04083339).Trial registration numberNCT04083339.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus is a powerful risk factor for heart failure (HF), with a 2.4-fold increase in HF incidence among men and a fivefold increase among women with diabetes.1 2 While diabetes is frequently linked to HF through the atherosclerotic process, HF is a more common initial presentation in diabetes than myocardial infarction.3 In fact, it is now well established that diabetes can directly contribute to the development of HF, with the severity and duration of hyperglycaemia and hyperinsulinaemia playing key roles.4 Diabetic cardiomyopathy (DbCM) is a distinct type of heart muscle disease caused by diabetes mellitus, independent of cardiovascular risk factors that arise secondary to diabetes such as coronary artery disease and hypertension.5 These abnormalities lead to metabolic dysregulation and oxidative stress resulting in structural and functional alterations in the myocardium, ultimately causing cardiac dysfunction.6 7 In its early stages, DbCM is classified as Stage B HF and has a high risk of progressing to symptomatic HF.8 However, until recently, no treatment had been proposed to slow the progression of DbCM to symptomatic HF.9

An important mechanism identified in the pathophysiology of DbCM is the augmented activation of the polyol pathway.9–14 Driven by hyperglycaemia, glucose is reduced to sorbitol within cells, contributing to oxidative stress and tissue injury that leads to myocardial fibrosis and cardiac remodelling. Given that aldose reductase is the initial and rate-limiting enzyme in this pathway, it has been identified as a potential target for the treatment of DbCM.15 To address this gap, the Aldose Reductase Inhibitor for Stabilization of Exercise Capacity in Heart Failure (ARISE-HF) trial was conducted to evaluate the efficacy of a novel aldose reductase inhibitor (AT-001) in preserving or improving functional capacity in individuals with long-standing but well controlled type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). After 15 months of follow-up, AT-001 did not significantly improve exercise capacity compared with placebo; however, there was a non-statistically significant trend toward slower disease progression.9

Understanding the impact of DbCM on key populations with T2DM is important. In this regard, a substudy of ARISE-HF revealed that black and Hispanic individuals with DbCM had lower use of therapies for T2DM, poorer ventricular function, lower physical activity and greater impairment in exercise capacity.16 Given that these characteristics result in black and Hispanic individuals having a higher risk of HF progression, it is plausible they may exhibit distinct treatment outcomes compared with other racial and ethnic groups. It remains unknown whether racial and ethnic differences influence the natural progression of DbCM or the therapeutic response to AT-001. To this end, this study aims to evaluate the natural disease progression stratified by race and to examine the racial differences in treatment response to AT-001 over 15 months of follow-up.

Methods

The rationale, study design and primary findings of the ARISE-HF trial have been reported in earlier publications.9 15 The ARISE-HF trial was an international, phase 3, randomised, placebo-controlled, double-blind clinical trial designed to evaluate the efficacy of AT-001 compared with placebo in preserving or improving exercise capacity assessed by peak oxygen uptake (peak VO2) during cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET).15 The trial also aimed to assess the safety and tolerability of AT-001. The main trial was funded by Applied Therapeutics, who worked collaboratively with the academic leadership of the trial regarding the design and the execution of the main trial. The sponsor had no role in funding or executing the present data analysis and had no role regarding decision to publish the present manuscript.

The ARISE-HF trial is registered on ClinicalTrials.gov with the identifier NCT04083339.

Selection criteria and study design

The ARISE-HF trial was conducted at 62 clinical sites across 10 countries. Full inclusion and exclusion criteria, as well as study site details, are provided in the online supplemental appendix. In summary, participants eligible for enrolment had to have a diagnosis of T2DM and be aged 60 years or older. Alternatively, participants between 40 and 60 years of age were eligible if they had T2DM for at least 10 years or an estimated glomerular filtration rate below 60 mL/min/1.73 m². Participants were also required to have a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of 45% or greater on echocardiography and meet at least one criterion for stage B HF, either with abnormal cardiac structure or function or elevated cardiac biomarkers. Notably, trial enrolment required participants to have well-controlled blood pressure and a glycated haemoglobin level below 7.5%. All glucose-lowering therapies were permitted except for thiazolidinediones. All eligible participants were required to have a reduced exercise capacity defined as a peak VO2 below 75% of age-matched reference controls.

We performed a post hoc analysis of clinical and safety outcomes stratified by race and ethnicity at 15 months. Due to low numbers of other racial and ethnic groups, the study population was divided into only three study groups: black, Hispanic and white participants. Race and ethnicity were self-reported by participants at the time of enrolment in the ARISE-HF trial. For treatment comparisons, the low-dose (1000 mg two times per day) and high-dose (1500 mg two times per day) AT-001 arms were pooled, given their similar efficacy and safety profiles as demonstrated in the trial (online supplemental appendix). The pooled AT-001 group was compared with the placebo group for all analyses. The inclusion of a low-dose arm in the parent trial was based on historical safety concerns with prior aldose reductase inhibitors as required by regulatory agencies. It was not appropriate or possible to involve patients or the public in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

Study outcomes

The primary outcome of the trial was the change in peak VO2 from baseline to 15 months between placebo and those randomised to receive AT-001. Uniformity across all sites was maintained by following a standardised CPET protocol, and a blinded central committee evaluated the outcomes to reduce bias and ensure objectivity. Peak VO2 was derived from primary breath-by-breath data, capturing the highest median value over a 30 s window in the final 90 s of exercise, reflecting aerobic capacity. Secondary outcomes included: changes in health-related quality of life, assessed using the Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire (KCCQ), with scores ranging from 0 to 100, where higher values indicate better health17; changes in physical activity, measured by the Physical Activity Scale for the Elderly (PASE)18; changes in N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) concentrations; and the incidence of adverse events.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were compared using the Student’s t-test and are reported as mean (±SD) if their distribution was normal or compared using the Kruskal-Wallis H test and reported as median (IQR) if their distribution was skewed. Categorical variables were compared using the χ² test and are described using frequencies and percentages. Ordinal variables were compared using the Kruskal-Wallis H test and reported as median (IQR).

The primary endpoint of change in peak VO2 from baseline to 15 months was analysed using a mixed model for repeated measures analysis of covariance with an unstructured covariance matrix, stratified by race (black, Hispanic and white). Least-square (LS) mean changes (with standard errors) in peak VO2 were estimated for each racial group, allowing for robust within-group comparisons. Pairwise comparisons of racial groups (eg, black vs Hispanic, black vs white and Hispanic vs white) were performed for changes in peak VO2.

The percentage of individuals with a ≥6% decrease in peak VO2 from baseline to 15 months was analysed using a logistic regression model, stratified by race (black, Hispanic and white). The model was adjusted for covariates based on randomisation stratification factors. Estimated ORs with associated two-sided 95% CIs were generated for each racial subgroup to assess the likelihood of a ≥6% decrease in peak VO2. Pairwise comparisons of these likelihoods were conducted to evaluate differences between racial groups. An interaction analysis was performed using a linear model that included treatment (placebo or AT-001), race (with white as the reference category) and their interaction terms to assess whether the effect of AT-001 on change in peak VO2 at 15 months differed by race. A univariable linear regression model was used to evaluate the association between sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors and glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists with the change in peak VO2.

Changes in NT-proBNP, KCCQ score and PASE score from baseline to 15 months were analysed using a mixed model for repeated measures approach, similar to that used for the primary efficacy outcome. The model was applied separately for each racial group (black, Hispanic and white) to estimate LS mean changes and corresponding SEs over time. Pairwise comparisons were conducted for NT-proBNP changes at 15 months to evaluate differences between black, Hispanic and white participants.

Statistical analysis was performed using R software V.4.4.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. No adjustment was made for multiple comparisons; therefore, analyses should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

Results

Of the 691 study participants with DbCM enrolled in the trial, 625 were included in this analysis. Sixty-six participants were excluded due to small sample sizes within their respective racial or ethnic subgroups, limiting meaningful statistical analysis. Of the remaining 625, 42 (7%) identified as black, 151 (24%) as Hispanic and 432 (69%) as white. The analysed cohort consisted of 415 participants randomised to AT-001 (209 to high-dose 1500 mg two times per day and 206 to low-dose 1000 mg two times per day) and 210 to placebo, all of whom were followed for 15 months. The baseline comparisons for these groups have been previously reported16 and are provided in online supplemental table 1 for convenience.

Tables 1–4 detail the outcomes at 15 months, comparing black, Hispanic and white participants receiving AT-001 versus placebo. Online supplemental tables 2-4 provide outcomes comparing low-dose AT-001 versus placebo among these racial and ethnic groups. Online supplemental tables 5-8 provide outcomes comparing high-dose AT-001 versus placebo among these racial and ethnic groups.

An exploratory interaction analysis using a linear model including treatment with AT-001, race and their interaction terms showed no statistically significant interaction between race and treatment for the primary outcome of change in peak VO2 at 15 months (online supplemental table 9).

Across all racial groups and pairwise comparisons in both the placebo and AT-001 groups, there were no significant differences in the proportion of participants experiencing a clinically significant (ie, ≥6%) decrease in peak VO2. However, in the placebo group, Hispanic participants had a higher rate of clinically significant decline in peak VO2 (43%) compared with black (33%) and white participants (33%), suggesting more clinically meaningful declines in peak VO2 among Hispanic participants.

Given the putative impact of SGLT2 inhibitor/GLP-1 receptor agonist use on change in peak VO2 in the trial and the use imbalance of these agents among black and Hispanic participants, univariable linear regression analysis was performed to assess the association between SGLT2i and GLP-1 receptor agonists with the change in peak VO2. SGLT2i use was not significantly associated with peak VO2 change (β=−0.05, 95% CI −0.62 to 0.52, p=0.86). GLP-1 receptor agonist use showed a trend toward a positive association with peak VO2 change but did not reach statistical significance (β=0.56, 95% CI −0.04 to 1.17, p=0.06).

Changes in health-related scores

Black participants who received placebo consistently showed the largest statistically significant declines in nearly all KCCQ scores (table 3), including the Overall Summary Score (−8.6), Clinical Summary Score (−10.6), Physical Limitation Score (−11.1) and Symptom Burden Score (−12.0), compared with Hispanic and white participants. These results were further supported by LS mean differences and a trend toward worse outcomes compared with white participants.

Discussion

In this post hoc analysis of the ARISE-HF trial of individuals with DbCM, well-controlled T2DM, stage B HF and impaired baseline functional capacity and health-related quality of life, significant racial and ethnic disparities in disease progression were observed (online supplemental graphical abstract). Black participants in the placebo group experienced the greatest declines in quality of life and physical activity measures, as evidenced by significant reductions in several measures of the KCCQ scores. Hispanic participants exhibited the largest decreases in peak VO2 (−1.67 mL/kg/min), followed by black participants (−0.74 mL/kg/min). In contrast, among participants treated with AT-001, changes in peak VO₂, quality of life and physical activity measures did not differ across racial and ethnic groups after 15 months of follow-up.

A major contributor to the findings in this study may be baseline differences in risk factors that predispose black and Hispanic individuals to accelerated clinical deterioration. A prior post hoc analysis of the racial differences in baseline characteristics of DbCM in the ARISE-HF trial demonstrated that black individuals had worse baseline ventricular function, lower KCCQ scores, reduced physical activity and lower functional capacity during CPET compared with other groups.16 Even after adjusting for confounders such as age and sex, black and Hispanic individuals exhibited significantly lower peak VO2 levels, which may partially explain the disparities in disease progression. Notably, black individuals had worse global longitudinal strain (GLS) values compared with white individuals. Given that impaired GLS is a well-established risk factor for progression to symptomatic HF,19 20 this may account for their faster clinical decline. Although participants in this study had preserved LVEF despite impaired GLS, these findings suggest that GLS could serve as an early indicator of impending clinically overt LV dysfunction16 and may presage clinical deterioration in DbCM. It is important to note that these observed racial differences likely reflect race as a marker of underlying social, clinical and economic factors, rather than intrinsic biological differences.

As previously reported, at baseline in ARISE-HF, black and Hispanic participants demonstrated the most impaired CPET parameters, while white participants generally exhibited more preserved results.16 Building on these results, this study demonstrated that Hispanic participants experienced the largest decreases in peak VO2 (−1.67 mL/kg/min), followed by black participants (−0.74 mL/kg/min). One factor that may partially explain these results is the higher body mass index (BMI) observed among black participants in the prior study (31 vs 30.6 kg/m²). Each unit increase in BMI was independently associated with a 39% reduction in peak VO2 levels after adjusting for multiple variables (p<0.001).16 These findings are aligned with those from Heart Failure: A Controlled Trial Investigating Outcomes of Exercise Training (HF-ACTION) trial,21 which found that a BMI >30 kg/m² was associated with lower KCCQ scores, diminished peak VO2 and an increased ratio of minute ventilation to carbon dioxide production (VE/VCO₂) slope among individuals with overt HF. These findings suggest that elevated BMI may not only predispose these participants to worse baseline functional capacity but could also contribute to their larger declines in peak VO2 observed during the study.

Black participants exhibited significantly lower baseline KCCQ scores in symptom stability, self-efficacy and overall summary measures compared with white participants. This disparity was exacerbated during the trial, as black participants on placebo showed the largest statistically significant declines in several KCCQ domains, including Overall Summary Score (−8.6), Clinical Summary Score (−10.6), Physical Limitation Score (−11.1) and Symptom Burden Score (−12.0). LS mean differences further supported these findings. These results underscore striking disparities in disease progression among black participants in the absence of intervention. Consistent with the worse KCCQ scores, black participants also experienced the most pronounced declines in physical activity levels, as reflected in PASE scores.

Prior studies have shown the role of race differences in functional capacity and physical activity with prior analyses from ARISE-HF, National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) and Dallas Heart Study, which support the role of baseline disparities in physical activity and fitness in driving worse outcomes.22 23 Another likely explanation for the observed differences in clinical progression is the impact of social drivers of health (SDOH). Development and progression of DbCM is likely exacerbated by prolonged exposure to uncontrolled diabetes, which can be influenced by social and economic factors. Access to and quality of diabetes care have long been areas of disparity,2 with black and Hispanic participants consistently exhibiting poorer glycaemic control and receiving less effective diabetes management compared with white participants.2 24–27 Limited access to education and healthcare resources may delay awareness, early detection and treatment of diabetes, leading to prolonged hyperglycaemia and higher DbCM risk. Furthermore, environmental and lifestyle factors—such as a lack of secure areas for exercise, limited availability of high-quality foods, increased stress and reduced time for physical activity—may further contribute to the worse outcomes observed in black participants in this study.28 29 Disparities in the utilisation of advanced diabetes management technologies also play a role including limitations in use of blood glucose monitoring30 or less frequent use of therapies such as SGLT2 inhibitors or GLP-1 receptor agonists by black and Hispanic participants in ARISE-HF. In our analysis, GLP-1 receptor agonists use showed a trend toward a positive association with peak VO2 change. This underutilisation of effective therapies may partly explain the observed disparities in disease progression. Although this post hoc analysis cannot establish causality, these imbalances may reflect unequal access to preventive care, specialty care, medication affordability and longitudinal diabetes management. Therefore, the observed differences in DbCM progression should be interpreted in the context of structural and social drivers of health rather than as intrinsic biological differences between racial or ethnic groups.

Limitations

The observational nature of this study limits its ability to establish causality, and the potential for residual or unmeasured confounding factors remains. Additionally, since the primary results of the study did not achieve statistical significance,9 subgroup analyses should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. Moreover, the small sample size within each racial or ethnic subgroup is a key limitation of this analysis. However, because the study was powered for the overall population rather than individual subgroups, the lack of statistical significance in smaller subgroups does not necessarily indicate a lack of association. The study’s duration of 15 months may also have been insufficient to fully capture the long-term effects of AT-001 on exercise capacity and clinical outcomes. Despite these limitations, this study provides valuable insights into the racial differences in the natural progression of DbCM and treatment response to aldose reductase inhibitors. This serves to identify potential patient populations and treatment schemes that warrant further investigation.

Conclusions

This observational study of the ARISE-HF trial highlights significant racial and ethnic disparities in disease progression and treatment response among participants with DbCM. Black and Hispanic participants who received placebo experienced greater declines in peak VO2, quality-of-life measures (eg, KCCQ scores) and physical activity levels compared with white participants. While treatment with AT-001 demonstrated non-significant trends toward mitigating some, but not all, of these disparities—particularly among black participants, who showed smaller declines in functional and quality-of-life measures—these findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating, given the neutral primary trial results and limited sample size of our analysis. Moreover, these disparities likely reflect race as a marker of underlying SDOH rather than intrinsic biological differences. Collectively, these findings highlight two critical insights: (1) black and Hispanic participants may have faster clinical progression to overt HF, potentially influenced by baseline differences in risk factors and SDOH and (2) AT-001 may warrant further investigation in high-risk subgroups within adequately powered, dedicated studies.
