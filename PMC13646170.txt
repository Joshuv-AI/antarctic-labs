Integrated evaluation of umbilical cord mesenchymal stromal cell-derived extracellular vesicles across skin-related cellular and zebrafish models

Peijun Zhao, Dan Xiao, Jingyu Xu, Qian Li, Yue Fang, Shiyun Xu, Feidi Xiao, Yaqi Qin, Yifu Wei, Yunfeng Gao, Cheguo Cai

Abstract

IntroductionMesenchymal stromal cell-derived extracellular vesicles (MSC-EVs) have shown biological effects in skin-related models, particularly in oxidative injury, wound repair, and photoaging. However, previous studies have used different MSC sources, EVs preparations, and experimental systems, and the activity of a defined EVs preparation across multiple skin-related processes has rarely been examined within a single study. Effects on sebocyte lipid metabolism and androgen-related responses are particularly poorly characterized.MethodsHuman umbilical cord mesenchymal stromal cell-derived extracellular vesicles (UC-MSC-EVs) were characterized and evaluated across complementary cellular and zebrafish models assessing oxidative injury, keratinocyte migration and barrier-related proteins, UVB-induced fibroblast senescence and extracellular matrix responses, sebocyte lipid and androgen metabolism, melanogenesis, and dermal papilla cell function.ResultsUC-MSC-EVs reduced UVB- and tert-butyl hydroperoxide-induced injury in HaCaT keratinocytes, decreased oxidative stress, and restored antioxidant enzyme activities. They also promoted keratinocyte migration, increased filaggrin and aquaporin-3 expression, and enhanced zebrafish tail-fin regeneration, increasing relative regenerated length from 60.24 ± 4.01% in the model group to 84.10 ± 5.82% at 48 h post-amputation. In UVB-exposed human skin fibroblasts, UC-MSC-EVs reduced cellular senescence and modulated extracellular matrix, inflammatory, and antioxidant responses. In oleic acid-stimulated SZ95 sebocytes, UC-MSC-EVs reduced lipid accumulation and lipid peroxidation and attenuated 5α-reductase activity and dihydrotestosterone production. In B16F10 cells stimulated with α-MSH, melanin content decreased from 147.60 ± 15.98% to 99.70 ± 7.98% of the untreated control at the highest tested UC-MSC-EVs concentration, accompanied by reduced tyrosinase activity and melanogenic gene expression. Pigmentation intensity was also reduced in zebrafish. In human dermal papilla cells, UC-MSC-EVs enhanced migration and increased alkaline phosphatase activity to 148.22 ± 4.72% of the control level, while increasing expression of hair growth factors and reducing androgen metabolism.DiscussionThese results characterize the biological activity of a single UC-MSC-EVs preparation across multiple skin-related experimental systems and provide a basis for further investigation of its bioactive components and dose-response relationships.

Introduction

The skin is a highly specialized organ that serves as the body’s primary barrier against environmental insults and helps maintain hydration and tissue integrity (). Cutaneous pigmentation contributes to photoprotection and local skin homeostasis (). Environmental exposures, particularly ultraviolet radiation and oxidative stress, can disrupt epidermal barrier function, extracellular matrix (ECM) organization, sebaceous activity, melanogenesis, and hair follicle-associated cellular functions (; ). These changes contribute to common skin-related conditions and phenotypes, including photoaging, sebaceous dysfunction, hyperpigmentation, and hair loss (; ). Although these conditions have distinct pathophysiological features, some of the underlying stress and regulatory processes may affect multiple components of the skin. Thus, skin dysfunction often involves changes in multiple biological processes rather than a single isolated alteration.

Current approaches to skin damage and dysfunction are generally designed for specific indications or biological targets. Mesenchymal stromal cell-derived extracellular vesicles (MSC-EVs) are membrane-bound particles carrying proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids that can modify recipient-cell functions (). As a cell-free product of MSCs, EVs may offer practical advantages for storage, handling, and formulation compared with viable-cell administration (). Human umbilical cord-derived MSCs are an attractive source for EVs production because the tissue can be obtained non-invasively and the cells exhibit high proliferative capacity (). These features have prompted increasing investigation of human umbilical cord MSC-EVs (UC-MSC-EVs) in regenerative and skin-related models.

Previous studies have demonstrated biological effects of MSC-derived EVs in several skin-related experimental settings. Liu et al. reported that UC-MSC-derived EVs enhanced HaCaT cell repair and reduced ultraviolet B (UVB)-induced cellular injury (). Hu et al. showed that adipose-derived MSC EVs accelerated cutaneous wound healing by promoting fibroblast migration, proliferation, and collagen synthesis (). Gao et al. further demonstrated that miR-1246-enriched adipose-derived MSC EVs alleviated UVB-induced photoaging through GSK3β-related autophagy regulation (). More recent studies have extended UC-MSC-EVs research to ECM regulation and pigmentation (). However, the available evidence has been generated using different MSC sources, EV preparations, experimental models, treatment conditions, and outcome measures, making it difficult to define the broader biological activity profile of a given UC-MSC-EVs preparation within a single study framework. In addition, sebaceous gland homeostasis is strongly influenced by lipid metabolism and androgenic regulation (; ). However, compared with wound repair and anti-aging, effects of MSC-EVs on sebocyte lipogenesis, lipid peroxidation, and androgen metabolism remain less extensively investigated.

Against this background, the present study evaluated human umbilical cord mesenchymal stromal cell-derived extracellular vesicles (UC-MSC-EVs) across a panel of complementary skin-related cellular and zebrafish models. Specifically, we investigated oxidative stress in keratinocytes, epidermal repair and barrier proteins, zebrafish tail-fin regeneration, UVB-induced fibroblast senescence and ECM remodeling, sebocyte lipogenesis and androgen metabolism, melanogenesis, and dermal papilla cell function. The objective was to determine the range of biological responses produced by the same UC-MSC-EVs preparation across these different experimental systems, with particular attention to the less studied effects on sebocyte lipid metabolism and androgen-related responses. By integrating these complementary models within a single study, we sought to obtain a broader view of the skin-related biological activities of UC-MSC-EVs.

Materials and methods

UC-MSC culture, EVs isolation, and characterization

UC-MSCs and UC-MSC-EVs were provided by the Shenzhen Beike Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shenzhen, China). All cellular materials used in this study were derived from a single donor, and only passage 4 (P4) UC-MSC were employed for cell cultivation and extracellular vesicles (EVs) preparation throughout the experiments.

UC-MSC were cultured in complete medium (Biological Industries) supplemented with 1% human platelet lysate, which was incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2. When the cells reached approximately 80% confluency, adherent UC-MSC and culture supernatants were separately collected for cell characterization and EVs preparation.

For EVs preparation, the culture supernatant was sequentially centrifuged at 300 g for 10 min and 2,000 × g for 10 min to remove dead cells and debris. The supernatant was further centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 30 min and then ultracentrifuged at 100,000 × g for 90 min using an ultracentrifuge (Beckman Coulter). The pellet was resuspended in precooled phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and subjected to a second ultracentrifugation at 100,000 × g for 90 min. The final pellet was resuspended in PBS and filtered through a 0.22 μm sterile filter to obtain purified UC-MSC-EVs. The morphology of UC-MSC-EVs were examined with a Hitachi H-7650 transmission electron microscope (Tokyo, Japan). Particle concentration and size distribution were analyzed using nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) on a NanoSight NS300 instrument (Malvern Instruments Ltd., Malvern, United Kingdom). The protein content of the UC-MSC-EVs was quantified with a Pierce BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Scientific, United States). EVs-associated proteins CD9, CD63, TSG101, ALIX, and the non-EVs cellular proteins GM130 and Calnexin were assessed by Western blotting.

The phenotypes of the UC-MSC were analysed via flow cytometry after incubation with CD90-BV421, CD29-PE, CD105-APC, CD73-PE, CD45-FITC, CD34- BV421, CD14-FITC, HLA-DR-PerCP, and the corresponding isotype control antibodies. All the antibodies were purchased from BD Pharmingen (San Diego, CA, United States). UC-MSC were analysed via a flow cytometer (BD FACS CaliburTM, United States). For functional assays, the PBS-resuspended UC-MSC-EVs preparation was diluted into the corresponding culture medium or zebrafish medium to the indicated final particle concentration. For the HaCaT oxidative-injury viability assays, the UVB-irradiated HSFs viability assay, and the B16F10 melanin content and tyrosinase activity assays, a PBS vehicle control corresponding to the maximum PBS volume introduced with the highest UC-MSC-EVs dose was included.

Cell culture and general experimental methods

Cell lines and culture conditions

HaCaT keratinocytes and B16F10 melanoma cells were purchased from Procell (Wuhan, China), human skin fibroblasts (HSFs) and human dermal papilla cells (hDPCs) were purchased from Shanghai Fuheng Biological Technology Co., Ltd., and SZ95 sebocytes were provided by Henan Academy of Sciences. HaCaT cells, HSFs, and hDPCs were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (P/S). SZ95 sebocytes were maintained in Sebomed basal medium (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) containing 10% FBS and 5 ng/mL recombinant human epidermal growth factor (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). B16F10 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% P/S. All cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan). For viability or cytotoxicity screening, cells were seeded in 96-well plates at the following densities: 1.8 × 104 cells/well for HaCaT cells and 2.0 × 104 cells/well for HSFs, hDPCs, SZ95 sebocytes, and B16F10 cells. After 24 h of attachment, cells were exposed to the treatments specified in the corresponding experimental subsections. At the end of the indicated treatment period, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well and incubated for 1 h. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (BioTek, USA).

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining

Immunofluorescence staining was performed on cells grown on glass coverslips. After the designated treatment, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS for 10–15 min at room temperature. After washing with PBS, cells were permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS for 15 min at room temperature and blocked with blocking buffer containing 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) or normal serum in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Cells were then incubated with target-specific primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with corresponding fluorescent secondary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with 1 μg/mL DAPI or Hoechst 33342 (Sigma-Aldrich, US) for 10 min at room temperature. After washing with PBS, coverslips were mounted onto glass slides with anti-fade mounting medium. Images were acquired using an Olympus IX73 fluorescence microscope (Tokyo, Japan). Marker-specific antibodies and treatment conditions are described in the relevant experimental subsections.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from treated cells using an RNeasy mini kit (Qiagen, Shanghai, China) and reverse-transcribed into cDNA using a PrimeScript RT reagent kit (Takara, Dalian, China). qRT-PCR was performed using SYBR Green Premix Ex Taq (Takara, Dalian, China) on a 7500 Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, USA). Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method with GAPDH as the reference gene. Primer sequences are listed in .

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Culture supernatants or cell lysates were collected after the designated treatments. Target proteins or metabolites were quantified using analyte-specific commercial ELISA kits according to the manufacturers’ protocols.

Flow cytometry for cell cycle analysis

Cells were harvested, washed with PBS, and fixed in 70% ethanol at 4 °C overnight. Fixed cells were stained with propidium iodide (0.05 mg/mL) in the presence of RNase A (1 mg/mL) and 0.3% Triton X-100 for 30 min in the dark. DNA content was analyzed using a BD FACSCalibur flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA), and the proportions of cells in G0/G1, S, and G2/M phases were determined using ModFit LT software (Verity Software House, USA).

Oxidative stress models and antioxidant assays in HaCaT cells

Establishment of oxidative stress models

HaCaT cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 2 × 104 cells/well and allowed to attach for 24 h. For UVB dose screening, the culture medium was replaced with PBS before irradiation, and cells were exposed to 312 nm UVB at 5–50 mJ/cm2. After irradiation, PBS was replaced with fresh complete medium and cells were incubated for 24 h before CCK-8 analysis. Based on the preliminary dose-response experiment, 30 mJ/cm2 UVB was selected as a sublethal condition that reduced cell viability to approximately 80% of the untreated control ().

For the tert-butyl hydroperoxide (tBHP) model, HaCaT cells were exposed to 25–800 μM tBHP in serum-free medium for 24 h. Based on the preliminary dose-response experiment, 100 μM tBHP was selected because it reduced cell viability to approximately 80% of the untreated control while retaining sufficient viable cells for evaluation of UC-MSC-EVs-mediated protection ().

UC-MSC-EVs treatment and viability assessment

Immediately after UVB irradiation or tBHP addition, HaCaT cells were treated with UC-MSC-EVs diluted in DMEM containing 10% FBS and incubated for 24 h. Final EVs doses were expressed as particles/mL. Cell viability was determined using the CCK-8 assay as described in .

Detection of intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) and free radicals

Following oxidative stress induction and EVs treatment, HaCaT cells were incubated with 10 μM 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA; Beyotime, Shanghai, China) or 5 μM dihydroethidium (DHE; Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark to detect intracellular ROS or superoxide levels, respectively. Fluorescence intensity was observed under a confocal microscope and quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, USA).

Determination of antioxidant enzyme activities

After treatment, HaCaT cells were lysed and the activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and glutathione peroxidase (GPX) were measured using commercial colorimetric assay kits (Beyotime, Shanghai, China, or Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Enzyme activities were normalized to total protein concentration determined by the BCA assay.

Keratinocyte migration, barrier protein expression, and zebrafish tail-fin regeneration assays

In vitro wound-healing assay

HaCaT cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 105 cells/well and cultured to 90% confluence. A sterile 200 μL pipette tip was used to create a linear scratch. Detached cells were removed by washing with PBS, and the medium was replaced with serum-free DMEM containing designated concentrations of UC-MSC-EVs. Wound closure was photographed at 0, 24, and 48 h using an inverted microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan), and the migration area was quantified using ImageJ software.

Barrier protein expression by immunofluorescence

HaCaT cells were treated with UC-MSC-EVs for 48 h and processed for immunofluorescence staining as described in . Rabbit anti-filaggrin (FLG) (1:500; Abcam, ab178435) and rabbit anti-aquaporin-3 (AQP3) (1:500; Abcam, ab125219) antibodies were used to assess barrier- and hydration-related protein expression. Fluorescence intensity was quantified for group comparisons.

Zebrafish tail fin regeneration assay

Zebrafish were maintained in a recirculating aquaculture system (water temperature: 24 °C–28.5 °C; pH: 6.5-7.5; hardness: 6–8 dGH; light/dark cycle: 14/10 h). Healthy embryos at 24 h post-fertilization (hpf) were randomly allocated to 6-well plates and exposed to UC-MSC-EVs at 1.67, 3.33, or 6.67 × 108 particles/mL, with untreated embryos serving as the control. The treatment solution (15 mL/well) was renewed every 24 h, and embryos were maintained at 28 °C ± 0.5 °C. At 72 hpf, caudal fin-fold amputation was performed under a stereomicroscope without damaging the notochord. After amputation, larvae were continuously exposed to the corresponding UC-MSC-EVs concentrations. At 48 and 72 h post-amputation (hpa), three larvae per group were anesthetized with 0.25 mg/mL tricaine (Sigma-Aldrich). Fin-fold regrowth was photographed, and the regenerated area was quantified relative to the amputated area immediately after injury.

UVB-induced senescence and ECM assays in HSFs

Establishment of a UVB-induced photoaging-related fibroblast model

HSFs were seeded in 6-well plates at 4 × 105 cells/well and cultured for 24 h. Cells were washed with PBS and exposed to 100 mJ/cm2 UVB to induce photoaging-related cellular changes (). After irradiation, cells were treated with 0.33, 0.67, or 1.33 × 108 particles/mL UC-MSC-EVs for 12h, 24 h, and 48 h. Cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 assay described in . Vitamin C (20 μM) was included as a positive control.

Senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-Gal) staining

Cellular senescence was evaluated using a SA-β-Gal staining kit (BC2580, Solarbio, Beijing, China). Briefly, treated HSFs were washed with PBS, fixed in SA-β-Gal fixative, and incubated with the staining working solution at 37 °C overnight. Three random fields per slide were imaged under a light microscope, and SA-β-Gal-positive cells were quantified using ImageJ.

ELISA quantification of ECM-related proteins and inflammatory cytokines

The levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, type I collagen (Col-I), elastin, and MMP3 were quantified using commercial ELISA kits (Shanghai Enzyme-Linked Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), and hyaluronic acid (HA) levels were quantified using an ELISA kit from Wuhan Saipei Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China), according to the manufacturers’ protocols.

COL1A1 and COL3A1 IF staining

Following UVB irradiation and UC-MSC-EVs treatment, HSFs were processed for immunofluorescence staining as described in using primary antibodies against COL1A1 and COL3A1. Fluorescence intensity was quantified for group comparisons.

ECM and antioxidant gene expression

qRT-PCR was performed as described in to quantify ECM-remodeling genes (COL1A1, COL3A1, Matrix metalloproteinase 1 [MMP1], Matrix metalloproteinase 3 [MMP3], Matrix metalloproteinase 9 [MMP9]), antioxidant genes (heme oxygenase-1 [HO-1], NAD(P)H quinone dehydrogenase 1 [NQO1], superoxide dismutase 2 [SOD2], CAT), and barrier-related genes (AQP3 and FLG).

Oleic acid (OA)-induced lipogenesis and androgen metabolism assays in SZ95 sebocytes

OA-induced excessive lipogenesis model

SZ95 sebocytes were seeded in 96-well plates at 2 × 104 cells/well and cultured for 24 h. When cells reached 80% confluence, oleic acid (OA; Sigma-Aldrich, US) was added to establish the in vitro excessive lipogenesis model. Based on dose-response screening, 400 μM OA was selected as the final induction concentration for subsequent experiments (). UC-MSC-EVs and 10 μM isotretinoin (positive control) were added simultaneously with OA stimulation, and cells were incubated for 48 h. Vehicle-treated cells without OA stimulation served as the blank control.

Intracellular lipid staining and quantification

After 24 h of treatment, cells were washed twice with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, and stained with freshly prepared Oil Red O (ORO) working solution (0.5% ORO in isopropanol diluted 3:2 with distilled water, filtered through 0.22 μm) for 30 min. Cells were differentiated in 60% isopropanol for 10 s, washed with PBS, and counterstained with hematoxylin for 5 min. Five random fields per well were imaged under an inverted microscope, and the red-positive area was quantified using Image-Pro Plus software (Media Cybernetics, USA).

For neutral-lipid visualization, cells were stained with Nile Red (5 μg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich) for 15 min at 37 °C and imaged by fluorescence microscopy. Lipid peroxidation was assessed using BODIPY 581/591 C11 (5 μM; Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 30 min, and the fluorescence shift from reduced (red) to oxidized (green) probe was quantified.

Androgen-lipogenic axis analysis

5α-reductase activity in SZ95 sebocytes was measured using a commercially available assay kit (Cayman Chemical, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Dihydrotestosterone (DHT) levels in cell culture supernatants were quantified by ELISA (). Gene expression of SRD5α1 and sterol regulatory element-binding protein 1c (SREBP-1c) was analyzed by qRT-PCR ().

Melanogenesis assays in B16F10 cells and zebrafish

α-melanocyte-stimulating hormone (α-MSH)-induced melanogenesis model

B16F10 melanoma cells were seeded at 5 × 104 cells/mL in 6-well plates and cultured for 24 h. Melanogenesis was stimulated with 100 nM α-MSH (Sigma-Aldrich) for 48 h in the presence or absence of UC-MSC-EVs (). Arbutin (1 mM) served as the positive control.

Melanin content and tyrosinase activity assays

Intracellular melanin was extracted using 1 N NaOH at 80 °C for 1 h, and absorbance was measured at 405 nm. Melanin content was normalized to total protein concentration. For tyrosinase activity, cell lysates were incubated with 0.1% L-3,4-dihydroxyphenylalanine (L-DOPA; Sigma-Aldrich) in PBS (pH 6.8) at 37 °C for 1 h, and dopachrome formation was monitored at 475 nm using a microplate reader.

Melanogenic gene expression

qRT-PCR was performed as described in to quantify microphthalmia-associated transcription factor (MITF), tyrosinase (TYR), tyrosinase-related protein 1 (TRP-1) and tyrosinase-related protein 2 (TRP-2).

Zebrafish embryo melanin deposition assay

Synchronized wild-type zebrafish embryos at 6–8 hpf were randomly distributed into 6-well plates (30 embryos per well) containing E3 embryo medium. Embryos were exposed to 1.67 × 108, 3.33 × 108, or 6.67 × 108 particles/mL UC-MSC-EVs. Fresh test medium was renewed every 24 h at 28 °C ± 0.5 °C. At 48 and 72 hpf, larvae were anesthetized with MS-222 solution, mounted in 3% methylcellulose for consistent orientation, and imaged using an Olympus SZX16 stereomicroscope (Tokyo, Japan). Melanin pigment intensity of the dorsal trunk region was quantified by ImageJ software. Untreated embryos incubated in E3 medium were set as the blank control.

hDPCs functional and androgen metabolism assays

hDPCs proliferation and migration

hDPCs were seeded in 96-well plates, and cell viability was assessed by CCK-8 () after 48 h of UC-MSC-EVs treatment. For migration assessment, hDPCs were seeded in 6-well plates, and a linear scratch was created as described in . Wound closure was monitored at 0 h and 24 h.

Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity

hDPCs were treated with UC-MSC-EVs for 48 h, and ALP activity was measured using a colorimetric assay kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) with p-nitrophenyl phosphate (pNPP) as the substrate. Absorbance was measured at 405 nm, and activity was normalized to total protein content.

Androgen metabolism and growth factor expression

5α-reductase activity in hDPCs was measured as described in . DHT levels were quantified by ELISA (). Gene expression of SRD5α1, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1), and fibroblast growth factor-7 (FGF-7) was analyzed by qRT-PCR ().

Cell cycle analysis

After 48 h of UC-MSC-EVs treatment, hDPC cell-cycle distribution was analyzed by propidium iodide staining and flow cytometry as described in .

Statistical analysis

All experiments were independently repeated three times. Technical replicate wells were included within each independent experiment as indicated by the experimental design. Technical replicates were averaged within each independent experiment, and the resulting values from three independent experiments were used for statistical analysis. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.3.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Two-group comparisons were performed using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests. For experiments with more than two groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple-comparison test was used. Exact sample sizes are reported in the corresponding figure legends. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characterization of UC-MSC-EVs

UC-MSC-EVs were isolated using a production strategy combining microcarrier-based culture with extracellular vesicle harvesting to enable large-scale preparation. The particle-to-protein ratio of the UC-MSC-EVs preparation was 1.11 × 108 particles/μg. TEM revealed that the UC-MSC-EVs had a typical saucer-like morphology, with a size of approximately 100 nm (). NTA was performed with a NanoSight instrument to measure the diameter of the extracellular vesicles, which had an average diameter of 100 nm (). Moreover, Western blot analysis confirmed the enrichment of canonical extracellular vesicle markers, including CD9, CD63, Tsg101, and Alix, whereas the negative markers GM130 and Calnexin were not detected (). Flow-cytometric analysis showed that the UC-MSCs used for EVs production were positive for CD90, CD73, and CD105, with CD29 also highly expressed, whereas CD79a, CD34, CD45, HLA-DR, and CD14 showed negative expression (). These results demonstrated the successful isolation and characterization of UC-MSC-EVs for subsequent functional analyses.

UC-MSC-EVs attenuate oxidative injury in keratinocytes

To determine whether UC-MSC-EVs exert cytoprotective effects under oxidative injury, we first evaluated their effects on HaCaT cell viability and found no significant reduction in viability within the tested concentration range (). This concentration range was therefore used for subsequent functional analyses.

Two complementary oxidative-stress models were then established using UVB irradiation and tBHP exposure. Preliminary CCK-8 dose-response experiments identified 30 mJ/cm2 UVB and 100 μM tBHP as sub-lethal conditions that produced measurable injury while retaining sufficient viable cells for assessment of protection (). Under both conditions, cell viability was significantly reduced, whereas UC-MSC-EVs treatment significantly increased viability relative to the corresponding injured model groups (). UVB and tBHP also increased hydroxyl radical accumulation, which was markedly attenuated by EVs treatment ().

Exposure to UVB and tBHP also decreased the activities of the endogenous antioxidant enzymes SOD, GPX, and CAT, whereas UC-MSC-EVs treatment significantly restored these antioxidant defenses ().

In UVB-irradiated HaCaT cells, fluorescence-based assays further showed lower intracellular ROS accumulation and reduced superoxide signals after UC-MSC-EVs treatment (). Together, the concordant changes demonstrate antioxidant and cytoprotective effects of UC-MSC-EVs in oxidatively stressed keratinocytes.

UC-MSC-EVs promote keratinocyte repair and zebrafish tail-fin regeneration

We next examined cellular processes relevant to epidermal repair. In UVB-injured HaCaT cells, UC-MSC-EVs treatment promoted cellular recovery in a dose-dependent manner, with the largest effect observed at 6.67 × 107 particles/mL after 48 h (). In wound-healing assays, 8.33 × 107 particles/mL UC-MSC-EVs increased wound closure at both 24 h and 48 h (). UC-MSC-EVs treatment also increased FLG and AQP3 fluorescence relative to the control group (). FLG is associated with epidermal differentiation and barrier function, whereas AQP3 mediates water and glycerol transport in the epidermis.

The zebrafish caudal fin regeneration assay was used as an in vivo model of tissue repair to complement the keratinocyte migration assay. This model allowed us to assess whether the repair-promoting effects of UC-MSC-EVs observed in vitro could also be detected at the tissue level in vivo. In the zebrafish tail-fin amputation model, the relative regenerated length of the model group reached 60.24% ± 4.01% at 48 h and 80.41% ± 5.47% at 72 h, normalized to uninjured controls. Treatment with UC-MSC-EVs markedly promoted fin regrowth, with the 1.67 × 108 particles/mL group showing the most pronounced enhancement, achieving 84.10% ± 5.82% relative regenerated length at 48 h and 93.72% ± 6.30% at 72 h ().

UC-MSC-EVs attenuate UVB-induced photoaging-associated changes in HSFs

To investigate the effects of UC-MSC-EVs on UVB-induced photoaging-associated changes in HSFs, their cytotoxicity was first assessed. The results showed that UC-MSC-EVs at concentrations of 0.33, 0.67, and 1.33 × 108 particles/mL were well tolerated by HSFs and were therefore selected for subsequent experiments (). UVB irradiation markedly reduced cell viability and increased the number of SA-β-Gal-positive senescent cells, whereas UC-MSC-EVs treatment significantly improved cell viability and reduced cellular senescence. The 0.33 × 108 particles/mL group showed the strongest effect, exceeding that observed with vitamin C ().

Because ECM degradation is a major feature of UVB-induced fibroblast dysfunction, we next examined ECM protein changes following UC-MSC-EVs treatment. Immunofluorescence staining showed that UVB exposure markedly reduced COL1A1 and COL3A1 protein expression, whereas UC-MSC-EVs treatment restored collagen I and collagen III fluorescence intensity in HSFs (). Consistently, ELISA analysis demonstrated that UC-MSC-EVs increased the levels of COL1A1, elastin, and hyaluronic acid, while reducing MMP3 levels in UVB-irradiated HSFs, and these changes showed similar trends to those observed in the Vtamin C-positive control group ().

UVB exposure also induced an inflammatory response, as indicated by elevated secretion of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6. Treatment with UC-MSC-EVs significantly reduced the levels of these pro-inflammatory cytokines, and the Vtamin C positive control produced a similar inhibitory response ().

To further examine transcriptional changes, qRT-PCR analysis was performed (). UVB irradiation altered the expression of genes involved in ECM remodeling, antioxidant defense, and barrier function, including COL1A1, COL3A1, Elastin, MMP1, MMP3, MMP9, HO-1, NQO1, SOD2, CAT, AQP3, and FLG. UC-MSC-EVs treatment partially or markedly reversed these UVB-induced transcriptional changes, broadly consistent with the protein-level and cellular responses observed in .

UC-MSC-EVs suppress OA-induced lipogenesis, lipid peroxidation, and androgenic responses in SZ95 sebocytes

To assess the effects of UC-MSC-EVs on sebocyte lipid metabolism, an OA-induced lipogenesis model was established in SZ95 sebocytes. UC-MSC-EVs showed no obvious cytotoxicity at concentrations ≤1.33 × 107 particles/mL, which were therefore used for subsequent assays (). ORO and Nile Red staining revealed that UC-MSC-EVs significantly attenuated OA-induced lipid deposition and lipid droplet accumulation, with changes occurring in the same direction as those observed with 10 μM isotretinoin (). Moreover, the results of BODIPY 581/591 C11 staining indicated that OA exposure also increased lipid peroxidation, whereas UC-MSC-EVs significantly reduced the oxidized BODIPY signal (green) (). 10 μM isotretinoin also suppressed OA-induced lipid peroxidation.

OA stimulation increased 5α-reductase activity and DHT production in SZ95 sebocytes, whereas UC-MSC-EVs treatment significantly reduced both readouts (). SRD5A1 and SREBP-1c expression were also decreased after EVs treatment (). The isotretinoin group showed the expected inhibitory effects on these androgenic and lipogenic responses. These results demonstrate that UC-MSC-EVs suppress sebocyte lipogenesis and lipid peroxidation, with concurrent downregulation of androgen-associated and SREBP-1c-related lipogenic responses.

UC-MSC-EVs inhibit melanogenesis in B16F10 cells and reduce pigmentation in zebrafish

We further evaluated the effects of UC-MSC-EVs on melanogenesis in B16F10 melanocytes, given that UV-associated oxidative stress can contribute to abnormal melanogenic responses. Based on B16F10 cell tolerability, doses of 0.33, 0.67, and 1.33 × 108 particles/mL were used for functional assays (). In 100 nM α-MSH-stimulated B16F10 cells, melanin content was elevated to 147.60% ± 15.98% of the control level, whereas UC-MSC-EVs at highest dose reversed it to 99.70% ± 7.98% (). Concurrently, tyrosinase activity, which was significantly upregulated by α-MSH, was dose-dependently suppressed by UC-MSC-EVs, reaching 33.53% ± 2.19% at the maximum concentration (). Notably, the inhibitory effects at this dose were comparable to those of the 1 mM arbutin positive control. At the transcriptional level, α-MSH stimulation upregulated the expression of MITF, TYR, TRP-1 and TRP-2, whereas UC-MSC-EVs treatment dose-dependently reduced their expression ().

UC-MSC-EVs also reduced melanin intensity in zebrafish (). ImageJ quantification demonstrated that UC-MSC-EVs treatment reduced zebrafish pigmentation relative to the 48-h baseline. Dose-dependent depigmenting effects were observed in both dorsal and lateral views. The most evident effect was observed at 6.67 × 108 particles/mL after 72 h of treatment, with melanin intensity reduced to 70.05% ± 8.0% (dorsal) and 62.31% ± 11.1% (lateral) relative to the control (). Together, the cellular and zebrafish results demonstrate consistent inhibitory effects of UC-MSC-EVs on melanogenesis.

UC-MSC-EVs enhance hDPC function and reduce androgen metabolism

To investigate the hair growth-related potential of UC-MSC-EVs, we assessed their effects on hDPCs. We first determined that UC-MSC-EVs concentrations up to 1.33 × 107 particles/mL did not compromise hDPC viability and thus used this range for subsequent assays (). UC-MSC-EVs treatment significantly increased scratch closure (). UC-MSC-EVs treatment also increased ALP activity, with the strongest effect observed at 0.67 × 107 particles/mL (approximately 148.22% ± 4.72% of the control; ). In parallel, 5α-reductase activity was dose-dependently reduced, with the greatest suppression at 1.33 × 107 particles/mL (approximately 86.71% ± 1.41% of the control; ). These results indicate enhanced dermal papilla cell functional activity accompanied by attenuated androgen metabolism in vitro.

UC-MSC-EVs decreased SRD5A1 expression while increasing VEGF, IGF-1, and FGF-7 expression (). Flow-cytometric analysis further showed a dose-dependent redistribution of hDPCs toward S and G2/M phases (), consistent with increased cell-cycle activity. These molecular and cellular changes are relevant to dermal papilla function, follicular growth support, and androgen responsiveness. Together, these findings show that UC-MSC-EVs influence multiple functional features of hDPCs, including migration, ALP activity, growth factor expression, cell-cycle distribution, and androgen metabolism.

Discussion

The present study provides a cross-model assessment of the biological activity of a single UC-MSC-EVs preparation. This is relevant because published skin-related MSC-EV studies differ substantially in MSC source, EVs preparation, dose, recipient cell type, and experimental conditions, making biological effects difficult to compare directly across studies. Recent studies have begun to evaluate MSC-EVs across more than one skin-related endpoint. For example, Zhang et al. assessed adipose- and umbilical cord-derived MSC-EVs in keratinocytes, fibroblasts, reconstructed skin, and animal photoaging models (), whereas Kee et al. examined UC-MSC-derived small EVs in relation to fibroblast ECM regulation and melanogenesis (). The present study extends this work by applying the same UC-MSC-EVs preparation across a broader set of complementary experimental systems and by including sebocyte lipid metabolism, lipid peroxidation, and androgen metabolism, which remain comparatively less studied. Together, these findings provide a cross-model biological activity profile of a single UC-MSC-EVs preparation across multiple skin-related processes.

UV-induced oxidative stress is a major driver of cellular senescence, inflammation, and ECM degradation in skin (; ). The responses observed in keratinocytes and fibroblasts are broadly consistent with the growing evidence that MSC-EVs can modify cellular responses to UV and oxidative stress. Zhang et al. reported that both adipose- and umbilical cord-derived MSC-EVs reduced UVB-induced ROS accumulation, senescence, inflammation, and ECM alterations in keratinocytes, dermal fibroblasts, and reconstructed skin, and further identified TIMP1/Notch1 signaling as a contributing mechanism (). Sun et al. similarly showed that UC-MSC-derived small EVs reduced oxidative stress and collagen degradation in UV-exposed dermal fibroblasts, with pregnancy zone protein linked to regulation of MMP-1 and DNA damage (). These mechanistic studies provide useful context for the antioxidant effects, cellular senescence, and ECM changes observed here. Our study extends these observations by evaluating stress responses together with keratinocyte migration and the barrier markers FLG and AQP3, which have established roles in epidermal barrier function and hydration (; ). The zebrafish caudal-fin assay, which captures tissue-scale dynamics during wound healing and regeneration (; ), provided tissue-level evidence of repair activity complementary to the cell-based migration assays.

Sebaceous gland homeostasis depends on tightly regulated lipid synthesis and androgenic control (; ). A less extensively studied finding was the effect of UC-MSC-EVs on sebocyte lipogenesis and lipid peroxidation in SZ95 sebocytes. Zhang et al. recently reported that adipose-derived MSC vesicles promoted SZ95 sebocyte proliferation and regulated SREBP-1 and lipid accumulation through PI3K/AKT signaling in a wound-healing context, with the response influenced by oxygen conditions (). In contrast, under the OA-induced lipogenic conditions used in our study, UC-MSC-EVs treatment reduced lipid accumulation and lipid peroxidation and was accompanied by lower SREBP-1c expression. In addition to using different MSC sources, the two studies addressed distinct sebocyte conditions: sebocyte activation upon tissue repair and OA-induced excessive lipid accumulation. The present study further extends published sebocyte work by examining androgen metabolism, including 5α-reductase activity, DHT production, and SRD5A1 expression. The different responses observed between the two studies may be related to differences in EVs source and sebocyte stimulation conditions. Our findings extend previous sebocyte studies by showing that UC-MSC-EVs also reduce lipid peroxidation and influence androgen metabolism in sebocytes.

Pigmentation is a major skin-related process influenced by oxidative and cellular stress (). Recent studies have shown that UC-MSC-derived small EVs reduced melanin synthesis in MNT-1 melanoma cells, although tyrosinase activity was not significantly altered (). By comparison, the present study showed concurrent decreases in melanin content, tyrosinase activity, and MITF, TYR, TRP-1, and TRP-2 expression in α-MSH-stimulated B16F10 cells. This pattern is closer to the findings of Lee et al., who reported reductions in melanin content, tyrosinase activity, and melanogenic markers following treatment of B16F10 cells with fibroblast-derived EVs (). Importantly, EVs effects on pigmentation are not uniformly inhibitory. Wang et al. found that MSC-derived vesicles promoted melanogenesis in primary melanocytes exposed to H2O2 while protecting the cells from oxidative injury (). The different directions of these responses suggest that EVs regulation of pigmentation is strongly influenced by recipient-cell state, experimental stimulus, EVs source, and dose. In the present study, the reduction in zebrafish pigmentation provided organism-level support for the anti-melanogenic effects observed in the α-MSH-stimulated B16F10 model.

Dermal papilla cells are key regulators of hair follicle development and cycling (; ), and both growth factor signaling and androgen metabolism are important determinants of their function in androgenetic alopecia (; ). Our findings are broadly consistent with recent studies of UC-MSC-derived EVs in hDPCs. Chen et al. showed that human UC-MSC-derived vesicles enhanced hDPC growth and increased the proportion of cells in S and G2/M phases, with PI3K/Akt/GSK-3β signaling implicated in the proliferative response (). The similar cell-cycle redistribution observed in our study supports this growth-related effect, while the changes in migration, ALP activity, VEGF, IGF-1, and FGF-7 extend the comparison to other aspects of hDPC function. More recently, Yu et al. demonstrated hair-follicle enlargement and anagen-associated responses in a DHT-induced androgenetic alopecia mouse model and linked these effects to EVs-enriched miRNAs and Wnt/β-catenin signaling (). In contrast to that in vivo study, the present work specifically examined androgen metabolism within cultured hDPCs and found reduced 5α-reductase activity and SRD5A1 expression in parallel with growth-supportive responses. Notably, our study examined growth-supportive functions and androgen metabolism within the same hDPC system, adding an androgen-regulatory dimension to previous reports of UC-MSC-EVs activity in dermal papilla cells.

In summary, this study defines a multi-model biological activity profile of a characterized UC-MSC-EVs preparation across several skin-related processes. In addition to the previously reported effects of MSC-EVs on oxidative stress, epidermal repair, and ECM regulation, our findings extend their biological activity to sebocyte lipogenesis, lipid peroxidation, androgen metabolism, melanogenesis, and dermal papilla cell function. The sebocyte results are particularly notable because lipid peroxidation and androgen metabolism have received comparatively limited attention in previous MSC-EVs studies. Further work will be useful to identify the EVs components underlying these activities and to determine their dose-response relationships and relevance in more complex skin models.
