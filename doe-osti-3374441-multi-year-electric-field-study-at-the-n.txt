1.0 Summary
 The Global Electric Circuit (GEC) of the atmosphere provides a unique perspective on Earth’s changing
 climate. Monitoring this global electrical signature provides details of the global nature of electrified
 clouds and thunderstorms. The North Slope of Alaska (NSA) is a unique location for collecting these
 electric field measurements. Besides being at the opposite pole from many previous electric field
 measurements taken at Russia’s Vostok station in Antarctica, this site provides a rare opportunity to use
 other instruments at the U.S. Department of Energy Atmospheric Radiation Measurement (ARM) User
 Facility’s NSA observatory at Utqiaġvik (formerly known as Barrow), such as the Ka-band ARM Zenith
 Radar (KAZR), upward-facing micropulse lidar (MPL), vertical profile of meteorological measures, and
 other aerosol measurements. Since 2017, Texas A&M University at Corpus Christi (TAMUCC) has
 established an observational instrument package, including CS110 electric fields and anemometer, at
 ARM’s NSA observatory at Utqiaġvik. This enabled the unique opportunity to not only provide
 information about the global signature of the GEC, but also the physical inputs to the local electric field,
 by analyzing the physical properties of the simultaneous cloud, wind, and aerosol properties occurring
 with the vertical electric field.

 The Multiple-Year Electric Field Study North Slope of Alaska (MYESNSA) field campaign has extended
 the arctic atmospheric electric field observation from 2021 to 2026. During the field campaign, the
 TAMUCC team visited the ARM NSA site in 2021 and 2024 to conduct regular maintenance of the
 CS110 sensors to ensure the continuous collection of observations of the atmospheric vertical electric
 field with consistent ground calibrations. The instrument is remotely monitored through Beyond-Trust, a
 service provided by ARM. The near-real-time data are transferred to a server at TAMUCC for analysis.
 After combining and quality controlling, the data sets from 2019 to 2025 have been uploaded to the ARM
 Data Center and shared with the public. The observations in 2026 will be uploaded to the ARM data
 server at the end of this year.

 Using the MYESNSA data, the extreme electric field events at Utqiaġvik are examined with the KAZR
 radar reflectivity (Lavigne et al. 2022, McShane et al. 2023, Lavigne et al. 2025). The relationships
 between cloud properties at NSA during polar nights and the electric field are explored
 (Lavigne and Liu, 2023, Lavigne et al. 2023). Along with the electric field data collected at Wallop
 Island, Virginia, Corpus Christi, Texas, and the Southern Ocean, we have examined the similarities and
 differences of fair-weather events at different ground sites, then linked them to the airborne electric field
 observations and the global thunderstorms and electrified shower clouds (Liu et al. 2026).

 Though the first eight years of measurements from the One-Year Electric Field Study North Slope of
 Alaska (OYESNSA) and MYESNSA field campaigns have already provided insight into these important
 questions and goals, a longer-term, high-quality data set is needed to ultimately provide the critical
 climatology to understand this truly global and ever-changing system. That is why we have requested a
 three-year extension to the MYESNSA field campaign to lengthen this data set. With the new field
 campaign “Monitoring Atmospheric Electric Field at North Slope of Alaska (MAEF-NSA)” extending
 the electric field collection till 2029, we anticipate more findings in the interannual variations of arctic
 cloud properties with robust statistical and long-term observations.

2.0 Results from 2021-2025 Measurements

 2.1 Extreme Electric Field at Utqiaġvik, Alaska
 The Multiple-Year Electric Field Study North Slope of Alaska (MYESNSA) field campaign was
 established in 2017 in part to observe the significantly electrified clouds in the Arctic and to monitor any
 potential changes in these events under the changing climate. As a precursor to thunderstorm activity,
 significantly electrified clouds that do not produce lightning, known as Electrified Shower Clouds
 (ESCs), are more prevalent than thunderstorms in the Arctic. During the years of 2017-2021, 79
 significantly electrified days with the maximum surface vertical electric field >+2,000 V/m are identified
 in Utqiaġvik, Alaska using a ground-based CS110 electric field mill, compared to just one thunderstorm
 day. All 79-cases are found between May and November. The reflectivity observations from the KAZR
 located within 100 m of the electric field mill are analyzed with the electric field observations. A
 composite analysis of the electric field (Ez) versus the reflectivity at -5oC shows that at least 5 dBZ is
 needed to observe a significant Ez of greater than +2,000 V/m. A case study analysis of the significantly
 electrified events displays classical convective-like events, including anvil, stratiform (bright band), and
 convective core regions. Several of these events include Ez values exceeding 15,000 V/m with no
 electrical breakdown. Multiple electrified events occur with the temperature of the entire column < 0 C,
 indicating highly electrified snowfall in the region. Assessment of the cloud properties that lead to these
 electrified events in the Arctic allows for an unprecedented understanding of the atmospheric electricity in
 the Arctic with a unique perspective. This research has been presented at the American Geophysical
 Union (Lavigne et al. 2022) and American Meteorological Society (McShane et al. 2023) meetings.

2.2 Comparisons of the Diurnal Variations of the Fair-Weather
 Electric Field with Global Electrified Clouds
The concept of the GEC is that the global electrified cloud acts as a “battery” to drive the GEC. It has
been validated with observations collected by ground-based, airborne, and spaceborne instruments in the
past. The relationships between the fair-weather electric field on the ground and global thunderstorms
have been verified mostly with diurnal variations at UTC time following the famous “Carnegie Curve”.
However, due to the complexity in local environments and the variety of electrified clouds around globe,
instantaneous relationships between the two are difficult to establish. In this project, we summarize the
correlations among simultaneous ground-based electric field observations at four sites at Utqiaġvik,
Corpus Christi, Wallops Island, and the Ross Sea to validate the “global” nature of the GEC (Figure 2,
Liu et al. 2026). Only small fractions of fair-weather periods are found having good correlations in the
electric fields among different sites, which demonstrate the complex local effects in these observations.
Next, we estimate the upward electric current using a statistical approximation from the cloud ice water
content in the mixed-phase levels derived using the Global Precipitation Mission (GPM) Ku-band
precipitation radar observations during 2014-2024. Results show a consistent diurnal variation with the
“Carnegie Curve” if the electrified clouds from the tropics and subtropics are included and those at high
latitudes are excluded. To examine the role of different types of electrified cloud play in the GEC,
airborne electric field observations from multiple field campaigns are compared. The vertical electric field
measurements on top of extra-tropical cyclones by airborne field mills are in general lower than those
over electrified clouds in the tropics and subtropics. This suggest that it is important to consider the
variations in contributions from different types of electrified clouds to the GEC.

Figure 2. Left) Electric field data are collected from four different locations: Utqiaġvik, Alaska
            (71.29°N, 156.78°W), Wallops Island (37.93°N, 75.46°W), Virginia, Corpus Christi
 (27.71°N, 97.32°W), Texas, and a 2.5-month cruise off the coast of Antarctica as shown in

 Ross Sea cruise also show a weaker amplitude of Ez variations and different phase,
 suggesting strong local effects.

2.3 Potential Impact of the GEC on the Properties of Arctic Cloud in
 Polar Night
We published a paper describing the potential influence that the fair-weather return current has on local
cloud properties in Utqiaġvik, Alaska during the polar night (Lavigne and Liu 2023). Using our vertical
electric field measurements, alongside the KAZR, MPL, sky radiometers on stand for downwelling
radiation (SKYRAD), and ceilometer located at the NSA site, we found that cloud and radiation
properties showed similar diurnal variability to that of the magnitude of the fair-weather return current
(Figure 3). During periods of maximum intensity of the fair-weather return current, persistent layered
clouds tended to be taller, thicker, and precipitate more numerous particles on the surface when compared
to periods of weaker fair-weather return magnitude. Furthermore, during the maxima in fair-weather
return current intensity, the downwelling longwave irradiance is found to be weaker, caused by the cloud
layer forming higher in the column. This leads to a slight diurnal temperature variability on the surface
during the polar night. Interestingly, this diurnal surface temperature variability and local cloud properties
during the polar night may indeed be influenced by the aggregate thunderstorm and electrified clouds
occurring all around the globe at that instance. Much more data at the site is needed to robustly confirm
this.

2.4 Summary of Key Results
 • The MYESNSA field campaign has collected much-needed electric field measurements in the
 Northern Hemisphere at a latitude of 71oN. Comparing MYESNSA observations with data from other
 sites show remarkable similarities between the diurnal variations of the fair-weather electric field at
 the North and South Poles, as well as the subtropics, indicating a truly global system.

 4

  • Using the MPL, a method for separating fair-weather from locally influenced electric field periods
 was created. Very similar inter-timestep variability was observed on the order of minutes to hours at
 the Utqiaġvik, Alaska and Corpus Christi, Texas sites. This further emphasizes the global nature of
 the system and possibly allows for future real-time global cloud analysis.
  • By combining the KAZR radar data, the extreme electric field events are investigated and suggest the
 existence of non-lightning but strongly electrified convective clouds in the Arctic.
  • During polar night, the properties of cloud at the NSA site show diurnal variations that are close to
 the diurnal variation of the fair-weather electric fields. These results imply a potential impact of the
 GEC on cloud in the Arctic.

4.0 Lessons Learned that Motivate Longer-Term
 Observations at NSA
  • Because the fair-weather electric field is an indicator of the global electric circuit system, it may be
 used to monitor global thunderstorm activities. To make this a valuable measure, long-term,
 consistent measurement is required. Based on the past eight years of operation, we believe it is
 possible to maintain a relatively cheap CS110 at the NSA observatory and collect reliable data
 continuously for a long term with little effort. This is an important step toward establishing a reliable
 way to monitor the change of electric systems both locally in the Arctic and globally from the GEC.
  • Compared to the simultaneous electric field measurements at Corpus Christi, Texas, Wallops Island,
 Virginia, and Utqiaġvik, Alaska, we have found similarities in the mean fair-weather electric field
 diurnal variations in general. However, there are also differences from Wallops Island between
 August and October, which could be related to the local effect. This leads to important next steps of
 understanding the local influences on the electric field. While more space missions are planned to be
 launched in high latitudes, it is important to understand these strong near-surface atmospheric electric
 fields for aviation safety.
  • It is important to monitor the long-term variations of the arctic cloud to understand the impact of the
 fast-warming environment on the local ecosystem. Atmospheric electric field is direct related to the
 local convective cloud in summertime, and the fair-weather electric field from the global electric
 circuit could have significant influences on cloud properties in winter.
