Scaling a Lipid-Lowering Clinical Decision Support Intervention Across U.S. Health Systems: Lessons Learned from the PROMPT-LIPID Multicenter Study
Authors: Bashar Kadhim, Farah Yasmin, Ralph Riello, Ambrish A Pandit, David Davidson, Erica Davis, Gemme Campbell Salome, JA Randy Englert, Laney K. Jones, Melissa Martin, F.Will Pohlman, Chloe Ferris, Thomas M. Maddox, Nihar R. Desai, Sharath Subramanian, Yu Yamamoto, Tariq Ahmad, F.Perry Wilson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe PRagmatic study Of Messaging to Providers about Treatment of HyperLIPIDemia (PROMPT-LIPID) single-system pragmatic, randomized trial demonstrated that an electronic health record (EHR)-embedded clinical decision support (CDS) intervention increased lipid-lowering therapy (LLT) intensification among patients with very high-risk atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD). To support broader dissemination, the parent PROMPT-LIPID Multicenter Scaling Study uses a prospective stepped-wedge design to evaluate the clinical effectiveness of the intervention across U.S. health systems using the Epic EHR platform. The present study describes the multicenter pre-implementation and deployment processes, including the barriers, mitigation strategies, and infrastructure associated with adapting and activating the PROMPT-LIPID CDS intervention across participating health systems.MethodsWe conducted a narrative synthesis of implementation lessons identified through investigator and site meetings, site reports, regulatory and contracting documentation, CDS build and validation activities, pharmacy workflow reviews, and implementation milestone records. Findings were organized into three practical domains: site onboarding and administrative processes, technological implementation, and clinical and operational workflow integration.ResultsEight external health systems were initially engaged, of which three (37.5%) proceeded to implementation and reached active-alert go-live. Implementation challenges clustered into three areas. Site-onboarding and administrative challenges included variable contracting and regulatory timelines, multilevel governance requirements, and difficulties sustaining site engagement; these were addressed through parallel initiation of regulatory and build activities, centralized coordination, standardized onboarding, and regular cross-site communication. Technological challenges included heterogeneous Epic configurations, inconsistent CDS logic and data definitions, dependence on local analytics, data-harmonization requirements, and secure data-transfer constraints. These were mitigated through site-specific mapping, a prepackaged XML toolkit, standardized data definitions, and a parameterized SQL framework deployed through Epic Template Reports. Operational challenges included clinician concerns regarding workflow integration, alert fatigue, competing priorities, variation in prescribing practices, and medication-access barriers. These were addressed through iterative CDS refinement, stakeholder engagement, silent validation, and linkage of CDS recommendations to pharmacy support pathways.ConclusionsThis manuscript presents an early narrative report of multicenter implementation lessons from the PROMPT-LIPID CDS intervention, describing practical barriers, solutions, and deployment infrastructure encountered across three external health systems that reached implementation. These findings may inform future multicenter deployment of EHR-based lipid-management CDS interventions; however, broader evaluation is needed to establish the generalizability, reproducibility, and sustainability of this approach. Clinical effectiveness and implementation outcomes, including LLT intensification, alert-firing, clinician-action, and adoption rates from the parent stepped-wedge study, will be reported separately.

Introduction

Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) remains a leading contributor to morbidity and mortality in the United States (U.S.), with nearly half of the population affected by cardiovascular disease (CVD) and a substantial proportion attributable to ASCVD[[1], [2]]. Cumulative exposure to elevated low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) is a well-established causative, modifiable risk factor; patients with very high-risk (VHR) ASCVD derive significant cardioprotective benefits from intensive lipid-lowering therapy (LLT)[3]. Despite compelling evidence and strong contemporary guideline recommendations targeting LDL-C < 55 mg/dL, fewer than one-third of eligible patients ever attain recommended LDL-C thresholds—underscoring an urgent unmet need for scalable, system-level interventions to close this knowledge-translation gap [[4], [5], [6]]. We previously demonstrated that the PRagmatic study Of Messaging to Providers about Treatment of HyperLIPIDemia (PROMPT-LIPID) clinical decision support (CDS) toolkit, embedded within the electronic health record (EHR) system, increased guideline-concordant LLT intensification, defined in the trial as initiation or intensification of high-intensity statin therapy, ezetimibe, or proprotein convertase subtilisin/Kexin type 9 inhibitors [PCSK9i], by 42% among 2500 patients with VHR ASCVD in a single-system randomized trial [7,8].

The successful translation of CDS interventions from single-system process improvement to multicenter implementation study is influenced by complex contextual, organizational, and workflow-related factors that directly affect adoption and sustainability. Understanding these operational requirements is essential for determining whether a CDS intervention can be adapted and deployed beyond the environment in which it was originally developed.

To support national dissemination, the parent PROMPT-LIPID Multicenter Study was launched as a prospective, multicenter stepped-wedge evaluation of a refined PROMPT-LIPID CDS toolkit[7]. A comprehensive description of the parent study methodology is provided in the supplementary protocol. The present manuscript focuses on the pre-implementation and deployment processes across participating health systems. It describes the principal site-onboarding and administrative, technological, and clinical and operational challenges encountered during multicenter deployment across geographically diverse U.S. health systems using a common EHR platform, together with the mitigation strategies and infrastructure developed to support standardized, high-fidelity implementation. These lessons may inform future efforts to deploy pragmatic EHR-based cardiovascular CDS interventions across health systems. Clinical effectiveness and prespecified implementation outcomes from the parent stepped-wedge study, evaluated using the RE-AIM framework, including reach, alert exposure, clinician engagement, and adoption, will be reported separately.

Methods

Study design and setting

The PROMPT-LIPID Multicenter Study was designed as a prospective, multicenter stepped-wedge evaluation of an EHR-embedded clinical decision support (CDS) intervention across four U.S. health systems using Epic (Epic Systems Corporation®, Verona, WI)[[7], [8], [9]]. The parent PROMPT-LIPID Multicenter Study prespecified clinical effectiveness and implementation-related outcomes organized according to the RE-AIM framework. These include LLT intensification, LDL-C goal attainment, clinical events, patient reach, alert exposure, clinician engagement and adoption, reasons for alert deferral, and site-level deployment costs. The outcomes, data sources, planned analyses, and reporting status are summarized in Supplementary Table S1. These outcomes will be reported separately after completion of the study follow-up. The present manuscript focuses on the pre-implementation and deployment experience and provides a narrative synthesis of operational lessons related to site onboarding, regulatory and governance processes, EHR build and adaptation, silent validation, and active-alert go-live across participating sites.

Yale New Haven Health, where the CDS intervention had been developed and tested in the original randomized clinical trial (RCT), served as the coordinating site for the multicenter implementation. The CDS remained active at Yale following completion of the original RCT; however, modifications were made to reflect updated lipid management recommendations from the 2026 ACC/AHA guidelines, and this updated Yale build served as the base case for implementation at three external participating health systems. The intervention was deployed across the three external participating health sites using a stepped-wedge implementation approach, with staggered site-level activation following completion of local regulatory, technical, governance, and operational approvals. Each participating site completed local institutional review board (IRB) review and data governance processes, while the study was coordinated centrally by Yale School of Medicine. Sites were onboarded according to individual deployment schedules, beginning with a 3-month silent validation period for baseline data collection, followed by activation of provider-facing CDS alerts. During the silent validation period, CDS logic was triggered, and data were collected in the background, but alerts were not displayed to clinicians. Intervention

The CDS intervention consisted of an interruptive Our Practice Alert (OPA) embedded within the Epic EHR, designed to identify patients with VHR ASCVD who were not receiving optimal LLT. When triggered, the OPA provided clinicians with patient-specific information incorporated into the intervention logic at the time of implementation, including high-intensity statins, ezetimibe, and PCSK9 inhibitor therapy. The CDS logic incorporated contemporary LDL-C targets (<55 mg/dL) and was adapted at each site to reflect local formularies and workflow requirements, with flexibility to incorporate additional therapies and future guideline updates.

The implementation strategy followed a structured, multiphase approach that included site engagement and readiness assessment, regulatory and contracting activities, governance approval, local EHR build and adaptation, technical testing, silent validation, active-alert go-live, and post-deployment monitoring. Narrative synthesis of implementation lessons

Implementation challenges were identified through routine multicenter investigator meetings, site-specific reports, regulatory and contracting documentation, CDS build and validation activities, pharmacy workflow reviews, and implementation milestone records. These sources reflected the experiences of site investigators, clinical champions, EHR and informatics personnel, regulatory and contracting teams, pharmacy stakeholders, data analysts, and members of the Yale coordinating team.

The available documentation and stakeholder feedback were reviewed narratively to identify recurring barriers, adaptations, mitigation strategies, and operational lessons encountered during multicenter deployment. The identified lessons were iteratively refined through discussion among members of the coordinating team and reviewed with participating site investigators for contextual accuracy. The final findings were categorized into three practical domains: site onboarding and administrative processes; technological implementation, including EHR build, CDS deployment, data extraction, and harmonization; and clinical and operational workflow integration, including pharmacy and medication-access processes.

Ethical considerations

The PROMPT-LIPID Multicenter study was governed by a central institutional review board approval process through the Biomedical Research Alliance of New York Institutional Review Board (BRANY). Yale University participation was approved under BRANY IRB File 24–08–144, Master File #2000,037,461. Participating health systems completed required local institutional approvals and data use agreements before implementation. Study data were stored centrally at the Yale School of Medicine Clinical and Translational Research Accelerator.

Implementation activities were preceded by multisite introductory meetings held in March and April 2024, followed by a formal kickoff meeting with committed sites in April 2025. The coordinating team used a centralized implementation approach that included central institutional review board coordination through BRANY, standardized CDS specifications, onboarding resources, and technical support from the Yale implementation team.

Although anticipated site effort for regulatory activities was modest, substantial variability was observed across participating organizations in regulatory approvals, contracting processes, governance reviews, and EHR implementation timelines. Among the three external implementation sites, the median time from initial site engagement to regulatory approval was 253 days (range, 162–318 days), and the median time from regulatory approval to CDS build initiation was 264 days (range, 164–285 days). Overall, the median time from regulatory approval to active-alert go-live was 417 days (range, 366–514 days). Barriers to site participation and study inclusion

Among the five health systems that did not proceed beyond the initial engagement and onboarding stages, several practical challenges were reported. These included inadequate funding to support local analyst effort and implementation activities, inability to identify a committed clinical champion to lead adoption, and competing institutional priorities that limited capacity to participate. Additional logistical barriers were also reported, including ongoing health system mergers and transitions that delayed governance approvals, as well as interoperability constraints that complicated EHR integration and CDS deployment. Data transfer infrastructure presented an additional barrier, as the study required secure Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA)-compliant transmission of data to the central coordinating site. Several sites lacked an approved data transfer method or a third-party vendor compatible with the central site’s data transfer requirements. Where a mismatch existed, the proposed transfer method required independent evaluation by a third-party security vendor to confirm alignment with each site’s institutional security requirements, a process that increased time burden and contributed to attrition among sites unwilling or unable to absorb the resulting delay. These findings highlight that organizational readiness, local resources, leadership engagement, technical compatibility, and secure data-transfer capacity were important determinants of whether initially engaged sites progressed to implementation.

Site-Onboarding and administrative challenges

Contracting and data-use agreements (DUA)

Execution of data-use agreements (DUA) between the coordinating center (i.e., Yale University) and participating institutions represented a major operational determinant of study initiation. The variability in institutional contracting requirements, ranging from acceptance of a standard DUA to full-contractual review due to the presence of industry sponsorship, became the rate-limiting factor for onboarding and OPA activation at select sites. To mitigate delays, contracting workflows were initiated upon preliminary site interest and conducted in parallel with IRB reliance approval, data governance, and Epic-build discussions, enabling sites to become activation-ready following contract execution. This multipronged approach was essential, given our study design required each site to complete an initial 3-month silent period before activating the PROMPT-LIPID CDS tool.

Central IRB coordination and reliance agreements

To address heterogeneity in local IRB workflows, the study team established a central IRB reliance agreement model wherein the coordinating center obtains approval for a master protocol, allowing all participating organizations to join as collaborating sites[10]. Within this framework, all protocol amendments are managed by the coordinating center with automatic propagation across all approved sites via a master institutional authorization agreement. This centralized model ensured site-wide consistency and enabled a uniform, efficient, and ethical approach that reduced regulatory delays. Participation in the central IRB reliance model required execution of reliance agreements between central and local IRBs, and the coordinating center provided real-time regulatory support during the reliance and IRB approval process.

Stakeholder engagement, data governance committees, and maintenance of project momentum

Each participating institution required engagement across numerous site-specific stakeholders (including information technology (IT), data privacy and security, EHR change management, cardiology/primary care, pharmacy, and OPA review). Coordinating buy-in while mitigating concerns regarding staffing, resources, and technical feasibility to implement an OPA adapted to site-specific clinical workflow frequently stalled implementation. Multiple committees required sequential approval before go-live, reflecting complex governance structures that contributed to delays in implementation timelines.

A modest institutional start-up budget helped defray any upfront costs for IRB review or other research fees, as well as any person-hours for IT expertise. These steps were essential to facilitate the timely navigation of internal approval procedures and helped reassure sites regarding the low operational burden, especially those concerned about personnel availability. Weekly site check-ins supplemented by monthly sponsor meetings with site-investigators maintained accountability and sustained project momentum.

Minimizing site burden through central coordination

Sites described substantial operational burden during onboarding, including analyst time required for data extraction and phenotype mapping, prolonged IRB procedures, and operational resource constraints. Sites also encountered challenges with consistent deployment across health systems, driven by variability in local EHR configurations, governance structures, and pharmacy workflows. Large multicenter stepped-wedge evaluations require careful coordination to ensure fidelity, consistent exposure definitions (i.e., silent and active periods), uniform follow-up, and alignment of study procedures across participating centers.

The PROMPT-LIPID Multicenter adopted a low-burden approach (i.e., centralizing resource-intensive activities to the coordinating center) that reduced site-level implementation overhead and facilitated consistent deployment, enabling active surveillance of OPA engagement, LLT intensification, and other quality improvement metrics across multiple diverse healthcare systems in near real-time. A structured pre-implementation “silent” phase, during which alerts fired in the background without clinician visibility, helped identify incorrect alert triggers and refine CDS logic before activation. In addition, regular cross-site coordination, including weekly meetings, facilitated the resolution of site-specific challenges and supported consistent implementation across participating institutions.

Technological challenges in prompt-lipid CDS toolkit implementation and data integration across heterogeneous epic systems

Variation in EHR build, infrastructure, and local CDS customization

Although participating sites used a common EHR platform (Epic Systems Corporation®; Verona, WI), each configuration was instance specific. Historical EHR build decisions, legacy system migrations, and unique institutional workflows (i.e., order preference lists, formulary considerations, and ASCVD value sets) influenced multiple aspects of the CDS intervention, including alert triggers, allergy and other grouper definitions, and default LLT prescribing parameters. Some systems lacked advanced CDS functionality for conditional SmartSet displays or flexible OPA programming logic, necessitating site-specific adaptations to ensure appropriate SmartSet suppression, encounter-sensitive logic, and alert prioritization functioned reliably.

As a result, CDS packages were transferred near “plug-and-play” but required careful calibration and discrete data remapping across sites. To ensure consistent deployment, the Yale informatics team developed a standardized, portable PROMPT-LIPID CDS toolkit eXtensible Markup Language (XML) package featuring an interruptive or passive OPA paired with a dynamically adaptive SmartSet tailored to each site’s Epic environment. A detailed mapping checklist and unified data dictionary guided sites to link laboratory results, LLT, VHR ASCVD groupers, and suppression logic. The Yale coordinating team engaged local Epic analysts to install CDS components, reconcile preference lists, refine SmartSet options to site formularies, and standardize OPA trigger logic for participating institutions. Before go-live, sites completed structured quality assurance (QA) testing with simulated patients, followed by a “silent period” to validate CDS functionality.

Fidelity monitoring and on-the-ground implementation barriers

Maintaining fidelity after deployment was equally essential. In this context, fidelity refers to ensuring that the PROMPT-LIPID CDS intervention was delivered consistently and as intended across participating sites, including accurate alert triggering, appropriate display of the OPA and linked SmartSet, preservation of the intended clinical workflow, and consistent capture of provider responses. With a broad provider sample across multiple institutions, the team implemented a comprehensive monitoring plan including monthly progress reports, OPA and SmartSet engagement audits, and qualitative categorization of dismissal rationale. These safeguards ensured the PROMPT-LIPID CDS toolkit was deliberately deployed and fully integrated into local workflows, maintaining consistent provider experience across heterogeneous Epic environments.

Multicenter data extraction, harmonization, and CDS infrastructure

Despite using the same EHR system, each site depended upon local analysts to translate study specifications (i.e., VHR ASCVD definitions, LDC-C thresholds, etc.) into independent Structured Query Logic (SQL) statements within Clarity or Caboodle. This resulted in redundant workflows and extensive analyst effort for highly variable and fragmented data output. Cross-site variation in discrete data elements like LDL-C laboratory codes, LLT print groups, and coding prefix conventions yielded inconsistent data extractions, significant data harmonization burden, and incomplete cohort identification. Consequently, even centrally distributed, uniform SQL scripts returned data with key field omissions due to misaligned local data models. Slow, ticket-based analytics workflows further delayed execution of standardized SQL scripts and routine cohort updates, hindering real-time monitoring and posing challenges for the stepped-wedge design, which required sequential site activation.

To mitigate these issues, the coordinating team developed the following solutions:•A parametrized SQL Framework Shared Across All Sites: A unified, parameterized SQL extraction framework embedded directly into Epic Clarity, a SQL server-based relational database within the Cogito suite, packaged as an Epic Template Report for each site to execute at-will. The tool featured flexible SQL logic individualized to each institution via simple parameter mapping.•Use of Epic Template Report to Run SQL Query Directly on Caboodle/Clarity: Once installed, unified SQL queries enabled local investigators to securely extract deidentified flat file data without additional analytics support. This transformative innovation circumvented analyst queues to furnish consistent and clean data outputs for all participating institutions.•Leveraging Epic Report Workbench for Site-Level Data Element Customization: Building on the unified SQL framework, our implementation leveraged Epic's Report Workbench to give each institution's security and compliance teams direct control over which data elements were included in the final output without requiring any modification to the underlying SQL query. Authorized non-technical staff could select or deselect columns flagged as potentially identifiable at their institution with a single click, effectively tailoring the report's output to meet local de-identification standards. This design was a critical operational advantage: it eliminated the need for a data analyst to interpret, modify, and re-validate the query at each site; a process that introduces risk of unintended, unreviewed changes to data structure and output balance. By decoupling column selection from query logic, we preserved the integrity and consistency of the underlying data pipeline while empowering institutions to meet their own compliance requirements autonomously.•Dynamic “Search Criteria” Layer for Cross-Site Data Variation: To account for variation in data storage, the team incorporated a dynamic search interface to the Epic Template Report. This feature allowed site investigators to input any concept term, such as medication or diagnosis. For example, searching “ezetimibe” would automatically retrieve all corresponding data elements, field identifications, and use frequencies within the local environment. Unified Data Architecture Enabling Streamlined Analytics and Direct REDCap Integration: The standardized data outputs generated by our cross-institutional pipeline conferred two compounding operational advantages. First, because all sites produced identically formatted flat files, data could be mapped directly to predefined REDCap instruments and uploaded in bulk with a single click, thus eliminating manual data entry and its associated time burden and transcription errors. Second, the uniformity of the data structure eliminated the need for site-specific data harmonization or manipulation before analysis. A single data pipeline and unified statistical codebase could be applied directly to the combined multi-site dataset, removing a common source of analytic overhead, reducing the risk of harmonization errors, and ensuring that results were directly comparable across institutions. Together, these properties transformed a traditionally fragmented multi-site data workflow into a cohesive, reproducible, and analytically ready infrastructure.

Establishing HIPAA-Compliant, reproducible multicenter data-transfer workflow

Successful multi-site data transfer to the coordinating center required idempotent, repeated data pulls via reliable, HIPAA-compliant mechanisms for deidentification and secure transmission. This was achieved by applying the HIPAA Safe Harbor method, removing all eighteen identifiers and assigning a unique 256-bit hashed study ID that remained locally linked to patient identities. The coordinating center implemented an encrypted cloud server (Wasabi Technologies, Inc.; Boston, MA) as the storage and transfer mechanism, allowing sites to upload their monthly extracts directly and securely. To accommodate variability in institutional security preferences and minimize onboarding delays, the workflow was designed to support Secure File Transfer Protocol (SFTP) as an alternative transmission pathway for sites whose security teams were more familiar with or had already approved this technology, hence eliminating the need for third-party risk assessments and avoiding site-level delays associated with adopting unfamiliar platforms. This dual-pathway approach ensured that all participating institutions could engage without operational friction, regardless of their existing infrastructure or compliance posture. The overall workflow provided a consistent, regulatory-compliant, and highly reproducible method for deidentified data transmission across multiple health systems. The coordinating center (i.e., Yale) maintained version-controlled datasets with detailed data dictionaries to facilitate accessibility.

Clinical and operational challenges to enable real-world LLT intensification

Provider engagement and workflow integration across sites

Provider engagement varied widely across centers, with concerns related to alert fatigue, competing clinical workloads, unfamiliarity with LLT intensification guidelines, and heterogeneous clinical practices between general and specialty settings. Frontline clinicians expressed concern about competing clinical priorities affecting engagement with alerts and emphasized the need for actionable and clinically relevant recommendations. Clinical leadership raised concerns regarding potential alert fatigue and the impact on productivity, while informatics teams emphasized data security and system performance.

As a countermeasure, we leveraged the prior PROMPT-LIPID CDS prototype to make several substantive improvements: reprogrammed identification logic to enhance the VHR ASCVD computational phenotype [11], updated target LDL-C thresholds to align with the latest clinical practice guidelines and performed further iterative refinement of heuristics and human-centered design principles with feedback from a multidisciplinary advisory group of domain experts. The OPA prominently displayed the most recent and relevant clinical information (i.e., LDL-C, total cholesterol, liver function tests, etc.) and provided tailored, guideline-concordant LLT recommendations via nested SmartSet. The ability to defer alerts with optional free-text justification allowed providers to preserve autonomy and quickly document reasons for deferring or dismissing the alert.

Variation in LLT prescribing culture across sites required adaptable integration of the PROMPT-LIPID CDS as a configurable adjunct to local workflows, enabling customization of OPA and SmartSet elements to accommodate existing protocols while preserving the core features of the intervention across sites. Educational sessions (i.e., pre-implementation demonstrations and feedback sessions), local clinical champions, and inclusion of site investigators in sponsor meetings served to increase provider engagement.

Pharmacy and medication-access challenges

Although PROMPT-LIPID enhanced LLT intensification, certain non-statin therapies like PCSK9i often required pharmacy benefits verification, prior authorizations (PAs) adjudication, or payer-mandated step edits. Health system-owned specialty pharmacies possess the infrastructure to routinely triage such payer hurdles on behalf of prescribers to optimize time-to-treatment. However, these comprehensive specialty services are not universally available among US healthcare systems. Hence, establishing a systematic pathway to support insurance authorization for PCSK9i prescribed through the PROMPT-LIPID CDS toolkit presented a major opportunity for medication access. Considerations for available specialty pharmacy resources, anticipated workflow redesign, and data-driven demonstrations of clinical and financial value for service extension were all pivotal to securing pharmacy stakeholder buy-in.

Linking CDS recommendations to medication-access infrastructure

To translate CDS-generated LLT recommendations into meaningful real-world LDL-C reductions, lipid-management CDS must be linked to medication-access infrastructure capable of addressing the downstream barriers that may prevent treatment initiation. This is particularly important for high-cost or access-sensitive therapies that require pharmacy benefits verification, prior authorization (PA), step-therapy documentation, affordability support, or specialty pharmacy coordination. Without such infrastructure, alerts may generate clinically appropriate recommendations that clinicians are unable to operationalize efficiently.

At Yale, this need was addressed through a pharmacoinformatic infrastructure known as Multi-Step Order Transmittal (MSOT), developed in collaboration with Yale Outpatient Pharmacy Services[12]. MSOT is an Epic pharmacy operations enhancement that automates the routing of selected electronic prescriptions to a centralized EHR-embedded processing queue for pharmacy benefits verification and insurance PA adjudication. Through MSOT, prescriptions for high-cost or access-sensitive lipid-lowering therapies can populate a workflow queue managed by pharmacy liaison staff trained to identify medication-access barriers and provide patient assistance. Integrating the PROMPT-LIPID CDS toolkit with this specialty pharmacy workflow supported continuity between prescribing recommendations and the processes required for medication access while reducing administrative burden on clinicians.

Although MSOT represents the local approach used at Yale, the broader operational lesson is applicable across health systems: lipid-management CDS should be paired with a defined pathway for benefits verification, prior authorization support, affordability assessment, and medication fulfillment. The specific model may vary according to locally available resources and may include an integrated specialty pharmacy, centralized authorization team, pharmacy liaison, or another medication-access workflow.

Discussion

The multicenter implementation of the PROMPT-LIPID toolkit offers initial implementation lessons relevant to scaling EHR-embedded cardiovascular CDS interventions across diverse healthcare environments. We identified recurring challenges related to site onboarding, governance, contracting, EHR configuration, data infrastructure, clinician workflow, and pharmacy operations, together with practical strategies used to address them across three geographically distinct U.S. health systems.

Because Yale served as the originating health system for the prior PROMPT-LIPID randomized trial and subsequently transitioned into the multicenter implementation phase, its updated CDS build served as the base case for site adaptation and cross-site comparison. Although all participating institutions utilized the Epic EHR platform, substantial variation was observed in governance structures, regulatory and contracting processes, pharmacy workflows, data architectures, and local implementation resources. Importantly, most barriers were not unique to individual institutions but recurred across sites, highlighting the need for a structured, generalizable implementation framework rather than isolated, site-specific solutions alone.

Our findings demonstrate that barriers to multicenter CDS deployment extend beyond technical build considerations. The organizational and administrative factors comprising governance approvals, contracting, institutional review requirements, and stakeholder engagement emerged as major determinants of implementation timelines. Time from initial site engagement to regulatory approval varied nearly twofold across institutions, despite deployment of a standardized intervention. These findings underscore that successful multicenter CDS implementation is influenced as much by organizational readiness and governance infrastructure as by the technical configuration of the CDS tool itself. The centralized IRB model, parallel initiation of contracting and regulatory activities, standardized onboarding procedures, and coordinated cross-site communication developed through PROMPT-LIPID may offer practical guidance for future multisite implementation initiatives.

Our findings reinforce the longstanding observations that the primary challenge in scaling CDS interventions is not the underlying clinical evidence itself, but the ability to translate, implement, and sustain that knowledge across heterogeneous healthcare environments. Differences in local EHR configurations, data models, and reporting infrastructure created substantial challenges for standardized deployment. Prior work identified similar barriers to scalable CDS implementation arising from variation in organizational workflows, clinical data models, resource availability, and local EHR architectures[13].

Prior work on dissemination of the ePneumonia CDS platform demonstrated that successful transfer of an effective EHR-based intervention across health systems requires substantial attention to interoperability, local adaptation, workflow integration, and implementation infrastructure beyond the core clinical logic itself[14]. Similarly, despite the use of a common EHR platform, participating PROMPT-LIPID sites demonstrated considerable heterogeneity in laboratory mapping, medication groupers, CDS configurations, and local data infrastructures. To address these challenges, we developed a standardized CDS toolkit, a parameterized SQL framework, Epic Template Reports, and a harmonized data architecture capable of generating reproducible outputs across institutions[15]. These resources transformed what is traditionally a site-specific informatics effort into reusable implementation infrastructure that may be adaptable to future CDS interventions beyond lipid management, such as chronic kidney disease, diabetes, heart failure, hypertension, and other cardiovascular conditions. Importantly, our experience reinforces the notion that standards alone are insufficient for successful CDS dissemination. Rather, scalable implementation requires practical tooling, centralized support, and operational infrastructure that make adoption feasible within real-world healthcare environments.17

Clinicians expressed concerns regarding workflow disruption, alert fatigue, competing clinical priorities, and productivity, while informatics and operational leaders emphasized system performance, governance requirements, and implementation feasibility. These findings reinforce the principle that successful CDS deployment requires alignment with existing clinical workflows, iterative refinement based on stakeholder feedback, structured validation processes, and sustained engagement of clinical, operational, and informatics stakeholders throughout implementation. The use of a pre-implementation silent validation phase, regular cross-site coordination meetings, and ongoing optimization of CDS functionality were instrumental in maintaining implementation fidelity while minimizing site burden.

A particularly important finding was the recognition that implementation success extends beyond CDS deployment itself and into the broader care delivery ecosystem. Medication-access barriers may prevent clinically appropriate recommendations from translating into treatment initiation, particularly for therapies requiring benefits verification, prior authorization, or affordability support. Accordingly, lipid-management CDS should be linked to a defined medication-access pathway capable of helping clinicians and patients operationalize treatment recommendations. The specific infrastructure may differ across health systems, but could include specialty pharmacy services, centralized prior authorization support, pharmacy liaisons, or other locally available medication-access resources. This finding highlights that effective CDS implementation requires attention not only to alert design and clinician workflow, but also to the downstream processes necessary to deliver the recommended therapy.

Taken together, the PROMPT-LIPID Multicenter provides practical lessons for deploying guideline-based lipid-management CDS across multiple health systems. In addition to evaluating the clinical effectiveness of the intervention, which will be reported separately, this manuscript describes the operational, technical, regulatory, governance, and workflow challenges encountered during deployment and the approaches used to address them. Importantly, overcoming these implementation barriers increases the likelihood that any clinical benefits ultimately demonstrated by PROMPT-LIPID can be successfully disseminated, adopted, and sustained across diverse institutions and patient populations. As healthcare systems increasingly seek practical approaches to translating evidence into routine care, the strategies described here may inform future CDS initiatives in cardiovascular disease and other chronic conditions. Although the PROMPT-LIPID CDS logic was designed around the guideline-concordant LLT intensification pathways evaluated in the original randomized trial, the platform can be modified to incorporate additional lipid-lowering therapies, evolving evidence, local formulary considerations, and future guideline updates.

Future directions

Future work may extend this model to EHR-agnostic settings, incorporate predictive modeling to identify patients most likely to benefit or be nonadherent, and expand to inpatient settings to enhance LLT intensification before hospital discharge. Additional future avenues include targeting high-risk primary prevention populations (i.e., diabetic patients, LDL-C ≥ 190 mg/dL, or adults aged 40–75 years with risk-enhancing features) through point-of-care EHR alerts informed by pooled risk estimation and coronary artery calcium (CAC) scores. Future iterations may also incorporate complementary alerts directed to medical assistants or other clinical staff to obtain point-of-care LDL-C testing at the time of rooming or staffing, ensuring that clinicians have the most up-to-date lipid values available when acting on the CDS recommendation. Lastly, although the study’s focus is on provider-facing CDS tools, patient-facing quality improvement strategies such as MyChart messaging or asynchronous disease-state education and counseling videos may provide an additive benefit.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, participating sites were not randomly selected, and only three of the eight initially engaged external health systems ultimately proceeded to implementation. The sites that advanced likely had greater organizational readiness, stronger informatics capacity, more engaged clinical and operational leadership, and greater willingness to participate in an industry-funded implementation initiative than those that did not. Consequently, the barriers observed in this study may underestimate the financial, technical, governance, and operational challenges faced by lower-resource, nonacademic, or less implementation-ready health systems. Successful deployment among the participating sites should therefore not be interpreted as evidence of scalability across the broader U.S. healthcare system. Second, although participating institutions represented geographically diverse healthcare settings, all sites utilized the Epic Inc. EHR platform. Implementation challenges may differ in organizations using alternative EHR systems, limiting the generalizability of certain technical and informatics findings. Third, the lessons presented were derived from the experiences of participating sites and stakeholders directly involved in the project. As a result, broader factors outside the immediate deployment process, including payer policies, regional regulatory requirements, patient perspectives, and other health-system constraints, may not have been fully captured. Finally, the implementation challenges and mitigation strategies described in this manuscript were identified through investigator meetings, site reports, regulatory and technical documentation, deployment activities, and stakeholder discussions rather than through formal qualitative interviews, which might have strengthened the interpretation of the findings. Although this approach provided pragmatic insights into real-world deployment, the findings should be interpreted as a narrative account of operational experience rather than as a formal qualitative analysis.Central Illustration. Ethical approval

The PROMPT-LIPID Multicenter study was governed by a central institutional review board approval process through the Biomedical Research Alliance of New York Institutional Review Board (BRANY). Yale University participation was approved under BRANY IRB File 24–08–144, Master File #2000,037,461. Participating health systems completed required local institutional approvals and data use agreements before implementation. Study data were stored centrally at the Yale School of Medicine Clinical and Translational Research Accelerator.
