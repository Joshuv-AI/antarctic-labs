Engineering dextranase for the directed preparation of low-molecular-weight dextran
Authors: Yan Qin, Yilin Ren, Lige Tong, Ge Liang, Yue Yuan, Lijun Meng, Hongyan Zhang, Qingyan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dextranase acts as a biocatalyst for the production of isomaltooligosaccharides (IMOs) and low-molecular-weight dextran (LMWD) in the food and pharmaceutical industries. Nevertheless, the naturally derived dextranase from Chaetomium gracile (CgDEX) suffers from unsatisfactory catalytic activity, which hinders its industrial utilization. We hypothesized that modification of non-conserved residues distal to the catalytic center could enhance the enzym's catalytic performance. In this work, candidate mutation sites were screened via multiple sequence alignment (MSA), followed by site-directed mutagenesis to engineer CgDEX and heterologous expression in Pichia pastoris. The resultant mutant exhibited a specific activity of 5185 ± 70 U/mg, representing an increase of 17.5 ± 2.3% relative to the wild-type enzyme. Molecular docking revealed strengthened hydrogen-bond interactions between catalytic residues and substrate upon mutation, consistent with the markedly elevated substrate affinity determined by enzyme kinetic assays. Hydrolysis of dextran by the mutant predominantly yielded IMOs with a degree of polymerization ranging from 2 to 4; additionally, the engineered enzyme could work synergistically with dextransucrase to synthesize LMWD. Collectively, this study verifies the feasibility of green manufacturing of functional oligosaccharides and LMWD using engineered CgDEX.

Background

Dextran is an inexpensive and readily available microbial polysaccharide consisting of glucose units linked via α-1,6 glycosidic bonds (Chen et al., 2024). Enzymatic hydrolysis of dextran yields high-value products including low-molecular-weight dextran (LMWD) and isomaltooligosaccharides (IMOs), both of which serve as indispensable functional additives in the food industry. Specifically, LMWD is widely used as thickener and humectant in food manufacturing (Chen et al., 2016); IMOs possess physiological functions of regulating intestinal flora and boosting immunity (Huang et al., 2020), and are applied as premium prebiotics in probiotic foods, dairy products and functional meal replacements (Patel & Goyal, 2011).

Dextranase (EC 3.2.1.11) is a hydrolase that specifically cleaves α-1,6 glycosidic bonds within dextran (Barzkar et al., 2022), categorized into exo-dextranase and endo-dextranase. Exo-dextranase sequentially truncates the polysaccharide from the reducing end to release glucose monomers (Meng et al., 2015), whereas endo-dextranase randomly breaks internal α-1,6 linkages to partially or fully degrade dextran into high-value LMWD and IMOs (Pittrof et al., 2021).

Dextranase is predominantly produced by bacteria and fungi (Khalikova et al., 2005). Compared with bacterial dextranase, fungal counterparts are better suited for industrial production (Punt et al., 2002). Chaetomium gracile, a filamentous fungus belonging to the phylum Ascomycota and genus Chaetomium, is an excellent industrial strain for dextranase fermentation (Eggleston et al., 2009; Punt et al., 2002). Nevertheless, native dextranase generally suffers from insufficient catalytic performance and fails to meet the requirements of large-scale industrial application (Barzkar et al., 2022). To improve the activity and thermostability of dextranase, most existing protein engineering efforts focus on residues around the catalytic center (Wei et al., 2023). For instance, (Ren et al., 2019) conducted site-directed mutagenesis on residues near the catalytic tunnel guided by B-factor analysis; mutant S357F retained approximately 30% higher residual activity than wild-type after incubation at 60 °C for 55 min. In another study, saturation mutagenesis was performed on residues within 2.9–5 Å of the isomaltotetraose (IMO4)-binding tunnel, and mutant H373R elevated the IMO4 yield from 24.50% (wild-type) to 69.81% (Wang et al., 2023). However, such mutations only brought marginal improvements in enzymatic activity.

Direct modification of residues at the catalytic center or substrate-binding pocket may disrupt substrate recognition and the catalytic hydrogen-bond network for enzymes with complex structures (Tong et al., 2025; Wei et al., 2023). Accumulating evidence confirms the vital roles of regions distal to active sites in modulating enzyme performance. For example, rational distal-site mutagenesis on cytochrome P450 monooxygenase generated an optimal double mutant with a 106-fold improvement in total turnover number (Dong et al., 2025), while distal mutation of endoglucanase EG5C-1 enhanced its catalytic efficiency toward soluble CMC by 10-fold (Wu et al., 2025). These findings demonstrate that rational engineering of non-conserved surface residues far from the catalytic center can effectively upgrade overall enzymatic properties without disturbing the active-site microenvironment (Kim et al., 2008; Yu et al., 2025; Zarifi et al., 2025). However, few studies have explored the functional contribution of distal residues to dextranase activity, leaving this research direction largely unexploited.

In this work, non-conserved sites distant from the substrate-binding tunnel of C. gracile dextranase (CgDEX) were selected for site-directed mutagenesis based on multiple sequence alignment (MSA) and structural prediction. Recombinant variants were constructed and heterologously expressed in Pichia pastoris, followed by high-throughput screening. Enzymatic activity and kinetic parameters of obtained mutants were characterized, and molecular docking was adopted to uncover underlying structural alterations caused by mutations, verifying the feasibility of the proposed engineering strategy. This study provides new insights into the structure-function relationship of CgDEX and supplies promising biocatalysts for controllable industrial production of IMOs and LMWD.

Materials and methods

Strains and reagents

Escherichia coli DH5α was used as the host strain for gene cloning, while E. coli Rosetta (DE3) and P. pastoris GS115 were used for heterologous expression. The plasmids pET-30a(+) and pPIC9K were employed as expression vectors; both the P. pastoris GS115 strain and the pPIC9K plasmid were originally obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Runcorn, UK). Dextran T20, T50, T70, T200, T500, and T2000 were purchased from Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). IMOs were obtained from Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). All reagents in this study were of analytical grade. Gene and primer synthesis were performed by Wuhan GeneCreate Biological Engineering Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China), DNA sequencing was performed by Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Expression and purification of CgDEX and its mutants

Cloning and expression of CgDEX in E. coli

The codon-optimized CgDEX gene was inserted into pET-30a(+) via homologous recombination after double digestion with EcoRI and NotI. After transformation into E. coli DH5α and sequencing confirmation, the recombinant plasmid pET-30a(+)-CgDEX was obtained. It was then transferred into E. coli Rosetta (DE3), and the recombinant cells were induced by Isopropyl-β-D-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) (final concentration 0.1 mM) at 16 °C for 16 h. Cells resuspended in 10 mL Na2HPO4-citric acid buffer (pH 5.5), and disrupted by sonication (power 50%, 2 s on, 3 s off, total 20 min). After centrifugation, the supernatant was collected as the crude dextranase extract.

Cloning and expression of CgDEX in P. Pastoris

The codon-optimized CgDEX gene fragment was inserted into double-digested pPIC9K (EcoRI and NotI) via homologous recombination and transformed into E. coli DH5α for plasmid amplification. The verified recombinant plasmid pPIC9K-CgDEX was then transformed into P. pastoris GS115. Transformants were spread onto MD agar at 30 °C for 48–72 h. Single colonies were screened on YPD agar with 300 and 800 μg/mL G418, followed by small-scale shake-tube culture to obtain the target strain pPIC9K-CgDEX/P. pastoris GS115. A single colony was inoculated into 5 mL YPD (100 mg/mL ampicillin) for 24 h activation, then 1% (v/v) was transferred to 70 mL BMGY and cultured for 16–18 h. Cells were harvested, resuspended in BMMY, and recombinant expression was induced with 1% methanol every 24 h for 96 h. After induction, the supernatant was collected as the crude dextranase solution.

Construction and expression of CgDEX mutants

The amino acid sequence of dextranase from Chaetomium globosum (GenBank accession No. MH045499) was used as the template for sequence alignment against CgDEX derived from C. gracile. Nine candidate residues were finally selected for site-directed mutagenesis by combining pairwise alignment results with MSA and structural analysis. Mutant primers were designed (Table S1). Using the recombinant plasmid pET-30a(+)-CgDEX as a template, PCR amplification was performed with the mutant primers, and the resulting linearized plasmids were transformed into E. coli DH5α competent cells to obtain the mutant recombinant plasmids. The corresponding variants were initially expressed in E. coli for preliminary activity screening. After identifying the beneficial mutations, the mutant gene fragments were subcloned into the pPIC9K vector and expressed in P. pastoris, the supernatant was collected as the crude dextranase solution.

Optimizing fermentation conditions

The mutant strain was cultured in BMMY medium at 220 rpm for 96 h, and the supernatant was collected by centrifugation to measure enzyme activity in each experiment. The highest activity observed under each set of conditions was defined as 100%, and the relative activities under other conditions were calculated accordingly. The final methanol concentrations of 0.5%, 1%, 1.5%, and 2% (v/v) were tested during the induction phase at 30 °C. Based on the optimal methanol concentration, induction temperatures of 20, 25, 27, 28, 30, and 35 °C were evaluated. Using the optimal temperature and methanol concentration, the effect of liquid loading volume was examined by culturing 30, 50, 100, 150, and 200 mL of BMMY medium in 500 mL flasks.

Purification of dextranase

The crude dextranase solutions of the wild-type CgDEX and its mutants purified using Ni-NTA affinity chromatography at a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min. The protein was detected by Sodium Dodecyl Sulfate-Polyacrylamide Gel Electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), and was measured by the Bradford method (Bradford, 1976). The purified protein was desalted against ultra pure water and used for further research.

Enzymatic characterization

Enzyme activity assay

Reducing-sugar content was determined by the 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS) method using a microplate reader (Tecan, Shanghai, China). The assay was performed as follows: 50 μL of the diluted enzyme solution was added to 350 μL of 3% dextran T70 (in 0.2 M citric acid-Na₂HPO₄ buffer, pH 5.5), and the reaction was carried out at 55 °C for 15 min. The reaction was terminated by adding 800 μL of DNS reagent (Miller, 1959), and the mixture was boiled in water for 5 min. After cooling to room temperature, the absorbance was measured at 540 nm. One unit (U) of enzyme activity was defined as the amount of enzyme required to release 1 μmol of glucose per minute, calculated according to a glucose standard curve. The inactivated enzyme was used as the blank control. Since Most reported specific activity measurements of dextranase employ 3% (w/v) dextran T2000 as the substrate, all specific activities in the present work were determined accordingly to facilitate cross-study comparison. The specific enzyme activity was calculated as follows: Specific enzyme activity (U/mg) = Enzyme activity (U/mL)/Protein concentration (mg/mL).

Effect of pH and temperature on enzyme activity and stability

The optimal temperature of the purified recombinant enzyme was determined by measuring enzyme activity at temperatures ranging from 35 °C to 90 °C (at 5 °C intervals). The highest activity was defined as 100%, and the relative activities at other temperatures were calculated accordingly. To evaluate thermostability, the diluted enzyme solution was incubated at temperatures ranging from 35 °C to 55 °C (at 5 °C intervals) for 0–60 min, and the residual activity was measured every 15 min, with the activity of the unincubated sample defined as 100%.

The optimal pH of the purified recombinant enzyme was determined by measuring enzyme activity in buffers of different pH values. The highest activity was defined as 100%, and the relative activities at other pH values were calculated accordingly. To evaluate pH stability, the purified enzyme was incubated in buffers from pH 3.0 to 12.0 at 4 °C or 30 °C for 24 h, after which the residual activity was measured, with the activity of the unincubated sample defined as 100%. Buffers used were citric acid-Na2HPO4 for pH 3.0–7.0, Tris-HCl for pH 7.0–9.0, and glycine-NaOH for pH 9.0–12.0.

Effect of metal ion on enzyme activity

Under optimal temperature and pH, different concentrations (1, 2.5, 5, and 10 mM) of metal ions (Fe2+, Zn2+, Mn2+, Cu2+, Pb2+, K+, EDTA-Na2, Fe3+, Na+, Li+, Ca2+, and Mg2+) were added separately to the reaction system. Enzyme activity in the catalysis system without extra addition of metal ion was defined as 100%, and the relative activities with extra addition of metal ion were calculated accordingly.

Substrate specificity

To assess the substrate preference of dextranase, the following substrates were prepared: dextran T20, T50, T200, T500, and T2000, soluble starch, agarose, chitosan, maltose, β-cyclodextrin, and sucrose at a concentration of 3%, and dextran T70 concentrations of 0.5%, 1%, 3%, 5%, and 7%. Enzyme activity was measured under the optimal temperature and pH conditions, with dextran T2000 as the reference substrate (defined as 100%). The relative activities for all other substrates were calculated accordingly.

Determination of the kinetic parameters

Dextran T70 was used as the substrate and prepared at concentrations ranging from 0.2 to 4 mg/mL in citric acid-Na2HPO4 buffer (pH 5.5). Enzyme activity was measured under the optimal temperature and pH conditions, and the protein concentration was determined by the Bradford method. The specific activity (U/mg) corresponding to different substrate concentrations was determined using enzyme activity and protein concentration.The Km and Vmax values were reported by using nonlinear fitting function of Origin 2022 software.

Molecular docking analysis

IMO4 was selected as the ligand. Its 3D structure was drawn using ChemDraw. Both wild-type CgDEX and its mutan were used as protein receptors for docking. Molecular docking was conducted using AutoDockTools-1.5.7. Based on the positions of the catalytic residues and the substrate-binding channel, the box coordinates enclosing the substrate-binding region were set to 2.967, 7.292, and 0.321 (x, y, z), and the box size was set to 62 × 70 × 66. The docking results were visualized and analyzed using PyMOL.

Product analysis

The purified mutant CgDEX (100 μL) was mixed with 700 μL of 3% dextran T70 in citric acid-Na₂HPO₄ buffer (pH 5.5) and incubated at 55 °C for 15–90 min (at 15 min intervals), 6 h, and 24 h. The hydrolysis products were analyzed by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Shanghai, China) using a Hypersil NH₂ 5 μm column. The column temperature was set at 30 °C, the mobile phase was an acetonitrile-pure water system, and the flow rate was 1 mL/min. The products were detected using a differential refractive index detector. Isomaltose (IMO2), isomaltotriose (IMO3), and IMO4 were used as standard substances at a concentration of 10 g/L.

Directed preparation of LMWD

Directed preparation of LMWD was achieved by combining dextransucrase and mutant CgDEX. In previous work, dextransucrase expressed in our laboratory synthesized high MW dextran (> 400 kDa) using 100 mM sucrose as the substrate at 30 °C and pH 5.6 for 10 min. In this experiment, mutant CgDEX was used to degrade the high MW dextran at 30 °C and pH 5.5. Samples were collected at different time intervals to obtain LMWD. HPLC (Thermo Fisher Scientific) analysis of the product MW was performed using a TSKgel G3000SWXL column, with a column temperature of 60 °C, a flow rate of 0.6 mL/min, and ultrapure water as the mobile phase.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate and repeated three times independently. Specific activity data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). One-way ANOVA was applied to evaluate significant differences under different fermentation conditions.

Results and discussion

Bioinformatics analysis of CgDEX

The protein encoded by the CgDEX gene consists of 595 amino acids, among which residues 1–19 constitute the signal peptide. And the theoretical molecular mass of the mature protein is 65.96 kDa. CgDEX, along with Aodex and DEX49A, belongs to glycoside hydrolase family 49 (GH49). In this study, AlphaFold2 was employed to predict the three-dimensional structure of the CgDEX protein (Fig. 1A). Asp378 and Asp397 act as the key catalytic residues of this enzyme (Wang et al., 2023). CgDEX shares up to 73% sequence identity with DEX49A, a glucanase from the GH49 family. Based on the structural features of DEX49A, a hydrogen bond can form between Asp319 and Tyr465 in CgDEX, thereby generating a tunnel-shaped substrate-binding pocket (Fig. 1B). This structural feature facilitates substrate access to the catalytic center and product dissociation, enhancing the substrate-binding affinity of the catalytic domain (Ren et al., 2019)，which of CgDEX spans Gln376 to Asp398 (Figs. The enzymatic activity of the crude enzyme solution reached 213 U/mL using 3% dextran T-70 as substrate, which was substantially higher than the expression level achieved in E. coli. Enzymatic characterization of CgDEX

CgDEX exhibited an optimal temperature of 55 °C and an optimal pH of 5.5 (Fig. 3A, B). Most dextranases documented in existing literature have optimal temperatures ranging from 25 to 60 °C (Cai et al., 2014; Sufiate et al., 2018). Weakly acidic environments can mitigate non-specific side reactions and the generation of undesirable byproducts (Wu et al., 2014), a trend consistent with the optimal reaction parameters of other Chaetomium-derived dextranases (Virgen-Ortíz et al., 2015). Recombinant CgDEX expressed in P. pastoris possessed the best thermal stability at 35 °C, retaining over 80% relative residual activity after 45 min of incubation (Fig. 3C). Expression, fermentation optimization, and enzymatic characterization of CgDEX mutants

Expression and purification of CgDEX mutants

The dextranase from C. globosum exhibits a specific activity of 7535.8 U/mg when assayed with dextran T2000 as substrate (Yang et al., 2018), which is higher than the specific activity of wild-type CgDEX determined in this study (6897 ± 63 U/mg). These two enzymes share an extremely high sequence identity of 92%, differing at only 57 amino acid residues. Meanwhile, MSA and conservation analysis were performed on dextranase sequences from multiple strains with sequence identity ranging from 40% to 95% (Fig. S6). Following the screening principle of selecting residues distant from the catalytic center, 9 non-conserved residues located 10–35 Å away from the active pocket were ultimately selected for single-site mutagenesis (Fig. S5, Fig. 5A).Fig. 5Mutation site and SDS-PAGE analysis of CgDEXA289V.(A) Structure of the mutation site. The nine mutants were heterologously expressed in E. coli for primary screening, and protein expression was verified via SDS-PAGE electrophoresis (Fig. S2). Among all variants, mutant A289V possessed the highest enzymatic activity (4.0 U/mL) and optimal thermal stability (Table S4, Fig. S3), and was therefore selected for subsequent in-depth characterization.

Mutant A289V was expressed in P. pastoris GS115 to yield the recombinant CgDEXA289V (Fig. 5B). The crude enzyme lysate displayed an activity of 403.7 U/mL, with a purification fold of 1.2 and a protein recovery rate of 46.4%. When dextran T70 was used as the substrate, its specific activity reached 5185 ± 70 U/mg (Table S5), representing a 17.5 ± 2.3% improvement relative to the wild-type enzyme. When assayed against dextran T2000, the purified CgDEXA289V achieved a specific activity of 8102 ± 109 U/mg. This activity falls within the moderate range of reported dextranase activities to date; for instance, the CgDEX expressed in Bacillus subtilis only reached 3539.02 U/mg using 3% dextran T2000 as substrate (Zhao et al., 2021).

Enzymatic characterization of CgDEXA289V

The optimal temperature and optimal pH of recombinant CgDEXA289V remained identical to those of wild-type, which were 55 °C and pH 5.5, respectively (Fig. 7A, B). The kinetic parameters of CgDEXA289V were determined as follows: Vmax = 6765 ± 40 μM/min/mg, Km = 0.23 ± 0.02 mg/mL, and kcat = 7893 ± 16 s−1. The kcat/Km value increased from 17 × 103 ± 20 mL/(mg·S) for the CgDEX to 34 × 103 ± 19 mL/(mg·S) for the mutant (Fig. 7F), representing a 2-fold enhancement (Table S6). This result indicated that the mutation strengthened substrate affinity and drastically elevated overall catalytic efficiency. CgDEXA289V exhibited identical substrate specificity to the wild-type enzyme; both exclusively hydrolyze α-1,6 glycosidic linkages (Table 2). Additionally, CgDEXA289V displayed improved tolerance to high concentrations of Mn2+. Supplementation with 10 mmol/L Mn2+ elevated enzymatic activity to 165% of the control level. Molecular docking of recombinant enzymes CgDEX and CgDEXA289V

PyMOL visualization indicated that IMO4 formed hydrogen bonds with ten amino acid residues of mutant CgDEXA289V (Fig. 8A), namely Asp88, Tyr305, Gln306, Lys317, Asp353, Gln376, Asp378, Gln381, Asp397 and Asp420. In contrast, wild-type CgDEX only established intermolecular interactions with IMO4 via five residues: Lys317, Asp319, Asp397, Asp398 and Trp427 (Fig. 8B). Hydrogen bonding serves as a critical noncovalent force governing protein-ligand binding (Schiebel et al., 2018). The strengthened intermolecular interaction between substrate and catalytic residues after mutation suggests improved enzyme-substrate affinity, consistent with the kinetic results: Km value decreased from 0.37 ± 0.03 mg/mL (wild type) to 0.23 ± 0.02 mg/mL for the mutant, whereas the Vmax increased from 5377 ± 120 to 6765 ± 40 μM/min/mg (Table S6). Samuel et al. (Samuel et al., 2023) verified in their research on dextranase Aodex that Gln418, Glu423 and Asp440 are located within the catalytic domain and may improve enzyme stability via stabilizing local structural conformation (Ren et al., 2019; Wei et al., 2023). In the present study, the mutant exhibited extra hydrogen bonds between Gln376, Gln381 inside the substrate-binding tunnel (Fig. 8A) and IMO4 compared with the wild-type enzyme (Fig. 8B), which may account for the slight improvement in thermostability of the mutant. The underlying detailed mechanism requires further investigation. Since the protein structure was predicted by AlphaFold, crystal structures of enzyme-substrate complexes of this type of enzyme are relatively scarce, and the substrate-binding region possesses a complicated architecture, the binding energies obtained from molecular docking are relatively weak. This also indicates that there remains enormous room for further research on the structure-function relationship of dextranase.

Product analysis of CgDEXA289V

Based on HPLC (Thermo Fisher Scientific) retention-time comparison with standard samples, the hydrolysates of CgDEXA289V were identified as IMO2, IMO3 and IMO4 (Fig. 9A), whose yields gradually increased with prolonged hydrolysis duration. No new products were generated after 24 h of hydrolysis, and glucose was undetectable throughout the reaction. Such product distribution is consistent with that of reported endo-dextranases (Wang et al., 2023) and differs from exo-dextranases that predominantly produce glucose (Meng et al., 2015). Therefore, CgDEXA289V was confirmed to be an endo-type dextranase. Peak-area percentage was adopted for semi-quantitative analysis to evaluate product composition and relative yields of the enzyme. After 24 h hydrolysis, the peak areas of IMO2, IMO3 and IMO4 all reached their maxima, with concentrations of 2.4 g/L, 1.0 g/L and 0.2 g/L and corresponding yields of 34.6%, 53.6% and 11.8%, respectively. The retention-time reproducibility was validated via three consecutive injections of mixed standards and enzymatic hydrolysates; the RSD values of retention time were below 0.5% for standard samples and 1.0% for hydrolysates, demonstrating favorable repeatability of the HPLC (Thermo Fisher Scientific) method. Directional preparation of LMWD by combining dextransucrase and CgDEXA289V

The elution times of dextran standards with MWs of 5, 20, 50, 100, and 400 kDa were 14.1, 12.6, 11.6, 10.7, and 10.1 min, respectively. Previously, the laboratory synthesized high MW dextran (Fig. 9B) (Zuo et al., 2024). Hydrolysis of this high MW dextran by CgDEXA289V yielded LMWD and oligosaccharides (Fig. 9C). At 1 min of hydrolysis, a small amount of high MW dextran (>400 kDa and 400 kDa) and 20–100 kDa traces of dextran remained. As hydrolysis became prolonged, the yield of LMWD increased: at 10 min, the amount of 50–100 kDa dextran increased; at 35 min, the hydrolysis products were mainly 5–20 kDa dextran with a small amount of oligosaccharides; at 1 h 30 min, the main products were dextran <5 kDa and oligosaccharides. The MW of dextran decreased with the extension of hydrolysis, which is consistent with the trend reported by Goulas et al. (Goulas et al., 2004).

The two-enzyme synergistic system is regarded as the optimal strategy for manufacturing LMWD (Wang et al., 2021). Unlike random degradation occurring during conventional acid hydrolysis of dextran (Chen et al., 2021), CgDEXA289V enables tunable degradation of dextran with diverse molecular weights. The molecular weight of resultant dextran gradually declines with extended hydrolysis time, demonstrating that the mutant CgDEXA289V can modulate the molecular-weight distribution of dextran. However, systematic process optimization, product characterization experiments and practical application trials in real food matrices are still required to verify its potential for precise, directional and controllable synthesis as well as its practical value in the food industry.

Conclusion

In summary, the dextranase gene from C. gracile was successfully heterologously expressed in P. pastoris in this study. Site-directed mutagenesis generated mutant CgDEXA289V with improved specific activity and thermostability, and subsequent fermentation optimization further boosted the enzyme expression level. Product characterization revealed that the mutant degrades high-molecular-weight dextran into low-molecular-weight fractions and converts dextran T70 into IMO2, IMO3 and IMO4. It should be noted that product identification and molecular weight evaluation were semi-quantitatively deduced mainly from HPLC (Thermo Fisher Scientific) retention times and peak areas relative to reference standards; further confirmation via structural verification (e.g., LC-MS) and absolute quantitative measurement is therefore required. Follow-up work will systematically evaluate its degradation efficiency and processing compatibility in representative real food matrices, as well as preliminarily explore the prebiotic functions of enzymatic hydrolysates. This work establishes a feasible research framework for downstream application development and identifies a promising biocatalytic enzyme candidate.
