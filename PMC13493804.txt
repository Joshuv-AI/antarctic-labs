Mapping the transition: the interplay of technology and natural disasters on sustainable energy in G20 nations
Authors: Hongshuai Qi, Tong Tian, Muhammad Wasif Hanif, Muhammad Asim Afridi, Wali Muhammad Khoso
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates how technological advancement, and natural disasters shape the energy transition in G20 economies over 2005–2023. The main objective is to measure the effects of environmental innovation and disaster exposure on four SDG 7 indicators: electricity access, clean cooking access, renewable energy share, and energy efficiency. Using a panel dataset from international sources, the study applies a two-way fixed effects model as a baseline and then uses Two-Stage Least Squares (2SLS) and System GMM to address endogeneity and dynamic effects. Technological advancement is measured by the log of environmental technology patents, while natural disasters are measured by the annual count of recorded events. The findings show that technological advancement strongly supports the transition by increasing clean cooking access and renewable energy share, and it is also significantly related to improvements in the efficiency indicator. Natural disasters show a mixed role: they are linked with weaker clean cooking access but a higher renewable energy share, suggesting that disasters may delay household energy improvements while pushing system-level renewable adoption. Sub-sample results indicate stronger technology effects in non-OECD G20 economies than in OECD members. These results imply that innovation policy and technology diffusion are essential for SDG 7 progress, while disaster recovery planning should protect household energy access and use rebuilding as an opportunity to expand resilient renewables. Overall, coordinated innovation and resilience strategies are necessary for a balanced and inclusive energy transition.Keywords: Energy transition, Technological advancement, Natural disasters, G20 economies, Renewable energy, Climate resilienceSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Environmental social sciences

Introduction

sThe global energy system is at a pivotal juncture, shaped by the dual imperative to sustain economic growth while rapidly reducing greenhouse gas emissions1. Because energy production and use constitute the dominant source of carbon dioxide emissions and a major share of overall greenhouse gas emissions accelerating the transition toward low-carbon, efficient, and resilient energy systems is a prerequisite for climate stability and human well-being2,3. This challenge is especially consequential for the Group of Twenty (G20) economies, which collectively account for a substantial share of global output, trade, and energy use4–6. Decisions made within the G20 regarding technology deployment, infrastructure investment, and policy design therefore carry outsized implications for global decarbonization and energy security. Traditional explanations of energy transition progress emphasize macroeconomic conditions, policy instruments, and resource endowments7,8. Yet the contemporary transition is increasingly shaped by two forces that are often examined separately: rapid technological advancement and escalating climate-related natural disasters. On the one hand, innovation in renewable generation, storage, smart grids, and digital energy management has lowered costs, expanded technical feasibility, and improved system integration thereby enabling higher renewable penetration and more efficient energy use3,9. Technological progress can also strengthen energy equity by expanding access to modern energy services and cleaner household energy options10.

On the other hand, climate change is associated with increased frequency and severity of extreme events such as floods, storms, heatwaves, and wildfires that can damage energy infrastructure, disrupt supply chains, and exacerbate energy poverty11,12. Importantly, disasters may not operate solely as negative shocks. Major events can act as “focusing” or “wake-up” moments that reveal vulnerabilities of centralized fossil-dependent systems and increase political and social demand for resilient, decentralized, and cleaner alternatives13,14. Consequently, natural disasters may have a dual effect: they can undermine short-term access and affordability while potentially accelerating longer-term structural shifts toward renewables and resilient systems. Despite growing interest in both innovation and climate risk, important empirical gaps remain. First, much of the literature evaluates technological change or disaster impacts in isolation, offering limited evidence on whether and how these forces jointly shape transition outcomes15,16. Second, energy transition is often proxied narrowly by renewable deployment, overlooking its multidimensional character. In policy terms, transition progress spans multiple SDG 7 outcomes: (i) access to electricity (SDG 7.1.1), (ii) access to clean cooking (SDG 7.1.2), (iii) renewable energy share in final consumption (SDG 7.2.1), and (iv) improvements in energy efficiency (SDG 7.3.1).

This study addresses these gaps by providing a panel-based empirical assessment of the determinants of SDG 7 transition outcomes across G20 economies over the period 2005–2023. Specifically, we examine (1) the direct effects of technological advancement and natural disasters on each SDG 7 indicator and (2) their interplay, assessed by testing whether technological capability moderates the impact of disaster exposure on transition outcomes. In addition, we explore two potential transmission channels changes in industrial structure and renewable energy capacity expansion to clarify mechanisms through which these drivers may operate. Finally, given substantial heterogeneity within the G20, we assess whether effects differ between OECD and non-OECD members, reflecting differences in development levels, institutional capacity, and vulnerability to climatic shocks.

The present study provides a systematic empirical assessment of how technological advancement and natural disaster exposure jointly shape four core dimensions of the energy transition electricity access, clean cooking access, renewable energy share, and energy efficiency in G20 economies over the period 2005–2023. Two notable gaps motivate this inquiry. First, although innovation and climate shocks are both recognized as powerful forces in energy systems, they are rarely examined together; as a result, we still lack evidence on whether technological capability can mitigate the disruptive effects of disasters on energy access or whether disasters systematically redirect innovation and investment toward cleaner pathways. Second, progress toward SDG 7 is frequently reduced to a single indicator, typically renewable deployment, which risks overlooking household-level outcomes such as clean cooking access and demand-side efficiency that may respond differently to the same drivers. The G20 context is particularly consequential: these economies account for most of the global energy consumption, greenhouse gas emissions, and clean-energy investment, and they are at the same time increasingly exposed to climate-related hazards while exhibiting deep internal heterogeneity between advanced OECD members and emerging non-OECD economies. By integrating technological advancement and natural disasters within a unified empirical framework and by measuring transition progress through four distinct SDG 7 indicators, this study aims to clarify whether innovation and disaster exposure act as complements or substitutes, and how their interplay differs across levels of development.

From a theoretical standpoint, this study builds on two complementary perspectives. The first, rooted in innovation and technology diffusion theories, holds that technological advancement lowers the cost of low‑carbon solutions, improves system integration through digital and smart‑grid tools, and expands decentralized access thereby directly advancing renewable deployment, energy efficiency, and household clean cooking uptake17. The second perspective draws on disaster resilience and “focusing events” theory, which conceptualizes natural disasters as exogenous shocks with a dual character. On the one hand, disasters destroy energy infrastructure, disrupt supply chains, and strain household livelihoods, which can undermine short‑run access and affordability, especially for clean cooking18. On the other hand, major disasters can act as “wake‑up calls” that expose the fragility of centralized fossil‑fuel systems and catalyse political and social demand for resilient, decentralized renewables19. Crucially, these forces need not operate independently: technological capability can moderate the impact of disasters providing resilient alternatives that protect access and accelerate rebuilding, or disasters may erode the fiscal and institutional capacity required to sustain innovation, making technology and disasters substitutes rather than complements.

Literature review

Conventional drivers of energy transition: economic conditions, policy, and resource endowments

A long-standing body of research explains energy transition outcomes through structural and macro-level determinants, particularly economic development, policy orientation, and resource endowments20,21. From this perspective, income levels and investment capacity shape both the demand for modern energy services and the ability of governments and firms to finance new infrastructure, extend grids, and support clean energy deployment22. In parallel, trade integration and industrial composition influence energy demand patterns and the speed of technological diffusion, while fiscal space and institutional capacity affect the continuity of transition policies over time23. Recent evidence shows a coupling coordination relationship between new quality productive forces and high-quality economic development in China, with the strength of this relationship varying across regions and over time24. This finding aligns with a broader body of research that treats economic development as a baseline condition enabling or constraining progress across transition objectives, including electrification, clean cooking adoption, renewable deployment, and efficiency improvements25.

Policy and resource conditions further determine how quickly systems can shift away from fossil fuels. A large share of the literature highlights policy tools such as renewable subsidies, carbon pricing, portfolio standards, and efficiency regulations as key market-shaping mechanisms that reduce investor risk and accelerate low-carbon adoption26. More recent contributions extend this policy lens to the digital economy, showing that digitalization can strengthen the energy‑environment nexus and facilitate renewable energy uptake27, and to energy security and environmental regulations, demonstrating that their combined effect on the energy transition differs markedly between high‑income and middle‑income countries28. At the same time, resource endowments define the feasible technology set: high solar and wind potential lowers the effective cost of renewables, while large fossil reserves and associated rents can create political economy frictions that discourage structural change. In combination, these drivers’ economic fundamentals, policy instruments, digital infrastructure, regulatory frameworks, and resource availability form the “traditional” explanatory framework for transition dynamics, and they remain essential controls in empirical work even when newer forces such as innovation and climate shocks are introduced29.

Technological innovation as an enabling force for decarbonization, access, and efficiency

More recent scholarship increasingly emphasizes technological innovation as a central engine of energy transition rather than a background factor30. A major reason is the sharp improvement in performance and cost competitiveness of key technologies solar photovoltaics, wind turbines, battery storage, and power electronics which has expanded the economic viability of low-carbon systems across a wider set of countries31. Evidence from OECD economies underscore this point, showing that environmental technology acts as a robust driver of sustainable energy transition even in contexts of policy uncertainty and natural resource dependence32. Importantly, innovation is not limited to generation hardware; firm‑level technological innovation shaped by governance mechanisms such as board independence and audit quality also underpins progress in digital technologies like smart grids, sensors, predictive maintenance, and demand‑side management, which have improved the operational flexibility of power systems and reduced integration barriers for variable renewables33. As a result, innovation is commonly theorized to influence both supply-side decarbonization and system-level efficiency by enabling more reliable, responsive, and optimized energy management34.

Innovation is also increasingly linked to the equity dimensions of transition, especially in contexts where centralized infrastructure expansion is slow or costly35. Decentralized solutions mini-grids, solar home systems, efficient appliances, and modern clean cooking technologies can expand access and reduce indoor air pollution in ways that complement large-scale grid investment. Recent evidence from China demonstrates that smart city initiatives and the digital economy facilitate energy security and accelerate the energy transition36, while the integration of digital and real economies has been shown to promote green innovation, further enabling equitable and sustainable energy outcomes37. Empirically, studies using patent-based indicators, R&D expenditures, or technology diffusion measures generally find that innovation is positively associated with renewable uptake and improvements in efficiency, though effect sizes vary substantially across governance and development contexts38,39. This heterogeneity is important: it implies that innovation may deliver higher marginal gains where energy infrastructure is less locked-in or where countries can “leapfrog” to newer technological pathways, rather than being constrained by legacy fossil-based systems.

Natural disasters: disruption, vulnerability exposure, and potential catalytic effects

A substantial literature views climate-related natural disasters primarily as negative shocks to energy systems, with consequences for reliability, affordability, and distributional outcomes40. Floods, storms, heatwaves, and wildfires can damage generation assets, transmission networks, and fuel logistics; they can also strain public budgets and disrupt household livelihoods, thereby deepening energy poverty and delaying investments that would otherwise support transition objectives41. In this framing, disasters worsen short-run energy access and service delivery, particularly in areas where systems are already fragile and institutional response capacity is limited42. This logic is especially relevant for clean cooking adoption and electricity access, where infrastructure interruption and household income shocks can produce immediate setbacks. At the same time, another strand of research argues that disasters can also trigger longer-term political and investment shifts, creating a pathway through which shocks become catalysts for structural change. Building on the “focusing events” idea, major disasters may elevate public attention and political urgency, revealing the vulnerabilities of centralized and fossil-dependent systems and legitimizing large-scale investments in resilience-oriented technologies43. In practice, this can translate into stronger incentives for distributed renewables, storage, microgrids, and grid modernization, especially when reconstruction spending is explicitly oriented toward resilience and decarbonization. Consequently, the net effect of disasters is theoretically ambiguous and may differ by outcome: they can plausibly undermine access and affordability in the short run while accelerating renewable adoption and resilience investments over longer horizons, depending on governance quality and recovery policy design44.

Key gaps and how this study responds

Despite the depth of scholarship on both innovation and disaster impacts, empirical evidence remains fragmented in ways that limit policy guidance. First, many studies estimate the effects of technology or climate shocks separately, which means we still know relatively little about whether they act as complements or substitutes in shaping transition outcomes45. Conceptually, these forces may reinforce each other if disaster exposure increases the value of resilient clean technologies and accelerates adoption, or they may counteract each other if disaster-related fiscal and welfare losses crowd out innovation and long-term investment46. Without explicitly examining interaction or moderation, it is difficult to infer whether technological capability reduces the vulnerability of transition progress to disasters, or whether disasters systematically accelerate innovation and deployment in some contexts while impeding it in others47.

Second, the literature often relies on narrow proxies for “energy transition,” typically focusing on renewable energy deployment, which risks overlooking the multidimensional nature of SDG 7 progress48. Transition is not simply a supply-side shift: it also involves household welfare outcomes such as electricity access and clean cooking adoption, as well as demand-side efficiency improvements49. These outcomes can respond differently to the same driver’s innovation can boost renewables and efficiency without immediately improving clean cooking, and disasters can catalyze renewables while worsening access in vulnerable communities. Third, cross-country heterogeneity is frequently acknowledged but not always tested systematically within comparable groups; in the G20, OECD and non-OECD members differ sharply in infrastructure maturity, institutional capacity, and marginal returns to technology adoption. Addressing these gaps motivates the present study’s focus on multiple SDG 7 indicators, the explicit examination of joint effects, and systematic comparison across OECD and non-OECD members to generate more targeted and credible policy implications (Nan et al. 2023; Sai et al. 2023).

While existing research typically examines technological innovation and natural disasters in isolation often using a single indicator such as renewable energy share this study makes three distinct contributions. First, it integrates both factors into a unified framework and explicitly tests their interaction, assessing whether technological capability moderates the impact of disaster exposure on energy transition. Second, it adopts a multidimensional view of the energy transition by examining four SDG 7 outcomes (electricity access, clean cooking, renewable share, and energy efficiency) that may respond in opposite directions to the same drivers. Third, it systematically compares OECD and non-OECD G20 economies, revealing substantial heterogeneity that one-size-fits-all analyses miss. Together, these elements provide a more complete and policy-relevant picture of how innovation and climate-related shocks jointly shape the energy transition in the world’s largest economies.

Data and methodology

Methodology

This study uses a panel of G20 economies over 2005–2023. The G20 group is chosen because it accounts for approximately 80% of global energy consumption, 75% of greenhouse gas emissions, and the majority of environmental technology patents, making it central to global energy transition dynamics. The sample deliberately includes both OECD and non‑OECD members, which ensures substantial variation in energy access gaps, renewable deployment, disaster vulnerability, and institutional capacity. This heterogeneity is essential for the study’s objective: to test whether technological advancement and natural disaster exposure affect SDG 7 outcomes differently across development contexts and whether their interplay varies systematically between mature and emerging energy systems. Additionally, consistent, high‑quality panel data are available for G20 countries from international sources, supporting a rigorous multi‑indicator analysis.

The baseline model is a two‑way fixed‑effects (TWFE) specification, which controls for time‑invariant country characteristics and common global shocks, isolating the within‑country effects of changes in technology and disasters on energy transition outcomes. Addressing endogeneity: 2SLS and dynamic SYS-GMM

Endogeneity concerns may arise for several reasons, including omitted variables, measurement error, and reverse causality (Bhat and Ordóñez Garcia 2021). Countries that progress faster in energy transition outcomes may also increase innovation activity and patenting, creating simultaneity. To address these concerns, we apply a Two-Stage Least Squares (2SLS) framework and a System Generalized Method of Moments (SYS-GMM) estimator, consistent with approaches recommended in the applied panel literature (Araya et al., 2021) and recent empirical energy-transition research (Komorowska et al., 2023).

Data and variables

Dependent variable

The energy transition is inherently multidimensional. To capture this complexity, the study adopts four SDG 7 indicators as dependent variables, consistent with established approaches in the energy transition literature (Araya et al. 2021; Bhat and Ordóñez Garcia 2021). SDG 7 aims to ensure access to affordable, reliable, sustainable and modern energy for all, providing a coherent framework for assessing transition progress beyond a single metric. We operationalize the dependent variables as follows: (1) Electricity Access (EC; SDG 7.1.1), measured as the percentage of the population with access to electricity; (2) Clean Cooking Access (CCT; SDG 7.1.2), measured as the percentage of the population using clean fuels and technologies for cooking; (3) Renewable Energy Share (RES; SDG 7.2.1), measured as renewables as a share of total final energy consumption; and (4) Energy Efficiency (EE; SDG 7.3.1), measured as GDP per unit of primary energy use (PPP-adjusted), where a higher value indicates improved efficiency and lower energy intensity. Indicators 7.a and 7.b are not included because they relate primarily to international cooperation and enabling infrastructure, whereas the objective here is to evaluate national transition outcomes.

Independent variable

The two primary explanatory variables are technological advancement and natural disaster exposure. We recognize that both constructs are broad, and the available proxies capture only selected dimensions. Technological Advancement (TA) is measured as the natural logarithm of environmental‑technology patent counts, using the transformation ln (1 + patents) to handle zero values. Patent data are sourced from OECD.Stat and follow standardized IPC‑based environmental technology classifications. Patents are a widely used indicator of innovation capacity and reflect the generation of new technical knowledge relevant to renewable energy and efficiency. However, they do not directly measure technology diffusion, commercial deployment, or firm‑level adoption channels that are also important in the theoretical framework. While more granular deployment data (e.g., renewable capacity additions, clean cooking device sales) would be desirable, consistent cross‑country panel data for all G20 members over the study period are not available. We therefore interpret the patent‑based measure as a plausible, albeit incomplete, proxy for the innovation efforts that enable both supply‑side decarbonization and household‑level energy improvements; the estimated coefficients should be read as associations between a country’s inventive activity and its subsequent transition outcomes, rather than as precise causal estimates of technology adoption.

Natural Disasters (ND) is measured as the annual count of recorded climatological, meteorological, and hydrological disaster events per country, obtained from the EM‑DAT International Disaster Database. A continuous count is adopted because it captures variation in exposure intensity across time and countries more effectively than a binary indicator. The count includes floods, storms, droughts, heatwaves, and similar events. We recognize that a simple count does not differentiate between a minor flood with limited damage and a catastrophic hurricane with extensive infrastructure destruction, and it does not directly measure economic losses, population affected, or mortality. This limitation implies that our measure may underestimate the impact of severe, low‑frequency disasters and attenuate the estimated disaster effects, particularly for outcomes sensitive to disaster intensity. Nonetheless, the annual event count is the most consistently available cross‑country measure for a long panel and is commonly used in macro‑level energy‑environment studies. We therefore treat it as a practical proxy for exposure to climate‑related shocks, while cautioning that the estimated disaster coefficients likely represent a lower‑bound, averaged effect. To examine the interplay with technological capability, we include the interaction term TA × ND in all relevant specifications.

Control variables

To isolate the effects of technology and disasters, we include controls widely used in the energy transition and development literature. These include: Urban Growth Rate (UGR), annual urban population growth; Trade Openness (TRADE), exports plus imports as a share of GDP; Natural Resource Rents (NRR), total natural resource rents as a share of GDP; Climate Change Performance (CC), proxied by greenhouse gas emissions per capita (log-transformed); Regulatory Quality (RQ) and Governance Quality (GQ), WGI indices capturing institutional capacity; Industrial Structure (IS), industry value added as a share of GDP; and Renewable Energy Capacity (REG), the log of installed renewable energy capacity per capita. To reduce skewness and support elasticity-style interpretation where appropriate, continuous variables are log-transformed where valid and meaningful. Variables that can take zero or negative values are treated carefully (e.g., using offsets or remaining in levels) to avoid invalid logarithmic transformations.

Although Regulatory Quality (RQ) and Governance Quality (GQ) are highly correlated (pairwise), they capture conceptually distinct institutional dimensions: RQ reflects the government’s ability to design sound policies and regulations, while GQ captures overall effectiveness and control of corruption. Furthermore, when we estimate alternative models retaining only one of the two variables at a time, the sign, significance, and magnitude of the core variables (TA, ND, and their interaction) remain unchanged. We therefore retain both variables in the main specifications to capture the broader institutional environment while confirming that multicollinearity is not a material concern.

Data descriptions

The dataset is constructed using internationally recognized sources to ensure cross-country comparability and robustness. Socioeconomic and SDG 7 access indicators are drawn from the World Bank’s World Development Indicators (WDI) and UN SDG databases. Energy mix indicators and renewable capacity data are obtained from the International Energy Agency (IEA) and the International Renewable Energy Agency (IRENA), while innovation indicators (environmental patents) are sourced from OECD.Stat. Natural disaster counts are taken from the EM-DAT International Disaster Database, and institutional quality indicators (RQ and GQ) are drawn from the Worldwide Governance Indicators (WGI). The final panel covers G20 countries over 2005–2023 (subject to data availability for specific variables), and the empirical specifications are estimated on an unbalanced panel where observations are retained whenever all variables required for a given model are available. Empirical findings

Descriptive statistics

Table 2 presents descriptive statistics for all variables used in the empirical analysis for G20 economies over 2005–2023. The results show clear differences across countries and years, which is important for examining how technology and natural disasters relate to energy transition outcomes. Electricity access (EC) has a high average value (96.5%), but the minimum value (62.1%) shows that some countries still had serious access gaps during part of the period. Clean cooking access (CCT) varies even more (mean 78.3%, standard deviation 28.4), indicating large differences in household energy conditions across the G20. Renewable energy share (RES) ranges from 0.1% to 58.2%, which highlights that countries are moving toward renewables at very different speeds. Energy efficiency (EE), measured as GDP per unit of primary energy use (PPP-adjusted), also shows large variation across the sample. The main explanatory variables also differ widely across countries. Technological advancement (TA) measured using environmental technology patents (log-transformed), ranges from very low levels to high patenting activity. Some countries report zero patents in some years, which is consistent with uneven innovation capacity. Natural disasters (ND), measured as the annual number of disasters per country, ranges from 0 to 22, showing that disaster exposure is not similar across the G20. The control variables including institutional quality (RQ, GQ), natural resource rents (NRR), trade openness (TRADE), and renewable capacity (REG) also vary considerably, which supports their inclusion in the regression models with country and year fixed effects.

A critical and novel finding is the significant positive interaction between technological advancement and natural disasters for clean cooking and renewable energy, suggesting that technological preparedness can transform disaster exposure into an opportunity for accelerating sustainable energy transitions. Conversely, the results highlight potential trade-offs, as increased trade openness and reliance on natural resource rents are consistently associated with setbacks in renewable energy share and efficiency, underscoring the complex governance challenges in aligning economic policies with energy sustainability goals.

Two-stage least squares (2SLS) results

To address potential endogeneity concerns mainly reverse causality and omitted variable bias this study applies an instrumental variable approach using Two-Stage Least Squares (2SLS). The key concern is that progress in SDG 7 outcomes may itself influence innovation activity, which could bias the fixed-effects estimates. Following the revised methodology, technological advancement (TA) is treated as endogenous and instrumented using its lagged values as internal instruments. All specifications include country fixed effects, year fixed effects, and the same set of control variables used in the baseline regressions. This strategy helps isolate variation in technological advancement that is less likely to be driven by contemporaneous changes in SDG 7 outcomes.

The 2SLS results reported in Table 5 largely support the baseline findings. Technological advancement remains positive and statistically significant for clean cooking access (CCT) and renewable energy share (RES) in the second-stage regressions, indicating that innovation in environmental technologies contributes meaningfully to progress in these two dimensions of the energy transition. For electricity access (EC), the estimated coefficient remains small and statistically weak, which is expected given that electricity access is already close to universal in many G20 countries and therefore exhibits limited variation over time. For energy efficiency (EE), technological advancement shows a positive and statistically significant coefficient. Since EE is defined as GDP per unit of primary energy use where higher values indicate greater efficiency this result suggests that technological progress contributes to more efficient energy use. This finding is consistent with the idea that innovation promotes productivity gains, cleaner technologies, and more efficient energy systems, reinforcing the role of technological advancement in improving energy performance. Natural disasters display a mixed pattern that aligns with the study’s main argument. Disaster occurrences are negatively associated with clean cooking access, suggesting that shocks may disrupt household-level energy transitions. At the same time, disasters are positively and significantly related to renewable energy share, which may reflect post-disaster reconstruction efforts, policy responses, or accelerated investment in renewable energy sources following major shocks.

Robustness check: system generalized method of moments (SYS-GMM) estimation

As an additional robustness check and to account for potential dynamic effects, this study employs the System Generalized Method of Moments (SYS-GMM) estimator. This approach is well suited to situations where SDG 7 outcomes may depend on their own past values and where key regressors such as technological advancement are potentially endogenous. SYS-GMM uses lagged levels and differences of the variables as internal instruments, allowing for control of country-specific heterogeneity, reverse causality, and persistence over time. The SYS-GMM results presented in Table 6 are broadly consistent with both the baseline and 2SLS estimates. Technological advancement remains positive and statistically significant for clean cooking access and renewable energy share, reinforcing the conclusion that innovation supports multiple dimensions of the energy transition. As in earlier models, the coefficient on electricity access is small and weak, reflecting the limited scope for further expansion in countries where electrification rates are already high. Importantly, technological advancement also exhibits a positive and statistically significant relationship with energy efficiency, confirming that innovation contributes to more efficient energy use when efficiency is measured as GDP per unit of primary energy consumption. This result strengthens confidence in the role of technological progress as a driver of sustainable energy outcomes. Natural disasters continue to show a negative association with clean cooking access and a positive, statistically significant association with renewable energy share. These findings support the study’s core argument that while disasters can hinder household-level energy transition outcomes, they may simultaneously be linked to faster renewable energy deployment, potentially through reconstruction processes and policy shifts following major shocks.

Because data are not available for all years, this part of the analysis is limited to 2005–2021, and this shorter period is stated clearly to avoid confusion. Table 7 shows that technological advancement has a positive and statistically significant coefficient, meaning that higher environmental innovation is associated with more renewable electricity generation. In this model, the coefficient for natural disasters is not statistically significant, suggesting that disasters do not show a clear relationship with renewable electricity generation once controls and fixed effects are included. Any interpretation of the size of these effects should match the model form (levels versus logs). Therefore, statements like “a 1% increase leads to a 6.4% increase” should only be used if the dependent variable and TA are both in logs and the coefficient is interpreted as an elasticity.

Discussion

This study investigates how technological advancement, and natural disasters jointly shape multiple dimensions of the energy transition in G20 economies. Drawing on two-way fixed effects, 2SLS, and system GMM estimators, the empirical analysis reveals that innovation acts as a consistent accelerator of clean cooking access, renewable energy deployment, and energy efficiency, while disaster exposure produces divergent effects hindering household-level energy improvements in vulnerable contexts but often coinciding with higher renewable shares at the system level. The interaction between technology and disasters further underscores that stronger innovation capacity can moderate the adverse consequences of shocks and even channel recovery toward cleaner energy pathways. These patterns are not uniform across the G20; they are considerably more pronounced in non-OECD member states.

Technology as a key driver of transition progress

Across all model specifications, technological advancement measured by environmental technology patents emerges as a robust and positive determinant of clean cooking access and renewable energy share. In the baseline two-way fixed effects model, a unit increase in the technology indicator is associated with a statistically significant rise in clean cooking access (coefficient 0.0418, p < 0.01) and in the renewable share of final energy consumption (0.0364, p < 0.05). These results remain stable under instrumental variable and dynamic panel estimation, confirming that innovation fosters the uptake of modern household energy services and accelerates the decarbonisation of energy supply. The finding aligns with the view that technological progress lowers costs, expands technical feasibility, and improves system integration, thereby enabling faster renewable penetration and more accessible clean cooking solutions.

The relationship with electricity access is, as expected, small and statistically insignificant in most specifications. Many G20 countries already maintain near-universal electrification rates, leaving limited within-country variation for technological shifts to explain. Energy efficiency defined as GDP per unit of primary energy use displays a consistent positive and significant association with technological advancement (e.g., coefficient 0.0126, p < 0.05 in Table 4; 0.0154, p < 0.05 in 2SLS; 0.0118, p < 0.05 in system GMM). This indicates that higher levels of environmental innovation are linked to more efficient use of energy, consistent with the expected role of advanced technologies in optimising industrial processes, building performance, and power systems. Contrary to an earlier interpretation, the sign and significance of the coefficient unambiguously support the conclusion that technological progress improves, rather than undermines, energy efficiency in the G20 context.

Natural disasters as both a barrier and a trigger

The evidence confirms that natural disasters exert a dual influence on the energy transition. In the baseline model, the direct coefficient of disaster occurrence on clean cooking access is negative but not statistically significant at conventional levels; however, the sub-sample analysis reveals a strongly negative and significant effect in non-OECD G20 economies (coefficient − 0.2124, p < 0.05). This suggests that disaster-related disruptions to household infrastructure, supply chains, and public budgets predominantly impede clean cooking adoption in countries with higher vulnerability and weaker coping capacity. In the aggregate full sample, therefore, the barrier effect is visible primarily through the lens of development heterogeneity rather than as a uniform global outcome.

Simultaneously, the number of disaster events is positively associated with the renewable energy share. The baseline estimate (0.2247, p < 0.10) and the 2SLS result (0.2511, p < 0.05) indicate that more frequent disasters tend to coincide with a higher proportion of renewables in final energy consumption. This pattern is consistent with the “focusing event” hypothesis, in which catastrophic shocks increase political and social demand for resilient, decentralised energy systems. Post-disaster reconstruction often creates windows of opportunity to scale up renewable generation, modernise grids, and reduce reliance on vulnerable fossil-fuel infrastructure. The positive and significant interaction term between technology and disasters (TA×ND) for clean cooking and renewable energy further corroborates this interpretation: countries with stronger technological capabilities are better positioned to turn disaster recovery into an accelerator of sustainable energy transitions.

The heterogeneous effects across OECD and non-OECD G20 Countries

The sub-sample estimations underscore important differences between OECD and non-OECD G20 members. In non-OECD economies, technological advancement carries large and significant coefficients for clean cooking access (0.0619, p < 0.01), renewable energy share (0.0527, p < 0.05), and energy efficiency (0.0185, p < 0.05). In OECD countries, by contrast, the same coefficients are smaller and statistically indistinguishable from zero. This contrast reflects the fact that non-OECD members typically face larger residual transition gaps and less entrenched legacy infrastructure, allowing new technologies to generate higher marginal gains. The interaction effect (TA×ND) similarly reaches significance only in the non-OECD sub-sample, indicating that technological capability is especially valuable for mitigating disaster impacts and channelling recovery into clean energy expansion where institutional and financial constraints are otherwise more binding. Natural disasters also reveal a differentiated profile: the negative effect on clean cooking access is confined to non-OECD economies, whereas the positive association with renewable energy share appears significant in this group. OECD members, with more robust social safety nets and diversified energy systems, appear less sensitive to disaster-induced setbacks in household energy outcomes. These findings highlight the need for differentiated policy approaches: in advanced economies, the emphasis may rest on system optimisation and flexibility, while in emerging G20 nations, accelerating technology diffusion and ensuring that disaster recovery explicitly addresses household energy access remain critical priorities.

The contribution of robustness checks

The robustness checks reinforce the credibility of the main results. The 2SLS strategy, which instruments technological advancement and its interaction with disasters using lagged values, produces coefficients that are consistent in sign, magnitude, and significance with the baseline fixed-effects estimates, addressing potential reverse causality. The system GMM estimator, which accounts for persistence in the dependent variables and dynamic endogeneity, similarly confirms the core findings and satisfies standard diagnostic tests (insignificant AR(2) and Hansen p-values). When the renewable energy outcome is replaced by the log of renewable electricity generation, technological advancement retains a positive and significant coefficient (0.0874, p < 0.05), strengthening the inference that innovation drives tangible expansion of clean energy output. Winsorising the data at the 5th and 95th percentiles leaves the key results unchanged, confirming that outliers do not drive the main conclusions. Collectively, these checks demonstrate that the relationships identified are not artefacts of a particular estimation method or sample composition.

Policy implications

The findings carry clear policy messages for G20 governments and international cooperation. First, strengthening innovation systems and accelerating the diffusion of environmental technologies especially modern clean cooking devices, distributed renewable solutions, and energy-efficiency technologies can substantially advance multiple SDG 7 indicators, with the largest gains expected in non-OECD settings where transition gaps remain wide. This calls for targeted support to green R&D, affordable technology transfer, and market-building initiatives that lower barriers to adoption. Second, disaster recovery frameworks must go beyond restoring pre-existing infrastructure. They should deliberately “build back better” by embedding resilience and decarbonisation into reconstruction investment prioritising decentralised renewables, microgrids, climate-proof grid components, and reliable clean cooking supply chains. Doing so can transform post-disaster moments from setbacks into structural opportunities for sustainable energy transitions. Third, the differentiated results across OECD and non-OECD members underline that policy design must be context-specific. OECD countries may focus on grid modernisation, storage integration, and demand-side flexibility, while non-OECD members stand to benefit most from concessional finance, institutional capacity building, and rapid expansion of both grid-based and off-grid modern energy services. Coordinating innovation, resilience, and access strategies will be essential to ensure that the energy transition in G20 nations is not only fast but also equitable and robust in the face of growing climate volatility.

Conclusion

This study set out to examine how technological advancement and natural disasters jointly influence the multidimensional energy transition in G20 economies over 2005–2023, using four SDG 7 indicators electricity access, clean cooking access, renewable energy share, and energy efficiency. Motivated by the limited evidence on the interplay between innovation and climate‑related shocks, and by the need to move beyond single‑outcome analyses, the study applied two‑way fixed‑effects, 2SLS, and System GMM estimators to a rich panel dataset, ensuring robust and credible empirical results50.

The findings offer a nuanced narrative. Technological advancement, proxied by environmental technology patents, emerges as the most consistent and powerful driver of transition progress. Its positive effect is particularly pronounced for clean cooking access and renewable energy share, and it is also significantly associated with improvements in the efficiency indicator. Natural disasters exhibit a dual character: they are linked with setbacks in household‑level clean cooking, reflecting the destruction of infrastructure and livelihoods, yet they are simultaneously associated with higher system‑level renewable energy shares, consistent with post‑disaster rebuilding that favours resilient, decentralized clean energy. Crucially, the significant positive interaction between technology and disaster exposure suggests that innovation capacity not only accelerates transition under normal conditions but can also moderate and in some cases transform the impact of disasters from a barrier into a catalyst, especially for clean cooking and renewable energy. Sub‑sample analyses further reveal that these effects are strongest in non‑OECD G20 economies, where transition gaps are larger and marginal returns to technology adoption are higher, whereas in OECD members the coefficients are weaker and often not significant, reflecting already mature energy systems and limited room for dramatic improvements on the chosen indicators.

These results carry several implications. First, the consistent importance of technological innovation underscores the need for sustained investment in green R&D, patenting, and technology diffusion, with a particular focus on clean cooking solutions and renewable integration technologies. Second, disaster recovery and climate adaptation strategies should be explicitly aligned with energy transition goals; “building back better” can turn post‑disaster reconstruction into an opportunity to expand renewable capacity, decentralize energy systems, and protect vulnerable households from losing access to clean cooking. Third, the heterogeneity across OECD and non‑OECD members cautions against uniform policy prescriptions: non‑OECD G20 economies stand to gain the most from technology transfer, affordable finance, and rapid household‑energy upgrading, while OECD economies may need to focus on system flexibility, storage, and deep efficiency gains to sustain progress. Finally, the interplay results imply that strengthening technological capabilities before disasters strike can enhance resilience and accelerate the transition in the aftermath, making innovation a form of proactive climate adaptation.

Limitations and future research

This study has several limitations that also motivate future research. Because the analysis relies on country-level data, it may mask important within-country variation, so future studies could use subnational or city-level datasets to better capture localized disaster impacts and uneven energy-access conditions. In addition, measuring disaster exposure using event counts does not reflect differences in intensity, damages, or people affected; subsequent work could incorporate severity-based measures (economic losses, affected population) and distinguish impacts by disaster type such as floods, storms, heatwaves, or wildfires. While patents are a useful proxy for technological capability, they do not fully represent diffusion and real-world adoption, suggesting the value of combining patent indicators with technology deployment measures, investment flows, or clean-energy capacity additions. Finally, the current study emphasizes direct effects, and future research could investigate deeper transmission mechanisms such as how disasters reshape energy policy, fiscal priorities, and private investment or how international finance and technology transfer influence transition pathways, especially in non-OECD G20 economies.
