 1.0 INTRODUCTION AND PURPOSE
Biologists first documented elk on the Hanford Site in 1972; since then, the elk herd known as
the Rattlesnake Hills Elk Herd (RHEH) has grown substantially (PNNL-13331, Population
Characteristics and Seasonal Movement Patterns of the Rattlesnake Hills Elk Herd: Status
Report 2000). Through the 1990s, the core range of the RHEH was focused on the portion of the
Hanford Reach National Monument known as the Fitzner-Eberhardt Arid Lands Ecology (ALE)
Reserve. More recently, larger numbers of elk have been occupying the U.S. Department of
Energy (DOE), Hanford Field Office (HFO), formerly the DOE, Richland Operations Office
managed portion- of the Hanford Site, known as central Hanford (Figure 1-1). This began with
bachelor groups of bulls occupying the site intermittently, and the herd has grown to resident
herds, including bulls, cows, and calves. The Washington Department of Fish and Wildlife
(WDFW) established a target herd size for the RHEH of less than 350 animals to minimize
damages on adjacent private agricultural lands (Washington State Elk Herd Plan–Yakima Elk
Herd [WDFW 2002]). Attempts to control the RHEH population through hunting on private
lands and a relocation effort during 2000 have failed to limit growth of the population toward the
WDFW target, and the herd exceeds 1,600 according to recent counts (PNNL-13331; DOE/RL-
2023-20, Hanford Annual Site Environmental Report for Calendar Year 2022). Although elk are
present on central Hanford year-round, they continue to move between areas offsite, the ALE
Reserve, and central Hanford throughout the year, and it is these movements that result in many
of the elk-vehicle collisions (EVC) that occur along Hanford Site roads and on the Washington
State highways bordering the site. In addition, changes in management strategies on the ALE
Reserve, including recent and potential future tribal elk hunts, may alter herd behavior and result
in additional animals transiting onto and off central Hanford.

HFO conducts elk monitoring on the primary range of the RHEH (Figure 1-1) to document elk
use areas and relative abundance, as well as changes in distribution (DOE/RL-2023-20).
Previously, the WDFW, the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, and the HFO contractors have
conducted elk counts over portions of the ALE Reserve and central Hanford but counts of the
area (including the ALE Reserve, McGee Ranch/Riverlands, and central Hanford) were not
routinely completed. The number of elk observed during aerial surveys provide a reference to the
RHEH wintering population, but not an exact count due to incomplete surveying of the primary
range. Post-calving summer population estimates for the RHEH were below 100 animals prior to
1990 and then expanded to over 600 animals by 2000 (PNNL-13331). In 2019, aerial elk counts
of the RHEH included photographic verification of the entire primary range for the first time.
During previous survey years, elk may have been present but not counted in portions of the
primary range not included in the aerial survey, although most of the known core use areas were
surveyed and counts likely included most of the herd. The 2019 survey estimated the RHEH
population at over 1,100, documenting a nearly two-fold population increase since 2000
(DOE/RL-2021-15, Hanford Site Annual Site Environmental Report for Calendar Year 2021).
This expansion of the elk population causes concern for adverse impacts to sensitive habitats and
safety concerns associated with EVCs.

Across the state of Washington, over 200 EVCs occur each year (Reducing the risk of wildlife
collisions [WSDOT 2024]). EVCs pose one of the greatest hazards to Hanford Site personnel,
especially when commuting to and from work. There are many factors that affect the potential of
an EVC at a particular location at a given time. These include the likelihood that an elk is
present, the likelihood that a vehicle is present, the likelihood of an elk avoiding a vehicle, and
the likelihood of a vehicle avoiding an elk. Additional subfactors that may impact the likelihood
of an EVC occurring at any specific place and time include time of day, ambient light,

 1

environmental conditions (e.g., precipitation, fog, ice), speed of vehicle travel, driver
attentiveness, habitat type, and others.

The purpose of this study was to evaluate the above-mentioned factors to determine where EVCs
are most likely to occur, and to determine which factors or subfactors could be modified to
reduce the potential for EVCs. The study focused on the following four factors relating to EVCs
at the Hanford Site:

 1. Update the known core activity areas for elk both on the ALE Reserve and central
 Hanford
 2. Evaluate documented EVC locations for patterns
 3. Model movements of elk between core activity areas to identify likely EVC areas
 4. Make recommendations to reduce EVCs.

 2.0 METHODS
This section provides methods for aerial/ground surveys, analysis of EVCs, movement modeling,
and collaring and hazing.

2.1 AERIAL AND GROUND SURVEYS
Staff conducted aerial surveys from a Cessna® 1 182 fixed-wing aircraft, performing a systematic
grid spaced approximately 1.24 mi (2 km) apart across the survey area (Figure 2-1). Survey
elevation was approximately 1,200 ft (366 m) above ground level, and aircraft speed was
approximately 120 mph (193 kph). In addition to the pilot, two surveyors conducted the aerial
surveys, one on each side of the aircraft. Surveyors scanned the area on their respective side of
the airplane, out to approximately 3,281 ft (1,000 m). Once an elk herd was discovered, the
aircraft circled to enable for an accurate count and for digital photographs to be taken. Each herd
was photographed one or more times to capture the entire extent of the herd, and photos were
used to confirm or amend visual counts after returning to the office. Elk herd locations were
recorded with a Global Positioning System (GPS) unit.

Driving surveys were conducted on the central Hanford portion of the survey area to gain a better
understanding of seasonal elk distribution. Driving surveys consisted of traveling generally
established routes covering all main roads and many of the secondary roads on the Hanford Site
(Figure 2-2) with two to three surveyors. Driving speeds ranged from 10 to 20 mph
(12 to 32 kph) on unimproved roads, 20 to 30 mph (32 to 48 kph) on paved roads, and posted
speed limits on highways for safety purposes (e.g., 60 mph [97 kph]). Surveyors scanned the
areas visible from the established roadways and recorded locations of observed elk. Surveyors
used binoculars, spotting scopes, and digital cameras to observe and document elk herds;
spotlights were used during low-light conditions. Staff approximated herd locations using paper
maps and then later digitized the positions using a Geographic Information System (GIS).

In addition to aerial and ground surveys, personnel documented the location and number of elk
while performing other job duties, termed “incidental observations.” Staff largely made these
observations from roadways while traveling between work locations; therefore, incidental
observation data are likely biased toward areas visible from paved roads. Personnel also
documented elk while traveling on unpaved two-track roads or while conducting pedestrian
surveys away from roadways. Staff collected data using a GPS and included the number and
location of animals observed. These incidental observations were compiled in a dataset along
with observations made during the formal aerial and ground surveys.

2.2 ELK-VEHICLE COLLISIONS
Vehicle collisions with elk are a significant safety concern on the Hanford Site. Many elk are
struck by vehicles each year while Hanford Site staff commute to and from work and between
work locations. EVCs occur in private and government-owned vehicles, causing property
damage and potential injuries to occupants; the collisions are often fatal for the animals involved.
Data about the location and timing of EVCs are often difficult to obtain, because collisions are
not always reported, animals can move and become hard to locate after being struck, and
carcasses can be removed from a public roadway by persons collecting carcasses under the
WDFW’s roadkill salvage program, which was initiated in 2016 (WDFW 2024).

Notwithstanding the limitations described above, information about the location and timing of
EVCs is obtained from the Hanford Site Biological Control organization, Hanford Patrol, or
other sources. These data are compiled to track the location and timing of EVCs. These data can
help calibrate movement models that predict animal travel routes across the site by showing
actual locations where EVCs occur. This study included collision data from September 2016
through August 2024.

Myers et al. 2008 assessed ungulate-vehicle collisions in eastern Washington over a 5-year
period and identified areas of EVC hot spots as sections of road 2 to 4 mi (3.2 to 6.4 km) in
length with more than one elk killed per year on average. The EVC data from the Hanford Site
were similarly analyzed to compare average annual EVC frequency over 3-mi (4.8-km) road
segments over a similar 5-year period.

2.3 MOVEMENT MODELING
The core use areas for elk on the ALE Reserve, McGee Ranch/Riverlands, and central Hanford
were defined using the kernel density estimation (KDE) tool available in ArcGIS® 2 Pro 3.0.3.
Input data for the KDE included the point data collected from the aerial, ground, and incidental
observations, including location and number of elk. The KDE tool generates a contour map from
the point data showing areas with the highest concentration of elk use. The following optimal
bandwidth function was used to calculate the search radius input into the KDE:

                                               hopt = [2/3n](1/4) σ
Where:

 n is the sample size

 σ is the standard distance 3

The standard distance was calculated using the spatial statistics tools in the ArcGIS Pro 3.0.3.
Grid cell size for all modeling raster outputs was 32.8 ft (10 m). The core areas were defined as
the KDE contour containing greater than 90 percent of the individual elk observations.

Circuit and random walk theories were applied to model elk movement patterns across the
Hanford Site using the following software: Circuitscape (“Circuitscape in Julia: High
Performance Connectivity Modelling to Support Conservation Decisions” [Anantharaman et al.
2020]) and Omniscape (“Omniscape.jl: Software to compute omnidirectional landscape
connectivity” [Landau et al. 2021]; Conserving Nature’s Stage: Mapping Omnidirectional
Connectivity for Resilient Terrestrial Landscapes in the Pacific Northwest, [McRae et al. 2016]).
Circuitscape and Omniscape model the landscape as a conductive surface with low resistances
assigned to landscape features that allow or promote animal movement (high flow) and high
resistances assigned to landscape features that diminish or restrict animal movement (low flow).
The landscape is represented as a large network or grid with each pixel having a resistance value.
Animal movements are modeled as random walkers across the grid. Essentially, the animal’s
movement is an electrical current moving across a large electrical circuit (the landscape) with the
electrical current providing movement probabilities. A resistance map was constructed for the
core range of the RHEH. Map inputs included barriers (e.g., cliffs, rivers, structures, fenced
areas); roads; agricultural areas; cities; slopes; and topographic position. The topographic
position raster was divided into canyon, gentle slope, steep slope, and ridge top. The topographic
position raster was created by employing the Corridor Designer Toolbox for ArcGIS
(CorridorDesigner: ArcGIS toolbox for designing corridors [Majka et al 2007]). A circle of
radius 1,640 ft (500 m) was used as the topographic position index neighborhood and with
applications of a 0-m canyons threshold, a 39 ft (12-m) ridge tops threshold, and a slopes
threshold of 6 degrees. Circuitscape modeled elk movement patterns between the KDE
calculated core areas, essentially between areas on Rattlesnake Mountain, McGee
Ranch/Riverland, and the Hanford Townsite. Omniscape provides a coreless approach by
applying Circuitscape iteratively in a moving window to predict omni-directional movement
patterns. The circular moving window had a radius of 0.6 mi (1 km). The source strength layer
was coarsened using a block size of three to reduce processing time.

Resistance values for landscape features were generally based on the Washington Connected
Landscapes Project: Statewide Analysis, which were adjusted for local conditions such as
modifications to the resistance values for roadways based on observed traffic levels at the
Hanford Site (Washington Connected Landscapes Project: Statewide Analysis
[WHCWG 2010]). The layers and corresponding resistance values used in the models at the
Hanford Site are provided in Table 2-1.

2.4 COLLARING AND HAZING
Collaring elk can provide daily and seasonal movement data to help better characterize potential
EVC hazards. GPS tracking collars remotely detect the exact location of collared animals.
Animal locations are read and stored at pre-set intervals, which can later be downloaded
remotely.

A Lotek LiteTrack 800 collar with GPS logging and remote download capability was selected for
this study. The remote download for this type of collar occurs over radio signal using a Lotek
PinPoint VHF Commander. Personnel conducted a range test to determine the required
proximity from the collar to download GPS data to ensure that data collected by the GPS could
be effectively retrieved via radio signal. Staff deployed a Lotek SRX800 telemetry receiver with
a 9-element antenna to increase radio detection and data downloading range as much as possible
(Figure 2-3).

There are areas of central Hanford where elk occupancy presents a serious hazard to vehicle
travel and other areas where elk can occupy with less risk to people. Hazing was planned to try
and condition animals to avoid high traffic areas. Hazing has been used in many areas across the
country to attempt to reduce human-elk conflict and to reduce ecological impacts associated with
high elk populations (Assessment of Methods for Redistributing Elk to Benefit Vegetation in
Rocky Mountain National Park [Walter et al. 2010]). Hazing success can vary widely depending
on the timing, persistence, and levels of habituation.

Staff completed 11 ground surveys between January 2021 and January 2024. Counts on central
Hanford ranged up to 396 elk in the winter and as low as 27 elk during the summer. Aerial

 11

surveys conducted within the same week detected more elk, indicating that ground surveys
undercount the total number of elk present on the Hanford Site, likely due to elk occupying areas
not visible from roadways.

The incidental observation dataset covers December 2019 through August 2024. There are
375 unique locations recorded in the incidental observation dataset, ranging from single animals
to herds up to 600. These data provide information about elk locations and abundance between
the quarterly flights and are used for modeling core use areas and movement corridors. Although
incidental observations of elk occurred across much of the Hanford Site, large herds typically
existed between the 100 Areas, the Hanford Townsite, and in the flats of the ALE Reserve along
Highway 240.

The completed elk location dataset obtained from aerial, ground, and incidental observations
included 944 elk locations that contained 30,775 individuals. Staff evaluated the locations to
determine if they overlap with areas of interest on the Hanford Site, including waste sites, rare
plant areas, and DOE/RL-96-32, Hanford Site Biological Resources Management Plan Level 5
(rare and irreplaceable) habitats (Table 3-2). Elk occurred in contamination areas, showing they
could potentially be a vector for contamination; elk are routinely sampled for contamination at
Hanford (DOE/RL-2024-10, Hanford Annual Site Environmental Report for Calendar Year
2023). Elk also occupied rare plant areas, riparian areas, and sensitive habitats, including vernal
pools (Figure 3-2). Elk also impacted translocated ground squirrel colonies, trampling burrows at
those locations. Prior studies documented elk impacting natural vegetation, including increasing
bare ground and reducing herbaceous cover, but the impacts of elk on these sensitive habitats at
the Hanford Site are currently unknown (Open File Report 02-208, Ecological Evaluation of the
Abundance and Effects of Elk Herbivory in Rocky Mountain National Park, Colorado, 1994-
1999). The extensive use by elk of priority conservation areas helps validate that model, which
was completed on the Hanford Site in 2019 (HNF-64135, Conservation Habitat Assessment and
Mitigation Prioritization (CHAMP) for the Hanford Site: Identifying Priority Conservation
Areas).

3.2 ELK-VEHICLE COLLISIONS
The EVC dataset includes animals recorded between September 2016 and August 2024. Over
that period, 126 elk EVCs occurred on the Hanford Site or on the bordering highways
(Figure 3-3). As described above, this is likely a subset of the total number of animals struck.

Data evaluation to determine if there was a seasonal trend in EVC frequency excluded partial
years (2016 and 2024). Winter months included January, February, and December; spring
included March, April, and May; summer included June, July, and August; and fall included
September, October, and November. Of the 104 elk struck during this period (2017 to 2023),
nearly half (45) of the EVCs occurred during the fall months, compared to only 13 in the spring
months, while summer (21) and winter (25) had moderate levels of EVCs. The season with the
greatest number of EVCs coincides with the peak breeding season, known as the rut (Figure 3-4).
EVCs in September and October, during the peak of the breeding season, account for 34 percent
of strikes during only 17 percent of the year. All elk location observations and EVC locations
divided by season are shown in Figure 3-5.

Linear regression was used to test if the number of elk struck varies by year, including only the
full years of data (2017 to 2023). The overall regression was not statistically significant at
α=0.05 (R2=0.26, F(1,5)=1.76, p=0.242), indicating that the number of EVCs is not varying
significantly over time (Figure 3-3). Similarly, linear regression did not show a significant
relationship between the number of elk strikes occurring by year by season and the number of elk
counted during aerial surveys conducted during the same periods on central Hanford at
α=0.05 (R2=0.001, F(1,13)=0.014, p=0.91).

Staff calculated EVCs per 3-mi (5-km) road segment by generating 1-mi (1.6 km) buffers around
each road mile marker and calculating the number of EVCs that occurred within the buffer for
each 3-mi (5-km) segment along all roadways. The dataset included 171 mi (275 km) of
roadway, and most buffered miles had no EVCs. A total of 66 buffered miles (106 km) had
EVCs. Several sections of roadway on and adjacent to the Hanford Site qualified as hot spots for
EVCs by having greater than one per year on average (Table 3-3). Highways 240 and 24 had the
greatest annual rate of EVC per 3-mi (5-km) segment at 3.4 and 1.6, respectively. On Hanford,
Route 4 South had 1.4 EVCs and Route 2 North had 1.0 EVC annually over a 3-mi (5-km)
segment. Comparing these data to those from Myers et al. 2008 suggests these four roadways
have some of the highest rates of EVC in the state of Washington (Figure 3-6).

Elk carcasses are regularly salvaged by members of the public on State Highway 240 and
Highway 24 along the borders of central Hanford. Although it is not permitted to remove

3.3 MOVEMENT MODELING
The map generated using the KDE for all elk locations in the dataset is shown in Figure 3-7.
Because most incidental observations of elk occur on central Hanford, the core areas were
modeled using the KDE with only the flight data and found that removal of the incidental
observations from the model resulted in only slight differences in the core areas on the ALE
Reserve. Thus, the final model of the core areas included incidental observations.

The contour line from the KDE containing greater than 90 percent of all elk observations
established the core areas of elk use on the Hanford Site, as shown in Figure 3-8. Core areas
centered on the ALE Reserve and McGee Ranch/Riverlands and between the 100-D Area and the
Hanford Townsite. These core areas provided the necessary data to proceed with the Circuitscape
model.

When small portions of the modeled core use areas overlapped major roadways, the overlapping
areas were removed to show potential movement routes and EVC hotspots prior to inputting the
core areas into the Circuitscape modeling software (Figure 3-8).

The Circuitscape model output provides potential elk movement routes between core areas
(Figure 3-9). Many highway and major road crossings of these routes coincide with EVC hot
spots. There are large swaths of ground with relatively little to impede or concentrate elk
movements, such as along Highway 240, and the model shows that elk movement likely occurs
at many locations across that road. In contrast, elk movement between the ALE Reserve and
central Hanford core areas are constrained between the 200 East Area and the Laser
Interferometer Gravitational Wave Observatory (LIGO), resulting in focused elk movements
across Route 4 South near the 200 East Area hill. Elk movements are similarly constrained
between the LIGO and Energy Northwest, focusing movements across Route 4 South just south
of the Wye Barricade. Elk movement is constrained between Energy Northwest and the
Columbia River, resulting in elk crossing Route 4 South north of the 300 Area. The EVC dataset
supports these observations, showing hotspots for EVCs at these locations. Although elk
movements may be focused in other areas, such as across Route 6, strikes may not occur in those
areas due to low traffic levels or other factors.

The highest rate of elk strikes occurred along Highway 240 between Richland and the
intersection with Route 10. This area had an average of 3.4 strikes annually in a 3-mi (5-km)
section. There are large agricultural fields and the Yakima River on the southern boundary of
Highway 240 at this location. Strikes at this location occurred mostly during the summer, when
food and water resources are limited in the shrub-steppe. These agricultural resources likely
attract elk across the road resulting in strikes.

The Omniscape model output shows that roads are the major impediment to elk moving freely
across the Hanford Site and displays likely movement routes on/off of the primary range
(Figure 3-10).

3.4 COLLARING AND HAZING
Staff conducted an evaluation as to the effectiveness and range of the remote download system
for the selected collar. On flat ground with a clear line of sight, GPS data downloading from the
collar using the radio receiver was effective out to 1 mi (1,600 m). From the top of a large hill,
GPS download range increased to 3 mi (5,000 m) and the radio beacon could be heard from 7 mi
(11,500 m). These ranges demonstrated that the GPS data could effectively be downloaded from
the collars at Hanford, because elk can easily be detected and approached within those distances.
The entire core range of elk on central Hanford could be monitored for radio beacons from a single
location on a road on top of Gable Mountain, and much of the core range is also within 3 mi (5,000
m) of that location, which would make for efficient downloading of GPS data. After range testing,
it was determined that collaring was cancelled as part of this study but may be considered in the
future.

Materials acquired to conduct hazing on elk herds included paintball markers, cracker shells, and
rubber bullets. Under HFO direction, hazing was cancelled prior to implementation.

 4.0 DISCUSSION
Movement of the RHEH can be characterized into three groups: (1) on/off the primary range,
(2) local/daily movement, and (3) movement between core areas within the primary range. These
movement types can each result in EVCs.

On/Off the Primary Range
Although a resident herd of 500 to 1,000 animals remain on the Hanford site and the ALE
Reserve throughout the year, there are another 1,200+ animals that move offsite. Where the elk
go is currently unknown, but additional information about these movements could help inform
regional elk management decisions, especially as the RHEH population continues to increase.
There is mostly contiguous habitat between the Hanford Site and the Yakima Training Center,
and seasonal movements likely occur between these two areas. Elk could also be moving
southwest toward the Yakama Nation reservation, and some elk movement does occur across the
Columbia River. These movements likely result in strikes along Highways 240 and 24.

Local/Daily Movement
Local movements occur between foraging areas and water. Elk may be more likely to avoid
vehicles during local movements, because they are accustomed to crossing these roadways on a
routine basis and may even be familiar with typical traffic patterns, but EVCs do still occur
during these local/daily movements. Although the Columbia River is a large and constant water
source, elk readily use artificial water sources when available (Figure 4-1 and Figure 4-2). Major
artificial water sources are present intermittently at the 100-N fire station, the 200 Area fire
station, the 200-E Powerhouse Ditch, and possibly other areas where water is discharged on the
ground surface. These temporary water sources result in elk frequently crossing major roadways
during local movements, increasing potential for EVCs. Preventing creation of artificial water
sources or restricting elk access to these locations could help reduce EVC risk. Minimizing
traffic on major roadways that traverse core areas would further reduce EVC risks. These
roadways include Route 2 South, Route 1, and the eastern half of Route 11A. Restricting through
traffic on these roads, especially during daily commuting times, would likely improve personnel
safety while traveling to and from work.

Movement Between Core Areas Within the Primary Range
Most of the EVCs on central Hanford likely occur during movements between core areas. This is
evidenced by the high number of strikes on roads along elk travel routes between core areas. A
high percentage of the EVCs at the Hanford Site happen in September and October during the
peak of breeding season. Rutting animals can exhibit behaviors such as increased movement and
aggression that make them more likely to be struck by vehicles, which may explain the higher
number of EVCs at this time of year.

The primary commuting routes onto the Hanford Site include Highway 240 and Route 4 South.
These two roadways have some of the highest rates of EVCs anywhere in the state of
Washington (Myers et al. 2008). To improve worker safety, several factors could be considered
to reduce potential for EVCs. One recent change to traffic patterns could significantly reduce
speed and EVCs along Route 2 South and Route 11A. These roads are being modified from two
lanes in each direction down to one lane in each direction, which is expected to result in lower
vehicle speeds by eliminating passing. Traffic patterns also vary over time as operations and
work areas start and end at different regions of the Hanford Site. The 2019 Coronavirus Disease
also significantly altered traffic in 2020. These are confounding variables when analyzing EVC
patterns.

Speed is one of the major factors influencing vehicle collision rates for deer and elk; roads with
posted speed limits of 60 mph (97 kph) or greater had unusually high ungulate-vehicle collision
rates (Myers et al. 2008). Altering speed limits at times of the year (e.g., fall) and times of the
day (low light) could potentially reduce EVCs. Encouraging site personnel to follow posted
speed limits through education and enforcement could also help reduce EVCs. The Benton
County Sheriff has a full-time deputy assigned to the Hanford Site with an emphasis on reducing
speed violations. Collared elk could also be used to trigger geo-fencing alerts to drivers, such as
flashing signs warning of elk near the roadway, which is being tested in other areas (New Study
Aims to Protect Elk on Roadways with Collar-Activated Signs [Humboldt Now 2023]). In other
areas around the state, wildlife overpasses and underpasses along with fencing have helped reduce
road strikes in high-risk areas; similar methods could be used at the Hanford Site.

In addition to personnel safety concerns, elk can cause significant impacts to vegetative
resources (Open File Report 02-208). Exclusion studies conducted in other areas to evaluate elk
impacts on vegetation, could be implemented at the Hanford Site and could identify whether
sensitive habitats are being adversely impacted (Open File Report 02-208). Collars would also
provide substantially more information about elk use of waste sites, rare plant, and rare habitat
areas. Elk could be a vector for noxious weeds by spreading weed seeds and by disturbing soil
that encourages establishment of noxious weeds. Noxious weed maps are currently under
development at the Hanford Site and a more comprehensive evaluation of elk use of noxious
weed infested areas will be possible in the future.

Data used to inform the Circuitscape and Omniscape models include incidental observations of
elk that are more likely to be biased towards roads and normal routes of travel, and continued
aerial surveys provide a better representation of elk use areas. The movement corridors mapped by
this study are modeled results, and collaring would provide actual observations of elk movements
across roadways on the Hanford Site, which could significantly enhance data resolution.

Understanding the movements of elk is an important component of predicting potential EVC hot
spots. Modeling is intended to identify movement corridors most likely to be used by elk during

 25

movement on/off the primary range, local/daily movement, and movement between core areas.
These corridors represent areas where elk are most likely to cross roadways, resulting in
potential EVCs. A better understanding of likely movement corridors can help with adaptive
management decisions, such as roadway closures or reduced speed zones that could reduce the
potential for EVCs.

One unexpected finding of this study was that the number of elk on central Hanford is not
correlated with the number of EVCs that occur. Based on this finding, simply reducing the
number of elk present may not significantly reduce the number of EVCs. As discussed in
Section 1.0, many factors influence the potential of an EVC occurring at a particular location at a
given time. One potential explanation for the lack of correlation between elk numbers and EVCs
is that driver awareness increases with the number of elk; drivers that are seeing elk regularly
may watch for animals more diligently and slow down in areas where elk are typically
encountered. In addition, activities that alter typical elk movements could result in short-term
increases in EVCs by shifting daily movement patterns. New construction, changes in traffic
patterns, or hunting/hazing could result in altered elk movements. These types of alterations
could effectively change an elk from an experienced animal that is aware of and avoids daily
traffic patterns in a particular area into a naïve or panicked animal that may be more likely to be
involved in an EVC. Activities that could alter typical elk movements should be evaluated for the
potential to affect EVCs.

Continued monitoring of the RHEH at the Hanford Site is recommended to document ongoing
increases in the population and to evaluate for changes in use areas that could impact other
resources, or personnel safety.
