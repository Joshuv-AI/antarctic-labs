Sleep circuit modulation: from animal models to human translation
Authors: Zixiao Yin, Yichen Xu, Yu-Ting Tseng, Wolf-Julian Neumann, Hagai Bergman, Huiling Tan, Jianguo Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSleep occupies one-third of human life and serves critical functions in memory consolidation, metabolic regulation and neural homeostasis. Sleep disorders affect >70 million people globally and are prevalent across neurodegenerative, psychiatric and neurological conditions. Recent advances in optogenetics and chemogenetics have enabled precise control of sleep–wake transitions in animal models, demonstrating that sleep can be modulated actively through targeted circuit manipulation. However, the translation of these findings to human applications faces substantial challenges.This review synthesizes current understanding of sleep circuit organization from animal studies, examines effects of direct brain stimulation on human sleep across diverse targets, analyses discrepancies between animal and human findings and proposes translational strategies.Although some principles appear conserved across species, significant differences in circuit architecture, technical capabilities and regulatory constraints necessitate novel approaches for human sleep modulation. We outline emerging solutions, including improved spatiotemporal precision, oscillatory targeting and disease-specific interventions that might bridge the translational gap.

Introduction

Sleep occupies approximately one-third of human life, yet its neural regulation remains incompletely understood despite decades of intensive research. Far from being a passive state, sleep serves essential functions including memory consolidation, synaptic homeostasis, metabolic waste clearance and immune regulation.1-4 The pervasive nature of sleep disruption across neurological and psychiatric disorders, from Alzheimer’s disease and Parkinson’s disease to depression and schizophrenia, underscores its fundamental importance to brain health.5,6 Moreover, even in healthy individuals, suboptimal sleep quality represents a major public health challenge, affecting work productivity, learning efficiency and athletic performance and serving as a risk factor for numerous chronic diseases.7,8

Sleep disorders represent a major public health concern, with epidemiological studies linking insufficient sleep to millions of lost working days annually across industrialized nations.9 This substantial impact has motivated the search for interventions that could optimize sleep efficiency. Consider the transformative potential: if we could enable patients to gain an additional 1 h of restorative sleep or allow healthy individuals to sleep 1 h less by enhancing sleep efficiency, the clinical and societal benefits would be significant, fundamentally altering how we approach both health and human performance.

Although sleep is a highly conserved biological process, the question of whether it can be modulated actively has now been answered definitively in animal models. Recent technological breakthroughs, particularly optogenetics and chemogenetics, have enabled unprecedented precision in manipulating sleep-regulatory circuits.10,11 Through activation/inhibition of specific neuronal populations, researchers can now induce immediate transitions from deep sleep to wakefulness or from wakefulness to sleep, with millisecond precision in rodents.12,13 These manipulations not only alter sleep–wake states but also enhance specific functions, improving memory consolidation, accelerating learning and even reducing pathological protein accumulation in disease models.14,15

This remarkable control over sleep in animal models raises a tantalizing question: can we achieve similar modulation of human sleep circuits? The potential translational opportunity is enormous, yet the path from rodent optogenetics to human therapeutic applications remains unclear. In this review, we first summarize the sleep-regulatory circuits identified through animal studies, then examine the growing literature on how direct brain stimulation affects human sleep. We analyse the discrepancies between animal and human findings, exploring why direct translation has proved challenging. Finally, we propose emerging solutions and realistic pathways that might enable therapeutic sleep circuit modulation in humans, potentially transforming both our understanding of sleep neurobiology and our ability to address sleep disruption across the spectrum of brain disorders.

Brain circuits for sleep transitions based on lessons from animal studies

Since Aserinsky and Kleitman’s discovery of distinct non-rapid eye movement (NREM) and REM states in 1953,16 extensive research has revealed multiple brain circuits regulating sleep–wake transitions through mutual inhibition and multilayered control. These circuits involve evolutionarily conserved brain regions that vary considerably in relative size and organization across species (Fig. 1A). Here, we overview the major sleep-regulatory circuits from animal studies, focusing on wake-promoting, NREM sleep-promoting and REM sleep-promoting networks (Fig. 1B). Wake-promoting circuits

The ascending arousal system consists of multiple interconnected nuclei that collectively maintain cortical activation during wakefulness.19-23 The locus coeruleus contains noradrenergic neurons that project throughout the neuraxis, displaying characteristic wake-on/REM-off firing patterns. These neurons maintain arousal through direct cortical projections and inhibition of sleep-promoting regions.24-26 Likewise, the dorsal raphe nucleus provides serotonergic input that sustains wakefulness, with neurons showing reduced activity during both NREM and REM sleep.27

The lateral hypothalamus contains hypocretin/orexin neurons that serve as master stabilizers of wakefulness. These neurons project to all major arousal centres, including the locus coeruleus, dorsal raphe nucleus, paraventricular thalamus and tuberomammillary nucleus, with their loss causing narcolepsy, highlighting their crucial role in maintaining consolidated wakefulness.28-31 The tuberomammillary nucleus provides histaminergic input that promotes cortical activation, while the basal forebrain supplies both cholinergic and GABAergic projections that regulate cortical state transitions.32-35

Non-REM sleep-promoting circuits

The ventrolateral preoptic area (VLPO) represents the primary sleep-promoting centre, containing GABAergic neurons that fire specifically during sleep.36 These neurons project to and inhibit all major arousal centres, implementing the ‘flip-flop switch’ model, whereby mutual inhibition between sleep and wake centres ensures rapid, complete state transitions.37 The median preoptic nucleus (MnPO) contains additional sleep-active populations that integrate circadian and homeostatic signals.38,39 Basal forebrain somatostatin–GABAergic neurons promote NREM by suppressing arousal.34

Recent discoveries have expanded our understanding of NREM regulation beyond the hypothalamus. The parafascicular nucleus of the thalamus contains glutamatergic neurons that promote NREM sleep through projections to the striatum.40 The nucleus accumbens contains adenosine A2A receptor-expressing neurons that induce sleep when activated, potentially mediating the somnogenic effects of adenosine accumulation.41,42 The thalamic reticular nucleus generates sleep spindles, aiding sleep and memory,43 and midbrain glutamatergic and frontal parvalbumin circuits enhance NREM via excitatory and inhibitory projections, respectively.44,45

REM sleep-promoting circuits

REM sleep generation involves specialized brainstem circuits with the sublaterodorsal tegmental nucleus serving as a critical node.46 Glutamatergic sublaterodorsal tegmental nucleus neurons activate before REM onset and project to glycinergic/GABAergic neurons in the ventromedial medulla that produce muscle atonia.47-49 The pedunculopontine and laterodorsal tegmental nuclei contain cholinergic neurons that contribute to REM phenomena including cortical activation and ponto-geniculo-occipital waves.50-52

The ventrolateral periaqueductal gray (vlPAG) contains GABAergic REM-off neurons that gate REM sleep entry.53 During wakefulness and NREM sleep, these neurons inhibit REM-generating circuits. Their suppression, combined with reduced activity in aminergic systems, permits REM sleep initiation. Recent work has identified melanin-concentrating hormone neurons in the lateral hypothalamus as REM-promoting elements that might coordinate REM timing with metabolic state.54,55

Lessons from animal studies

Several key principles emerge from decades of animal circuit manipulation studies that inform translational efforts.

First, sleep is actively regulated through dynamic circuit interactions. The mutual inhibition between sleep and wake centres creates bistability that prevents intermediate states,56 and multiple pathways ensure robust state control even when individual components fail. This distributed architecture is likely to have evolved to ensure that sleep–wake regulation remains stable despite local circuit damage.

Second, sleep modulation is clearly feasible. Optogenetic activation of sleep-promoting neurons in the VLPO can rapidly induce NREM sleep.57 Conversely, stimulation of orexin neurons triggers immediate awakening from deep sleep.13 Chemogenetic approaches extend these effects, sustaining altered sleep states for hours and demonstrating both acute and chronic modulation capabilities.58

Third, multiple intervention targets exist. Sleep can be promoted through activation of sleep centres (VLPO and MnPO), inhibition of arousal centres (locus coeruleus and dorsal raphe nucleus) or modulation of state-specific phenomena (thalamic spindles and hippocampal ripples). This diversity of targets provides flexibility for therapeutic approaches.

Fourth, circuit manipulation affects sleep function. Optogenetic induction of slow oscillations during NREM sleep enhances memory consolidation.59 Conversely, selective disruption of hippocampal sharp-wave ripples during post-training consolidation impairs hippocampus-dependent spatial learning while leaving global sleep architecture largely unchanged.60 In animal models of Alzheimer’s disease, restoring or augmenting slow-wave activity via circuit-level interventions reduces amyloid pathology and rescues cognitive deficits.61,62 These findings suggest that targeted sleep modulation could serve therapeutic purposes beyond simply treating insomnia.

The effect of direct brain stimulation on human sleep

Direct brain stimulation in humans, primarily through deep brain stimulation (DBS), provides unique insights into human sleep circuit function. Although these interventions are performed for clinical indications rather than sleep research per se, they offer an invaluable window into human sleep regulation. More than 200 000 patients worldwide have received DBS for various conditions,63 with electrodes placed in diverse brain regions that often overlap with sleep-regulatory circuits identified in animals (Fig. 2). Basal ganglia stimulation

The subthalamic nucleus (STN) represents the most common DBS target, primarily for Parkinson’s disease. High-frequency stimulation (130 Hz) generally improves subjective sleep quality,64-68 although objective polysomnographic changes are more modest.69,70 The improvements appear to result from suppression of pathological beta oscillations that fragment sleep, with beta power showing inverse relationships with slow-wave activity during NREM sleep,71-75 which has been validated both in non-human primates73 and in humans.71 In rodents, neurons in the medial STN expressing corticotrophin-releasing hormone play a key role in mediating arousal, defensive behaviours and transitions out of REM sleep in response to threats (e.g. predator cues).76 Optogenetic activation of these medial STN corticotrophin-releasing hormone neurons promotes wakefulness and suppresses REM sleep, whereas inhibition reduces arousal and allows sustained REM under stress.76

Interestingly, the sleep effects of STN-DBS in humans might be disease specific. Patients with Parkinson’s disease consistently show sleep improvement with STN-DBS, whereas patients with dystonia show more variable responses.77,78 Those with Meige syndrome, a form of cranial dystonia, may experience motor benefits without sleep enhancement despite identical stimulation parameters.77 This suggests that STN stimulation for patients with Parkinson’s disease might improve sleep primarily by suppressing disease-specific pathological activity, such as exaggerated beta oscillations, rather than through a universal modulation of sleep–wake circuits.

Globus pallidus internus (GPi) stimulation produces heterogeneous sleep outcomes that might depend on precise electrode location.79-85 Approximately half of patients with Parkinson’s disease experience clinically meaningful sleep improvement, particularly those with electrodes in the sensorimotor territory of the left GPi. The remainder show minimal change or paradoxical worsening, especially with ventral contact activation.85 In rodents, stimulation of the entopeduncular nucleus (EP), corresponding to the GPi in humans, at high frequencies (130–180 Hz) consistently enhances locomotor activity and reduces akinesia in Parkinson’s disease models (e.g. 6-OHDA-lesioned rats), potentially promoting wakefulness indirectly through motor-related arousal,86 via striato-EP projections.87

The external globus pallidus (GPe) shows even less consistent effects in humans,85 with only isolated reports of improvement in insomnia.88 This contrasts sharply with rodent studies, where GPe stimulation robustly promotes sleep through GABAergic projections that inhibit arousal centres including the locus coeruleus and tuberomammillary nucleus.89 Unilateral GPe-DBS (180 Hz, 100 μA) or optogenetic stimulation (20 Hz) significantly increases both NREM and REM sleep.89 Conversely, GPe lesions in rodents decrease sleep by almost half and increase arousal,90 supporting the sleep-promoting role of the region.

The nucleus accumbens demonstrates complex bidirectional sleep modulation.91-99 Initial stimulation or parameter adjustments frequently trigger reduced sleep requirement and insomnia, sometimes accompanied by hypomanic symptoms requiring parameter modification.92,93,98 Yet other patients, particularly those treated for addiction or impulsive-aggressive disorders, show paradoxical sleep improvements including increased total sleep time and enhanced sleep efficiency.95,99 This bidirectionality aligns with rodent studies identifying distinct accumbal subpopulations: adenosine A2A receptor-expressing neurons that promote sleep versus dopamine D1 receptor-expressing neurons that promote wakefulness.100,101 The variable human responses are likely to reflect differential engagement of these opposing populations based on precise electrode location and stimulation parameters.

Thalamic stimulation

The anterior nucleus of the thalamus (ANT), increasingly targeted for drug-resistant epilepsy, demonstrates consistent voltage-dependent effects on sleep architecture.102 High-amplitude stimulation frequently disrupts sleep continuity, with patients reporting insomnia, frequent awakenings and vivid dreams.103,104 However, reducing stimulation amplitude during nighttime hours can preserve seizure control while minimizing sleep disruption.102 Some patients paradoxically report improved sleep quality despite polysomnographic evidence of fragmentation, suggesting complex interactions between seizure control and sleep perception.105 In epileptic rodent models, ANT stimulation primarily shows anticonvulsant effects that might indirectly support sleep by reducing seizure-related disruptions,106 although direct sleep promotion has not been demonstrated extensively.

Ventral intermediate nucleus (VIM) stimulation for tremor produces variable sleep effects.107-112 Paediatric and female patients appear particularly susceptible to initial sleep disruption following VIM stimulation,107,112 showing reduced sleep efficiency and increased insomnia. A notable phenomenon occurs in essential tremor patients, who experience natural tremor suppression during sleep preoperatively but develop breakthrough tremor during sleep post-DBS.113 This necessitates continued nighttime stimulation at reduced amplitudes and suggests that DBS might interfere with endogenous sleep-dependent tremor suppression mechanisms. The mechanisms underlying these effects remain unclear, because rodent studies of motor thalamus (ventrolateral thalamus, which is a homologue for VIM) stimulation have focused primarily on motor rather than sleep outcomes.114

Interestingly, the centromedian–parafascicular (CM-Pf) complex shows consistent sleep enhancement effects across multiple patient populations.115-120 Stimulation improves sleep architecture in both Parkinson’s disease116 and epilepsy,118 with patients showing increased slow-wave sleep, reduced fragmentation and improved subjective sleep quality. Moreover, CM-Pf and centrolateral nuclei are emerging targets for disorders of consciousness, potentially via arousal and sleep–wake control.121 This aligns with rodent studies implicating the parafascicular nucleus and related intralaminar thalamic regions in maintaining NREM sleep stability and cortical rhythms,122 although direct promotion of sleep onset remains undemonstrated. Low-frequency stimulation (5–10 Hz) of the centrolateral nucleus in chronic pain patients yields robust sleep benefits, with 70%–80% of patients reporting improved sleep quality that is correlated with but exceeds pain relief alone.115 However, continuous high-frequency stimulation in minimally conscious patients can, paradoxically, disrupt circadian rhythms,119 necessitating scheduled nighttime cessation.

Limbic system targets

Subgenual cingulate stimulation for depression often produces early improvements in sleep disturbances,123-125 particularly terminal insomnia (early morning awakening), frequently preceding other symptom improvements.124 This pattern suggests engagement of circuits regulating sleep–mood interactions, as supported by rodent studies where activation of the infralimbic cortex (the rodent homologue of subgenual cingulate) induces sleep-preparatory behaviours, such as nesting, and promotes NREM sleep entry with reduced latency and enhanced delta power.126 In humans, however, not all sleep phenotypes respond equally; hypersomnia and initial insomnia appear less responsive than middle insomnia to subgenual stimulation.

Capsular targets, including the anterior limb of the internal capsule and ventral capsule/ventral striatum, demonstrate more complex sleep effects. Acute stimulation frequently causes transient sleep disruption and insomnia during initial programming,91,127-130 with most patients returning to baseline sleep patterns after parameter adjustment.91 Recent electrophysiological evidence suggests that these effects might relate to stimulation-induced hyperarousal, potentially mediated through beta oscillations that could serve as biomarkers for closed-loop systems designed to mitigate sleep disruption.131,132 This mirrors rodent internal capsule electrical stimulation, which evokes inhibitory postsynaptic currents in thalamocortical cells and suppresses NREM sleep spindles,133 potentially fragmenting sleep architecture. Some patients experience hypomanic symptoms, including decreased sleep need, particularly at higher stimulation intensities or during initial adjustment periods.128,129 This can be related to the bidirectional rodent ventral striatum effects, where adenosine A2A receptor neuron activation promotes slow-wave sleep,134,135 whereas inhibition of dopamine D1 receptor pathways drives wakefulness and arousal akin to hypomania.42,135

The bed nucleus of the stria terminalis (BNST), emerging as a promising target for obsessive-compulsive disorder and depression, shows similar bidirectional effects on sleep architecture.136-141 Although sleep disturbances, including insomnia, are common during initial programming,137,141 careful parameter adjustment typically resolves these effects. This can be echoed in rodent optogenetic studies, where excitation of BNST GABAergic neurons promotes arousal from NREM sleep (increasing wake by 40%–60%) but inhibition enhances NREM bouts.142

More novel targets show intriguing preliminary results. Habenula stimulation for treatment-resistant depression appears to improve subjective sleep quality significantly, an effect that emerges early and persists throughout follow-up periods.143 This aligns with rodent models, where lateral habenula DBS reduces REM disinhibition and normalizes NREM delta power in depression-like states, although optogenetic excitation can mildly fragment sleep efficiency.144

Fornix and nucleus basalis of Meynert (NBM) stimulation for cognitive disorders show no adverse sleep effects,145,146 with some patients experiencing improved sleep quality and significant Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index score improvements post-surgery.147-149 In rats, phase-dependent closed-loop fornix DBS bidirectionally modulates hippocampal theta power during locomotion, offering a potential therapeutic approach to enhance memory circuit function in Alzheimer’s disease.150 For the NBM, stimulation is reported to suppress sleep and promote wakefulness,151,152 highlighting the role of the cholinergic-rich NBM in enhancing cortical arousal.

Brainstem stimulation

The pedunculopontine nucleus (PPN) demonstrates notable effects on REM sleep architecture. Multiple studies report moderate increases in REM sleep duration with PPN stimulation active compared with off conditions.153-157 This REM enhancement occurs alongside improvements in daytime alertness; an unusual combination not typically seen with other targets or medications. Lower-frequency stimulation (typically 20–30 Hz) seems favourable for enhancing both nocturnal sleep architecture and daytime vigilance, whereas higher-frequency stimulation (60–70 Hz) may induce daytime sleepiness in some patients,158,159 suggesting potential frequency-specific engagement of different neuronal populations. The conservation of PPN REM-promoting effects between rodents, monkeys and humans represents a clear example of preserved sleep circuit function across species. In all three, PPN cholinergic neurons show increased firing during REM sleep, and their stimulation enhances REM duration and density; for instance, optogenetic activation of PPN cholinergic neurons in mice boosts REM bout frequency and duration by 50%–100% while increasing REM density via ponto-geniculo-occipital-like waves.160,161 The sleep benefits appear partly independent of motor improvements in patients with Parkinson’s disease and parkinsonian monkeys, because some with modest motor improvement nonetheless report changes in sleep architecture,153,162,163 suggesting direct modulation of sleep-regulatory circuits rather than secondary effects.

PAG stimulation for chronic pain shows variable sleep effects that are correlated primarily with analgesic efficacy.164-166 Although some patients report improved sleep quality proportional to pain reduction,165 the relationship is not straightforward. Certain patients experience transient drowsiness during initiation of stimulation that resolves despite continued effective analgesia.166 The vlPAG, identified as an REM-gating region in rodents where GABAergic neurons suppress REM onset but promote it via inhibition,50,167 has not been targeted specifically in human studies, leaving questions about conservation of this circuit element.

Hypothalamic stimulation

Posterior hypothalamic stimulation, used for cluster headache and behavioural disorders, shows condition-specific sleep effects. In cluster headache, most patients maintain normal sleep–wake cycles,168-174 with some reporting improved sleep quality and reduced nocturnal attacks.175 However, polysomnographic studies reveal variable changes, with some patients showing disrupted sleep architecture.176 In contrast, patients with impulsive-aggressive syndromes more consistently show sleep improvements, including normalized circadian rhythms and improved sleep continuity that can persist even after discontinuation of stimulation,177-181 suggesting potential neuroplastic changes.

The contrast with rodent studies is notable here. In rodents, posterior hypothalamic stimulation reliably triggers arousal through activation of orexin neurons; for instance, optogenetic excitation of orexin neurons increases wake transitions from NREM/REM and sustains arousal via downstream monoaminergic/cholinergic activation.182,183 The absence of similar wake-promoting effects in humans might reflect anatomical differences; the human orexin field is more diffuse and might not be engaged effectively by focal stimulation.184 Alternatively, the chronic nature of human DBS versus acute rodent experiments might allow compensatory adaptations that mask direct circuit effects.

Lateral hypothalamic stimulation for obesity provides another example of modulatory differences. Despite targeting the orexin-rich region that potently promotes wakefulness in rodents, human studies show minimal impact on sleep architecture. Patients show increased resting metabolic rate during sleep without corresponding changes in sleep quality or structure,185,186 suggesting that metabolic and sleep functions of the lateral hypothalamus might be differentially accessible to electrical stimulation in humans.

In contrast, the anterior hypothalamus (homologous to the rodent VLPO, a core sleep-promoting hub) remains an unexplored target for direct human DBS, despite compelling preclinical evidence of its therapeutic potential. In rodents, low-frequency optogenetic or chemical stimulation of VLPO GABA/galanin neurons induces robust NREM sleep.57,187 This conservation underscores the role of the VLPO in the hypothalamic flip-flop switch, yet human translation has been limited by the small size of the target nucleus, challenges in precise functional localization, and the absence of a clear clinical indication that would justify the surgical risks in current practice. Although other hypothalamic targets (e.g. posterior hypothalamus for narcolepsy and lateral hypothalamus for obesity) have been explored, VLPO targeting might warrant consideration in patients with severe, medication-refractory primary insomnia who have failed conventional treatments, a population not yet evaluated systematically for invasive neuromodulation but one that could potentially benefit from this approach as surgical techniques and targeting precision continue to improve. Currently, only indirect imaging or lesion data infer its potential sleep-stabilizing function without interventional proof.188-190

Technical differences in stimulation approaches

The primary limitation lies in stimulation technology itself. Animal studies increasingly use optogenetics to activate or silence genetically defined neuronal populations with millisecond precision. By expressing light-sensitive channels selectively in GABAergic sleep-promoting neurons or glutamatergic wake-promoting neurons, researchers can modulate specific cell types without affecting neighbouring populations with opposing functions. In contrast, human DBS uses electrical stimulation that indiscriminately affects all neurons within several millimetres of the electrode.191 This mismatch in spatial resolution means that human stimulation inevitably activates heterogeneous populations that might exert opposing influences on sleep.

Even if spatial targeting were perfected, electrical stimulation differs fundamentally from optogenetic modulation in its physiological effects.192 Optogenetics mimics natural activation patterns, generating action potentials that propagate normally through axons and release neurotransmitters at physiological rates. Electrical stimulation, particularly at the high frequencies used in DBS (100–180 Hz) is likely to create an ‘informational lesion’ through multiple mechanisms including depolarization block, synaptic depletion and activation of passing fibres.193 The therapeutic effects might result more from disrupting pathological activity than reproducing normal physiology. Furthermore, most human DBS delivers continuous or scheduled stimulation rather than the state-dependent modulation possible in animal studies, missing opportunities for targeted intervention during specific sleep stages or in response to physiological signals.

Evolutionary divergence in circuit architecture

Even with identical stimulation technology, evolutionary divergence has created significant differences in sleep-regulatory circuit organization between rodents and humans. Comparative neuroanatomy reveals that although homologous structures exist, their relative sizes, internal organization and connectivity patterns differ substantially; human brains, being ∼1000-fold larger in volume, exhibit relatively smaller subcortical nuclei and more distributed arousal–sleep networks compared with the compact, clustered arrangements in rodents.194,195 This architectural difference might reflect the evolution of more distributed sleep control in larger brains, making focal stimulation less effective.

Brain size and neocortical expansion, especially of frontal cortical regions, are likely to contribute to these differences. In humans, cortical regions show evidence of local and state-divergent sleep activity across subcortical and hippocampal structures, suggesting that top-down cortical influence over sleep-regulatory circuits is substantial.196,197 Human phenomena such as insomnia, ruminative wakefulness and voluntary sleep restriction hint that wake-promoting cortical activity can override sleep drive in ways less evident in rodent models. As a result, stimulation protocols optimized in rodents might not translate directly to humans unless cortical wake-promoting activity is accounted for or modulated.

Enhanced homeostatic compensation and network resilience

The human brain may demonstrate more notable resilience to focal perturbations of neural circuits in comparison to rodents, potentially owing to higher network redundancy.198 The optogenetic silencing of the rodent VLPO dramatically reduces sleep, whereas patients with extensive surgical lesions of the anterior hypothalamus (encompassing the VLPO region) owing to tumour resection often exhibit more modest long-term sleep disruptions following initial postoperative recovery.199,200 Although immediate post-surgical effects can be substantial (e.g. hypersomnia or fragmented sleep), most patients recover near-normal sleep patterns, indicating robust compensation through alternative circuits.

This resilience manifests clinically in the phenomenon of adaptation observed in DBS patients. Patients experience initial sleep disruption with new stimulation parameters (such as insomnia with wake-promoting targets or hypersomnia with sleep-promoting regions) that normalize over weeks to months without parameter adjustment.91,107 This adaptation, rarely observed in acute animal experiments, suggests active circuit reorganization that maintains sleep homeostasis despite continued perturbation. Network-level compensation201 might also explain the modest effects of human brain stimulation. When one sleep-regulatory node is modulated artificially, parallel circuits might increase or decrease their activity to maintain overall sleep–wake balance. This systems-level homeostasis, although potentially adaptive for surviving brain injury, presents a fundamental challenge for therapeutic sleep modulation.

Ethical and practical constraints

Beyond biological differences, ethical considerations fundamentally constrain human research in ways that do not apply directly to animal studies. The principle of beneficence prevents implantation of electrodes solely for research, limiting investigations to patients with clinical indications for brain stimulation. These patients have underlying pathologies that may alter sleep circuits, making it difficult to determine whether the effects of stimulation reflect normal physiology or disease-specific responses. Practical limitations complicate translation further. Animal studies use genetically identical subjects in controlled environments with standardized light cycles, temperature and noise levels. Human studies involve genetically diverse populations with varied medications, comorbidities, lifestyle factors and sleep environments. These uncontrolled variables introduce noise that might obscure the effects of stimulation.

Identification of conserved mechanisms and characterization of human sleep circuits

Rather than assuming direct homology between rodent and human circuits, systematic validation across species can identify which mechanisms are truly conserved and which require human-specific investigation. Non-human primates serve as a crucial bridge here, because their sleep architecture, including consolidated nocturnal sleep, similar NREM/REM proportions and comparable slow-wave characteristics more closely resemble humans, while permitting invasive recordings and manipulations not possible in clinical populations.73,202,203 Recent marmoset studies reveal that thalamo-cortical rhythms including mu and alpha oscillations show greater similarity to human patterns than to rodent patterns, validating this comparative strategy.202 In addition, evolutionarily ancient brainstem and hypothalamic circuits demonstrate more reliable conservation. The role of the PPN in REM sleep regulation appears consistent from rodents through primates to humans, with cholinergic neurons showing similar firing patterns and with stimulation producing comparable increases in REM duration across species.204 Likewise, the conservation of neurochemical principles, including adenosine accumulation promoting sleep, GABAergic inhibition of arousal centres inducing sleep states and monoamines maintaining wakefulness,205,206 suggests that targeting these molecular mechanisms might translate more reliably than anatomically precise interventions.

Direct investigation of human sleep physiology has become increasingly feasible through advanced technologies that do not rely on animal model extrapolation. Simultaneous EEG–functional MRI during sleep reveals how subcortical structures coordinate cortical rhythms in humans, identifying regions such as the anterior insula, whose involvement in sleep–wake transitions was not predicted from rodent literature.207,208 Intracranial recordings during presurgical epilepsy evaluation offer temporary access to multiple brain regions simultaneously, confirming that the hierarchical coupling between slow oscillations, spindles and hippocampal ripples observed in rodents is preserved in humans, which is essential for memory consolidation.209,210 Chronic local field potential recordings from DBS electrodes implanted for movement disorders or psychiatric conditions provide months to years of subcortical activity data in naturalistic home environments, building an atlas of human sleep circuit dynamics across brain regions and patient populations.72,211-213

Treatment landscape and the complementary role of neuromodulation

Before discussing advanced neuromodulatory approaches, the existing treatment landscape for sleep disorders must be acknowledged. Cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia represents a first-line treatment for chronic insomnia, demonstrating sustained efficacy in multiple randomized trials.214 Pharmacological options include benzodiazepine receptor agonists, orexin receptor antagonists, melatonin receptor agonists and sedating antidepressants, each with specific indications.215 However, ∼20%–40% of patients either do not experience a clinically meaningful response to cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia or discontinue therapy,216 and pharmacological treatments frequently carry risks (tolerance, dependence and residual daytime impairment) that limit long-term use.215 For conditions such as idiopathic hypersomnia, treatment options beyond symptomatic management remain limited.217

Circuit-level neuromodulation might address these gaps by targeting neural substrates directly. Non-invasive brain stimulation techniques have been reviewed extensively elsewhere,218,219 demonstrating promising effects on sleep parameters. Several human studies report that non-invasive stimulation (electrical220,221 or acoustic222,223) delivered during sleep can modestly enhance slow-oscillation activity and, in some cases, produce small to moderate improvements in memory consolidation. Yet these effects can be inconsistent in the literature,224 often depend on narrow task conditions and are limited by small samples, variable methodologies and, frequently, inadequate sham controls. As a result, current findings should be interpreted cautiously, although the risk–benefit profile of these techniques remains favourable for broad use.

The question of when invasive approaches are preferable for sleep circuit modulation requires consideration of multiple factors. For severe, treatment-refractory sleep disorders causing profound functional impairment (e.g. dangerous excessive daytime sleepiness) or severe quality-of-life reduction, the surgical risks of DBS (e.g. haemorrhage 1%–3%, infection 3%–5%)225,226 might become acceptable, particularly as techniques improve and complication rates decrease.225 Additionally, for patients already requiring DBS for movement disorders, epilepsy or psychiatric conditions, optimizing parameters to improve both primary symptoms and sleep provides dual benefits without additional surgical risk. An often-overlooked consideration is that successful DBS might reduce or eliminate medications that themselves disrupt sleep architecture. Many dopaminergic agents,227 antiepileptics228 and psychiatric medications229 fragment sleep, suppress REM or cause daytime sedation. If DBS can replace or reduce these medications while improving the primary condition, the net effect on sleep may be beneficial even without directly targeting sleep circuits.

That said, emerging non-invasive technologies might eventually enable deep brain target access without surgery. Transcranial focused ultrasound can modulate deep structures, including the thalamus, with millimetre-scale resolution.230 And temporal interference stimulation applies interfering electrical currents to reach subcortical regions while minimizing cortical effects.231 These approaches could potentially validate targets identified in animal studies before considering invasive interventions, although clinical applications for sleep disorders remain early-stage investigations.

Targeting sleep oscillation with closed-loop approaches

Given the challenges of achieving cellular specificity and the species differences in anatomical circuit organization, modulating characteristic sleep oscillations offers a pragmatic alternative strategy. Sleep oscillations (e.g. slow waves, spindles and theta rhythms) show greater conservation across species than anatomical circuit details, are accessible through both invasive and non-invasive methods, and directly relate to sleep functions including memory consolidation and sleep maintenance.2 Slow-oscillation enhancement has been shown to improve declarative memory consolidation across several stimulation methods. Phase-locked acoustic stimulation, transcranial electrical stimulation and closed-loop DBS targeting cortical or thalamic regions all demonstrate the possibility to amplify slow-wave activity, with behavioural benefits correlating with the degree of oscillation enhancement.232-234 This suggests that achieving specific physiological states defined by electrographic signatures might be more important than the precise anatomical substrate or circuit mechanism generating them.

This oscillation-focused approach naturally leads to closed-loop stimulation strategies. Traditional DBS delivers continuous, fixed stimulation, without considering moment-to-moment changes in brain activity. During sleep, however, the brain cycles through distinct vigilance states with unique neurophysiology, making a one-size-fits-all stimulation strategy suboptimal. Closed-loop systems integrate continuous monitoring of neural signals, real-time processing to identify physiological states or features, and adaptive stimulation delivery based on detected activity.63,235,236 This matching of intervention to physiological context could, in theory, enhance efficacy while reducing side effects. The rationale for closed-loop sleep modulation is particularly compelling. Sleep–wake transitions occur spontaneously, according to homeostatic and circadian drives. Interventions during inappropriate states might be ineffective. Activating sleep-promoting circuits during established wakefulness when arousal systems are strongly active might produce minimal effect, whereas the same stimulation during drowsiness could facilitate sleep onset.212 Modern machine learning algorithms classify sleep stages from local field potentials or scalp EEG, with accuracy exceeding 85% and latency within hundreds of milliseconds,237-239 enabling state-specific interventions. In theory, for excessive daytime sleepiness, wake-promoting stimulation can trigger automatically upon detection of drowsiness. For insomnia, sleep-promoting stimulation activates specifically during post-lights-out wakefulness or nocturnal awakenings, then discontinues once stable sleep is achieved. The remaining challenge is to determine exactly when and how stimulation should be delivered in each state. Optimizing these parameters is likely to determine whether closed-loop DBS improves sleep outcomes meaningfully.

A key part of this optimization lies in moving from coarse to fine control. Beyond gross state classification, closed-loop systems can target specific oscillatory phases within sleep stages, functioning as neural ‘pacemakers’ that entrain or amplify endogenous rhythms. The mechanism parallels cardiac pacemakers; rather than imposing independent patterns, neural pacemakers support the intrinsic rhythms of the brain by providing appropriately timed input. Stimulation delivered at the up-phase of slow waves amplifies oscillation amplitude, potentially through resonance,240 providing additional depolarizing drive when cortical networks are already transitioning to active states.222 This is fundamentally different from open-loop stimulation arriving at random phases, which might partly cancel rather than enhance endogenous activity. Importantly, both invasive and non-invasive platforms can implement such approaches, but they all depend on precise temporal control.241 Slow-wave stimulation generally tolerates latencies of only a few hundred milliseconds, whereas spindle-targeted stimulation requires even tighter precision. These constraints are particularly challenging for commercially embedded implantable pulse generators, where real-time, low-delay detection pipelines remain a central technological barrier moving forwards.242

Disease-specific approaches and practical implementation pathways

An alternative strategy is to focus on disease-specific sleep disruptions, which might allow more efficient translation and greater clinical impact than universal enhancement approaches. Parkinson’s disease exemplifies an ideal scenario where pathological beta oscillations (13–30 Hz) in the subthalamic nucleus and the pallidum fragment both movement and sleep.71-73,243 High-frequency STN-DBS suppresses these oscillations, improving motor symptoms and sleep quality through potentially shared mechanisms.72 Similar opportunities exist in epilepsy, where programming algorithms can reduce stimulation amplitude during sleep to minimize fragmentation while maintaining seizure control.102 In psychiatric conditions, DBS targeting regions such as the subgenual cingulate can improve both mood symptoms and associated sleep disturbances,123,124 although the degree to which sleep disruption contributes to the primary pathophysiology varies across individuals. The highly practical near-term pathway involves optimizing sleep as a secondary outcome in patients already receiving DBS for movement disorders, epilepsy or psychiatric conditions. Importantly, optimizing sleep outcomes requires identifying which neural targets most influence sleep physiology (where), determining the sleep stages or oscillatory events during which stimulation should be delivered (when) and developing programming strategies that not only balance symptom control with sleep quality but also modulate specific sleep oscillations when beneficial (how). As human evidence accumulates from these studies and as surgical complexity decreases through device miniaturization, the benefit–risk calculation might eventually favour invasive approaches for carefully selected patients. For broader populations, non-invasive closed-loop systems using acoustic or transcranial electrical stimulation, in addition to transcranial focused ultrasound capable of reaching deeper targets, represent realistic pathways, serving individuals with mild to moderate insomnia or those seeking cognitive enhancement.

Conclusion

The translation of sleep circuit modulation from animal models to human applications represents both a formidable challenge and an enormous opportunity. The ability to induce immediate sleep–wake transitions, enhance specific sleep functions and ameliorate disease pathology in animal models provides a compelling rationale for developing human interventions. However, the path from rodent optogenetics to human therapeutics is far from straightforward. The road ahead remains long and will require sustained collaboration between basic scientists, engineers, clinicians and regulatory bodies. Ethical considerations must be balanced carefully with potential benefits, and expectations must remain grounded in biological reality. Although we are far from the dramatic, immediate sleep control seen in optogenetically modified mice, more modest yet clinically meaningful modulation is increasingly within reach. Standing on the cusp of major advances in sleep modulation, successful translation could move it beyond an experimental tool to a therapeutic reality with important clinical and societal benefits.
