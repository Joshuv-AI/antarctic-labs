Stage-aware deep learning with adversarial data synthesis enables prospective season-scale A/H1N1 influenza forecasting in China
Authors: Lirong Zhang, Haocheng Zhou, Yuxin Zhu, Panpan Ma, Jiaxin Yuan, Yunxiao Qiu, Huayu Qiao, Jiaxiong Zheng, Suyang Liu, Liping Li, Zicheng Cao
Published: 2027

ABSTRACT
Accurate influenza forecasting is essential for public health preparedness, yet many models require future covariates, provide limited interpretability, and degrade under post-pandemic regime shifts. We propose a Stage-Aware Multimodal Neural Network (SAMNN) that integrates multimodal temporal features using configurations adapted to heterogeneous transmission regimes and supports season-ahead scenario forecasting using adversarially synthesized covariates. Using 13 years of national A/H1N1 surveillance data from mainland China (2011∼2024), SAMNN was evaluated across three epidemiologically distinct seasons spanning ∼20-fold differences in peak intensity. SAMNN achieved R2 values of 0.82–0.95, 0.82-0.97, and 0.55-0.97 for 1-week-, 2-week-, and 4-week-ahead forecasting, respectively, and generally maintained competitive performance relative to five baseline models across forecast horizons. SHAP attribution showed that epidemiological signals dominated predictions, with context-dependent contributions from climatic and social-context features. To support prospective scenario forecasting, we used an adversarial synthetic covariate-generation pipeline to produce season-ahead forecasts for 2024/25 using information available at the prespecified forecast origin.

Introduction

Seasonal influenza remains a persistent global public health threat, causing an estimated 3-5 million severe cases and 290,000–650,000 respiratory deaths annually worldwide (Influenza (Seasonal), n. d.). In China, influenza epidemics typically peak during winter months, though the timing, intensity, and dominant subtype vary considerably across seasons, with A/H1N1 driving several notable outbreaks since its pandemic emergence in 2009 (Xie et al., 2024). Accurate short-term forecasting of epidemic intensity—including peak timing, magnitude, and duration—is essential for guiding vaccination campaigns, optimizing hospital resource allocation, and implementing timely public health interventions (Pei et al., 2021). However, the inherent complexity of influenza transmission dynamics, shaped by viral evolution, population immunity, environmental conditions, and human behavior, poses substantial challenges for forecasting systems (Eales et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024).

Traditional mechanistic approaches, including susceptible-exposed-infected-recovered (SEIR) compartmental models, offer epidemiological interpretability and enable scenario-based analysis (Dong et al., 2020). However, these models require accurate real-time parameterization of transmission rates, contact patterns, and immunity levels—information that is often unavailable or subject to considerable uncertainty during emerging outbreaks (Jr et al., 2023). Moreover, mechanistic models struggle to accommodate non-stationary dynamics arising from antigenic drift, behavioral shifts, or exceptional perturbations such as pandemics, limiting their robustness when transmission regimes fundamentally change (Cheng et al., 2025). Data-driven approaches, including ensemble machine learning methods and deep neural networks, have demonstrated competitive accuracy for short-term influenza forecasting by learning complex nonlinear relationships directly from surveillance data (W. Huang et al., 2025). Representative data-driven benchmarks include gradient boosting decision-tree models such as LightGBM and XGBoost, kernel-based methods such as support vector regression, and sequence-learning architectures such as LSTM and Transformer networks, which have been widely used as nonlinear comparators for infectious disease time-series forecasting. Yet these approaches face three critical limitations that constrain their operational utility. First, most models treat influenza as a generic time series without encoding epidemiological context or regime information, causing performance degradation when conditions diverge from historical training distributions. Second, machine learning and deep learning models typically rely on lagged epidemiological and environmental features for prediction, enabling only retrospective evaluation or short-term forecasting within windows where predictor values are already observed; they cannot address the fundamental challenge of prospective forecasting where future covariate values are unavailable, severely limiting their application to pre-season planning and scenario-based preparedness. Third, deep learning architectures often function as “black boxes,” providing accurate predictions without transparent attribution of which input features drive model outputs—a critical gap for public health decision-makers who require mechanistic understanding to design targeted interventions.

The COVID-19 pandemic has further complicated influenza forecasting by introducing unprecedented regime shifts in respiratory pathogen dynamics. Non-pharmaceutical interventions implemented to control SARS-CoV-2 transmission—including border closures, lockdowns, mask mandates, and social distancing—suppressed circulation of multiple respiratory pathogens globally, and China's “dynamic zero-COVID” policy (2020∼2022) resulted in exceptionally low influenza activity for nearly three years (Feng et al., 2021; Q.-M. Huang et al., 2022; ‘Scientific and Targeted Prevention and Control Measures to Optimize COVID-19 Response’, 2023). Following policy relaxation in late 2022, influenza activity rebounded with atypical timing, unprecedented intensity, and altered subtype composition compared to pre-pandemic seasons, and subsequent years have continued to diverge from historical patterns (L. Chen, et al., 2025; Du et al., 2023; Shaman & Galanti, 2020). These regime shifts present distinct forecasting challenges: models trained predominantly on pre-pandemic data may fail to capture post-pandemic transmission dynamics, while models trained across heterogeneous regimes without explicit stage encoding may conflate fundamentally different epidemiological processes. Outstanding questions relevant to these challenges include the relative contributions of climate versus contact behavior to influenza seasonality, the extent and duration of “immunity debt” accumulated during suppressed circulation periods, and the trajectory of contact pattern recovery across populations—uncertainties that collectively motivate forecasting frameworks capable of integrating multiple data modalities while maintaining robustness under non-stationarity (Chen et al., 2025; Q.-M. Huang et al., 2022). We focused on A/H1N1 because subtype-specific forecasting avoids conflating distinct immune histories, replacement patterns, and transmission dynamics across influenza subtypes. Although A/H3N2 may dominate in some seasonal epidemics, A/H1N1 has remained epidemiologically important in China since its 2009 pandemic emergence and showed marked fluctuations during the post-COVID period, making it a suitable subtype-specific test case for evaluating a regime-aware forecasting framework. Extension to multi-subtype forecasting, including A/H3N2 and subtype-interaction modeling, remains an important future direction.

Here, we present the Stage-Aware Multimodal Neural Network (SAMNN), a deep learning framework addressing these limitations through three methodological innovations: (1) multimodal learning using configurations adapted to the epidemiological information available at each forecast origin, including explicit pre-versus post-pandemic stage encoding when post-pandemic training observations were available; (2) an adversarial synthetic data generation pipeline that resolves the fundamental challenge of prospective forecasting by synthesizing future covariates in the absence of both surveillance data and predictor features, enabling season-scale influenza activity forecasting prior to epidemic onset; and (3) SHAP-based feature attribution providing transparent, interpretable diagnostics that reveal how heterogeneous input modalities drive predictions across different epidemic regimes. Using national weekly surveillance data of A/H1N1 from mainland China spanning 13 years (2011-2024), we evaluated SAMNN through a two-stage strategy: pseudo-prospective evaluation across three epidemiologically distinct seasons (2018/19, 2022/23, and 2023/24) with comparison against five established baseline models, followed by prospective season-scale scenario forecasting for the 2024/25 epidemic season using information available at the prespecified forecast origin. This approach bridges the critical gap between retrospective model validation and operational deployment. Our study advances influenza forecasting methodology by showing that a regime-aware multimodal framework can retain competitive performance across distributional shifts, while adversarial synthetic data generation provides a practical strategy for prospective scenario forecasting when future covariates are unavailable.

Materials and methods

Data sources and study period

National influenza surveillance data were obtained from the China National Influenza Center (CNIC; https://ivdc.chinacdc.cn/cnic/), covering mainland China from January 5, 2011, through May 1, 2024. The predictive target—the A/H1N1 Composite Index—was calculated as the product of the percentage of influenza-like illness (ILI%) among outpatient and emergency department visits and the A/H1N1 positivity rate among laboratory-tested samples (Wei et al., 2024):CompositeIndext=ILI%t×PH1N1,twhere PH1N1,t=NH1N1,tNtotal,t×100% denotes the proportion of A/H1N1-positive samples at week t. This composite formulation integrates syndromic surveillance with virological confirmation, capturing both healthcare-seeking behavior and pathogen-specific circulation intensity. NA/H1N1,t is the number of A/H1N1-positive samples, and Ntotal,t is the total number of tested samples.

Meteorological data for 212 prefecture-level cities were obtained from Visual Crossing Weather (https://www.visualcrossing.com), comprising 12 daily variables: temperature (°C), diurnal temperature range (DTR; °C), relative humidity (%), visibility (km), solar radiation (W/m2), cloud cover (%), absolute humidity (g/m3; derived via Clausius–Clapeyron equation; Supplementary Methods S1), wind speed (m/s), wind direction (degrees), solar energy (kWh/m2), UV index, and apparent temperature (°C). Daily city-level values were aggregated to national weekly means.

Social-context features were constructed at the national weekly level, comprising three variables: holiday, trend stage, and an A/H1N1 flu-season indicator. The flu-season indicator was defined using epidemiological week numbers, where weeks 40∼52 and weeks 1∼14 of the subsequent year were coded as 1 and all other weeks as 0. Holiday was derived from China's official statutory holiday calendar and encoded as a binary indicator, where a week was coded as 1 if it contained at least one statutory holiday day and 0 otherwise. Trend stage captured major turning points in epidemic intensity: the national weekly A/H1N1 Composite Index was smoothed using a 4-week moving average, first-order differences were computed, and minor fluctuations were filtered using a threshold to identify significant change points. Weeks corresponding to detected change points were encoded as 1 (onset of an upward trend) or −1 (onset of a downward trend), and all remaining weeks were encoded as 0 (no change point) (algorithm details in Supplementary Methods S2).

The study period was divided into three epidemic regimes based on COVID-19 pandemic timeline and policy measures in China: (1) pre-pandemic (January 5, 2011–December 25, 2019), representing baseline influenza dynamics without SARS-CoV-2 circulation; (2) pandemic (January 1, 2020–October 26, 2022), characterized by China's dynamic zero-COVID policy including border controls, lockdowns, and mass testing; and (3) post-pandemic (November 2, 2022–May 1, 2024), following policy relaxation initiated in early November 2022. This temporal classification was used as a categorical input in the stage-aware 2023/24 configuration.

Feature engineering and preprocessing

The epidemiological branch comprised lagged A/H1N1 Composite Index values at 1∼4 weeks: Lagk(Xt)=Xt−k,k∈{1,2,3,4}, capturing temporal autocorrelation and epidemic momentum (Wei et al., 2024). The climate branch included 12 meteorological variables aggregated to weekly resolution. The social branch comprised three features: (1) A/H1N1 flu season indicator (binary; weeks 40∼52 and 1∼14), (2) holiday indicator (binary; based on China's statutory holiday calendar), and (3) trend stage (categorical; identifying epidemic inflection points via 4-week moving average smoothing and first-order differencing; Supplementary Methods S2). For the 2023/24 configuration, the stage branch encoded pre-versus post-pandemic status among the retained observations.

All predictors were aligned to weekly resolution, missing surveillance values were handled during preprocessing, continuous inputs were standardized using the training data, and rows lacking the required lagged inputs were excluded. A 4-week moving average of the A/H1N1 Composite Index was used only to derive the trend-stage indicator.

Forecasting design and horizon construction

The primary task was 1-week-ahead forecasting, and additional 2-week- and 4-week-ahead evaluations used the same target definition, preprocessing pipeline, held-out seasons, and evaluation metrics. SAMNN, LightGBM, SVR, LSTM, and Transformer used lags 1–4, 2–5, and 4–7 weeks for the 1-, 2-, and 4-week-ahead settings, respectively. The final XGBoost configuration used three horizon-specific inputs: lags 1–3, 2–4, and 4–6 weeks, respectively. Non-epidemiological covariates retained target-week alignment; therefore, the multi-horizon analyses were interpreted as retrospective, covariate-conditioned pseudo-prospective evaluations rather than fully real-time forecasts.

Three seasons were held out chronologically: 2018/19, 2022/23, and 2023/24. For each evaluation, models were trained on eligible historical observations preceding the test season and were evaluated on the same held-out weekly target dates. For prospective scenario forecasting of the 2024/25 season, synthetic future covariates were projected into the trained SAMNN to generate week-by-week A/H1N1 Composite Index forecasts. Future climatic covariates were generated using WGAN-GP, whereas mixed-type epidemiological and social-context covariates were generated using CTGAN; implementation details are provided below.

SAMNN model architecture

The overall architecture and workflow of the Stage-Aware Multimodal Neural Network (SAMNN) are summarized in Fig. 1. SAMNN was implemented as a common multimodal forecasting framework with configurations adapted to the epidemiological information available at each forecast origin (Fig. 1). All configurations included epidemiological, climatic, and social-context branches processed through modality-specific encoders, while an additional epidemic-stage branch was incorporated in the 2023/24 configuration (Supplementary Methods S3). For the 2018/19 and 2022/23 evaluations, the encoded epidemiological, climatic, and social-context representations were combined through direct feature fusion. For the 2023/24 evaluation, after post-pandemic observations had become available, an explicit stage branch, learnable softmax-weighted fusion, and increased weighting of retained post-pandemic samples were incorporated. In the 2023/24 configuration, encoded branch representations were concatenated and passed through a fully connected layer with softmax activation to generate branch-specific weights w={w1,w2,w3,w4}, where Σwᵢ=1. Each branch embedding is scaled by its corresponding branch-specific softmax weight, and the weighted branch representations are then concatenated to form the fused representation. For the 2024/25 scenario analysis, a forecast-origin-specific direct-fusion configuration was used, with time-period information retained among the model inputs and greater weight assigned to post-pandemic training observations.

Adversarial synthetic data generation

To generate covariates unavailable at the forecast origin, WGAN-GP was used for continuous meteorological variables, whereas CTGAN was used for mixed-type epidemiological and social-context variables. WGAN-GP applies Wasserstein optimization with gradient-penalty regularization, while CTGAN uses mode-specific normalization and conditional sampling for tabular data (Supplementary Methods S6-S7; Fig. S2).

Distribution fidelity between real and synthetic data was assessed using three metrics: Wasserstein distance, Kullback–Leibler divergence, and Jensen–Shannon (JS) divergence (Supplementary Methods S8). Synthetic-data disclosure, overfitting, and distinguishability were assessed using nearest-neighbor overlap rate, diversity ratio, direct-leak rate, and real-versus-synthetic classification performance (Supplementary Methods S9). Visual validation was performed through kernel density comparisons and t-SNE embeddings (Fig. S1).

Model comparison and statistical analysis

SAMNN was compared with LightGBM, XGBoost, support vector regression, long short-term memory networks, and Transformer using the same target definition, predictor domains, held-out seasons, weekly target dates, forecast-horizon alignment, and evaluation metrics. Model-specific sample construction, input representation, and optimization procedures followed the requirements of each architecture. Epidemiological lags were shifted according to forecast horizon, whereas non-epidemiological covariates retained target-week alignment across all models. LightGBM, XGBoost, and support vector regression used flattened sliding-window inputs, while LSTM and Transformer used sequential inputs; historical training weeks were prepended to retain the first held-out target weeks. Model-specific settings and hyperparameters are provided in Supplementary Table S7.

Performance was assessed using R2, mean absolute error, root mean squared error, Pearson correlation, mean prediction bias, and peak prediction error. Mean bias and peak error were defined as predicted minus observed, and uncertainty in mean bias was estimated using paired bootstrap resampling with 10,000 iterations and two-sided bootstrap P-values. SHAP-based model interpretation

SHAP values were computed for the held-out weekly observations using KernelExplainer with 300 coalition samples per explanation. In the Sankey-bubble visualization, branch-to-predictor links represent aggregated predictor contributions, predictor-to-season links represent season-specific mean absolute SHAP values, and bubble size and color represent overall predictor importance.

All analyses were performed in Python 3.9.0 using TensorFlow, PyTorch, SciPy, scikit-learn, SHAP, LightGBM, XGBoost, and related scientific-computing libraries.

Results

SAMNN retrospective forecasting A/H1N1 intensity across epidemiologically distinct seasons

To assess SAMNN's predictive capacity under varying epidemic conditions, we performed retrospective one-week-ahead forecasting across three seasons selected to represent distinct epidemiological contexts: the pre-pandemic 2018∼2019 season (moderate intensity with typical winter dynamics), the post-pandemic 2022∼2023 season (exceptionally high intensity following relaxation of China's dynamic zero-COVID policy), and the 2023∼2024 season (low intensity with attenuated transmission). For each evaluation, SAMNN was trained on eligible historical data preceding the test season and validated on held-out weekly observations. Predicted trajectories were compared against national surveillance data, with prediction uncertainty represented by residual-based 95% uncertainty intervals derived from training-residual dispersion (Fig. 2A–C). Residual diagnostics were conducted to identify systematic prediction biases across epidemic phases (Fig. 2D–F).

SAMNN accurately captured epidemic dynamics across all three seasons despite a 20-fold range in observed peak intensity (0.17–3.32; Fig. 2A–C: blue epidemiological curve). During the 2018–2019 pre-pandemic season, predicted values closely tracked observations, with the predicted peak (1.75) deviating by only 5.4% from the observed peak (1.85); the residual-based 95% uncertainty interval [1.67, 1.83] narrowly missed the observed peak, and a small average underestimation was observed (mean bias = −0.1213; 95% bootstrap CI: −0.1914 to −0.0694; Fig. 2A–D). For the high-intensity 2022∼2023 season—characterized by an unprecedented post-pandemic rebound—SAMNN maintained robust performance, predicting a peak of 3.20 against the observed 3.32 (relative error = 3.6%). The residual-based 95% uncertainty interval [3.09, 3.30] did not fully cover the observed peak, indicating slight underestimation of uncertainty at extreme intensities; residual analysis revealed systematic underestimation before the peak followed by overestimation during the decline phase (maximum absolute residual = 0.75; Fig. 2B–E), consistent with the inherent challenge of forecasting rapid intensity changes during exceptional outbreaks. In the low-intensity 2023-2024 season, SAMNN tracked subtle fluctuations despite low signal-to-noise conditions; although the relative peak error was larger (predicted 0.13 vs. observed 0.17; 23.5%), this reflected the compressed dynamic range rather than degraded model performance, as absolute prediction errors remained minimal (mean residual = 0.00) and the wider residual-based 95% uncertainty interval [0.00, 0.27] encompassed the observed peak (Fig. 2C–F). Bootstrap bias analysis indicated that SAMNN showed no significant mean bias in two of the three one-week-ahead evaluation seasons (2022/23: mean bias = 0.0853, 95% bootstrap CI: −0.0312 to 0.2248, P = 0.1788; 2023/24: mean bias = 0.0011, 95% bootstrap CI: −0.0077 to 0.0095, P = 0.8059), whereas a small but statistically significant underestimation was observed in 2018/19 (mean bias = −0.1213, 95% bootstrap CI: −0.1914 to −0.0694, P < 0.001).

SAMNN outperforms baselines and supports season-scale A/H1N1 scenario forecasting

To support prospective season-scale scenario forecasting when future covariates were unavailable, we used an adversarial synthetic-data generation pipeline. This approach enabled scenario generation before future observations became available, whereas assessment of real-world forecast accuracy still requires comparison with subsequently observed surveillance data. We employed WGAN-GP to generate climatic features and CTGAN to synthesize social-context and epidemiological features, with the aim of approximating the observed marginal and joint feature distributions within each data modality. Distribution fidelity assessment (Table S2–S3) demonstrated acceptable similarity between synthetic and real data for most features (16/19 features with Jensen-Shannon divergence <0.10), with particularly high fidelity for epidemiological variables that dominate SAMNN predictions (median JS divergence = 0.047). Notable limitations included reduced fidelity for sparse categorical variables (Trend stage JS divergence = 0.633) and heavy-tailed continuous distributions (Solar radiation JS divergence = 0.112). Visual validation via kernel density estimates and t-SNE embeddings (Fig. S1) confirmed distributional overlap for the majority of features. This synthetic data framework enabled two complementary evaluation strategies: pseudo-prospective evaluation across historical seasons with known outcomes, and prospective season-scale scenario forecasting for the 2024/25 season where observational data were not yet available.

Using the same target definition, preprocessing pipeline, held-out seasons, weekly target dates, and evaluation metrics, we compared SAMNN with LightGBM, XGBoost, SVR, LSTM, and Transformer (Fig. 3A–C; Supplementary Table S7). SAMNN ranked first for R2, MAE, and RMSE across all three one-week-ahead evaluations and showed competitive performance for the remaining metrics. For the 2018/19, 2022/23, and 2023/24 seasons, SAMNN attained R2 values of 0.95, 0.94, and 0.82, respectively (Fig. 3D), substantially exceeding baseline ranges of 0.53∼0.88, 0.19∼0.72, and 0.04∼0.75 for the corresponding seasons. During the high-intensity 2022/23 post-pandemic rebound, SAMNN achieved MAE = 0.19 and RMSE = 0.34, corresponding to approximately 2.0∼3.4-fold lower errors compared to baseline models (MAE range: 0.38∼0.66; RMSE range: 0.67∼1.17; Fig. 3E and F). Pearson correlation coefficients for SAMNN were 0.98, 0.97, and 0.96 across the three seasons, compared to baseline ranges of 0.91-0.99, 0.82-0.95, and 0.35∼0.99, respectively (Fig. 3G). Mean prediction biases for SAMNN were −0.12, 0.09, and 0.00 across the three seasons (Fig. 3H). SAMNN achieved the smallest absolute peak errors in 2018/19 and 2022/23 (−0.11 in both seasons), while its peak error remained small in 2023/24 (0.04); baseline peak errors ranged from −2.51 to 0.53 across seasons (Fig. 3I). Between the 2018/19 and 2022/23 evaluations, SAMNN's R2 decreased by only 1% (0.95 to 0.94), whereas baseline reductions ranged from 13% to 74%. To demonstrate prospective scenario-forecasting capability, we applied the adversarial generation pipeline to synthesize the complete 2024/25 feature set—using information available at the prespecified forecast origin—and generated season-scale one-week-ahead forecasts using SAMNN trained on 2011∼2024 historical data (Fig. 3J). Across the full prospective horizon, SAMNN predicted a single dominant epidemic peak of 1.176 (residual-based 95% uncertainty interval, 1.034∼1.319) occurring in the week of 18 December 2024 (Fig. 3J; Table S5). Using a peak-relative threshold (≥10% of the predicted peak; 0.1176), the longest continuous period of elevated activity lasted 3 consecutive weeks (4∼18 December 2024; Table S5). The generated trajectory provided a proof-of-concept season-scale scenario forecast under synthetic future-covariate conditions. Comparison with subsequently observed surveillance data remains necessary to assess its real-world forecast accuracy.

Across the same three held-out seasons, SAMNN maintained high R2 values at the 2-week and 4-week horizons in 2018/19 and 2022/23, whereas 4-week-ahead performance decreased in the low-intensity 2023/24 season. SAMNN ranked first for R2, MAE, and RMSE in eight of the nine season–horizon combinations, with the exception of the 2023/24 4-week forecast. These findings support competitive longer-horizon performance under the retrospective covariate-conditioned design while indicating greater difficulty when epidemic signals are weak (Supplementary Table S1; Supplementary Fig. S3).

Interpretable feature attribution reveals regime-dependent predictive contributions

To interpret the multimodal forecasting framework, we employed SHAP to quantify the contribution of heterogeneous feature modalities across the three distinct epidemic regimes (Fig. 4A). The attribution analysis revealed that autoregressive epidemiological signals provided the dominant predictive contribution across all seasons, accounting for 78.9% in 2018/19, 97.0% in 2022/23, and 67.8% in 2023/24 (Table S4). However, the relative weight of environmental and social-context features varied across epidemic regimes. During the high-intensity 2022/23 post-pandemic rebound, SAMNN relied predominantly on recent transmission intensity, with the climatic contribution attenuated to 2.9%. Conversely, in the lower-intensity 2023/24 recovery season, climatic feature contribution increased to 31.5%, suggesting that environmental information may provide greater complementary predictive value when autoregressive epidemic signals are weaker. Granular analysis of individual feature importance identified specific lagged signals and meteorological parameters as the dominant predictors within their respective branches (Fig. 4B). The 4-week lagged A/H1N1 Composite Index emerged as the single most influential variable globally (mean absolute SHAP = 0.524), consistently exceeding shorter 1-3-week lagged indicators (mean absolute SHAP range: 0.099-0.242). This suggests that the model captured short-to medium-term epidemic momentum rather than merely extrapolating the immediately preceding observation. Among climatic predictors, absolute humidity (mean absolute SHAP = 0.021) and solar radiation (0.015) showed the highest contributions, consistent with their potential relevance to influenza transmission conditions. Although individual social-context indicators such as holiday timing and trend stage contributed minimally to the global average (mean absolute SHAP <0.002), their non-zero attribution in specific contexts indicates that SAMNN can incorporate sparse behavioral signals as supplementary information. Overall, these findings suggest that SAMNN primarily learned from autoregressive epidemic signals while adaptively integrating climatic and social-context information under heterogeneous epidemic regimes.

Discussion

Seasonal influenza remains a major global public health burden, with epidemic intensity exhibiting substantial inter-annual variability driven by viral evolution, immunity dynamics, and environmental and behavioral factors. Accurate short-term forecasting is essential for vaccination planning and hospital preparedness, yet non-stationary dynamics challenge conventional approaches that treat influenza as a generic time series (W. Huang et al., 2025). Here, we developed SAMNN as a multimodal deep learning framework with configurations adapted to the epidemiological information available at different forecast origins (Moon et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2023). Using 13 years of national surveillance data from mainland China (2011∼2024), we demonstrated that SAMNN achieves robust performance across epidemiologically distinct seasons, outperforms established baselines, and provides interpretable feature attribution illuminating regime-dependent predictive contributions.

SAMNN's performance across a 20-fold range in observed peak intensity (0.17∼3.32) underscores its capacity to generalize across heterogeneous epidemic conditions. During the unprecedented 2022∼2023 post-pandemic rebound, SAMNN maintained high accuracy (R2 = 0.94; peak error = 3.6%), whereas baseline models exhibited substantially greater performance degradation (R2 range: 0.19∼0.72; peak errors up to 2.51). During the 2022/23 post-pandemic rebound, the direct-fusion SAMNN configuration maintained high predictive accuracy despite the marked change in epidemic intensity, suggesting that the common multimodal representation retained predictive value under substantial distributional shift. For the 2023/24 evaluation, an explicit stage branch, learnable weighted fusion, and stage-informed sample weighting were incorporated after post-pandemic training observations became available (O'Dea & Drake, 2022; Parag & Donnelly, 2020). The limited R2 decrease between the 2018/19 and 2022/23 evaluations, compared with the larger degradation observed for several baseline models, supports the robustness of the common SAMNN framework under substantial distributional shift. Feature attribution revealed epidemiological signals as the primary predictive engine (67.8∼97.0% of total attribution), with the 4-week lagged composite index as the most influential variable (mean absolute SHAP = 0.524), suggesting SAMNN captures medium-term epidemic momentum rather than merely extrapolating immediate observations (Papagiannopoulou et al., 2024; Tsang et al., 2024). The model exhibited context-dependent weighting: during the hyper-intense 2022∼2023 outbreak, autoregressive signals overwhelmed auxiliary modalities (climate contribution: 2.9%), whereas the lower-intensity 2023∼2024 season permitted greater climatic (31.5%) and social (0.68%) influence—the latter increasing nearly 4-fold compared to prior seasons, potentially reflecting heightened behavioral heterogeneity following relaxation of control measures.

Our attribution results suggest that at the 1-week forecast horizon and national scale, autoregressive epidemiological signals dominate short-term prediction, with climate variables providing complementary but secondary value—likely because meteorological effects on transmission are substantially captured by recent epidemiological observations. The composite index integrating syndromic surveillance with virological confirmation proved valuable by capturing both healthcare-seeking behavior and pathogen-specific circulation. The adversarial synthetic data pipeline addresses a fundamental challenge in epidemic forecasting by supporting prospective scenario generation and internal stress-testing before real-world validation becomes available; distribution fidelity assessment confirmed acceptable similarity for epidemiological features (median JS divergence = 0.047), validating utility for internal stress-testing despite imperfect replication of sparse categorical variables (Sun & Dumontier, 2025).

This study has several limitations. National-level aggregation masks geographic heterogeneity in transmission dynamics across China's provinces, limiting regional applicability (Ben-Nun et al., 2019). SAMNN targets A/H1N1 exclusively, ignoring potential interactions with co-circulating subtypes that may influence seasonal dynamics (Wang et al., 2022). Post-pandemic data covered only two evaluation seasons, precluding determination of whether elevated social feature importance represents transient adjustment or persistent regime change. Although we added 2-week- and 4-week-ahead pseudo-prospective evaluations, these analyses were retrospective and covariate-conditioned rather than fully real-time operational forecasts. Further validation is therefore needed for longer-horizon deployment in settings where all future covariates must be forecast in advance (Mathis et al., 2024). Additionally, the slight systematic underestimation during the 2018/19 pre-pandemic season indicates that calibration may vary across epidemic seasons and warrants evaluation in additional independent seasons (Osthus & Moran, 2021; Tsang et al., 2024).

In conclusion, SAMNN integrates multimodal forecasting, forecast-origin-specific configurations, and interpretable attribution within a common framework. Its performance across contrasting epidemic seasons and the prospective scenario-forecasting demonstration support further evaluation for influenza surveillance applications. Future priorities include spatial disaggregation, multi-pathogen integration, real-time long-horizon deployment, and prospective validation against observed surveillance data.
