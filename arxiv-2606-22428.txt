On k k -limited domination: complexity and Cartesian products
Aleksandra Tepeh

Abstract
A dominating set is called k k -limited if every vertex in the set has at most k k neighbors outside it. The minimum cardinality of a k k -limited dominating set is the k k -limited domination number, denoted by γ k L ​ ( G ) \gamma_{k}^{\mathrm{L}}(G) . We prove that, for every fixed integer k ≥ 2 k\geq 2 , deciding whether a graph admits a k k -limited dominating set of size at most ℓ \ell is 𝖭𝖯 \mathsf{NP} -complete. In addition, a systematic study of k k -limited domination in Cartesian products is initiated. In particular, we establish general lower and upper bounds for γ k L ​ ( G ​ □ ​ H ) \gamma_{k}^{\mathrm{L}}(G\square H) , show that both are sharp, and derive exact values for several natural families of graph products. Among others, we obtain exact results for rook graphs, Cartesian products of k k -coronas, certain grid graphs, and several cases involving prisms and hypercubes.

1
Introduction

A dominating set of a graph
G
is a set of vertices such that every vertex outside the set has a neighbor in it, and the minimum size of such a set is the domination number
\gamma(G)
. Over the years, domination has been studied from many different perspectives and on many graph classes; we refer to the monographs
[
12
,
13
,
14
]
for background and further references. At the same time, several central open problems in domination theory remain unresolved, among them Vizing’s conjecture on domination in Cartesian products
[
3
]
, and the problem of determining the domination number of hypercubes, which is known exactly only in a limited number of cases
[
11
,
18
,
19
]
.

Recently, in
[
2
]
, the notion of
k
-limited domination was introduced as a natural variant of classical domination. Many previously studied domination variants impose additional requirements on vertices outside the dominating set; for instance, in
k
-domination
every vertex outside the set must have at least
k
neighbors in it
[
8
]
, while in
[1,k]
-domination
every vertex outside the set must have at least one and at most
k
neighbors in it
[
4
,
15
]
. By contrast,
k
-limited domination places the restriction on the vertices inside the dominating set. In the usual domination model, one seeks a dominating set of minimum cardinality, but no restriction is imposed on how many vertices outside the set may be assigned to the same dominator. In many situations, however, this is unrealistic: if the selected vertices represent facilities, service providers, or communication nodes, for example, then each of them can typically handle only a limited number of external users. This leads to the requirement that every chosen vertex have at most
k
neighbors outside the dominating set. In this way,
k
-limited domination preserves the covering aspect of classical domination while adding a local restriction on the number of external vertices assigned to each dominating vertex.

Formally, as introduced in
[
2
]
, let
G
be a graph and let
k
be an integer such that
0\leq k\leq\Delta(G)
. A set
D\subseteq V(G)
is called a
k
-limited dominating set
if
D
is a dominating set of
G
and

|N_{G}(u)\setminus D|\leq k\qquad\text{for every }u\in D.

The minimum cardinality of a
k
-limited dominating set of
G
is the
k
-limited domination number
of
G
, denoted by
\gamma_{k}^{\mathrm{L}}(G)
. We will refer to the above local degree condition as the
k
-limited constraint
. In
[
2
]
, the parameter was defined in the range
0\leq k\leq\Delta(G)
, where the two extreme cases are essentially trivial: if
k=0
, then every vertex of a
k
-limited dominating set has no neighbors outside the set, which forces
\gamma^{\mathrm{L}}_{0}(G)=|V(G)|
, while for
k=\Delta(G)
one recovers the classical domination number. Thus, for graphs with
\Delta(G)\geq 2
, the genuinely interesting range is
1\leq k\leq\Delta(G)-1
. As we shall see in Section 2, however, the definition extends naturally to all integers
k\geq 0
; in particular, for every
k\geq\Delta(G)
one has
\gamma_{k}^{\mathrm{L}}(G)=\gamma(G)
. This extension will be convenient in our study of Cartesian products.

The initial study in
[
2
]
laid the foundations for the systematic investigation of this parameter. It established general bounds on
\gamma_{k}^{\mathrm{L}}(G)
and determined exact values for several basic graph families. It also showed that, for
efficient graphs
, that is, graphs admitting a dominating set in which every vertex is dominated exactly once, the parameter coincides with the classical domination number once
k
is sufficiently large, and that under suitable structural assumptions one can derive improved upper bounds in terms of
\gamma(G)
.
In addition, strong links with other domination-type parameters were uncovered. In particular, a close connection with
(1,t)
-domination
was established, where every vertex outside the dominating set must have at least one neighbor in the set, while every vertex inside the set must have at least
t
neighbors in the set
[
6
,
7
]
. For connected
d
-regular graphs and
1\leq k<d
, this relation takes the precise form

\gamma_{k}^{\mathrm{L}}(G)=\gamma_{1,d-k}(G)
.
Finally, the case
k=1
was studied in more detail via its connection with the packing number, leading to further structural results and characterizations.

The present paper continues this line of research in two directions. First, we investigate the algorithmic complexity of the parameter and prove in Section
3
that
k
-Limited Dominating Set
is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete for every fixed integer
k\geq 2
. Second, we initiate a systematic study of
k
-limited domination in Cartesian products. Before turning to these two topics, in Section
2
we collect the definitions and auxiliary results needed later. The Cartesian product aspect is treated in Section
4
, where we establish general bounds for
\gamma_{k}^{\mathrm{L}}(G\square H)
, show that both are sharp, and derive exact values for several natural families of products, including rook graphs, Cartesian products of
k
-coronas, certain grid graphs, and selected cases involving prisms and hypercubes. We conclude in Section
5
with several open problems suggested by our results, particularly in the case of hypercubes.

Preliminaries

Let
G
be a finite simple graph. Its vertex set and edge set are denoted by
V(G)
and
E(G)
, respectively, and its order by
n(G)=|V(G)|
. For a vertex

, we write

for the open neighborhood of

and
\deg_{G}(v)
for its degree. The maximum degree of
G
is denoted by
\Delta(G)
. When the graph is clear from the context, we omit the subscript. Also, for ease of notation, we write
[k]=\{1,\dots,k\}
for every positive integer
k
.

A set
D\subseteq V(G)
is a
dominating set
of
G
if every vertex of
V(G)\setminus D
has a neighbor in
D
. The minimum cardinality of a dominating set of
G
is the
domination number
of
G
, denoted by
\gamma(G)
. Let
k
be an integer such that
0\leq k\leq\Delta(G)
. A dominating set
D\subseteq V(G)
is called a
k
-limited dominating set
if

|N_{G}(u)\setminus D|\leq k
for every
u\in D
.
The minimum cardinality of a
k
-limited dominating set of
G
is the
k
-limited domination number
of
G
, denoted by
\gamma_{k}^{\mathrm{L}}(G)
,
[
2
]
.

If
k=0
, then every vertex of a
k
-limited dominating set has no neighbors outside the set, and hence
\gamma^{\mathrm{L}}_{0}(G)=|V(G)|
. On the other hand, if
k=\Delta(G)
, then every dominating set of
G
is automatically
k
-limited, and therefore
\gamma_{k}^{\mathrm{L}}(G)=\gamma(G)
. Moreover, if
D
is a
k
-limited dominating set of
G
, then it is also a
(k+1)
-limited dominating set of
G
. Indeed, for every
u\in D
, the inequality
|N_{G}(u)\setminus D|\leq k
immediately implies
|N_{G}(u)\setminus D|\leq k+1
. Therefore

\gamma^{\mathrm{L}}_{k+1}(G)\leq\gamma^{\mathrm{L}}_{k}(G)
for every
0\leq k<\Delta(G)
.
Consequently,
\gamma_{k}^{\mathrm{L}}(G)
admits a natural extension to all integers
k\geq 0
, and in particular
\gamma_{k}^{\mathrm{L}}(G)=\gamma(G)
for every
k\geq\Delta(G)
. This also explains why in
[
2
]
the focus was on the nontrivial range
1\leq k\leq\Delta(G)-1
.

Observation 1
.

Let
G
be a graph. Then

\gamma^{\mathrm{L}}_{0}(G)\geq\gamma^{\mathrm{L}}_{1}(G)\geq\gamma^{\mathrm{L}}_{2}(G)\geq\cdots\geq\gamma^{\mathrm{L}}_{\Delta(G)}(G)=\gamma(G).

Moreover,
\gamma_{k}^{\mathrm{L}}(G)=\gamma(G)
for every
k\geq\Delta(G)
.

The following general bounds for
k
-limited domination were proved in
[
2
]
.

Theorem 2
.

[
2
]

Let
G
be a connected graph of order
n\geq 3
, and
1\leq k<\Delta(G)
. Then

\max\left\{\gamma(G),\ \left\lceil\frac{n}{k+1}\right\rceil\right\}\leq\gamma_{k}^{\mathrm{L}}(G)\leq n-k.

Several graph families were also considered in
[
2
]
. In what follows, we will use the
k
-limited domination numbers of paths and complete graphs.

Proposition 3
.

[
2
]

Let
k
be a positive integer. Then the following holds.

(i)

For every
n\geq 3
we have
\gamma_{1}^{\mathrm{L}}(P_{n})=\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil
.

(ii)

For every
n\geq 3
and
k
such that
1\leq k<n
we have
\gamma_{k}^{\mathrm{L}}(K_{n})=n-k
.

As already mentioned, one of the main goals of this paper is to initiate a systematic study of
k
-limited domination in Cartesian products. We therefore conclude this section by recalling the following definition.
The
Cartesian product

G\square H
has vertex set

V(G\square H)=V(G)\times V(H)
, where two vertices

(g,h)
and
(g^{\prime},h^{\prime})
are adjacent if and only if either

g=g^{\prime}
and
hh^{\prime}\in E(H)
, or
h=h^{\prime}
and
gg^{\prime}\in E(G)
. As usual, we write
n(G)=|V(G)|
and
n(H)=|V(H)|
for the orders of the respective factors.

We first show that
|D^{\prime\prime}|\leq|D^{\prime}|
.
For every neighbor
v_{r}
of

|D^{\prime}\cap V(S^{ir})|\geq k+2=|C^{ir}|

so replacing

C^{ir}

Moreover, for the specific neighbor

|D^{\prime}\cap V(S^{ij})|\geq k+3,

and hence on the gadget
S^{ij}
the replacement decreases the size by at least
1
.
This compensates for the addition of
v_{i}
, and therefore

|D^{\prime\prime}|\leq|D^{\prime}|
.

Next, we verify that
D^{\prime\prime}
is still a
k
-limited dominating set of
G^{\prime}
.
First, domination is preserved.
Each modified gadget
S^{ir}
is dominated by
C^{ir}
, and since
c^{ir}\in C^{ir}
, both original vertices
v_{i}
and
v_{r}

All other vertices of
V(G^{\prime})\setminus D^{\prime\prime}

Hence these vertices remain dominated as well.
It remains to check that the

The newly added original vertex
v_{i}

G^{\prime}

Now let

If

For

.

Finally, the modification ensures that the original vertex

k
-Limited Dominating set

Theorem 5

For every fixed integer

, the

In the case

Hence the graph

.

2
-Limited Dominating set

4
Cartesian product

We next investigate how
k
-limited domination behaves under the Cartesian product of graphs.
The following theorem provides general lower and upper bounds for
\gamma_{k}^{\mathrm{L}}(G\square H)
.
We get the lower bound by a direct counting argument, while the upper bound is obtained by taking an optimal
k
-limited dominating set in one factor and repeating it in every layer corresponding to the other factor.

Theorem 7
.

Let
G
and
H
graphs, and let
k\geq 1
be an integer.
Then

\left\lceil\frac{n(G)\,n(H)}{k+1}\right\rceil\leq\gamma_{k}^{\mathrm{L}}(G\square H)\leq\min\big\{\gamma_{k}^{\mathrm{L}}(G)\,n(H),\,\gamma_{k}^{\mathrm{L}}(H)\,n(G)\big\}.

Proof.

Let
D
be a
k
-limited dominating set of
G\square H
.
For each vertex
x\in D
, the set
\{x\}\cup\bigl(N_{G\square H}(x)\setminus D\bigr)

has size at most
k+1
, since
|N_{G\square H}(x)\setminus D|\leq k
by the
k
-limited constraint.
Moreover, because
D
dominates
G\square H
, every vertex of
G\square H
belongs either to
D
or to
N_{G\square H}(x)\setminus D
for some
x\in D
.
Hence

V(G\square H)\subseteq\bigcup_{x\in D}\Bigl(\{x\}\cup\bigl(N_{G\square H}(x)\setminus D\bigr)\Bigr),

and therefore

n(G)\,n(H)=|V(G\square H)|\leq\sum_{x\in D}(k+1)=(k+1)|D|,

which yields the lower bound.

For the upper bound, let
D_{G}\subseteq V(G)
be a
\gamma^{\mathrm{L}}_{k}(G)
-set.
Let

D=D_{G}\times V(H)\subseteq V(G\square H)
.
We claim that
D
is a
k
-limited dominating set of
G\square H
.

Let
(u,v)\in V(G\square H)\setminus D
be arbitrary. Then
u\notin D_{G}
. As
D_{G}
is, in particular, a dominating set of
G
, there exists a
vertex
x\in D_{G}
with
ux\in E(G)
. Then
(u,v)
and
(x,v)
are adjacent
in
G\square H
, and
(x,v)\in D
. Hence
D
dominates
G\square H
.

To verify the
k
-limited constraint now let
(u,v)
be an arbitrary vertex in
D
. Then
u\in D_{G}
and
v\in V(H)
.
There are two types of neighbors of
(u,v)
in
G\square H
:

(u^{\prime},v)
with
u^{\prime}u\in E(G)
,
and
(u,v^{\prime})
with
vv^{\prime}\in E(H)
.
Since
D=D_{G}\times V(H)
, for every
v^{\prime}\in V(H)
we have
(u,v^{\prime})\in D
. Since
D=D_{G}\times V(H)
, every neighbor of second type also belongs to
D
.
Therefore

N_{G\square H}((u,v))\setminus D=\{(u^{\prime},v):u^{\prime}u\in E(G),\ u^{\prime}\notin D_{G}\},

and consequently

|N_{G\square H}((u,v))\setminus D|=|N_{G}(u)\setminus D_{G}|.

Because
D_{G}
is a
k
-limited dominating set of
G
, we have

|N_{G}(u)\setminus D_{G}|\leq k
for every
u\in D_{G}
.
Hence

|N_{G\square H}((u,v))\setminus D|\leq k
for every
(u,v)\in D
.
Thus
D
is a
k
-limited dominating set of
G\square H
, and so

\gamma_{k}^{\mathrm{L}}(G\square H)\leq|D|=|D_{G}|\,n(H)=\gamma_{k}^{\mathrm{L}}(G)\,n(H).

By symmetry, interchanging the roles of
G
and
H
yields

\gamma_{k}^{\mathrm{L}}(G\square H)\leq\gamma_{k}^{\mathrm{L}}(H)\,n(G).

Taking the minimum of the two upper bounds completes the proof.
∎

In the subsequent subsections, we show that both bounds in Theorem
7
are sharp, and we derive closed formulas for
\gamma_{k}^{\mathrm{L}}
for several well-known families of graphs.

4.1
Rook graphs

To show that the upper bound in Theorem
7
is tight, we now turn to Cartesian products of complete graphs, also known as
rook graphs
.

It is easy to see that for every nontrivial connected graph
H

there exists a
k
-limited dominating set in
K_{k+2}\square H
of size
2n(H)
,
namely the set
D=\{x_{1},x_{2}\}\times V(H)
, where
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
are arbitrary vertices of
K_{k+2}
.
Consequently,

\gamma_{k}^{\mathrm{L}}(K_{k+2}\square H)\leq 2n(H)
.
Whether this bound is attained depends on the structure of
H
. For instance, if
H=K_{k+1}
, then, by Theorem
7
and Proposition
3
, we have

\gamma_{k}^{\mathrm{L}}(K_{k+2}\square H)\leq k+2<2n(H)
.
On the other hand, equality does hold in the special case when
H=K_{k+2}
, as shown in the following proposition.

Proposition 8
.

Let
k
be a positive integer. Then
\gamma_{k}^{\mathrm{L}}(K_{k+2}\square K_{k+2})=2(k+2)
.

Proof.

Let
m=k+2
. Then
\Delta(K_{m})=m-1=k+1
, and hence
1\leq k<\Delta(K_{m})
. Thus
\gamma_{k}^{\mathrm{L}}(K_{m})=m-k=2
by Proposition
3
. Applying Theorem
7
with
G=H=K_{m}
yields

\gamma_{k}^{\mathrm{L}}(K_{m}\square K_{m})\leq\gamma_{k}^{\mathrm{L}}(K_{m})\,|V(K_{m})|=2m=2(k+2).

To complete the proof it remains to show that

\gamma_{k}^{\mathrm{L}}(K_{m}\square K_{m})\geq 2m
.

Let
D
be a
\gamma_{k}^{\mathrm{L}}(K_{m}\square K_{m})
-set, and let

V(K_{m}\square K_{m})=\{(i,j):1\leq i,j\leq m\}
.
For each
j\in[m]
we define the
j
th
row

R_{j}=\{(i,j):1\leq i\leq m\}
,
and for each
i\in[m]
the
i
th
column

C_{i}=\{(i,j):1\leq j\leq m\}
.
For each
j\in[m]
let
r_{j}=|D\cap R_{j}|
, and for each
i\in[m]
let

c_{i}=|D\cap C_{i}|
. Then

\sum_{j=1}^{m}r_{j}=|D|=\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}.

We first note that no row and no column contains exactly one vertex of
D
, as the opposite case contradicts the
k
-limited constraint.
Thus
r_{j}\neq 1
for all
j\in[m]
and
c_{i}\neq 1
for all
i\in[m]
.

Now suppose, to the contrary, that
|D|\leq 2m-1
. Then

\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}r_{j}=\frac{|D|}{m}\leq\frac{2m-1}{m}<2,

hence there exists a row
R_{j_{0}}
with
r_{j_{0}}\leq 1
.
Since
r_{j_{0}}\neq 1
, we must have
r_{j_{0}}=0
, that is, the
row
R_{j_{0}}
contains no vertex in
D
.
Applying the same argument to the sum
\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}
shows that there exists a column
C_{i_{0}}
with
no vertex in
D
.
But then the vertex
(i_{0},j_{0})
has no neighbor in
D
, contradicting the
fact that
D
is a dominating set of
K_{m}\square K_{m}
.

Therefore
|D|\geq 2m
. Combining this inequality with the upper bound
derived above concludes the proof.
∎

Corollary 9
.

For every positive integer
k
, the upper bound in Theorem
7
is attained.

4.2
Cartesian products of
k
-coronas

By considering Cartesian products of
k
-coronas we now show that the lower bound in Theorem
7
is sharp as well.

Recall that the
corona product

G\circ H
is obtained from one copy of
G
and
n(G)
disjoint copies of
H
by joining each vertex of
G
to all vertices in the corresponding copy of
H
.
In particular,
G\circ\overline{K_{k}}
is obtained from
G
by attaching
k
leaves to each vertex of
G
, and we call this graph the
k
-corona
of
G
.

Lemma 10
.

Let
k\geq 1
and let
F
be a graph. Let
G=F\circ\overline{K_{k}}
. Then

\gamma_{k}^{\mathrm{L}}(G)=\frac{n(G)}{k+1}.

Proof.

Let
U=V(F)
, viewed as a subset of
V(G)
.
Since every vertex of
U
has exactly
k
pendant neighbors outside
U
, while all its remaining neighbors also belong to
U
, the set
U
is a
k
-limited dominating set of
G
.
Hence

\gamma_{k}^{\mathrm{L}}(G)\leq|U|=\frac{n(G)}{k+1}.

For the reverse inequality, let
D
be a
\gamma_{k}^{\mathrm{L}}(G)
-set.
For each
u\in U
, let
L_{u}
denote the set of the
k
leaves adjacent to
u
, and set

B_{u}=\{u\}\cup L_{u}.

Then the sets
B_{u}
, for
u\in U
, form a partition of
V(G)
.
Moreover, for each
u\in U
, at least one vertex of
B_{u}
must belong to
D
, since otherwise none of the leaves in
L_{u}
would be dominated.
Therefore

|D|\geq|U|=\frac{n(G)}{k+1}.

Combining the two inequalities yields the result.
∎

Now Theorem
7
immediately gives the following.

Proposition 11
.

Let
k\geq 1
and let
F
and
F^{\prime}
be graphs. Let
G=F\circ\overline{K_{k}}
and
H=F^{\prime}\circ\overline{K_{k}}
. Then

\gamma_{k}^{\mathrm{L}}(G\square H)=\frac{n(G)\,n(H)}{k+1}.

Proof.

By Lemma
10
,

\gamma_{k}^{\mathrm{L}}(G)=\frac{n(G)}{k+1}
and

\gamma_{k}^{\mathrm{L}}(H)=\frac{n(H)}{k+1}
.
Therefore the upper bound in Theorem
7
gives

\gamma_{k}^{\mathrm{L}}(G\square H)\leq\frac{n(G)\,n(H)}{k+1}
.
On the other hand, the lower bound in the same theorem and the identity

n(G\square H)=n(G)\,n(H)
yield the reverse inequality.
∎

Corollary 12
.

The lower bound in Theorem
7
is attained for every positive integer
k
.

4.3
Grid graphs

Having established the sharpness of both bounds in Theorem
7
, we now focus on more specific results for the case
k=1
.
We begin with
grid graphs
, that is, Cartesian products of two paths, for which Theorem
7
already yields an exact formula whenever at least one of the two paths has even order.

Lemma 13
.

Let
m,n\geq 1
be integers, where at least one of them is even. Then

\gamma_{1}^{\mathrm{L}}(P_{m}\square P_{n})=\frac{mn}{2}.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, assume that
m
is even. Then
mn
is even, and the lower bound in Theorem
7
gives

\gamma_{1}^{\mathrm{L}}(P_{m}\square P_{n})\geq\left\lceil\frac{n(P_{m}\square P_{n})}{2}\right\rceil=\left\lceil\frac{mn}{2}\right\rceil=\frac{mn}{2}.

On the other hand, Theorem
7
and Proposition
3
yield

\gamma_{1}^{\mathrm{L}}(P_{m}\square P_{n})\leq\min\left\{\frac{mn}{2},\left\lceil\frac{n}{2}\right\rceil m\right\}=\frac{mn}{2}.

Hence equality holds.
∎

On the other hand, if
2\leq m\leq n
are both odd, determining the
1
-limited domination number of
P_{m}\square P_{n}
appears to be more challenging.
In this case, Theorem
7
gives

\frac{mn+1}{2}\leq\gamma_{1}^{\mathrm{L}}(P_{m}\square P_{n})\leq\min\left\{\frac{(m+1)n}{2},\frac{(n+1)m}{2}\right\}=\frac{mn+m}{2}=m\Big\lceil\frac{n}{2}\Big\rceil,

where the last equality uses the assumption
m\leq n
.
We will show that when
m=3
, equality holds in the upper bound.
To this end, we first fix the notation.

For an integer
n\geq 2
let
G_{n}=P_{n}\square P_{3}
. More precisely, let

V(G_{n})=\{a_{i},c_{i},b_{i}:1\leq i\leq n\}
,
and

E(G_{n})=\{a_{i}c_{i},\,c_{i}b_{i}:1\leq i\leq n\}\,\cup\,\{x_{i}x_{i+1}:x\in\{a,c,b\},\,1\leq i\leq n-1\}.

For
1\leq i\leq n
we call

T_{i}=\{a_{i},c_{i},b_{i}\}

the
i
-th column
of
G_{n}
; it induces a copy of
P_{3}
.
Given a
1
-limited dominating set
D\subseteq V(G_{n})
we define, for
each column
T_{i}
,

t_{i}=|D\cap T_{i}|\in\{0,1,2,3\}
.
We call
(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})
the
column weight sequence
of
D
, and denote it briefly by
(t_{i})
.
A column
T_{i}
is called
empty
if
t_{i}=0
and
full
if
t_{i}=3
.

First we analyze possible column weight sequences of

1
-limited dominating sets.
We view consecutive subsequences of
(t_{i})
as
patterns
, encoded by the corresponding strings over the alphabet
\{0,1,2,3\}
.
For example, if for some
j
we have
(t_{j},t_{j+1},t_{j+2})=(0,3,3)
, we say that
(t_{i})
contains the pattern
033
at position
j
.

In addition to forbidden local patterns, one of the key observations, formalized in the following lemma, is that every column of weight
0
or
1
has a
private neighbor
of weight
3
, that is, no two distinct columns of weight
0
or
1
share the same neighboring column of weight
3
.

Lemma 14
.

Let
n\geq 3
and let
D
be a
1
-limited dominating set of
G_{n}
. Then the following holds.

(a)

None of the following patterns occurs in
(t_{i})
:

01,\ 10,\ 12,\ 21,\ 20,\ 02,\ 000,\ 111,\ 230,\ 032,\ 030,\ 130,\ 031,\ 131.

(b)

Let
S=\{i\in[n]:\ t_{i}\in\{0,1\}\}
. Then for every
i\in S
there exists
j\in[n]
with
|i-j|=1
and
t_{j}=3
.
Moreover, there is an injective map

p:S\to[n]

such that
|p(i)-i|=1
and
t_{p(i)}=3
for all
i\in S
.

Proof.

A straightforward case analysis on two or three consecutive columns shows that the configurations corresponding to the patterns listed in (a) are incompatible with
1
-limited domination.
To prove (b), let
i\in S
, so
t_{i}\in\{0,1\}
.

Assume first that
t_{i}=0
. By (a), none of the patterns
01,02,10,20
can occur, and hence each neighbor of
T_{i}
has weight either
0
or
3
.
If
1<i<n
and neither neighboring column had weight
3
, then

(t_{i-1},t_{i},t_{i+1})=(0,0,0)
, contradicting the forbidden pattern
000
.
Thus in this case there exists a neighboring column of weight
3
.
The same conclusion holds when
i\in\{1,n\}
. Indeed, if
i=1
, then by (a) neither
01
nor
02
can occur, so the only remaining possibilities are
t_{2}=0
or
t_{2}=3
. The case
t_{2}=0
is impossible, since then not all vertices of
T_{1}
are dominated. Hence
t_{2}=3
. The case
i=n
is symmetric.

Assume now that
t_{i}=1
. By (a), none of the patterns
10,01,12,21
can occur, and hence each neighbor of
T_{i}
has weight either
1
or
3
.
If
1<i<n
and neither neighboring column had weight
3
, then

(t_{i-1},t_{i},t_{i+1})=(1,1,1)
, contradicting the forbidden pattern
111
.
Thus in this case there exists a neighboring column of weight
3
.
It remains to consider the boundary cases. By symmetry, it suffices to treat
i=1
.
Suppose, to the contrary, that
t_{1}=t_{2}=1
.
Let
x
be the unique vertex of
D\cap T_{1}
, and let
y
be the unique vertex of
D\cap T_{2}
.
First note that
x\neq c_{1}
, since otherwise
x
would have both
a_{1}
and
b_{1}
as neighbors outside
D
, contradicting the
1
-limited condition.
Hence, by symmetry, we may assume that
x=a_{1}
.
Since
b_{1}
must be dominated and
x
does not dominate it, we must have
y=b_{2}
.
But then the vertex
y
has both
b_{1}
and
c_{2}
as neighbors outside
D
, again contradicting the
1
-limited condition.
Therefore
t_{2}\neq 1
, and since (a) excludes
t_{2}=2
, we must have
t_{2}=3
.
The case
i=n
is symmetric.
Thus, whenever
t_{i}\in\{0,1\}
, there exists a neighboring column of weight
3
.

Finally, we show that two distinct columns of weight
0
or
1
cannot share the same neighboring column of weight
3
. Indeed, if there exists
\ell
such that

t_{\ell}\in\{0,1\}
,
t_{\ell+1}=3
, and
t_{\ell+2}\in\{0,1\}
, then

(t_{\ell},t_{\ell+1},t_{\ell+2})
is one of the forbidden patterns

030,031,130,131
, contradicting (a).

Therefore, for each
i\in S
we may choose a neighboring index
p(i)\in[n]
with
|p(i)-i|=1
and
t_{p(i)}=3
,
and the previous paragraph shows that this choice yields an injective map
p:S\to[n]
.
∎

Proposition 15
.

Let
n\geq 3
be an odd integer. Then
\gamma_{1}^{\mathrm{L}}(G_{n})\geq\frac{3}{2}n+\frac{3}{2}
.

Proof.

Let
D
be a
\gamma_{1}^{\mathrm{L}}(G_{n})
-set, and let
(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})
be the corresponding column weight sequence. Define

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=\{i\in[n]:t_{i}\in\{0,1\}\},\qquad

\displaystyle O

\displaystyle=\{i\in[n]:t_{i}=1\},

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle=\{i\in[n]:t_{i}=2\},\qquad

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=\{i\in[n]:t_{i}=3\}.

By Lemma
14
(b), there exists an injective map

p:S\to[n]

such that
|p(i)-i|=1
and
t_{p(i)}=3
for all
i\in S
.
Let

R=H\setminus p(S)
.
Since
p
is injective, we have
|H|=|S|+|R|
.
Now

|D|=\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}=|O|+2|T|+3|H|=|O|+2|T|+3|S|+3|R|,

while

n=|S|+|T|+|H|=2|S|+|T|+|R|.

Therefore

|D|-\frac{3}{2}n=|O|+\frac{1}{2}|T|+\frac{3}{2}|R|.

Hence it suffices to prove that

|O|+\frac{1}{2}|T|+\frac{3}{2}|R|\geq\frac{3}{2}.

If
|R|\geq 1
, then we are done. Thus assume
R=\emptyset
. Then
|H|=|S|
, and so

n=|S|+|T|+|H|=2|S|+|T|
.
Since
n
is odd, it follows that
|T|
is odd.
If
|T|\geq 3
, then
\frac{1}{2}|T|\geq\frac{3}{2}
, and again we are done.
Thus
|T|=1
.
We claim that then
|O|\geq 1
.

Suppose for a contradiction that
|O|=0
. Then every index in
S
corresponds to an empty column.
Let
r
be the unique index with
t_{r}=2
.
By Lemma
14
(a), the patterns
02
and
20
are forbidden. Hence every column adjacent to
T_{r}
has weight
3
.
Since
R=\emptyset
, every full column belongs to
p(S)
, and hence is adjacent to some empty column.
If
r=1
, then
t_{2}=3
, and the empty column adjacent to
T_{2}
must be
T_{3}
, yielding the forbidden pattern
230
.
If
r=n
, then
t_{n-1}=3
, and the empty column adjacent to
T_{n-1}
must be
T_{n-2}
, yielding the forbidden pattern
032
.
If
1<r<n
, then both neighboring columns of
T_{r}
have weight
3
. Since
R=\emptyset
and
|O|=0
, each of these full columns must be adjacent to an empty column on its other side. Hence

(t_{r-2},t_{r-1},t_{r},t_{r+1},t_{r+2})=(0,3,2,3,0),

which contains the forbidden patterns
032
and
230
.

This contradiction proves that
|O|\geq 1
. Consequently, in the remaining case
|T|=1
we have

|O|+\frac{1}{2}|T|\geq 1+\frac{1}{2}=\frac{3}{2},

which completes the proof.
∎

The above observations can now be summarized as follows.

Theorem 16
.

For every integer
n\geq 2
, we have
\gamma_{1}^{\mathrm{L}}(P_{n}\square P_{3})=3\,\Big\lceil\frac{n}{2}\Big\rceil
.

Proof.

For even
n
the proof follows from Lemma
13
. For odd
n
, Proposition
15
gives us
\gamma_{1}^{\mathrm{L}}(P_{n}\square P_{3})\geq 3\,\Big\lceil\frac{n}{2}\Big\rceil
, while the opposite inequality clearly follows from Theorem
7
.
∎

4.4
Hypercubes

Hypercubes form a particularly well-structured class in which
k
-limited domination can be studied explicitly: they are highly symmetric, regular, and arise recursively as iterated prisms.
In this subsection we first recall a useful reduction for regular graphs,
then discuss prisms, and finally apply these observations to
Q_{d}
.

In
[
2
]
the following was proved for regular graphs.

Proposition 17
.

[
2
]

Let
G
be a connected
d
-regular graph of order
n\geq 3
and let
k
be an integer such that
1\leq k<d
. Then
\gamma_{k}^{\mathrm{L}}(G)=\gamma_{1,d-k}(G)
.

Before turning specifically to hypercubes, let us briefly discuss what can be said about
k
-limited domination on general prisms.
Recall that the
prism
over a graph
G
is the Cartesian product
G\square K_{2}
.
Several domination parameters behave particularly well on prisms. In particular, it was shown in
[
1
]
, and subsequently reproved by different arguments in
[
10
]
and
[
17
]
, that if
G
is a bipartite graph, then
\gamma_{t}(G\square K_{2})=2\gamma(G)
.

Now let
G
be a connected
d
-regular bipartite graph. Then
G\square K_{2}
is
(d+1)
-regular, and Proposition
17
applied to
G\square K_{2}
with
k=d
gives

\gamma_{d}^{\mathrm{L}}(G\square K_{2})=\gamma_{1,1}(G\square K_{2})=\gamma_{t}(G\square K_{2})
.
Combining this with the above identity yields the following.

Corollary 18
.

Let
G
be a connected
d
-regular bipartite graph with
d\geq 2
. Then

\gamma_{d}^{\mathrm{L}}(G\square K_{2})=2\,\gamma(G).

Hypercubes fit naturally into this picture as for every
d\geq 1
we have the recursive representation

Q_{d+1}=Q_{d}\square K_{2}
,
so that
Q_{d+1}
is precisely the prism over
Q_{d}
. Since
Q_{d}
is
d
-regular and bipartite, Corollary
18

immediately yields the following relationship.

Corollary 19
.

Let
d\geq 1
be an integer. Then

\gamma_{d}^{\mathrm{L}}(Q_{d+1})=\gamma_{t}(Q_{d+1})=2\,\gamma(Q_{d})
.

For hypercubes, exact formulas for the classical domination number are known only for small values (see Table
3
) and for two distinguished infinite families. Namely, if
d=2^{r}-1
, then

\gamma(Q_{d})=2^{\,2^{r}-r-1}
, while for
d=2^{r}
one has
\gamma(Q_{d})=2^{\,2^{r}-r}
.
The first formula follows from the existence of perfect codes in
Q_{2^{r}-1}
, see
[
11
]
, while the second is due to van Wee
[
18
]
.
Consequently, Corollary
19
immediately yields exact values for
\gamma^{\mathrm{L}}_{d-1}(Q_{d})
in the corresponding families.

Corollary 20
.

Let
r\geq 1
be an integer.
If
d=2^{r}
, then
\gamma^{\mathrm{L}}_{d-1}(Q_{d})=2^{\,2^{r}-r}
, and
if
d=2^{r}+1
, then
\gamma^{\mathrm{L}}_{d-1}(Q_{d})=2^{\,2^{r}-r+1}
.

While the previous corollaries concern the extremal case
k=d
,
the opposite end
k=1
leads to a simpler behavior. In fact, optimal
1
-limited dominating sets in
Q_{d}
occupy
exactly one half of the vertices. We include a short proof, which also illustrates how the general Cartesian product bounds
become tight on hypercubes.

Proposition 21
.

For every integer
d\geq 1
,

\gamma_{1}^{\mathrm{L}}(Q_{d})=2^{d-1}
.

Proof.

Since
Q_{d}
has
2^{d}
vertices, the lower bound in Theorem
2
gives
\gamma_{1}^{\mathrm{L}}(Q_{d})\geq\left\lceil\frac{2^{d}}{2}\right\rceil=2^{d-1}
.

To prove the opposite inequality, recall that for an integer
d\geq 1
,
Q_{d}
can be seen as the graph with

V(Q_{d})=\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{d}):x_{i}\in\{0,1\}\text{ for all }i\in[d]\}
,
where two vertices are adjacent if and only if they differ in exactly one coordinate. Let
D=\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in\{0,1\}^{d}:\ x_{1}=0\}
. Clearly
|D|=2^{d-1}
. We claim that
D
is a
1
-limited dominating set of
Q_{d}
. Take an arbitrary vertex
y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{d})\notin D
, so
y_{1}=1
. Then the vertex

x=(0,y_{2},\dots,y_{d})\in D

differs from
y
in exactly one coordinate, hence
x
and
y
are adjacent
Q_{d}
. Therefore
D
dominates every vertex outside
D
. To verify the
1
-limited constraint, let
x\in D
. Among the
d
neighbors of
x
, exactly one has first coordinate
1
, while the remaining
d-1
neighbors have the first coordinate equal to
0
and thus also lie in
D
.
Consequently,
x
dominates exactly one vertex of
V(Q_{d})\setminus D
, and the
1
-limited constraint holds.
Thus
\gamma_{1}^{\mathrm{L}}(Q_{d})\leq|D|=2^{d-1}
, and together with the lower bound we conclude

\gamma_{1}^{\mathrm{L}}(Q_{d})=2^{d-1}
.
∎

5
Conclusion

The notion of
k
-limited domination was introduced in
[
2
]
as a restricted variant of classical domination, motivated by situations in which dominating vertices cannot serve arbitrarily many vertices outside the dominating set. The present paper shows that the parameter is not only well motivated, but also structurally rich and mathematically nontrivial.

This is reflected already in the computational complexity of the problem. While we proved that
k
-Limited Dominating set
is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete for every fixed integer
k\geq 2
, the extremal case
k=1
remains unresolved. Our reduction relies in an essential way on the assumption
k\geq 2
, and the corresponding forcing mechanism breaks down for
k=1
. This suggests that the case of
1
-limited domination requires a different approach, and leads naturally to the following question.

Problem 22
.

Is
1
-Limited Dominating Set

\mathsf{NP}
-complete?

Another direction pursued in this paper concerns the behavior of
k
-limited domination under the Cartesian product. We established general lower and upper bounds for
\gamma_{k}^{\mathrm{L}}(G\square H)
, proved that both are sharp, and derived exact values for several natural graph families. In particular, for
k=1
all exact values obtained in this paper attain the general upper bound from Theorem
7
. This is not the typical behavior for arbitrary
k
: for example,

\gamma^{\mathrm{L}}_{2}(K_{2}\square K_{1,3})=3
,
whereas Theorem
7
yields only the upper bound
4
. Nevertheless, our results suggest that the extremal case
k=1
may be special, which leads to the following question.

Problem 23
.

Is it true that for all connected graphs
G
and
H
,

\gamma^{\mathrm{L}}_{1}(G\square H)=\min\big\{\gamma^{\mathrm{L}}_{1}(G)\,n(H),\,\gamma^{\mathrm{L}}_{1}(H)\,n(G)\big\}\,?

A particularly natural case to examine is the Cartesian product of two paths. In this paper we proved that

\gamma_{1}^{\mathrm{L}}(P_{m}\square P_{n})=\frac{mn}{2}

whenever at least one of
m
and
n
is even, and that

\gamma_{1}^{\mathrm{L}}(P_{n}\square P_{3})=3\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil
.
This leaves open the case when
m
and
n
are both odd, and leads to the following question.

Problem 24
.

Is it true that for all integers
1\leq m\leq n
,
\gamma^{\mathrm{L}}_{1}(P_{m}\square P_{n})=m\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil
?

We conclude by discussing
k
-limited domination in hypercubes. For the classical domination number
\gamma(Q_{d})
, a useful overview of the current state of knowledge is provided by the recent paper of Wu and Chen
[
19
]
, whose appendix collects the best known lower and upper bounds for dimensions up to
33
. In addition, for dimensions divisible by
6
, their main theorem improves some of the previously best known lower bounds. The gray cells in Table
3
reflect the corresponding part of this picture up to dimension
17
, while incorporating the mentioned improvement in the case
d=12
. The remaining entries are then arranged around these values in the setting of
k
-limited domination. Since for
k\geq d
we have
\gamma_{k}^{\mathrm{L}}(Q_{d})=\gamma(Q_{d})
, an asterisk indicates that the corresponding value coincides with the one in the gray cell above it. The entries immediately above the gray cells represent the values of
\gamma_{d-1}^{\mathrm{L}}(Q_{d})
, which, by Corollary
19
, are equal to the total domination number
\gamma_{t}(Q_{d})
, and therefore to twice the domination number of the
(d-1)
-dimensional hypercube. The values in the first row follow from Proposition
21
.

For
d
-regular graphs, and in particular for hypercubes
Q_{d}
, the identity
\gamma_{k}^{\mathrm{L}}(Q_{d})=\gamma_{1,d-k}(Q_{d})
,
arising from Proposition

k

(1,t)

t=1

t\geq 2
seem to remain largely open. In particular, the parameter
\gamma^{\mathrm{L}}_{d-2}(Q_{d})
, equivalent to

-domination, appears to be a natural next direction for further research, especially in light of the recent interest in this parameter

.
