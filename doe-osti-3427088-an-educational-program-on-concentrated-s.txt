Executive Summary
The objective of this project was to design and implement a comprehensive educational and
applied research program in Concentrated Solar Power aka Concentrating Solar Power aka
Concentrated Solar Thermal Power (CSTP) and heliostat technologies at Northeastern
University. In alignment with the U.S. Department of Energy’s Heliostat Consortium (HelioCon)
goals, the project aimed to expand student and public understanding of CSTP systems while
simultaneously contributing to workforce development and the broader decarbonization strategy.
A particular emphasis was placed on integrating hands-on student design projects and publicly
disseminating educational content relevant to CSTP systems.

The project addressed a critical gap in renewable energy education: CSTP and heliostats, despite
their importance in utility-scale solar energy, are rarely included in standard mechanical
engineering programs. This project established new pathways for students to engage with the
topic through the creation of a 4-credit graduate/senior elective course, development of five
industry-facing short courses, and the inclusion of CSTP-based capstone design projects. Over
two academic years, 36 students across six senior design teams developed and tested
technologies such as deformable heliostats, beacon-based tracking systems, and solar-powered
pyrolizers for biomass-to-biochar conversion. Concurrently, 30 undergraduate and graduate
students were enrolled in the new academic course centered around CSTP principles.

To ensure the relevance and accessibility of the short course content, the project team engaged
with industry professionals, technical policy stakeholders, and potential course participants
through structured surveys and informal consultations. Feedback from 28 respondents guided the
structure, length, and delivery format of the courses - resulting in a modular design broken into
five workshops. The feedback emphasized the need for flexible, asynchronous delivery and
practical case studies, particularly in areas such as heliostat control, thermal storage, and solar
fuel production. This engagement helped align the courses with the evolving knowledge demands
of the renewable energy workforce and ensured that participants from both technical and policy
backgrounds could meaningfully benefit from the material.

The research and educational activities advanced the understanding of heliostat control systems,
optical performance under misalignment, and thermal system integration in solar-driven
pyrolysis applications. Methods and designs explored in this project proved to be both
technically effective and economically feasible at the lab scale. Prototypes were constructed
using commercially available components and custom-fabricated elements, demonstrating that
meaningful performance improvements can be achieved with modest material and fabrication
costs, supporting the feasibility of student-led research in this field.

The public benefit of this project is twofold. First, it cultivates a pipeline of engineers trained to
be familiar with CSTP principles, an essential workforce need identified by the Department of
Energy for achieving its 2030 cost and deployment targets. Second, it contributes openly
accessible educational materials, course content, and experimental frameworks to the broader
community, enabling other institutions to adopt or adapt similar programming. Through outreach
activities, curriculum integration, and technical exposure, this project contributes to a more
informed and capable renewable energy workforce while supporting innovation in heliostat and

the course and its outcomes, with plans to publish it in the ASME Open Access Journal of

1 Background
Concentrating Solar Thermal Power (CSTP) has become increasingly central to the U.S.
Department of Energy’s strategy for delivering dispatchable, low-cost renewable electricity.
Through the Solar Energy Technologies Office (SETO), the DOE has launched several
initiatives – most notably, the Heliostat Consortium for Concentrating Solar Thermal Power
(HelioCon) – to reduce the cost and improve the reliability of heliostat systems. Heliostat
systems are a core element of CSTP systems, which can occupy anywhere between 30-50% of a
power tower’s capital cost. In addition to seeking technological advancement, HelioCon
emphasizes workforce development and academic engagement as critical enablers for long-term
CSTP scalability (DOE SETO, 2021; HelioCon, 2023). This includes funding educational
programs, fostering interdisciplinary collaborations, and supporting the creation of curricular
content that builds national expertise in heliostat design, operation, and integration.

Several university-led initiatives have emerged to meet this need. Rice University’s “A Brighter
Future” program focuses on public education and CSTP demonstrations in underserved urban
areas, developing instructional toolkits for schools and community colleges (Rice University,
2024.) The DOE’s upcoming Solar Thermal Collegiate Competition will give undergraduate
teams nationwide hands-on experience designing CSTP systems in a competitive format (DOE
HelioCon, 2024.) Additionally, academic researchers have focused on pushing the technical
boundaries of heliostat systems.

The project undertaken at Northeastern University has helped to compliment these national
efforts by embedding heliostat and CSTP education into the engineering curriculum through a
combination of graduate coursework, senior capstone design projects, and publicly available
instructional resources. Similar to Rice University’s initiatives, Northeastern’s effort aims to
expand awareness and understanding of solar-thermal technologies. However, it is distinguished
by its long-term integration into formal academic structures and its emphasis on repeatedly
supported, design-based learning through its senior capstone program. Unlike more research-
driven efforts focused on technical innovation, Northeastern’s program leverages the state-of-
the-art findings to ensure students are exposed to current challenges and real-world solutions to
CSTP deployment. In doing so, the project directly contributes to the DOE’s goal of building a
technically capable workforce while also establishing a sustainable model for CSTP education at
the university level.

2 Project Objectives
This project, An Educational Program on Concentrated Solar Power and Heliostats for Power
Generations and Industrial Processes, directly supports the U.S. Department of Energy Solar
Energy Technologies Office (DOE-SETO) and HelioCon Consortium’s broader aim of achieving
a levelized cost of energy (LCOE) of $0.05/kWh for baseload CSTP. Given that heliostat fields
account for up to 50% of direct system costs in power tower CSTP systems, this project seeks to
contribute to the reduction of cost and improvement of performance through a dual approach: (1)
developing a technically proficient workforce pipeline, and (2) advancing CSTP heliostat
innovation through undergraduate Capstone Mechanical Engineering Design projects and
graduate-level research and experimentation.

By combining rigorous technical education with hands-on research and outreach activities, the
project’s overarching objective is to cultivate a new generation of engineers and scientists
capable of designing, validating, and commercializing next-generation heliostat systems. In
doing so, the program contributes to DOE-SETO’s 2030 goals while establishing a sustainable
education and research ecosystem at Northeastern University, and beyond.

3 Significance, Innovation, and Broader Impact
The project is significant in three dimensions. The development of fully accredited CSTP
curriculum and its translation into modular industry courses offers a replicable model for
academic institutions looking to expand CSTP instruction. The use of design-based learning and
experimental validation in the CSTP receiver, deformable mirrors, and heliostat controls
provides undergraduates with real-world engineering challenges tied to national goals. Lastly, the
designs created under this program not only demonstrate technical viability but offer promising
directions for future optimization and cost reduction.

Project Results and Discussion: This section documents the progress made against the
Statement of Project Objectives (SOPO), organized by task. Each subsection provides a task-
level analysis of results, supported by subtasks and measurable performance outcomes. The
discussion integrates quantitative comparisons, quality metrics, student involvement, and
research impacts spanning the two-year period.

Task 1: Outreach, Advertising, and Recruitment (Y1Q1 – Y1Q2)
Northeastern University executed a comprehensive outreach plan to engage undergraduate
students in CSTP-related research opportunities. Two first-year engineering students were
recruited and supported to complete summer research experiences, which culminated in
presentations and documented findings. Over 50 students were engaged at the university-wide
Showcase Of Undergraduate Research and Creative Endeavors (SOURCE) event. One
mechanical engineering undergraduate student was placed as a dedicated heliostat research co-op
for Fall 2023. A project-specific website was launched to provide educational material and
promote participation in CSTP programs.

These activities satisfy SOPO Task 1 and Subtasks 1.1-1.3, which called for undergraduate
engagement and the re-establishment of engineering co-op positions in partnership with NREL
and Sandia National Laboratories.

Task 2: Development of University and Industry Coursework (Y1Q1 –
Y1Q3)
A graduate-level course, ME5680: Applications of Concentrating Solar Thermal Technologies,
was developed and approved as a 4-credit offering. The course covered a range of topics
including solar geometry, heliostat design, ray optics, solar receiver modeling, and CSTP-based
energy storage. It was first delivered in Spring 2024 with 13 enrolled students – evaluation
metrics included weekly homework, midterm and final exams. Scores ranged from 68-98%
demonstrated strong absorption of the course material.

In parallel, five short courses were created for industry professionals. These modular sessions
ranged from 2-4 hours each and included narrated lecture content, simulation exercises using
relevant industry software, and additional reading. The short courses were finalized and prepared
for online distribution and on-ground weekend delivery.

These results fulfill task 2.1 and 2.2, along with Milestones 1.4 and 1.6, which required full
course syllabi, content creation, and delivery plans.

Task 3: Development of Capstone Mechanical Engineering Design
Projects (Y1Q1)
Three student capstone teams were launched to investigate heliostat-related innovations. These
projects included:

 • A deformable heliostat mirror using linear actuators to enhance focus
 • A solar pyrolizer for pine needle biomass conversion
 • A closed-loop solar tracker using Light Dependent Resistors (LDRs)
Each project produced research summaries, planning documents, and early-stage prototypes.
Teams also prepared bills of materials, safety documentation, and design schematics. This early
foundation enabled more advanced prototyping in Year 2, and these outcomes satisfy Task 3 and
Milestone 1.1.

Task 4: Administrative Compliance (Y1Q4)
ME5680 was formally listed in the Northeastern University course catalog in accordance with
university policy. Advertising and student registration were completed in advance of the Spring
2024 term. All administrative tasks required to comply with SOPO Task 4 and Milestone 1.8
were completed successfully.

Task 5: Course Delivery (Y2Q1)
The ME5680 course was delivered in Spring 2024 and included weekly remote sessions,
recorded lectures, and take-home assessments. Student participation was active, and engagement
was high during interactive review sessions. The five short courses were finalized, packaged, and
prepared for asynchronous and in-person delivery starting November 2024. Certificates of
Participation are to be provided upon course completion.

These results satisfy Task 5 and Milestones 2.1 through 2.4.

Task 6: Execution of Capstone Mechanical Engineering Design
Projects (Y1Q1–Y2Q4)
Three primary capstone design teams continued into Year 2 and executed advanced CSTP
research projects:

 • The deformable heliostat teams achieved a 7.5x irradiance gain by shaping an acrylic
 mirror using a controllable backing structure.
 • The solar pyrolizer teams developed two working biomass-to-biochar system using a
 gravity fed parabolic solar dish and a parabolic solar trough and auger-fed chamber.
 • The closed-loop tracking teams developed a dual-axis prototype responsive to real-time
 beacon signals for improved heliostat orientation.

Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions: Over the course of this two-year initiative,
Northeastern University successfully designed and implemented an integrated educational and
research program focused on advancing heliostat and Concentrating Solar Thermal Power
(CSTP) technologies. The project was successful in fulfilling all core objectives as outlined in
the Performance Work Statement, with all Phase I and Phase II deliverables completed on
schedule.

A 4-credit graduate-level course, Applications of Concentrating Solar Thermal Technologies
(ME5680), was developed, approved, and delivered in Spring of 2024. The course introduced
students to heliostat field design, solar optics, thermal energy storage, and system-level
simulation using tools such as SolTrace and SolarPILOT. Thirteen students enrolled in the
course and completed weekly assignments, projects, and take-home exams. Assessment results
indicated strong performance in thermodynamic and energy systems analysis, with some noted
gaps in ray tracing and optical modeling, which will be addressed in future course iterations. The
course catalogue description is provided below.

Course Corequisites: None

Course Description: Focuses on the analysis of concentrating solar thermal technologies at the
design and system levels. Discusses simulation techniques to calculate the performance of
concentrating solar power (CSP) technologies, including comparisons to other solar systems in
terms of efficiency and cost. Includes topics involving solar systems designs of heliostats,
concentrating dishes, and troughs. Presents geometric optics and convolution techniques and
integrates them with incident solar predictions to calculate the performance of CSP systems.
Covers the control and tracking systems required to maximize performance; the thermal effects
that occur at the high solar energy fluxes and high temperatures in CSP systems; and the
potential advantages in energy generation, energy storage, and biogas manufacturing and means
to predict them.

Short Course I: Introduction to CSTP Fundamentals
Target Audience: Participants new to the CSTP field with a technical background, policy
analysts, or early-stage researchers.

 • Overview of existing CSTP facilities, including layout, technologies, and limitations
 • Fundamental solar radiation characteristics: intensity, intermittency, and geographic
 variability
 • Key distinctions between energy and power, highlighting the critical need for storage and
 dispatchability
 • Introduction to solar field control: understanding why CSTP isn’t about aligning one
 mirror, but managing the behavior of thousands of heliostats working in concert
 • Discussion of capital costs, land use, and $/kWh targets, which help frame the economic
 realities of CSTP deployment
 • Basic receiver geometry and orientation considerations, with a focus on incident solar
 angle, reflection geometry, and the importance of thermal and optical tradeoffs.

This course serves as the intellectual gateway into CSTP, grounding participants in the systemic
challenges and design imperatives of the field. By blending technical fundamentals with policy-
relevant insight, this short course helps to bridge the gap between science and application.

Short Course II: Modeling, Ray Tracing, and Heliostat Control
Target Audience: Participants newly hired into CSTP roles or those seeking greater depth
following completion of Course I.

 • Introduction to the quantitative modeling of solar radiation, including both theoretical
 equations and software-based approaches.
 • Distinction between design-based vs. operational solar input data, with the latter involving
 live local weather conditions and short-term forecasting.
 • Ray tracing fundamentals and their role in CSP optical efficiency modeling (e.g., optical
 loss factors from shading, spillage, cosine effects).
 • In-depth presentation of SolarPILOT and SolTrace, two of the most widely used CSP
 simulation tools.
 • Identification of control challenges in heliostat fields, including latency, misalignment,
 field-wide optimization, and daily recalibration.

This course empowers participants with tools and methods to go from conceptual design to
simulation-ready models. It links the theoretical understanding of light behavior to actual field
control practices, an area of critical importance for scaling CSP. This content is highly relevant to
experimental and ray tracing-based research initiatives.

Short Course III: Receiver Design and Thermal Transfer Mechanics
Target Audience: Individuals currently working in CSTP or those who have completed course
II and wish to understand component-level thermal design.

 • Survey of receiver geometries and working fluids, including molten salts, pressurized
 gases, and particle-based systems.
 • Application of heat transfer principles (especially radiation) to real receiver
 configurations.
 • Tradeoffs in material selection, emphasizing survivability at high energy flux, cost, and
 operational lifetime.
 • Introduction to “element” models, which form the basis of commercial thermal
 • simulation software, and how to interpret and implement boundary conditions.
 • Lifecycle and safety considerations of working fluids, including decomposition, volatility,
 and environmental impact.

This course connects optical collection to thermal conversion, a critical link in the CSP chain. By
examining the material science and thermodynamics underpinning receiver design, it offers
participants the tools to not only select appropriate working fluids but also understand and
anticipate operational failure modes. This is particularly valuable for student research aiming to
design novel receiver configurations capable of maximizing heat retention while minimizing
losses.

Short Course IV: Energy Storage and System Integration
Target Audience: Experienced CSTP professionals or advanced students aiming to simulate
complete solar-thermal systems, particularly energy storage integration.

 • Introduction to thermal energy storage systems, especially molten salt and steam-based
 configurations.
 • Survey of electrical and chemical energy storage strategies, including batteries and fuel-
 based approaches.
 • Analysis of alternative storage methods, such as using biomass or waste-to-energy for
 hybrid CSP systems.
 • Strategies for modelling complete CSP systems by integrating optical models with
 thermal and energy storage models.
 • Application of microeconomics and thermoeconomics, using second-law efficiency
 principles to evaluate system-wide cost-effectiveness and usable energy recovery.

This course stands out by encouraging systems-level thinking, challenging students to evaluate
how energy flows through a CSP plant not just thermally, but economically.

The emphasis on load-matching, simulation integration, and cost analysis equips participants to
address the grand challenge of CSP: making renewable thermal energy dispatchable and
competitive with fossil fuels.

Short Course V: CSTP-Driven Fuel Production and Manufacturing Potential
Target Audience: Participants with background in CSP design and simulation, now focused on
advanced applications and commercialization.

 • Overview of electricity generation pathways, including steam turbine cycles and their
 adaptations in CSP.

 • Introduction to solar thermochemical fuel production, including hydrogen generation via

 • Economic rationale for using CSP as a platform for synthetic fuel production,

This final course addresses the cutting edge of solar thermal R&D: the use of CSP not just for

strategies for CSP. Course V’s content intersects with the broader goals of leveraging solar
energy beyond the grid—into fuels, materials processing, and heat-intensive manufacturing.

Deformable Heliostat, Phase 1, 2023-2024

Figure 1: Mechanical engineering students Steven Beil, Victoria Budike, Paige Farrin, Benjamin Hansel,
 Benjamin Lynch, and Nicole Pollis designed an acrylic mirror. The shape of the mirror is adjustable by
pulling on its center. Their innovation significantly increases solar energy concentration, paving the way
 for advancements in heliostat arrays.

 Figure 2: A photo taken during testing of the Phase I deformable heliostat mirror, with the
  prototype to the left of the image and the result of the deflected mirror deflection—forming a
 cross-like shape—pictured center.

Typically, heliostats consist of a reflective surface and two actuators that tilt the mirror as the sun
moves throughout the day to continuously concentrate light on a stationary target. Current
heliostats are not on track to meet the Department of Energy’s 2030 goal of 5¢/kWh, so this team
set out to explore how a continuously deforming reflective surface can help meet that goal.

The Deformable Heliostat features an acrylic mirror that is pulled into concave shape using a
linear actuator. This curvature increases concentration and intensity of light as opposed to the

 20

standard flat heliostat. After months of iterations and testing, the team determined that this
deformable model was successful by the following metrics: increasing

concentration up to 45.72%, minimizing the effects of aberrations, and increasing intensity by a
factor of 7.5. This model paves the way for further research into deformable solutions in heliostat
technology and has the potential to be used in utility scale heliostat arrays.

 21

Deformable Heliostat, Phase 2, 2024-2025

Figure 3: Mechanical engineering students Adam DiBiase, Adam Frankenfield, Ian McCarthy,
Ana Olmos, and Nolan Smithwick designed an acrylic mirror, the corners of which can be bent.
 Their innovation allows for increased solar energy concentration on a target and lessens the
 frequency of rotating the entire mirror to track the sun.

Central Receiver Systems (CRS) use thousands of heliostats, or sun-tracking mirrors, to
concentrate light onto a central tower to produce electricity. Current reports estimate CRS could
see a 50% increase in overall efficiency simply from design and cost improvements of its
heliostats. To achieve this goal, and help further the transition to renewable energy, this team
designed a novel heliostat that uses deformation of the mirror surface as an alternative to
traditional sun-tracking methods. The DEFLEX (Deformable Focusing Light Experimental)

Heliostat features an acrylic mirror that can be manually deformed at each corner. This
deformation creates off-axis parabolic shapes which can shift the focal point of its reflection as
the sun moves over certain distances throughout the day. The curvature of the mirror also
increases concentration of light as opposed to the standard flat heliostat. Through iteration,
simulation, and testing, this team determined that a deformable heliostat can complement
traditional methods to increase efficiency and concentration of a CRS plant.

Heliostat Sun Tracker, Phase 1, 2023-2024

Figure 5: Mechanical engineering students Ryan Cervone, Emmanuel Mendez, Julian Newquist, Andrew
Pflaumer, and Daniel White set out to develop a closed-loop control system for heliostat mirrors capable
 of tracking a collimated light source (a light source with parallel rays).

Heliostats are movable mirrors that function by reflecting concentrated sunlight at a target to
create thermal energy and produce electricity. They are used in a wide array of concentrating
solar vthermal power (CSP) plants and follow a programmed movement throughout the day. This
open-loop system can lead to the misalignment of the sunlight rays on the target, and these
tracking errors decrease the overall efficiency of the heliostat. A possible solution includes
employing a closed-loop tracking system, where the light values from photoresistors are used to
correct tracking errors. In this phase of the project, the team designed a mirror that could track
the position the sun throughout the day, a first step to achieving this goal. This was accomplished
with the assistance of stepper motors connected to the heliostat’s metal frame. Changes of the
sun’s azimuth and elevation angles were followed diurnally. However, this system did not reflect
sunlight on a target.

 24

Figure 6: The closed-loop tracking heliostat pictured outside ready for testing. The system is wired to an
 Arduino microcontroller for actuation and data capture.

Heliostat Sun and Tower Tracking Project, Phase 2, 2024-2025

  Figure 7: Mechanical engineering students Ashnu Cassod, Stephen Catalina, Aura Herce-Duchini,
 Carter Hughes, James Minardi, and George Primoff designed a successful closed-loop heliostat, which
 both tracks the sun and reflects the light to a target tower.

Existing CSP fields use an open-loop system, relying on astronomical data to focus on the
sunlight.

However, if a heliostat’s position were to change due to weather or environmental
conditions, open-loop heliostats have no way to check if they are aiming at the receiver.
Therefore, we are developing a closed-loop heliostat system for the United States Department of
Energy to improve accuracy and efficiency of heliostats. This team’s solution consists of three
primary units: a solar tracker that detects the location of the sun, a self-moving mirror that
reflects the light to target, and a receiver that acts as a locating beacon for the mirror and a target
for the reflected sunlight. The solar tracker’s algorithm uses feedback from four lux sensors to
find the azimuthal and zenith angles of the sun; it then sends those angles to the mirror, which
uses those inputs to calculate the bisecting vector between the sunlight and the receiver to
reflect the light at the target. To improve precision of solar tracking, a cap was added to the solar
tracker, which blocked the sunlight unless the rays were normal to the lux sensors within 0.1°.
Furthermore, limit switches and proximity sensors were added to both the mirror and tracker to
decrease error accumulation over time, using a homing sequence. Lastly, a beacon was added to
the receiver, which the mirror can use to detect its position. It was estimated that, at scale, if the
mirrors are ideally positioned, then the power production of the plant could potentially increase.
However, even a small increase in the overall efficiency of the plant would result in millions more
MWH produced per year, and it would make it more economical to expand the use of CSP
plants.

Solar Dish – Biomass Pyrolizer, 2023-2024

 Figure 9: Mechanical engineering students Robert Egan, Emily Leung, Michael Lo, Shaked Lotem, and
 Vito Montesa adapted a solar-tracking pyrolysis reactor to convert pine needles into biochar. The team
  modified an existing solar tracking system and built an auger system to process the solid product and
 were able to achieve a temperature of 550°C. Their project not only aids forest fire prevention (by
 consuming dead pine needles that could serve as fuel) but also produces biochar, which can be used as
 fertilizer material.

Rising global temperatures have caused an increase in forest fires devastating neighborhoods,
depleting natural resources, and harming wildlife. These fires can cause such damage due to
excess biomass found on forest floors. Therefore, this project aims to mitigate the harm caused
by these fires by incentivizing the cleanup of pine needles from forest floors by building a device
– a solar pyrolizer - to convert this biomass to a more useful resource. Implementing a synthesis
of two previous Capstone projects, the team adapted the solar-tracking low-density polyethylene
(LDPE) pyrolysis reactor and combined it with the mission of a pine needle pyrolizer.
The team adopted the concentrating solar dish developed by the previous teams and improved
its foundation by modifying the electronics for the solar-tracking system. They also rehauled the
feeder system to control the flow rate of pine needles via an auger and collect solid product
compared to the gas produced by LDPE pyrolysis. Outdoors testing showed the new system
could reach a temperature of at least 550°C, well above the initial, manually adjusted solar dish
group's maximum of 480°C and the necessary minimum needle pyrolysis temperature of 300°C.

Solar Trough for Pyrolysis of Waste Pine Needles, 2024-2025

  Figure 13: Mechanical engineering students Nicole Guadagno, Elyse Hall, Isabella Morizio,
  Ben Orton, Ariella Sakhai, and Alexandra Vergara-Anglim designed and constructed a solar
  trough and used it to track the sun. By feeding crushed and pulverized dry pine needles in the
 trough, they were able to heat up a tubular reactor at its focal axis and generate biochar.

A parabolic trough collector reflects sunlight onto a focal tube, facilitating a seamless feeding
process and a moderate temperature pyrolysis process that can yield the highest ratio of biochar
relative to other products (bio-oil and biogas). The system consists of the parabolic trough
collector (PTC) system and its mechanical fixtures, a continuous feeder mechanism to move the
pine needles through the pyrolysis chamber, and a collection device for the bio-oil and biochar
produced. Rotating the trough to follow the relevant motion of the sun throughout the day
optimized the production of biochar. This will allow for the greatest conversion of solar energy
to the heat needed for the thermal pyrolysis of the pine needles at the focal tube of the reflective
parabolic trough of the pyrolyzer. The project aims to design, build, and test a uniaxial rotating
parabolic solar thermal trough to pyrolyze dried pine needles into biofuel products, including
biochar, bio-oil, and biogas. The main advantage of the trough over the dish is that it enables
continuous feeding of the biomass.

4 Student Research Contributions
This project benefitted from the diverse and impactful research of several student contributors,
each advancing different aspects of CSP innovation.

 • Ben Lynch led a graduate research project focused on the development and experimental
 validation of a secondary concentrator for power tower CSTP systems. His work
 introduced a novel compound parabolic concentrator (CPC) geometry designed to serve
 as a passive optical enhancement placed in front of a traditional receiver. The CPC
 improves optical efficiency by tolerating heliostat misalignment and concentrating light
 onto the receiver surface over a wider acceptance angle. Through physical prototyping,
 photodiode-based irradiance testing, and MATLAB modelling of system-wide thermal
 and financial performance, Ben demonstrated that his CPC could increase irradiance and
 reduce mirror field size without sacrificing thermal output. His research, supported by
 Northeastern faculty and collaborators at NREL and Sandia National Labs, is supported
 by a provisional patent and is part of a broader initiative to reduce CSTP system costs and
 improve field-level reliability.
 • Mateo Cifuentes, another graduate researcher, explored an alternative CSTP paradigm by
 integrating axially graded index lenses with a compact power tower design. His lens-
 based system eliminates the need for solar tracking by focusing sunlight through optical
 materials with tailored refractive index gradients, directing it to a hemispherical receiver.
 Mateo designed and fabricated Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS)-based prototypes,
 performed ray-tracing analysis, and carried out experimental lux measurements, showing
 significant increases in light concentration compared to baseline setups. His work
 underscores the feasibility of stationary CSTP systems with reduced spatial and
 operational demands, pointing toward new possibilities for solar integration in
 constrained environments.
 • Kyla Hampton and Courtney Jackson, two undergraduate engineering students,
 conducted a practical investigation into heliostat reflectance loss due to soiling and its
 mitigation through cleaning methods. Using a controlled indoor setup with a light meter
 and sand-soiled mirrors, they tested various combinations of cleaning agents (deionized
 water, Windex, Rain-X) and tools (squeegee, microfiber towel, soft brush). Their results
 provided insight into the effects of different agents on reflectivity recovery, as well as
 wear-and-tear issues such as scratching. The study highlighted best practices for
 maintaining heliostat field performance, especially in arid environments where dust
 accumulation is a significant concern.

Together, these projects reflect the multidisciplinary nature of CSTP research—from optical
design and thermal simulation to system maintenance—and demonstrate the strong student-led
innovation taking place at Northeastern University.

Path Forward:
Moving forward, the foundation for this project has been fully institutionalized within
Northeastern University’s engineering curriculum. The graduate-level course Applications of
Concentrating Solar Thermal Technologies is now an approved offering in the Department of
Mechanical and Industrial Engineering and will continue to be available to students beyond the

A summary of the covered content is provided below:

In November 2024, Dr. Gregory Kowalski, Emeritus Professor at Northeastern University and
ASME Fellow, delivered a seminar in the HelioCon Series that highlighted a transformative
initiative to weave Concentrating Solar Power (CSP) technologies into Northeastern’s
engineering curriculum. Supported by Professors Hameed Metghalchi and Yiannis Levendis, the
effort sought to embed CSP concepts across undergraduate, graduate, and professional programs
so that students were exposed to system-level design, thermal performance analysis, economic
feasibility, and sustainability considerations.

At the heart of the initiative stood the graduate-level course Applications of Concentrated Solar
Power (ME5680). First piloted as a special-topics seminar in Spring 2024 and later approved for
official rollout in Spring 2025, the course adopted a seminar format with weekly student-led
discussions, critical readings, numerical modeling assignments, and problem sets. Using Gilles
Flamant’s textbook Concentrating Solar Thermal Energy: Fundamentals and Applications,
students simulated solar input, performed ray-tracing of heliostat fields, predicted receiver
efficiency, and integrated storage models; skills that prepared them for advanced roles in CSTP
system design.

 37

Academic instruction was complemented by extensive experiential learning. The initiative
leveraged Northeastern’s Capstone Senior Design program, LSAMP summer research, and
graduate project opportunities to ground CSTP concepts in hands-on projects. Capstone teams
designed a solar pyrolizer dish for converting forest debris into biochar, developed a deformable
heliostat mirror to correct optical aberrations, and constructed a wireless closed-loop solar-
tracking system. These projects addressed challenges such as precise azimuth-zenith tracking,
radiative heat-transfer optimization, and mirror cleaning strategies, thereby translating classroom
theory into functional prototypes.

Project outcomes proved promising. The solar pyrolizer reached 550 °C, validating biomass
conversion potential, while heliostat projects demonstrated flux-targeting improvements and
tractable cost-saving strategies. Several students—including Benjamin Lynch - parlayed their
work into a provisional patent, poster presentations, and entry into the Gordon Engineering
Leadership Program, underscoring the initiative’s professional impact.

To broaden outreach, the team designed a suite of five modular CSTP short courses, each
spanning roughly 25–28 hours and broken into 30–45 minute segments. A survey of 28
prospective participants guided content sequencing, which covered solar-resource modelling,
heliostat control, receiver and storage design, and solar-fuel production.

Industry-standard software such as SolarPILOT and SolTrace featured prominently, and each
workshop incorporated thermoeconomic case studies to ground theory in real-world decision-
making.

Course assessments revealed strong engagement - 100 percent homework submission and
sustained attendance - but identified weaknesses in ray tracing, geometric optics, and radiative
transfer. In response, future iterations were scheduled to include supplemental lectures and
strengthened numerical-methods instruction under the leadership of Prof. Umit Coskun.

Taken together, the seminar and associated curricular enhancements positioned Northeastern
University as an emerging hub for CSTP education and research. By fusing formal coursework,
project-based learning, and professional development workshops, the initiative cultivated a talent
pipeline equipped to advance high-temperature solar technologies and forged collaborations with
NREL, Sandia, and industry stakeholders -contributing meaningfully to the broader transition
toward sustainable energy systems.
