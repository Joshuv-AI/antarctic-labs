Vehicles

 Vehicles

Electric Vehicles

 Electric Vehicles

 • Electric vehicles (EVs) use electricity, which improves fuel economy and
 lowers fuel costs. (DOE, n.d.)
 • There are 4 types of EVs:
 ◦ Battery electric vehicles (BEVs)
 ◦ Plug-in hybrid electric vehicles (PHEVs)
 ◦ Hybrid electric vehicles (HEVs)
 ◦ Fuel Cell Electrics Vehicles (FCEVs)

                                                                              National Laboratory of the Rockies | 4

Types of EVs

 Types of EVs
BEV
 • Powered by an electric motor that uses energy stored in a battery.
 • The battery is charged by plugging the vehicle in to charging equipment.
 • Can travel about 100 to 400 miles on a single charge, depending on model and battery options.
PHEV
 • Powered by an internal combustion engine and an electric motor that uses energy stored in a battery.
 • Battery is charged by plugging the vehicle into charging equipment.
HEV
 • Powered by an internal combustion engine and an electric motor that uses energy stored in a battery.
 • The battery is charged through regenerative braking, not by plugging in.
FCEV
 • Powered by a propulsion system similar to that of electric vehicles, where energy stored as hydrogen is
 converted to electricity by the fuel cell.
 • Fueled with pure hydrogen gas stored in a tank on the vehicle.

Heavy Duty Fleet Electrification

 Heavy Duty Fleet Electrification
• Medium- and heavy-duty electric vehicles are being increasingly deployed due to increasing battery ranges,
  lower battery costs, reduced operating and maintenance expenses, and a competitively low cost of
  ownership.
• The shift from traditional diesel vehicles to heavy-duty electric trucks raises concerns about range
  limitations, insufficient recharging infrastructure, and recharge times.

Natural Gas Vehicles

 Natural Gas Vehicles
• Natural gas fuels more than 175,000 vehicles across the United States.
• Natural gas vehicles (NGVs) are commercially available in the United States.
• Natural gas used for transportation is available domestically and is supported
  by existing distribution infrastructure.
• NGVs are used for high-mileage, centrally fueled fleet operations, particularly
  in areas with access to compressed natural gas (CNG) fueling infrastructure.
• For vehicles that require an extended driving range, liquefied natural gas (LNG) Workers at the City and County of Denver Platte

• NGVs generally have a lower driving range than that of comparable gasoline or
  diesel vehicles.
• Increasing onboard fuel storage capacity can extend driving range, though
  additional tanks add weight and may reduce cargo capacity.

 Source: Alternative Fuels Data Center, Natural Gas Vehicles, n.d.

Types of NGVs

 Types of NGVs
• Heavy-duty NGVs, as well as many medium-duty models, are
  available from original equipment manufacturers (OEMs).
• NGVs are categorized into three configurations:
 ◦ Dedicated: Vehicles run exclusively on natural gas.
 ◦ Bi-fuel: Vehicles have two separate fuel systems and can operate
 on either natural gas or gasoline.
 ◦ Dual-fuel: Vehicles that operate primarily on natural gas with
 diesel fuel used for ignition assistance; typically limited to heavy-
 duty applications. Photo: GRTC Transit System

• Vehicles may also be converted for natural gas operation by
  qualified system retrofitters.
• Additional information about natural gas vehicles. Source: Alternative Fuels Data Center, Natural Gas Vehicles, n.d.

Propane

 Propane
• Propane is the third most common transportation
  fuel worldwide, behind gasoline and diesel.
• Also referred to as liquefied petroleum gas (LPG) or
  propane autogas.
• Propane is stored as a colorless, odorless liquid
  under pressure in a tank.
• When pressure is released, the liquid propane
  vaporizes into a gas that is used in combustion. Photo: Matthew Jeffers, NREL

 Source: Alternative Fuels Data Center, Propane Fuel Basics, n.d.

Fuel Cell Electric Vehicles

 Fuel Cell Electric Vehicles
• Fuel cell electric vehicles (FCEVs) use hydrogen, which can be
  produced from a variety of energy sources and is used in fuel cells to
  generate electricity.
• FCEVs have higher energy conversion efficiency than conventional
  internal combustion engine vehicles.
• Light-duty FCEVs can be refueled at retail stations in under 5 minutes
  and typically provide a driving range exceeding 300 miles.
• Medium- and heavy-duty FCEVs can accommodate bigger tanks,
  however, they may face weight limitations due U.S. Department of
  Transportation regulations that could reduce the total load potential.
• Light-duty FCEVs are commercially available in limited quantities.
 Photo by Dennis Schroeder, NREL

• The FCEV market is emerging for buses, marine vessels, ground
  support equipment, medium- and heavy-duty trucks, material
  handling equipment (e.g. forklifts), and stationary applications.
 Source: Alternative Fuels Data Center, Fuel Cell Electric Vehicles, n.d.

Aviation, Marine and Freight

 Aviation, Marine and Freight

Aviation Fuel

 Aviation Fuel
 • Sustainable Aviation Fuel (SAF) is produced from non-petroleum feedstocks.
 • Feedstocks for SAF include fats, greases, oils, food and yard waste from municipal
 solid waste, woody biomass, and other feedstocks.
 • SAF can be blended, with limits, with conventional jet fuel at levels ranging from
 10% to 50%, depending on the feedstock and the production process.
 • SAF, once blended with Jet A, meets ASTM D1655 and is used in existing aircraft
 and infrastructure.
 • According to the International Civil Aviation Organization, over 360,000 commercial
 flights have used SAF at 46 airports.
 • There are 6 domestic SAF production facilities

 Source: Alternative Fuels Data Center, Sustainable Aviation Fuel, n.d.

Renewable Diesel

 Renewable Diesel
 • Renewable diesel is produced from fats and oils, such as waste greases and animal fats and
 soybean oil, and is processed to be chemically equivalent to petroleum diesel.

 • Renewable diesel can be used as a replacement for petroleum diesel or blended with
 petroleum diesel.

 • Renewable diesel offers many benefits, including:

 ◦ Engine and infrastructure compatibility—Renewable diesel meets the ASTM D975
 specification, which allows it to be used in diesel engines and existing infrastructure.

 ◦ Greater flexibility—Renewable diesel is a replacement for diesel. It can be blended with
 any amount of diesel or can fully replace diesel. Renewable diesel can be made from
 multiple feedstocks, and can be made at plants that also produce aviation fuel.

 Source: Alternative Fuels Data Center, Renewable Diesel, n.d.

                                                                                                           National Laboratory of the Rockies | 28

Biodiesel Fuel

 Biodiesel Fuel
 • Biodiesel is a biodegradable fuel made from animal fats, vegetable oils, or recycled cooking oil (ASTM
                   D6751) (NREL, 2024)
 • Biodiesel is manufactured domestically and is a substitute for petroleum diesel.
 • Biodiesel and conventional diesel vehicles are the same. Although light-, medium-, and heavy-duty diesel
 vehicles are not alternative fuel vehicles, almost all can run on biodiesel blends.
 ◦ Before using biodiesel, check your engine original equipment manufacturer (OEM) recommendations to
 determine the allowable blend for your vehicle
 • Biodiesel fueling stations are available across the United States.
 ◦ Some state laws require petroleum diesel to contain a small percentage of biodiesel.
 • Using biodiesel as a vehicle fuel improves public health and the environment, provides safety benefits, and
 contributes to a resilient transportation system.
 ◦ In its pure, unblended form biodiesel causes less damage than petroleum diesel if spilled or released to
 the environment.
 ◦ It is less combustible than petroleum diesel.
 ◦ Biodiesel can improve lubricity of diesel fuel, reduces friction within the moving parts and avoiding
 additional wear. Source: Alternative Fuels Data Center, Biodiesel Fuel Basics, n.d.

Considerations

 Considerations
• What are your transportation requirements? What duty cycles or
  cargo/passenger capacity needs must be met?
• What are vehicle, fuel and infrastructure costs and what incentives are
  available? Have you considered both up-front and longer-term costs/savings
  opportunities?
• What infrastructure is needed to support fueling and/or charging?
• Where would fueling and/or charging stations be built?
• What education may be needed to support alternative-fuel based vehicles
  and EVs and related infrastructure?
