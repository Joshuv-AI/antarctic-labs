 ABSTRACT

 This work focuses on the development and implementation of strategies to accelerate thermochemical
calculations within Multiphysics Object-Oriented Simulation Environment (MOOSE)-based multiphysics
simulations, particularly for applications in molten salt reactors (MSRs). We highlight the inherent com-
plexity of nuclear materials, which require a multiscale approach to accurately model their behavior across
various physical domains, including mechanical, chemical, and thermal phenomena. Thermochemical
equilibrium calculations are crucial for predicting material properties and enhancing the fidelity of these
simulations. The integration of Thermochimica, a Gibbs energy minimizer, into MOOSE allows for the
direct minimization of Gibbs energy at every point on the mesh. However, the computational cost of such
integration is significant. To address this, we explored acceleration strategies such as multi-threading
support and the use of a thermodynamic ValueCache to reduce redundant calculations. Additionally, we
investigated modifications to Thermochimica to enable phase constraints and improve its coupling with
phase-field models, which are essential for simulating microstructural evolution and corrosion in MSRs.
These efforts aim to optimize the computational efficiency and accuracy of multiphysics simulations,
thereby supporting the development of reliable and efficient nuclear materials for next-generation reactor
technologies.

 1. INTRODUCTION

 Nuclear materials exhibit inherently complex behavior due to the interplay of multiple physical phenomena
across a wide range of spatial and temporal scales. Accurately capturing their performance necessitates
a multiscale modeling and simulation framework that integrates diverse physics domains. These include
mechanical processes such as dislocation dynamics, fracture, and stress-driven diffusion; chemical phenomena
like corrosion, oxidation, and reactive transport; and thermal effects encompassing heat conduction, phase
transformations, and species migration. Each of these mechanisms contributes to the overall material response
and must be considered in a coupled manner to achieve predictive fidelity [1].
 The multiscale modeling paradigm seeks to bridge atomistic, mesoscopic, and macroscopic scales by
systematically transferring information from lower to higher length scales. This hierarchical approach enables
the incorporation of fundamental mechanisms — such as defect evolution and microstructural changes — into
continuum-level models, thereby enhancing the predictive capability of simulations. In the context of nuclear
materials, such an approach is essential for understanding degradation mechanisms, optimizing performance,
and ensuring long-term reliability under extreme operating conditions.
 Equilibrium calculations, grounded in the fundamental laws of thermodynamics, utilize system-specific
information, namely, the Gibbs energy functions of all possible species, along with temperature, pressure, and
overall composition, to determine key thermodynamic properties. These include the stable phases and species
present at equilibrium, the total Gibbs energy of the system, and the chemical potentials of the constituents.
Additionally, such computations enable the derivation of other thermophysical properties, such as enthalpy
and heat capacity, which are essential for accurate materials modeling.
 The prediction of material properties is intrinsically linked to the equilibrium phase assemblage. In
the absence of a thermochemistry solver, these properties are often estimated using empirical correlations
derived from experimental data or simplified polynomial approximations. However, such approximations are
typically valid only within the specific conditions under which they were developed and can fail to capture
certain behaviors, limiting their applicability and potentially compromising the fidelity of multiphysics
simulations under varying conditions. Thermochemical equilibrium calculations address this limitation by
enabling property predictions across a wide range of physical states, thereby enhancing the predictive accuracy
of simulation frameworks. For instance, the phase-field model requires knowledge of the Gibbs energies,
chemical potentials, and thermodynamic driving forces associated with the phases present in the system. At
phase interfaces, where multiple phases may coexist, phase-field models face challenges in estimating the
local phase assemblage. In such scenarios, a thermochemistry solver provides critical input to the phase-field
module, ensuring thermodynamic consistency and improving the robustness of the simulation.
 MSRs are gaining prominence as a next-generation nuclear technology due to their inherent safety features,
high operating temperatures (approximately 700 ◦ C), and potential for reduced nuclear waste generation
[2]. However, the realization of MSRs at scale hinges on the development of structural materials capable of
withstanding the extreme chemical and thermal environments within the reactor core. These include challenges
such as corrosion and precipitation of by-products in the colder regions of the salt loop, which can impede
fluid flow and compromise reactor efficiency [3]. Addressing these issues requires high-fidelity multiphysics
modeling tools that can simulate both materials degradation and reactor safety phenomena. Several modeling
frameworks based on the MOOSE have been developed to this end [4]. For instance, phase-field methods are

 1

employed to capture microstructural evolution and corrosion at the mesoscale, Poisson-Nernst-Planck (PNP)
models are used for macroscale corrosion and deposition processes, and Pronghorn is utilized for species
transport and off-gassing simulations [5, 6]. These models often rely on thermodynamic data from the Molten
Salt Thermal Properties Database-Thermochemical (MSTDB-TC), with Thermochimica serving as the Gibbs
energy minimizer [7] .
 Despite its utility, the direct coupling of Thermochimica with MOOSE-based tools presents significant
computational challenges, limiting the scalability of multiphysics simulations. This work investigates ac-
celeration strategies to reduce the computational burden of such coupling without compromising accuracy.
Specifically, we implement a data-partitioning tree to cache previously computed results and interpolate
thermodynamic properties for nearby compositions, thereby reducing redundant calculations. Additionally,
the current implementation of Thermochimica lacks the capability to impose phase constraints, rendering it
unsuitable for direct integration with phase-field models. To address this limitation, we explore modifications
to Thermochimica that enable its coupling with phase-field simulations in MOOSE, thereby broadening its
applicability in modeling complex materials behavior in MSRs.

 1.2. Thermochimica Integration in MOOSE
 The open-source Gibbs energy minimizer Thermochimica was previously integrated into the chemical
reactions module of MOOSE. This coupling enabled direct minimization of Gibbs energy at every point
on the mesh and allowed thermodynamic databases such as the Molten Salt Thermodynamic Database -
Thermochemical (MSTDB-TC). This coupling was achieved through the user-facing ChemicalComposition
action and Thermochimica-facing ThermochimicaData user object template class.

1.2.1. MOOSE Objects for Thermochimica Calculations

 The ChemicalComposition action in the MOOSE framework serves as the user interface for initializing
GEM capabilities in a finite element (FE) / finite volume (FV) simulation. This action is responsible for
configuring the Thermochimica environment by creating necessary simulation variables, reading the thermo-
dynamic database associated with the material system, and setting consistent units for temperature, pressure,
and elemental composition. It optionally allows users to define initial elemental amounts and mandates
the specification of chemical phases and species whose concentrations are to be tracked and output. These
user-defined entities are checked against the thermodynamic database to ensure consistency. Furthermore, the
action automatically generates all auxiliary variables required for the output of key thermodynamic quantities,
including phase concentrations, chemical potentials, and vapor pressures.

 2. THERMOCHEMISTRY ACCELERATION

 Gibbs energy minimization using Thermochimica inside nonlinear, tightly-coupled MOOSE solves can
rapidly become a performance bottleneck. This is a direct consequence of the global optimization problem
that must be solved to compute the thermodynamic equilibrium. The size of the Hessian matrix used in Gibbs
energy minimization [7, 16] is the sum of all number of system components, number of solution phases present
and number of stoichiometric phases present, i.e. C + Λ +  Ω and the optimization time scales cubically
with the number of system components in the system O C3 . This is illustrated in Figure 1 which shows the
change in average wall-clock times for Thermochimica calculations as the number of system components, in
this case the chemical elements, in the system changes.

 In MOOSE based solves, the Gibbs energy minimization must be performed at every node or elements
depending on the formulation being used which can very rapidly increase the computational requirements.
Furthermore, in transient solves, the calculations must also be repeated at every time step making simulations
with directly coupled Thermochimica solves prohibitively expensive. This section describes the acceleration
methods for thermodynamic equilibrium being implemented in MOOSE and their application in Pronghorn
simulations of off-gassing in MSR.

 2.1. Thermochimica multi-threading support
 In MOOSE-based multiphysics simulations that incorporate thermodynamic calculations, the evaluation of
thermochemical equilibria via Thermochimica often constitutes a significant portion of the total computational
cost. One conventional strategy to mitigate this overhead is parallelization using the Message Passing Interface
(MPI), which distributes the workload across material points. However, this approach is constrained by
the scalability limitations of certain preconditioners, particularly those based on direct solvers such as LU
decomposition, which are inefficient for problems with a small number of degrees of freedom or require an
increased number of nonlinear iterations, thereby amplifying the number of Thermochimica calls. In such
scenarios, thread-level parallelism presents a more promising alternative. While nonlinear solves in MOOSE
remain parallelized over MPI ranks, material property evaluations can be distributed across lightweight threads,
potentially improving performance without incurring the overhead associated with MPI communication.
 Thermochimica, however, is not inherently thread-safe due to its legacy Fortran architecture, which relies
on global variables for data sharing across subroutines. In a multi-threaded context, this shared memory model
leads to race conditions and data corruption, as concurrent threads overwrite each other’s state variables. To
enable thread-level parallelism while preserving correctness, we developed a process-based workaround. Each
thread spawns a dedicated child process using the POSIX fork() system call, thereby creating an isolated
memory space for each Thermochimica instance. Modern operating systems implement a copy-on-write
mechanism, ensuring that memory duplication is minimal unless modifications occur, thus keeping the
memory overhead low.
 Communication between the parent thread and its corresponding child process is facilitated through

 6

a shared memory buffer (allocated via mmap()) and a bidirectional UNIX domain socket (created using
socketpair()). When a thermodynamic calculation is required, the parent thread writes the input parame-
ters—temperature, pressure, and composition—into the shared memory and signals the child process via the
socket. The child process reads the input, invokes Thermochimica, and writes the results back to the shared
memory before signaling completion. This architecture not only enables safe and efficient multi-threaded
execution of Thermochimica but also supports block-restricted simulations with distinct thermodynamic
databases. For instance, one region of a simulation may require molten salt speciation, while another may
involve solid-phase equilibria in an evolving alloy system—each handled independently by separate Ther-
mochimica instances.
 This capability was originally developed in FY-2024 and documented in the milestone report titled
Integration of Thermochimica into MOOSE-based Molten Salt Reactor Loop Simulations [6]. It is briefly
summarized here for completeness, as the underlying approach offers a viable alternative for accelerating
multiphysics simulations in scenarios where the frequency or distribution of Thermochimica calls may not
align well with the acceleration strategy proposed in this work.

 2.2. Thermodynamic ValueCache
 To address the computational bottleneck associated with repeated thermodynamic equilibrium calculations
in MOOSE-based multiphysics simulations, we implemented a caching strategy that reuses previously
computed Thermochimica results when subsequent input states are sufficiently similar. This approach
leverages a k-dimensional tree (k-d tree) data structure to organize and query the thermodynamic state space
efficiently. When a new thermodynamic calculation is requested, the k-d tree is queried for the nearest neighbor
in the input space—defined by temperature, pressure, and composition (called thermodynamic space in the
subsequent discussion). If the Euclidean distance to the nearest neighbor falls within a user-defined tolerance
radius, the cached result is reused directly, thereby bypassing the need for a full Gibbs energy minimizer
(GEM). This mechanism significantly reduces the number of expensive Thermochimica calls, particularly in
simulations where the thermodynamic state varies smoothly across the spatial domain.
 In cases where the nearest neighbor is found but lies outside the specified tolerance, the cached result is
still utilized as an initial guess for the new Thermochimica calculation. This reinitialization strategy has been
shown to accelerate convergence of the nonlinear solver within Thermochimica, as the starting point is often
close to the final equilibrium state. Consequently, even when a full solve is required, the computational cost is
reduced relative to a cold start. This dual benefit, bypass in some cases and accelerated convergence in others,
makes the caching approach broadly effective across a range of simulation scenarios.
 The caching mechanism has been implemented in MOOSE through the ValueCache class, which manages
the storage and retrieval of thermodynamic results. The behavior of this system is illustrated in Figure 2.
At the first evaluation point on the finite element mesh (green dot), the cache is empty, necessitating a full
Thermochimica solve. The result is then stored in the cache. At a subsequent point (blue dot), a nearest
neighbor is found but lies outside the user-defined tolerance. In this case, the cached result is used to initialize
the new calculation. As the simulation progresses and the cache becomes more populated, the likelihood of
finding a sufficiently close neighbor increases. When such a neighbor is found (orange dot), the cached result
is reused directly, and the Thermochimica call is entirely avoided.

 The effectiveness of the ValueCache has been evaluated across a number of test problems designed to
mimic realistic thermodynamic variations in MSR simulations. A key parameter governing the trade-off
between accuracy and performance is the user-defined tolerance for nearest neighbor matching. Smaller
tolerances yield higher fidelity by reducing the approximation error introduced by reusing cached results, but
at the cost of increased computational effort due to more frequent full equilibrium calculations. Conversely,
larger tolerances enhance performance by maximizing cache reuse, albeit with a potential loss in accuracy.
This tunable parameter allows users to balance computational efficiency against thermodynamic precision
based on the specific requirements of their simulation.

2.2.1. k-Nearest Neighbors and Barycentric Interpolation

 The effectiveness of a single nearest-neighbor-based caching strategy is inherently constrained by the
structure of the thermodynamic phase space. In particular, when the output phase information is not explicitly
known or encoded, reliance on a single nearest neighbor can lead to erroneous predictions especially in regions
proximate to phase boundaries. In such cases, the nearest neighbor in the input space (i.e., temperature, pressure
and composition) may reside in a different phase region than the query point, resulting in a discontinuous or
physically inconsistent estimate of the thermodynamic property.

 In the context of thermodynamic equilibrium prediction, the k-nearest neighbors (k-NN) algorithm offers
a data-driven, non-parametric interpolation strategy for estimating thermodynamic properties at unsampled
state points. By identifying the k closest reference points in a multidimensional space defined by intensive and
extensive variables (e.g., temperature, pressure, composition), k-NN computes property estimates as a weighted
average of the neighboring values, typically using inverse-distance weighting. This method is particularly
advantageous in scenarios where the thermodynamic landscape is irregular or exhibits discontinuities, as it does
not impose a predefined functional form. However, its performance is sensitive to the local data density and
the choice of distance metric, which can influence both accuracy and smoothness of the interpolated surface.
Barycentric interpolation provides a geometric approach leveraging the state-space. The thermodynamic
property is interpolated using barycentric coordinates, which represent the relative contribution of each vertex
of the simplex to the query point. This approach ensures continuity and local linearity, making it particularly
suitable for high-dimensional interpolation tasks.

 3. PHASE-FIELD COUPLING

 The phase-field model has been widely used in literature to model the evolution in processes such as
solidification, grain growth, and phase separation [17–19] and the MOOSE phase field module is widely
adopted for simplifying the implementation of simulation tools that employ the phase field model. By
combining phase-field models with Gibbs energy minimization (GEM) softwares, we can employ sophisticated
optimization strategies to improve the simulation of realistic systems [20]. Thermochimica can compute the
thermodynamics quantities of interest for the elemental composition and phase-fractions supplied by the
phase-field model. Such coupling, however, requires additional constraints to be imposed during optimization.
Figure 5 shows a schematic comparing the equations solved in standalone GEM versus the coupled solve.
Specifically, an additional constraint is imposed that enforces the phase-fractions in the GEM solve to be
equal to the phase-field phase-fraction values. Thermochimica then needs to solve for the parallel tangent

condition on all possible phases in the system while enforcing this constraint. The GEM returns the element
potentials, mole fractions of species, and driving forces for phase evolution to the phase-field model.

i.e. gchem in equations (13) and (14).

 In this section, we detail the development of an approach for directly coupling Thermochimica with the
MOOSE phase field module followed by thermodynamics surrogates for phase-field models of molten salt
corrosion. The readers are referred to the companion report [21] for additional details on the phase-field
corrosion model theory and implementation. We provide a brief description of the model here for context and
primarily focus on the electrochemical aspects of the thermodynamics and the surrogate development work.

 3.1. Phase-field theory

microstructural features such as grains and phases are represented using continuous non-conserved variables
called order parameters (OPs), and component atomic fractions are represented using conserved variable
fields. The evolution of these continuous variables is a function of the Gibbs energy and can be defined as a
system of partial differential equations (PDEs). Thus, to define the kinetics of the system, the Gibbs energy
must be described as a function of the continuous variables representing the microstructure [22].

benefit of switching to a surrogate model based approach is the potential to leverage modern computing
paradigms such as use of graphics processing units (GPUs) and tensor computing libraries such LibTorch.

 3.3. Gibbs Energy Surrogate for Phase-Field
 The computational cost of direct coupling between a phase-field model and Thermochimica limits the
solve to 1D simulations with only a few elements. To improve the computational efficiency of these simulations,
we developed a pipeline for training neural network surrogates on the thermodynamics using PyTorch. The
schematic for the pipeline is shown in Fig. 6. In the future, these surrogates can be used to replace the
simplified energy approximations needed in the current molten salt corrosion phase-field [23].

Figure 6. Multistep approach for training Gibbs energy surrogates for Fe-Ni-Cr system in FCC alloy and
MSFL phases.

 To model the thermodynamics of the corrosion of a Fe – Ni – Cr alloy in molten LiF – BeF2 (FLiBe) would
typically involve a GEM solve on a 6 element system. To simplify the phase-field solve, we typically represent
the system thermodynamics for only the specific elements of interest (Fe – Ni – Cr). On the alloy side, we
use the face-centered cubic (FCC)-phase Computer Coupling of Phase Diagrams and Thermochemistry
(CALPHAD) description of thermodynamics of the Fe – Ni – Cr system from literature assessments [24].
 To obtain the thermodynamics of the molten salt, we used Thermochimica to calculate the Gibbs energy
of the Fe – Ni – Cr – Li – Be – F system, such that the system always contains the right number of moles of
elements to create a mixture of x FeF2 , y NiF2 , z CrF2 , (2 LiF + BeF2 ). This ensures one mole of the Li2 BeF4
molten salt, with the moles of the structural element fluorides varying between 10−8 –21 moles. The lower
limit was based on the threshold below which Thermochimica considers the moles of an element to be zero,
and the upper limit was set to get a mole fraction of the metal impurity in the salt of upto 0.3. A total of
3001 points were sampled in this range, with 1000 points sampled logarithmically to capture the extrema
of the data range and 2000 points sampled with a uniform random distribution to capture the center of the
data range. The last point was specifically pure Li2 BeF4 , used as the reference energy of the salt system. The
Gibbs energy of the system was calculated for all these points using Thermochimica’s CalculationList
mode, at a temperature of 873.15 K and a pressure of 1 atm.

 4. CONCLUSION

 This work presents an approach for accelerating thermochemical equilibrium calculations in MOOSE-
based multiphysics simulations, with a focus on molten salt reactor applications. By integrating Thermochim-
ica and implementing multi-threading and caching strategies, we significantly reduce the computational burden
of Gibbs energy minimization. The development of a thermodynamic ValueCache and the use of k-nearest
neighbor interpolation further enhance performance, particularly in large-scale simulations. Additionally,
