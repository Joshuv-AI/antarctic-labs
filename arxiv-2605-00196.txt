Modeling Stock Returns and Volatility Using Bivariate Gamma Generalized Laplace Law
Tomasz J. Kozubowski, Andrey Sarantsev, James A. Spiker

Abstract
. We consider a generalization of the variance-gamma (generalized asymmetric Laplace) distribution, defined as a normal mean - variance mixture with a gamma mixing distribution. While this model is typically studied in the univariate setting, we assume that the gamma mixing variable is observed alongside the primary variable, resulting in a bivariate framework. In this setting, maximum likelihood estimation becomes significantly simpler than in the standard univariate case, reducing to a form of classical linear regression. We derive explicit expressions for the resulting estimators. For certain parameter configurations, the estimators exhibit nonstandard convergence rates, exceeding the usual square-root rate. Finally, we illustrate the applicability of this model in financial contexts by analyzing stock index returns and associated volatility for several major indices.

1.
Introduction

The Laplace distribution with location parameter
\delta\in\mathbb{R}
and variance
\sigma^{2}>0
, given by the probability density function (PDF)

(1.1)

f(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\sigma}\exp\left(-\frac{\sqrt{2}|x-\delta|}{\sigma}\right),\quad x\in\mathbb{R},

along with its numerous generalizations, has played an increasingly important role in probability and applied modeling over the past several decades (see, e.g.,
[
10
]
). This distribution is a special case of a
mean–variance Gaussian mixture
model: a Laplace random variable
Y
with the PDF in (
1.1
) admits the stochastic representation

(1.2)

Y=\delta+\mu X+\sigma\sqrt{X}Z,

where
\mu=0
,
Z
is standard normal, and
X
is standard exponential, independent of
Z
. Allowing
\mu\neq 0
yields the more flexible Asymmetric Laplace (AL) family (see, e.g.,
[
10
]
), which in turn generalizes naturally to the Generalized Asymmetric Laplace (GAL) distribution (see, e.g.,
[
11
]
) when
X
follows a gamma distribution. Since a GAL random variable can be viewed as Gaussian with a stochastic, gamma distributed variance (and mean), this model is also known as the
variance gamma
distribution, introduced in
[
13
]
in a financial context (see also the survey
[
7
]
). Historically, however, its roots reach much earlier: as discussed in
[
11
]
, it appeared already in 1929 as the distribution of the empirical correlation coefficient based on bivariate Gaussian samples
[
16
]
.

The GAL family enjoys several attractive properties: Finite moments of all orders and a moment generating function finite in a neighborhood of zero (see, e.g.,
[
7
]
). The parameter
\mu
controls skewness (
\mu=0
implies symmetry), while the shape parameter
\alpha
of the mixing gamma distribution governs kurtosis. Together with location and scale, these four parameters allow the model to match arbitrary mean, variance, skewness, and kurtosis, offering much greater flexibility than the classical normal family.

This flexibility makes the GAL distribution especially useful in finance, where asset returns often exhibit heavier than Gaussian tails
[
10
, Section 8.4]
,
[
18
]
. A common interpretation models stock prices as geometric Brownian motion evolving in business time: A nonlinear, random time scale represented by a nondecreasing Lévy subordinator that accelerates with market activity. This leads to a subordinated Brownian motion, or
Laplace motion
, whose increments follow an (asymmetric) Laplace law (see
[
6
]
and
[
10
, Section 8.4]
). While univariate mean-variance Gaussian mixtures with conditional distribution

(1.3)

Y\mid X\sim\mathcal{N}(\delta+\mu X,\sigma^{2}X),

with
X
distributed as Gamma, have been extensively studied (see, e.g.,
[
2
]
) and have found broad application in hierarchical modeling, Bayesian inference, stochastic processes, clustering, and machine learning, among others, the bivariate extension (where
X
and
Y
arise jointly as dependent random components) appears to have received relatively little attention.

In this work, we address this gap by extending the GAL model to a bivariate setting. Specifically, we introduce a joint distribution for
(X,Y)
where
Y
follows (
1.2
) with
X
gamma distributed and independent of
Z
. We refer to this new family as the
Bivariate Gamma-Generalized Laplace
(BGGL) distribution. Compared with the univariate GAL model, the maximum likelihood estimators (MLEs) of the BGGL parameters are remarkably tractable: in the bivariate formulation, the MLEs of
\mu,\delta
, and
\sigma
correspond to those from the classical least squares regression (without intercept), while the MLEs for the gamma parameters of
X
are classical and available in closed form. Below are our main contributions:

•

We derive explicit expressions for the estimators and establish their asymptotic properties as
n\to\infty
. In Theorem
4.1
we prove asymptotic normality for
\alpha>1
, while Theorems
4.7
and
4.3
reveal a striking phenomenon: for
\alpha\leq 1
, the MLE converges at a faster than classical
\sqrt{n}
rate. These are the principal results of the paper.

•

It is well known that the Fisher information may be infinite when the underlying density becomes unbounded or irregular (as, for example, in the uniform distribution on
[0,\theta]
). The BGGL family exhibits a related phenomenon - it is a curved exponential family: although parameterized by five parameters, its sufficient statistics span a six-dimensional space. We demonstrate this property.

•

We also explore a financial application, where
Y
represents stock market returns (e.g., S&P 500) and
X
is derived from the Volatility Index (VIX). This setting provides a natural modification of
[
15
]
, incorporating a stochastic volatility model for the time series
(V,X)
.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
introduces the BGGL model and its fundamental properties, including the joint PDF, MGF, characteristic function, connections to Lévy processes, exponential family representation, Shannon entropy, and Fisher information. Section
3
presents the MLE framework. The results on the asymptotic distributions of the MLEs are presented in Section
4
. Simulations confirming the distinct convergence behavior for
\alpha\leq 1
versus
\alpha>1
are included in Section
5
. Section
6
illustrates a financial application. Detailed proofs and auxiliary results appear in the Appendix.

2.
Bivariate Gamma Generalized Laplace Distribution: Basic Properties

In this section we formally define the BGGL distribution and account for its basic properties. The random variable
X
in the definition below follows gamma distribution with shape parameter
\alpha>0
and scale parameter
\beta>0
, denoted by
\mathrm{GAM}(\alpha,\beta)
and given by the PDF

(2.1)

f(x)=\frac{x^{\alpha-1}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\beta^{\alpha}e^{-\beta x},\quad x>0.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
Y
admit the stochastic representation (
1.2
), where
X\sim\mathrm{GAM}(\alpha,\beta)
and
Z\sim N(0,1)
(standard normal), independent of
X
. Then, the random vector
(X,Y)
is said to have the BGGL distribution with vector-parameter
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\alpha,\beta,\delta,\mu,\sigma)
. We denote this distribution by
\mathrm{BGGL}(\alpha,\beta,\delta,\mu,\sigma)
and write
(X,Y)\sim\mathrm{BGGL}(\alpha,\beta,\delta,\mu,\sigma)
.

The notation BGGL stands for
B
ivariate distribution with
G
amma and
G
eneralized
L
aplace marginals. Indeed, by construction, the marginal distribution of
X
in this bivariate model is gamma distribution. On the other hand, the distribution of
Y
is GAL (
G
eneralized
A
symmetric
L
aplace), with the PDF given by (see
[
10
, (4.1.10), (4.1.30)]
or
[
7
, (1.1), (2.18)]
):

(2.2)

f_{Y}(y)=\frac{\sqrt{2}e^{\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2\tilde{\sigma}}(\frac{1}{\kappa}-\kappa)(y-\delta)}}{\sqrt{\pi}\tilde{\sigma}^{\alpha+\frac{1}{2}}\Gamma(\alpha)}\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}|y-\delta|}{\kappa+\frac{1}{\kappa}}\right)^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}}K_{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2\tilde{\sigma}}\left(\frac{1}{\kappa}+\kappa\right)|y-\delta|\right),\quad y\in\mathbb{R},

where
K_{s}(\cdot)
is the modified Bessel function of the second kind with index
s
. The definition of
K_{s}
is taken from
[
7
, Appendix]
, or can be found in
[
14
, Chapter 10]
; see the Appendix of this article. In (
2.2
), we let

(2.3)

\tilde{\sigma}=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{\beta}},\,\,\,\tilde{\mu}=\frac{\mu}{\beta},\,\,\,\kappa=\frac{\sqrt{2}\tilde{\sigma}}{\tilde{\mu}+\sqrt{2\tilde{\sigma}^{2}+\tilde{\mu}^{2}}}>0.

Note that while the BGGL distribution is driven by five parameters, the one-dimensional variance-gamma distribution of
Y
in (
1.2
) has only four parameters: the parameters
\sigma
and
\beta
will not be identifiable. The main properties of GAL distributions are well-known - see, for example, the survey
[
7
]
and references therein. This random variable has finite moments of all orders, and its moment generating function (MGF) is finite in a neighborhood of zero. The parameter
\mu
is responsible for skewness: if
\mu=0
then the distribution is symmetric. The parameter
\alpha
is called the
shape
and is responsible for the kurtosis. The four parameters allow to pick any mean, variance, skewness and kurtosis, thus giving more flexibility than the classic normal family of distributions. However, as seen above, the density (
2.2
) is complicated as it contains the Bessel special function. The exception is the case
\alpha=1
, where we obtain AL distribution with an explicit form of the PDF (see, e.g.,
[
10
, (3.0.8)]
. We can then explicitly compute the maximum likelihood estimation (MLE), see the monograph
[
10
, Section 3.5]
.

2.1.
The joint density

By (
1.3
), the conditional density of
Y\mid X=x
is Gaussian:

f(y\mid x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi x}\sigma}\exp\left[-\frac{(y-\delta-\mu x)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}x}\right].

Straightforward algebra shows that the
joint density of
(X,Y)
takes on an explicit form as follows, for
x\geq 0
and
y\in\mathbb{R}
:

(2.4)

\displaystyle f(x,y)=f(y\mid x)f(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{1}{\sigma}\frac{\beta^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}x^{\alpha-3/2}\exp\left[-\left(\beta x+\frac{(y-\delta-\mu x)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}x}\right)\right].

This can also be written in the form:

(2.5)

f(x,y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{1}{\sigma}\frac{\beta^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}x^{\alpha-3/2}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\frac{\mu^{2}+2\beta\sigma^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}x+\frac{(y-\delta)^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\frac{1}{x}\right\}+\frac{\mu(y-\delta)}{\sigma^{2}}\right],

which aids in finding the conditional distribution of
X
given
Y=y
. Indeed, a close examination of the PDF in (
2.5
) shows that this conditional distribution is a
generalized inverse Gaussian
(GIG) distribution with parameters

(2.6)

a=2\beta+\frac{\mu^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\geq 0,\quad b=\frac{(y-\delta)^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\geq 0,\quad p=\alpha-1/2\in\mathbb{R},

denoted by
\mathrm{GIG}(a,b,p)
and given by the PDF

(2.7)

f(x)=\frac{(a/b)^{p/2}}{2K_{p}(\sqrt{ab})}x^{p-1}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left(ax+\frac{b}{x}\right)},\quad x\geq 0.

This fact is crucial in setting-up an estimation algorithm for the parameters of GAL distribution via EM optimization scheme.

2.2.
The integral transforms

The bivariate moment generating function (MGF) of the BGGL distribution is given by

(2.8)

\displaystyle M(s,t)=\mathbb{E}\left(e^{sX+tY}\right)=e^{\delta t}M_{X}(s+\mu t+0.5\sigma^{2}t^{2}),

where
M_{X}(u)=\mathbb{E}[e^{uX}]
is the MGF of
X

(see Proposition 1 in
[
20
]
). It is well known, see for example
[
5
, Appendix B, page 526, Problem 4]
, that
M_{X}(u)=(\beta/(\beta-u))^{\alpha}
,
u<\beta
. This leads to the following result.

Proposition 2.1
.

The joint moment generating function of
(X,Y)
is given by:

(2.9)

\displaystyle\begin{split}M(s,t)&=e^{\delta t}\beta^{\alpha}\left[\beta-(s+\mu t+0.5\sigma^{2}t^{2})\right]^{-\alpha},\\
(s,t)&\in D:=\{(s,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:s+\mu t+0.5\sigma^{2}t^{2}<\beta\}.\end{split}

The domain
D
of the MGF in (
2.9
) contains a neighborhood of zero. From here we can see that
\mathbb{E}[X^{k}|Y|^{l}]<\infty
for all
k,l\geq 0
. We could also compute these moments as partial derivatives of the MGF (
2.9
) at
s=t=0
:

(2.10)

\mathbb{E}[X^{k}Y^{l}]=\left.\frac{\partial^{k+l}M}{\partial s^{k}\partial t^{l}}\right|_{s=t=0}.

But it seems to us easier to compute them from scratch using the representation (
1.2
), which we do in the following subsection. The characteristic function (Fourier transform of joint density (
2.5
) in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
) is immediately found from (
2.9
), with
\mathrm{i}=\sqrt{-1}
:

\varphi(s,t):=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\mathrm{i}(sX+tY)}\right]=M(\mathrm{i}s,\mathrm{i}t)=e^{\mathrm{i}\delta t}\beta^{\alpha}\left[\beta-\mathrm{i}s-\mathrm{i}\mu t+0.5\sigma^{2}t^{2}\right]^{-\alpha}.

The BGGL distribution is infinitely divisible, since for any
n\in\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,\ldots\}
the function
\varphi^{1/n}(s,t)
is also a characteristic function (of a BGGL distribution with parameters
\alpha
and
\delta
changed to
\alpha/n
and
\delta/n
, respectively). In other words,
(X,Y)\sim\mathrm{BGGL}(\alpha,\beta,\delta,\mu,\sigma)
can be represented as a sum of independent and identically distributed (IID) BGGL random variables as follows:

(X,Y)=(X_{1},Y_{1})+\cdots+(X_{n},Y_{n}),\quad\mbox{IID}\quad(X_{i},Y_{i})\sim\mathrm{BGGL}(\alpha/n,\beta,\delta/n,\mu,\sigma).

2.3.
Two-dimensional Lévy process

Since the BGGL distribution is infinitely divisible,
one can define a two-dimensional Lévy process with this bivariate distribution. Let
G=(G(t),t\geq 0)
be a subordinator (a nondecreasing Lévy process) built on gamma distribution

\textsc{GAM}(\alpha,\beta)
with shape
\alpha
and rate
\beta
, so that

G(t)-G(s)\sim\Gamma(\alpha(t-s),\beta),\quad t>s\geq 0.

Further, let
W=(W(t),\,t\geq 0)
be a Brownian motion with drift
\mu
and diffusion
\sigma^{2}
, independent of
G
, so that

W(t)-W(s)\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu(t-s),\sigma^{2}(t-s)),\quad t>s\geq 0.

Then the following two-dimensional Lévy process has BGGL increments:

(2.11)

\{{\bf Y}(t),\,t\geq 0\}=\{\bigl(G(t),W(G(t))\bigr),\,t\geq 0\}.

Indeed,
\bigl(G(t),W(G(t))\bigr)-\bigl(G(s),W(G(s))\bigr)\sim\mathrm{BGGL}(\alpha(t-s),\beta,0,\mu,\sigma^{2})
.
This generalizes the construction of the (symmetric or asymmetric) Laplace motion
W(G(t))
(the second component of (
2.11
)) see
[
10
, Section 4.2]
, especially
[
10
, Theorem 4.2.1, (4.2.2)]
. For the background, see
[
7
, Section 4.2]
.

2.4.
Moments

The joint moments
\mathbb{E}[X^{k}Y^{l}]
, where
(X,Y)
follows a BGGL distribution, can be found either using the MGF via (
2.10
) or via the stochastic representation (
1.2
). Using the latter, we obtain the result below.

Lemma 2.1
.

The mean vector and the covariance matrix of
\left(X,Y\right)\sim\mathrm{BGGL}(\alpha,\beta,\delta,\mu,\sigma)
are as follows:

(2.12)

\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\alpha}{\beta}&\delta+\mu\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\end{bmatrix}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\alpha}{\beta^{2}}&\mu\frac{\alpha}{\beta^{2}}\\
\mu\frac{\alpha}{\beta^{2}}&\frac{\mu^{2}\alpha}{\beta^{2}}+\frac{\sigma^{2}\alpha}{\beta}\end{bmatrix}.

Moreover, the correlation of
X
and
Y
is given by:
\rho=\mu\left[\mu^{2}+\sigma^{2}\beta\right]^{-1/2}
.

Proof.

The mean and variance of marginals for Gamma and Variance-Gamma in (
1.2
) are well known. For the Gamma component
X
, from
[
5
, page 526]
we get:

(2.13)

\mathbb{E}[X^{r}]=\begin{cases}\Gamma(\alpha+r)/(\beta^{r}\Gamma(\alpha)),\quad r>-\alpha;\\
+\infty,\quad r\leq-\alpha.\end{cases}

Plugging in
r=1
and
r=2
, or looking at
[
5
, Appendix B, subsection 2.2]
:

(2.14)

\mathbb{E}[X]=\frac{\alpha}{\beta},\quad\mathrm{Var}(X)=\frac{\alpha}{\beta^{2}},\quad\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]=\frac{\alpha+\alpha^{2}}{\beta^{2}}.

For the variance-gamma component
Y
in (
1.2
), one can see the mean and variance from
[
13
, subsection 2.7]
. For the first moment, since
\mathbb{E}[Z]=0
and
\mathbb{E}[Z^{2}]=1
, by independence of
X
and
Z
, and using (
2.14
), we obtain

(2.15)

\mathbb{E}(Y)=\mathbb{E}\left(\delta+\mu X+\sigma\sqrt{X}Z\right)=\delta+\mu\cdot\mathbb{E}X+\sigma\cdot\mathbb{E}\sqrt{X}\cdot\mathbb{E}Z=\delta+\mu\frac{\alpha}{\beta}.

Using (
2.14
) and (
2.15
), we compute the second moment and the variance of
Y
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[Y^{2}\right]&=\delta^{2}+2\delta\mu\mathbb{E}\left[X\right]+\mu^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[X^{2}\right]+\sigma^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[X\right]=\delta^{2}+2\delta\mu\frac{\alpha}{\beta}+\frac{\alpha^{2}+\alpha}{\beta^{2}}\mu^{2}+\sigma^{2}\frac{\alpha}{\beta},\\
\mathrm{Var}\left[Y\right]&=\mathbb{E}\left[Y^{2}\right]-(\mathbb{E}\left[Y\right])^{2}=\frac{\mu^{2}\alpha}{\beta^{2}}+\frac{\sigma^{2}\alpha}{\beta}.\end{split}

Finally, let us compute the covariance. Using (
1.2
) and (
2.14
), we get:

(2.16)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left(XY\right)&=\mathbb{E}\left[X(\delta+\mu X+\sigma\sqrt{X}Z)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\delta X+\mu X^{2}+\sigma X^{\frac{3}{2}}Z\right]\\
&=\delta\mathbb{E}\left[X\right]+\mu\mathbb{E}\left[X^{2}\right]+\sigma\mathbb{E}[X^{3/2}]\cdot\mathbb{E}[Z]=\delta\frac{\alpha}{\beta}+\mu\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}+\alpha}{\beta^{2}}.\end{split}

From (
2.16
), we can calculate the covariance of
X
and
Y
. Finally,

\rho=\frac{\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(X)}\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(Y)}}=\frac{\mu}{\sqrt{\mu^{2}+\sigma^{2}\beta}}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 2.1
.

Note that the correlation, which has the same sign as
\mu
, vanishes when
\mu=0
(in which case the distribution of
Y
is symmetric about zero). However, even though
X
and
Y
are uncorrelated in this case, they are not independent.

In this section we derive

of

\left(X,Y\right)\sim\mathrm{BGGL}(\alpha,\beta,\delta,\mu,\sigma)

[

{\bf X}

(2.17)

H({\bf X})=-\mathbb{E}\log f({\bf X}).

The result for the BGGL distribution follows from the following proposition, proven in the Appendix, which provides Shannon entropy for any bivariate distribution of

Proposition 2.2
.

If

is conditionally Gaussian as in (

), where

(2.18)

H((X,Y))=H(X)+H(Z)+\frac{1}{2}\left(\log\sigma^{2}+\mathbb{E}[\log X]\right),

where
H(X)=-\mathbb{E}\log f(X)
is the Shannon entropy of

and
H(Z)=(1/2)\log(2\pi e)

For

2.6.
Curved exponential family and Fisher information

Straightforward derivations show that the BGGL family given by the PDF (
2.4
) is an exponential family. However, it is not a classic family, where the dimension of sufficient statistics is the same as that of the parameter space. Here, the parameter vector has five coordinates:

(2.21)

\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\alpha,\beta,\delta,\mu,\upsilon),

where, for convenience, we denoted
\upsilon=\sigma^{2}
. In this notation, the joint PDF (
2.4
) can be written as

(2.22)

f(x,y)=h(x,y)\exp\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{6}\eta_{j}(\boldsymbol{\theta})T_{j}(x,y)-A(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right\},

where
h(x,y)=(1/\sqrt{2\pi})x^{-3/2}
for
x>0
and

(2.23)

A(\boldsymbol{\theta})=-\log\frac{\beta^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}+\frac{\mu\delta}{\upsilon}+\frac{\log\upsilon}{2}.

Further, in (
2.22
) we have

(2.24)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\eta_{1}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\alpha\quad&\mbox{and}\quad T_{1}(x,y)=\ln x;\\
\eta_{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=-\beta-\frac{\mu^{2}}{2\upsilon}\quad&\mbox{and}\quad T_{2}(x,y)=x;\\
\eta_{3}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=-\frac{1}{2\upsilon}\quad&\mbox{and}\quad T_{3}(x,y)=\frac{y^{2}}{x};\\
\eta_{4}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{\delta}{\upsilon}\quad&\mbox{and}\quad T_{4}(x,y)=\frac{y}{x};\\
\eta_{5}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=-\frac{\delta^{2}}{2\upsilon}\quad&\mbox{and}\quad T_{5}(x,y)=\frac{1}{x};\\
\eta_{6}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{\mu}{\upsilon}\quad&\mbox{and}\quad T_{6}(x,y)=y.\end{split}

In general, this is a
curved exponential family
, rather than a full one: the dimension of the vector of sufficient statistics,
{\bf T}:=(T_{1},\ldots,T_{6})
, exceeds the number of parameters in (
2.21
). See the discussion in
[
5
, subsection 1.6.3]
. Other examples of such distribution families include, for instance,
\{\mathcal{N}(\mu,\mu^{2})\mid\mu\in\mathbb{R}\}
, as given in
[
5
, Example 1.6.9]
. Moreover, the BGGL family cannot, in general, be written in
canonical form
(see
[
5
, page 54 and Example 3.4.6]
). However, in the special case
\delta=0
, the natural parameters satisfy
\eta_{4}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\eta_{5}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=0
, and the number of parameters matches the number of sufficient statistics (both equal to four). In this case, the model reduces to a full exponential family that admits a canonical representation. See also a useful article
[
4
]
on asymptotic normality for MLE in exponential models.

2.7.
Fisher information matrix

Under the parametrization in (
2.21
), the Fisher information matrix
\mathcal{I}
is a
5\times 5
matrix representing the covariance matrix of the gradient random vector
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\log f(X,Y)
(see, e.g.,
[
5
, Section 3.4.2]
):

(2.25)

\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{\theta}):=\mathrm{Cov}\left(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\log f(X,Y)\right)\quad\mbox{for}\quad(X,Y)\sim\mathrm{BGGL}(\boldsymbol{\theta}).

Since
\mathbb{E}\left(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\log f(X,Y)\right)=0
, the
ij
-th element of this matrix is given by

(2.26)

\mathcal{I}_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\partial\log f(X,Y)}{\partial\theta_{i}}\frac{\partial\log f(X,Y)}{\partial\theta_{j}}\right],\quad i,j=1,\ldots,5.

A close examination of the PDF
f(x,y)
reveals that the terms of
\mathcal{I}
corresponding to
(\alpha,\beta)
and
those corresponding to
(\delta,\mu,\upsilon)
can be obtained separately, resulting in a block-diagonal matrix.
In fact, the
2\times 2
upper block corresponding to
(\alpha,\beta)
is the same as the Fisher information matrix for the classic gamma family given by the PDF (
2.1
) (see, e.g.,
[
9
, Chapter 17, subsection 7.2]
).
This matrix is known to be:

(2.27)

\mathcal{G}(\alpha,\beta)=\begin{bmatrix}\psi^{\prime}(\alpha)&-\beta^{-1}\\
-\beta^{-1}&\alpha\beta^{-2}\\
\end{bmatrix}.

Recall that here
\psi(\cdot)=(\ln\Gamma(\cdot))^{\prime}
is the digamma function.
Routine derivation shows that the elements
I_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
for
i=1,2
(corresponding to
\theta_{1}=\alpha
or
\theta_{2}=\beta
) and
j=3,4,5
(corresponding
\theta_{3}=\delta,\theta_{4}=\mu,\theta_{5}=\upsilon
) are zero, and the Fisher information matrix is block-diagonal with two blocks: an upper-left block, corresponding to
(\alpha,\beta)
, and a lower-right block, corresponding to
(\delta,\mu,\upsilon)
. The calculation of the
3\times 3
lower-right block corresponding to
(\delta,\mu,\upsilon)
is straightforward via (
2.26
), where we have:

(2.28)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{\partial\ln f(x,y)}{\partial\delta}&=-\frac{\mu}{\upsilon}+\frac{y}{\upsilon x}-\frac{\delta}{\upsilon x},\\
\frac{\partial\ln f(x,y)}{\partial\mu}&=-\frac{\mu}{\upsilon}x+\frac{y}{\upsilon}-\frac{\delta}{\upsilon},\\
\frac{\partial\ln f(x,y)}{\partial\upsilon}&=\frac{(y-\delta-\mu x)^{2}}{2\upsilon^{2}x}-\frac{1}{2\upsilon}.\end{split}

Recall that
\upsilon=\sigma^{2}
. We shall omit routine derivations leading to the result below.

Theorem 2.1
.

If
\alpha>1
, the Fisher information matrix for the
\mathrm{BGGL}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is

(2.29)

\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\begin{bmatrix}\psi^{\prime}(\alpha)&-1/\beta&0&0&0\\
-1/\beta&\alpha/\beta^{2}&0&0&0\\
0&0&\beta(\alpha-1)^{-1}\sigma^{-2}&\sigma^{-2}&0\\
0&0&\sigma^{-2}&\alpha\beta^{-1}\sigma^{-2}&0\\
0&0&0&0&0.5\sigma^{-4}\\
\end{bmatrix}.

The restriction
\alpha>1
in Theorem
2.1
is related to the derivation of the diagonal element corresponding to
\delta
. Indeed, a routine derivation utilizing (
1.2
) and (
2.28
) leads to

(2.30)

\frac{\partial\ln f(X,Y)}{\partial\delta}=\frac{Y-\mu X-\delta}{\upsilon X}=\frac{\sigma\sqrt{X}Z}{\upsilon X}=\frac{Z}{\sigma\sqrt{X}}.

Since
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
has finite second moment and is independent of
X
, the right-hand side of (
2.30
) has finite second moment if and only if
\mathbb{E}[(1/\sqrt{X})^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[X^{-1}]<\infty
. Plugging in
r=-1
into
[
5
, page 526, exercise 4(a)]
and using
\Gamma(\alpha)=(\alpha-1)\Gamma(\alpha-1)
, we conclude that

(2.31)

\mathbb{E}[X^{-1}]=\begin{cases}\beta/(\alpha-1),\quad\alpha>1;\\
+\infty,\quad\alpha\in(0,1].\end{cases}

Therefore, the gradient vector
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\log f(X,Y)
has finite second moment if and only if
\alpha>1
.

The derivation of the
3\times 3
bottom-right block of the Fisher information matrix (
2.25
) corresponding to
(\delta,\mu,\upsilon)
is quite general, and can be related to a linear model with stochastic covariates (see, e.g.,
[
5
, Example 6.2.1]
). Routine algebra shows that this block is given by
\upsilon^{-1}\mathrm{diag}(\mathcal{H},0.5\upsilon^{-1})
,
where

(2.32)

\mathcal{H}:=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{E}\left(X^{-1/2}\cdot X^{-1/2}\right)&\mathbb{E}\left(X^{1/2}\cdot X^{-1/2}\right)\\
\mathbb{E}\left(X^{1/2}\cdot X^{-1/2}\right)&\mathbb{E}\left(X^{1/2}\cdot X^{1/2}\right)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{E}[X^{-1}]&1\\
1&\mathbb{E}[X]\end{bmatrix},

provided that the required expectations in (
2.32
) are finite. Since in the gamma case we have
\mathbb{E}[X]=\alpha/\beta
and, by (
2.31
),
\mathbb{E}[1/X]=\beta/(\alpha-1)
(for
\alpha>1
), we recover the lower-right block of
\mathcal{I}
in Theorem
2.1
. See also
[
12
]
for another case where Fisher information is infinite and the convergence rate is different than
n^{1/2}
.

3.
Parameter Estimation

Given a bivariate random sample
(X_{i},Y_{i})
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, from
BGGL(\alpha,\beta,\delta,\mu,\upsilon)
given by the PDF (
2.5
), where, as before,
\upsilon=\sigma^{2}
, the
log-likelihood
(LL) function
\ell(\alpha,\beta,\delta,\mu,\upsilon)

can be easily found to be

(3.1)

\ell=n\left[C+g(\delta,\mu,\upsilon)+h(\alpha,\beta)\right],

where we define

(3.2)

\displaystyle\begin{split}C&=-\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi)-\frac{3}{2}\cdot\overline{\log X},\\
g(\delta,\mu,\upsilon)&=-\frac{1}{2}\cdot\log\upsilon-\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\frac{(Y_{i}-\delta)^{2}}{2\upsilon X_{i}}-\frac{\mu^{2}}{2\upsilon}\overline{X}+\frac{\mu}{\upsilon}(\overline{Y}-\delta),\\
h(\alpha,\beta)&=\alpha\log\beta-\log\Gamma(\alpha)+\alpha\cdot\overline{\log X}-\beta\cdot\overline{X},\end{split}

and the notation
\overline{U}
denotes the empirical average of the
\{U_{i}\}
. Clearly, the search for the maximum likelihood estimators (MLEs) can be carried out for
\delta
,
\mu
, and
\upsilon
separately from
\alpha
and
\beta
via maximization of the functions
g
and
h
in (
3.2
), respectively.

3.1.
The MLEs of
\alpha
and
\beta

The MLEs of the parameters
\alpha,\beta
of the gamma mixing distribution, which arise by maximizing the function
h
in (
3.2
), are well known; see for example
[
9
, Chapter 17, subsection 7.2]
. It is easy to see that, for any fixed
\alpha>0
, the function
h
in (
3.2
) is maximized by a unique value

(3.3)

\beta(\alpha)=\frac{\alpha}{\overline{X}}.

A substitution of this
\beta(\alpha)
into the function
h
results in the following function of
\alpha
:

(3.4)

h(\alpha,\beta(\alpha))=\alpha\log\alpha-\alpha\log\overline{X}-\log\Gamma(\alpha)+\alpha\,\overline{\log X}-\alpha.

The function (
3.4
) needs to be maximized with respect to
\alpha
.
However, the derivative of this function,
(d/d\alpha)h(\alpha,\beta(\alpha))=\log(\alpha)-\psi(\alpha)-(\log\overline{X}-\overline{\log X})
, is a continuous, strictly decreasing function on
(0,\infty)
, with the limits of
\infty
and
-(\log\overline{X}-\overline{\log X})
at zero and infinity, respectively (see, e.g., Lemma 3 in
[
1
]
). Moreover, by the concavity of the logarithmic function, the limit at infinity is negative (unless all sample values are the same, which occurs with probability 0). Consequently, with probability 1, there exist a unique MLE of the parameter
\alpha
, which satisfies the likelihood equation

(3.5)

w(\alpha)=\log(\alpha)-\psi(\alpha)=\log\overline{X}-\overline{\log X}

and requires (straightforward) numerical methods to solve. In conclusion, the MLEs of
\alpha
and
\beta
exist and are unique (with probability 1), and admit the stochastic representation

(3.6)

\begin{bmatrix}\hat{\alpha}_{n}\\
\hat{\beta}_{n}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&1/\overline{X}\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}w^{-1}(\log\overline{X}-\overline{\log X})\\
w^{-1}(\log\overline{X}-\overline{\log X})\end{bmatrix},

where
w^{-1}(\cdot)
is the inverse function of
w(\cdot)
given by the left-hand side in (
3.5
).

3.2.
The MLEs of
\delta
,
\mu
, and
\sigma^{2}

The MLEs of the parameters
\delta
,
\mu
, and
\upsilon=\sigma^{2}
are easily obtained by maximization of the function
g(\delta,\mu,\upsilon)
in (
3.2
), leading to explicit values presented in the result below, proven in the appendix.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
(X_{1},Y_{1}),\ldots,(X_{n},Y_{n})
be a random sample from
BGGL(\alpha,\beta,\delta,\mu,\upsilon)
distribution given by the PDF (
2.5
) where
\upsilon=\sigma^{2}
and
n\geq 2
. Then, the MLEs of
\delta
,
\mu
, and
\upsilon
exist and are unique with probability one, and are given by

(3.7)

\hat{\delta}_{n}=\frac{\overline{Y}\cdot\overline{X}^{-1}-\overline{(X^{-1}Y)}}{(\overline{X})^{-1}-\overline{X^{-1}}},\quad\hat{\mu}_{n}=\frac{\overline{Y}-\hat{\delta}_{n}}{\overline{X}},

(3.8)

\hat{\upsilon}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\left[X_{i}^{-1/2}Y_{i}-X_{i}^{-1/2}\hat{\delta}_{n}-X_{i}^{1/2}\hat{\mu}_{n}\right]^{2}.

If all the values of
X_{i}
are equal to the same
X>0
, which occurs with probability 0, then the three MLEs are not unique. This can be seen from the arguments given in the last section of the Appendix, where other special cases are discussed as well.

Further properties of the estimators are established in the result below. These properties are well known in regression settings with error variance proportional to the explanatory variables, as discussed briefly at the end of this section.

Theorem 3.2
.

The MLEs
\hat{\delta}_{n}
and
\hat{\mu}_{n}
in (
3.7
) are unbiased. Moreover, the distribution of
(\hat{\delta}_{n},\hat{\mu}_{n})
, conditional on
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
, is bivariate Gaussian:

\begin{bmatrix}\hat{\delta}_{n}\\
\hat{\mu}_{n}\end{bmatrix}\sim\mathcal{N}_{2}\left(\begin{bmatrix}\delta\\
\mu\end{bmatrix},\sigma^{2}\begin{bmatrix}\sum X_{k}^{-1}&n\\
n&\sum X_{k}\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\right).

In addition, while the estimator
\hat{v}_{n}
is biased for
\sigma^{2}=v
, it is asymptotically unbiased. Finally,

(3.9)

s^{2}=\frac{n}{n-2}\hat{v}_{n}=\frac{1}{n-2}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\left[X_{i}^{-1/2}Y_{i}-X_{i}^{-1/2}\hat{\delta}_{n}-X_{i}^{1/2}\hat{\mu}_{n}\right]^{2}

is an unbiased estimator of
\sigma^{2}
and
(n-2)s^{2}\sim\chi^{2}_{n-2}
.

Let us note that the maximum likelihood estimators of
\mu
and
\delta
coincide with least squares estimators. To see this, consider the representation

(3.10)

Y_{i}=\delta+\mu X_{i}+\sigma\sqrt{X_{i}}\,Z_{i},\quad Z_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1),\quad i=1,\ldots,n.

This model can be viewed as a
generalized
(or
weighted
) least squares problem, where
\mu
and
\delta
are chosen to minimize

(3.11)

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\bigl(Y_{i}-\delta-\mu X_{i}\bigr)^{2}}{X_{i}}.

See, for example,
[
5
, p. 112]
for background on weighted least squares. Equivalently, we may transform (
3.10
) to obtain a model with homoscedastic errors by dividing both sides by
\sqrt{X_{i}}
:

(3.12)

X_{i}^{-1/2}Y_{i}=X_{i}^{-1/2}\delta+X_{i}^{1/2}\mu+\sigma Z_{i},\quad i=1,\ldots,n.

This yields a linear regression model with constant error variance. Although the transformed model does not include a standard intercept term, this does not affect its statistical properties. Applying ordinary least squares (OLS) to (
3.12
) leads to minimization of

(3.13)

\mathcal{S}(\mu,\delta)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[X_{i}^{-1/2}Y_{i}-X_{i}^{-1/2}\delta-X_{i}^{1/2}\mu\right]^{2}.

For further discussion of OLS, see the classic textbook
[
5
]
, Section 2.2, Theorem 6.1.4, Corollary 6.1.1, and Proposition 6.1.1.

4.
Asymptotic Distributions of the Estimators

Consider first the case
\alpha>1
, where the MLE
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is asymptotically normal and efficient, see
[
5
, Section 6.2.2]
. Recall the notation
\upsilon=\sigma^{2}
.

Theorem 4.1
.

If
\alpha>1
, we have weak convergence to a 5-dimensional Gaussian distribution:

\sqrt{n}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta})^{\top}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\mathcal{N}_{5}(\mathbf{0},\mathcal{I}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\theta})),\quad n\to\infty,

where
\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is the Fisher information matrix provided in Theorem
2.1
.

Since
\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
consists of three blocks, the same can be said about its inverse
\mathcal{I}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
: It has blocks of size
2\times 2,2\times 2,1\times 1
corresponding to
(\alpha,\beta)
,
(\delta,\mu)
and
\upsilon
. Therefore,
(\hat{\alpha}_{n},\hat{\beta}_{n})
,
(\hat{\delta}_{n},\hat{\mu}_{n})
and
\hat{\upsilon}_{n}
are asymptotically independent. This observation leads to the following three corollaries.

Corollary 4.1
.

For
\alpha>1
, the asymptotic distribution of
\hat{\alpha}_{n}
and
\hat{\beta}_{n}
is bivariate Gaussian:

\sqrt{n}\begin{bmatrix}\hat{\alpha}_{n}-\alpha&\hat{\beta}_{n}-\beta\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\mathcal{N}_{2}\left(\mathbf{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\alpha,\beta}\right),\quad n\to\infty,

where the limiting covariance matrix (recall the digamma function
\psi(\cdot)=(\ln\Gamma(\cdot))^{\prime}
)

(4.1)

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\alpha,\beta}=[\alpha\psi^{\prime}(\alpha)-1]^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
\beta&\beta^{2}\psi^{\prime}(\alpha)\\
\end{bmatrix}

is the inverse of the
2\times 2
-matrix
\mathcal{G}(\alpha,\beta)
given by (
2.27
).

Corollary 4.2
.

For
\alpha>1
, the asymptotic distribution of
\hat{\delta}_{n}
and
\hat{\mu}_{n}
is bivariate Gaussian:

\sqrt{n}\begin{bmatrix}\hat{\delta}_{n}-\delta&\hat{\mu}_{n}-\mu\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\mathcal{N}_{2}\left(\mathbf{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\delta,\mu}\right),\quad n\to\infty,

with the limiting covariance matrix

(4.2)

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\delta,\mu}=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\beta}\begin{bmatrix}\alpha(\alpha-1)&-\beta(\alpha-1)\\
-\beta(\alpha-1)&\beta^{2}\\
\end{bmatrix}

Corollary 4.3
.

For
\alpha>1
, the asymptotic distribution of
\hat{\upsilon}_{n}
is:

(4.3)

\sqrt{n}(\hat{\upsilon}_{n}-\upsilon)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\mathcal{N}(0,2\sigma^{4}),\quad n\to\infty.

Remark 4.1
.

As shown below, the asymptotic distribution of
\hat{\alpha}_{n}
and
\hat{\beta}_{n}
as well as that of
\hat{\upsilon}_{n}
, given in Corollaries
4.1
and
4.3
, respectively, carry over to the case
\alpha\in(0,1]
. However, this is no longer so for
\hat{\delta}_{n}
and
\hat{\mu}_{n}
.

Note that the asymptotic variance of
\hat{\delta}_{n}
given in Corollary
4.2
becomes zero for
\alpha=1
. This is related to the fact that the corresponding entry of the Fisher information matrix (
2.25
) is infinite in this case. However, under a different normalization, the asymptotic distribution of
\hat{\delta}_{n}
and
\hat{\mu}_{n}
is still bivariate normal, with the two estimators becoming asymptotically independent. On the other hand, for
\alpha<1
, the Gaussianity of the asymptotic distribution breaks down. These facts are presented in the two results stated below, starting with the case
\alpha\in(0,1)
.

Theorem 4.2
.

For
\alpha\in(0,1)
we have:

(4.4)

{\bf D}_{n}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n}-\boldsymbol{\theta})^{\top}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\mathbf{W}^{\top}=(W_{\alpha},W_{\beta},W_{\delta},W_{\mu},W_{\upsilon})^{\top},\quad n\to\infty,

where

(4.5)

{\bf D}_{n}=\mathrm{diag}(\sqrt{n},\sqrt{n},n^{1/(2\alpha)},\sqrt{n},\sqrt{n})

is a
5\times 5
diagonal matrix and the limiting vector
{\bf W}
has four independent components:

(4.6)

\displaystyle\begin{split}(W_{\alpha},W_{\beta})^{\top}&\sim\mathcal{N}_{2}(\mathbf{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\alpha,\beta}),\\
W_{\delta}&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\sigma\beta^{-1/2}[\Gamma(\alpha+1)]^{1/2\alpha}\xi_{\alpha}^{-1/2}Z,\\
W_{\mu}&\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}\beta/\alpha),\\
W_{\upsilon}&\sim\mathcal{N}(0,2\sigma^{4}).\end{split}

Here, the matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\alpha,\beta}
is taken from (
4.1
), the two random variables
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and
\xi_{\alpha}
are independent, and
\xi_{\alpha}
is a stable subordinator defined via its Laplace transform:

(4.7)

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-u\xi_{\alpha}}\right]=\exp\left[-\Gamma(1-\alpha)u^{\alpha}\right],\quad u\geq 0.

For the case
\alpha=1
, we have a different scaling (
\sqrt{n\ln n}
instead of
n^{1/(2\alpha)}
), and a different limiting distribution connected with
\hat{\delta}_{n}
, as detailed in the result below.

Theorem 4.3
.

For
\alpha=1
we have:

(4.8)

{\bf D}_{n}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n}-\boldsymbol{\theta})^{\top}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}{\bf W}^{\top}=(W_{\alpha},W_{\beta},W_{\delta},W_{\mu},W_{\upsilon})^{\top},\quad n\to\infty,

where, instead of (
4.5
), this time the diagonal matrix
{\bf D}_{n}
is given by

{\bf D}_{n}=\sqrt{n}\cdot\mathrm{diag}(1,1,\sqrt{\ln n},1,1)

and, instead of (
4.6
), the limiting vector
\mathbf{W}
has four independent components:

(4.9)

\displaystyle\begin{split}(W_{\alpha},W_{\beta})^{\top}&\sim\mathcal{N}_{2}(\mathbf{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1,\beta}),\\
W_{\delta}&\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}/\beta),\\
W_{\mu}&\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}\beta),\\
W_{\upsilon}&\sim\mathcal{N}(0,2\sigma^{4}),\end{split}

where
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1,\beta}
is the same matrix as in (
4.1
) with
\alpha=1
.

Remark 4.2
.

In all of the above results, the MLE
\hat{\upsilon}_{n}
may be replaced by the classical estimator
s^{2}
defined in (
3.9
).

Remark 4.3
.

We emphasize that, in all three cases, the estimators
(\hat{\alpha}_{n},\hat{\beta}_{n})
,
(\hat{\delta}_{n},\hat{\mu}_{n})
, and
\hat{\upsilon}_{n}
are asymptotically independent. Moreover, in the nonstandard case
\alpha\leq 1
, the estimators
\hat{\delta}_{n}
and
\hat{\mu}_{n}
are also asymptotically independent. In contrast, in the regular case
\alpha>1
, these two estimators are not asymptotically independent, as the corresponding off-diagonal element of the limiting covariance matrix is nonzero. Finally, for
\alpha\geq 1
, the limiting distribution is multivariate Gaussian, whereas for
\alpha<1
it is not, as it involves a stable subordinator.

Remark 4.4
.

The limiting variance
\sigma^{2}_{\infty,\mu}
of the (asymptotically normal) estimator
\hat{\mu}_{n}
depends nonlinearly on
\alpha
. For
\alpha>1
, it follows from Corollary
4.2
as the second diagonal element of the limiting covariance matrix (
4.2
). For
\alpha=1
, it is obtained from Theorem
4.3
via the third relation in (
4.9
). Finally, for
\alpha<1
, it follows from Theorem
4.7
, again from the third relation in (
4.6
). Collecting these cases, we obtain

\sigma^{2}_{\infty,\mu}=\frac{\sigma^{2}\beta}{\min(\alpha,1)}.

An intuitive explanation is as follows. The joint finite-sample distribution of
(\hat{\delta}_{n},\hat{\mu}_{n})
is given in Theorem
3.2
, from which the asymptotic behavior of
\hat{\mu}_{n}
can be deduced as
n\to\infty
. For
\alpha>1
, both diagonal elements of the inverse covariance matrix are of order
n
, and thus the limiting distribution of
(\hat{\delta}_{n},\hat{\mu}_{n})
is not of product form. In contrast, for
\alpha\leq 1
, the term
\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}^{-1}
grows faster than
n
, which leads to asymptotic independence of
\hat{\delta}_{n}
and
\hat{\mu}_{n}
. Consequently, the limiting variance
\sigma^{2}_{\infty,\mu}
is determined independently of the asymptotic behavior of
\hat{\delta}_{n}
.

There are many well-documented cases in the literature where maximum likelihood estimators do not exhibit the usual asymptotic normality. In particular, such behavior may arise when the support of the distribution depends on the unknown parameters. A classical example is the uniform distribution on
[0,\theta]
, where
\theta>0
is unknown. In this case, the MLE of
\theta
is the sample maximum
X_{(n)}
, and it is well known that
n\bigl(\theta-X_{(n)}\bigr)
converges in distribution to an exponential random variable. Thus, the convergence rate is
n
, rather than the usual
\sqrt{n}
. Another example arises in location families of the form
g(x;\mu)=f(|x-\mu|)/2
, where
x,\mu\in\mathbb{R}
and
f(\cdot)
is a PDF on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
with
f(0+)=\infty
. In this case, the MLE of
\mu
may fail to exist; see
[
8
,
17
]
. Indeed, the density
g(\cdot;\mu)
has a singularity at
x=\mu
, which leads to irregular behavior. Related phenomena are discussed in
[
12
]
. In the BGGL model, we observe a similar effect for
\alpha<1
: the convergence rate deviates from
\sqrt{n}
, and the limiting distribution is no longer Gaussian.

Simulation

To illustrate the behavior of the maximum likelihood estimators (MLEs) for the parameters
\delta
,
\sigma
, and
\mu
, we conducted a simulation study. Samples of size
n=50
and
n=500
were generated by first simulating Gamma and standard normal random variables, and then constructing generalized asymmetric Laplace (GAL) observations using fixed parameter values of
\delta
,
\sigma
, and
\mu

5000
times for each sample size.

\alpha

\alpha=0.25

\alpha=2

\alpha=5
, for which the distribution is closer to normality. In all cases, the scale parameter is fixed at
\beta=1
.

. Overall, the estimators of
\mu
,
\delta

.

6.
Financial Modeling

6.1.
Background

Consider a stock market index, say Standard & Poor 500 (S&P 500) weekly data. Let
S(t)
be end-of-week closing data. The
price returns
are defined as log changes in price:
Y(t)=\ln S(t)-\ln S(t-1)=\ln\frac{S(t)}{S(t-1)}
. For some indices, we can observe its volatility index measured daily. Let
V(t)
be the value of this index: weekly average of daily closing prices. It plays the role of the standard deviation of
Y(t)
. Measuring volatility attempts to capture the observation that this standard deviation is not constant over time. During crises, volatility is high, but during normal times, it is low. Eras of high volatility alternate with eras of low volatility. In other words, volatility has
persistence:
If it is high this week it is likely to stay high next week. If it is low this week it is likely to stay low next week. To model this, one can use autoregression of order 1. Since
V
is always positive, it is natural to apply the logarithmic transform,

(6.1)

\ln V(t)=a+b\ln V(t-1)+\zeta(t),

with IID
\zeta(t)
with
\mathbb{E}[\zeta(t)]=0
. Having interpreted
V(t)
as the standard deviation of returns
Y(t)
, we can write
Y(t)=V(t)\xi(t)
for IID
\xi(t)
. More generally, we can model

(6.2)

Y(t)=c+hV(t)+V(t)\eta(t),

where
\eta(t)
are IID with mean zero. Fitting (
6.1
) and (
6.2
) is the content of the article
[
15
]
, for monthly data 1986–2024. In turns out that
\eta
is well-explained by a normal distribution, but
\zeta
is not. A more appropriate distribution for
\zeta
is, in fact, variance-gamma (GAL). But even this fitting is challenging, see
[
15
, Section 3]
. Also,
\eta
and
\zeta
are correlated.
One could possibly model
(\eta,\zeta)
using bivariate GAL, since normal distribution is a limiting case of a one-dimensional GAL. This is left for future research. The model (
6.1
) and (
6.2
) looks similar to a classic Heston stochastic volatility (SV) model with two innovation sequences, as opposed to GARCH models with only one innovation sequence. For background on SV, see the monograph
[
3
]
. But the SV models consider volatility to be hidden and only returns to be observed. This is why in classic SV models volatility needs to be inferred using hidden Markov models or other complicated statistical techniques. In the model given by (
6.1
) - (
6.2
) we observe both the returns and the volatility. Thus, we can estimate
a,b
and find the distribution of
\zeta(t)
separately from
c,h
and
\eta(t)
.

6.3.
Interpretation

We stress that conceptually this BGGV model is similar to (
6.1
) and (
6.2
). It has returns on the left-hand side of an equation, and a function
X
of volatility times a standard normal random variable on the right-hand side of this equation. This is similar to (
6.2
). The function
X
of volatility is not exactly volatility at a given time. Rather, it depends on the volatility at times
t
and
t-1
. More precisely,
X(t)
captures how large is this week’s volatility compared to what you would expect given last week’s volatility, according to this autoregression (
6.1
). One can view this
X
as unexpected or surprise volatility. We are able to extract IID terms from volatility to model them together with returns by a simple bivariate distribution instead of a time series model like
[
15
]
or GARCH or SV.

To conclude, here we incorporated stochastic volatility inside returns and modeled the latter using IID. In fact, we switched back from stochastic volatility models to modeling returns as IID and index levels as geometric random walk (but with non-normal increments).

7.1.
A technical lemma

Fix numbers
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}>0
and
y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}
with
\overline{x}=(x_{1}+\cdots+x_{n})/n
and
\overline{y}=(y_{1}+\cdots+y_{n})/n
.

Lemma 7.1
.

We have:

(7.1)

w(\delta)=\frac{(\overline{y}-\delta)^{2}}{\overline{x}}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{(y_{i}-\delta)^{2}}{x_{i}}\leq 0,\quad\delta\geq 0.

Proof.

Observe that
\overline{y}_{n}-\delta=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_{i}-\delta)
,
so the inequality
w(\delta)\leq 0
is equivalent to

(7.2)

\Bigl[\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_{i}-\delta)\Bigr]^{2}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{(y_{i}-\delta)^{2}}{x_{i}}

However, this follows from Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,
(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}b_{i})^{2}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}^{2}
, if we identify
a_{i}=\sqrt{x_{i}}
and
b_{i}=(y_{i}-\delta)/\sqrt{x_{i}}
.
∎

7.2.
Proof of Proposition
2.2

Without loss of generality, we shall assume that
X
has continuous distribution with the PDF
f_{X}(\cdot)
. By the mixture representation (
1.2
), the joint PDF of
(X,Y)
is given by

(7.3)

f(x,y)=f(y\mid x)f_{X}(x),

where the conditional density of
Y
given
X
is normal:

(7.4)

f(y\mid x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi x}}\frac{1}{\sigma}\exp\left[-\frac{(y-\delta-\mu x)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}x}\right],\,\,\,x>0,\,y\in\mathbb{R}.

This is the PDF of the conditional normal distribution in (
1.3
). By definition of the Shannon entropy,

(7.5)

H((X,Y))=-\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\log[f(x,y)]f(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x.

Applying (
7.3
) to (
7.5
), we get:

\displaystyle H((X,Y))

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{\infty}\log[f_{X}(x)]f_{X}(x)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(y|x)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{X}(x)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\log[f(y|x)]f(y|x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

Apply Shannon entropy formula
H(Y\mid X=x)
to conditional distribution of
Y
given
X
:

\displaystyle H((X,Y))

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{\infty}\log[f_{X}(x)]f_{X}(x)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(y|x)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{X}(x)H(Y\mid X=x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

Applying (
7.3
) and using the definition of Shannon entropy

for only
X
, we obtain

\displaystyle H((X,Y))

\displaystyle=H(X)+\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2}f_{X}(x)\,\log[2\pi e\sigma^{2}x]\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=H(X)+\frac{1}{2}\log[2\pi e\sigma^{2}]+\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2}f_{X}(x)\,\log x\,\mathrm{d}x.

Here,
H(Y|X=x)=\frac{1}{2}\log[2\pi e\sigma^{2}x]
is the Shannon entropy of the (normal!) conditional distribution of

, whose PDF is given in (
7.4
). This proves the result.

7.3.
Proof of Theorem
3.1

First, observe that for any particular values of
\delta
and
\upsilon
, the maximum value of

is obtained by that value of

that maximizes the function

This is a simple quadratic function of

\mu(\delta)=\frac{\overline{Y}-\delta}{\overline{X}}.

Here, the
\overline{X}
and
\overline{Y}
are the sample means of the
\{X_{i}\}
and
\{Y_{i}\}
, respectively. This leaves us with the problem of maximizing the function

(7.8)

r(\delta,\upsilon)=-\frac{1}{2}\log\upsilon+\frac{1}{2\upsilon}\left\{\frac{(\overline{Y}-\delta)^{2}}{\overline{X}}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{(Y_{i}-\delta)^{2}}{X_{i}}\right\},\quad\delta\in\mathbb{R},\upsilon\geq 0.

At this stage, we again consider
\upsilon>0
to be fixed, and focus on the function of
\delta
given by the curly bracket in (
7.8
),

(7.9)

w(\delta)=\frac{(\overline{Y}-\delta)^{2}}{\overline{X}}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{(Y_{i}-\delta)^{2}}{X_{i}},\quad\delta\in\mathbb{R}.

Clearly, this is a quadratic function
\delta
,
w(\delta)=a\delta^{2}+b\delta+c
, where

(7.10)

a=\frac{1}{\overline{X}}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{X_{i}},\,\,\,b=2\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{Y_{i}}{X_{i}}-\frac{\overline{Y}}{\overline{X}}\right),\,\,\,c=\frac{\overline{Y}^{2}}{\overline{X}}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{Y_{i}^{2}}{X_{i}},

unless the leading coefficient

in (

) is equal to zero. This situation occurs if all of the
\{X_{i}\}
are the same. In the latter case, the coefficient

in (
7.10
) is also zero, and we have

for all
\delta\in\mathbb{R}
with

as in (

). In this case the parameter
\delta
is not uniquely estimable. However, this occurs with probability zero.

In the rest of the proof, we shall assume that not all values of the
\{X_{i}\}
are the same, so that

is truly a quadratic function. By Lemma

, we have

The formulas (10.30.2) and (10.30.3): if

Lemma 7.2
.

Define the function

As

(7.33)

\phi(t)=\mathbb{E}\exp\left[-t\beta[\Gamma(\alpha+1)]^{-1/\alpha}\xi_{\alpha}\right]=\exp\left[-\frac{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}{\Gamma(1+\alpha)}\beta^{\alpha}t^{\alpha}\right],\quad t\geq 0,

is the Laplace transform of the random vector on the right-hand side of (
7.32
). First, note

(7.34)

\phi_{n}(t)=\mathbb{E}\exp\left[-tn^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}^{-1}\right]=\left[\psi\left(n^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}t\right)\right]^{n},

where
\psi(t):=\mathbb{E}\exp\left[-t/X\right]
for
t\geq 0
is the Laplace transform of the random variable
1/X
with
X\sim\mathrm{GAM}(\alpha,\beta)
. Straightforward algebra shows that

(7.35)

\psi(t)=\frac{\beta^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{\alpha-1}e^{-\beta x-t/x}dx.

To evaluate this integral, we use the modified Bessel function of the second kind. We change the scale in (
7.35
) inside the integral to make it look like the one in (
7.14
):
u=\beta x
and
\mathrm{d}u=\beta\,\mathrm{d}x
and

\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{\alpha-1}e^{-\beta x-t/x}\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{u}{\beta}\right)^{\alpha-1}e^{-u-t\beta/u}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\beta}=\beta^{-\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}u^{\alpha-1}e^{-u-t\beta/u}\,\mathrm{d}u.

This matches the integral in (
7.14
) with
z^{2}/4=t\beta\Leftrightarrow z=2\sqrt{t\beta}
and
\alpha=-\nu
. Then

(7.36)

\psi(t)=\frac{\beta^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\cdot\beta^{-\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}u^{\alpha-1}e^{-u-t\beta/u}\,\mathrm{d}u=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{0}^{\infty}u^{\alpha-1}e^{-u-t\beta/u}\,\mathrm{d}u.

Plug (
7.14
) into (
7.36
) and get:

(7.37)

\psi(t)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\frac{2^{-\alpha+1}K_{-\alpha}(2\sqrt{t\beta})}{(2\sqrt{t\beta})^{-\alpha}}=\frac{2(t\beta)^{\alpha/2}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}K_{-\alpha}(2\sqrt{t\beta}).

By (
7.15
), we rewrite (
7.37
) as

(7.38)

\psi(t)=\frac{2(t\beta)^{\alpha/2}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}K_{\alpha}(2\sqrt{t\beta}).

In turn, after plugging (
7.18
), and using the first convergence in Lemma
7.2
, the formula (
7.38
) becomes

(7.39)

\psi(t)=\frac{F_{\alpha}(2\sqrt{t\beta})}{\Gamma(\alpha)2^{\alpha-1}}\Rightarrow\ln\psi(t)=\ln F_{\alpha}(2\sqrt{t\beta})-\ln F_{\alpha}(0+).

Define the new variable

(7.40)

h_{n}:=2n^{-1/(2\alpha)}(\beta t)^{1/2}\Leftrightarrow n=\frac{2^{2\alpha}(t\beta)^{\alpha}}{h_{n}^{2\alpha}}.

By (
7.40
), (
7.39
) and some algebra, we rewrite (
7.34
) as

(7.41)

\ln\phi_{n}(t)=n\ln\psi\left(n^{-1/\alpha}t\right)=\frac{\ln(F_{\alpha}(h_{n}))-\ln F_{\alpha}(0+))}{h_{n}^{2\alpha}}\cdot 2^{2\alpha}t^{\alpha}\beta^{\alpha}.

Now,
h_{n}\to 0+
as
n\to\infty
. Applying Lemma
7.2
, we have:

(7.42)

\ln\phi_{n}(t)\sim-\frac{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}{\Gamma(1+\alpha)}2^{-2\alpha}\cdot 2^{2\alpha}t^{\alpha}\beta^{\alpha}=-\frac{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}{\Gamma(1+\alpha)}\beta^{\alpha}t^{\alpha}.

Plugging this into (
7.42
), we get (
7.33
). This concludes the proof.
∎

7.6.
Proof of Theorem
4.3

We closely follow the proof of Theorem
4.7
. The same remark at the beginning of the proof of Theorem
4.3
holds here. The two other equations (
7.25
) and (
7.26
) get modified as follows: The conditional distribution given
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
of

\begin{bmatrix}(n\ln n)^{\frac{1}{2}}&0\\
0&n^{\frac{1}{2}}\end{bmatrix}\left(\begin{bmatrix}\hat{\delta}_{n}\\
\hat{\mu}_{n}\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}\delta\\
\mu\end{bmatrix}\right)

is also bivariate normal, with vector mean zero and covariance matrix given by

\sigma^{2}\begin{bmatrix}(n\ln n)^{\frac{1}{2}}&0\\
0&n^{\frac{1}{2}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}^{-1}&n\\
n&\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}(n\ln n)^{\frac{1}{2}}&0\\
0&n^{\frac{1}{2}}\end{bmatrix}.

We can compute this covariance matrix using simple matrix arithmetic as

\sigma^{2}\begin{bmatrix}(n\ln n)^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}^{-1}&(\ln n)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\\
(\ln n)^{-\frac{1}{2}}&n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\end{bmatrix}.

Thus it is sufficient to prove the following lemma.

Lemma 7.4
.

If
\{X_{i}\}
are IID random variables where
X_{i}\sim\mathrm{GAM}(\alpha=1,\beta)
,
i\in\mathbb{N}
, then as
n\to\infty
, we have convergence in distribution:

(7.43)

\begin{bmatrix}(n\ln n)^{-1}&0\\
0&n^{-1}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}^{-1}\\
\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\end{bmatrix}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}\begin{bmatrix}\beta\\
\alpha/\beta\end{bmatrix}.

Proof.

We closely follow the proof of Lemma
7.3
. We need only to show

(7.44)

\frac{1}{n\ln n}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}X_{i}^{-1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\beta.

To show (
7.44
), we prove convergence of Laplace transforms. Take
\alpha=1
in (
7.39
). We get
F_{1}(0+)=1
. And replace
n^{1/\alpha}
with
n\ln n
in (
7.34
). Then the Laplace transform
\phi_{n}
of the left-hand side in (
7.44
) has logarithm

(7.45)

\ln\phi_{n}(t)=n\ln\psi\left(t/n\ln n\right)=n\ln F_{1}\left(2\sqrt{t\beta/n\ln n}\right).

Now, apply Lemma
7.2
for the case
\alpha=1
: The right-hand side of (
7.45
) is equivalent asymptotically to

n\cdot\frac{1}{2}\left(2\sqrt{\frac{t\beta}{n\ln n}}\right)^{2}\cdot\ln\left(2\sqrt{\frac{t\beta}{n\ln n}}\right)=n\cdot 2\frac{t\beta}{n\ln n}\cdot\left(-\frac{1}{2}\ln n\right)=-t\beta.

This proves that
\phi_{n}(t)\to e^{-t\beta}
as
n\to\infty
, and the right-hand side is the Laplace transform of the constant random variable
\beta
. This completes the proof of (
7.44
).
∎

7.7.
The MLE when all
X_{i}
have the same value
X

Indeed, in this case the function
g(\delta,\mu,\upsilon)
in (
3.2
) simplifies to

(7.46)

g(\delta,\mu,\upsilon)=-\frac{1}{2}\log\upsilon+\frac{\mu}{\upsilon}(\overline{Y}-\delta)-\frac{1}{2\upsilon Xn}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_{i}-\overline{Y})^{2}-\frac{1}{2\upsilon X}(\overline{Y}-\delta)^{2}-\frac{\mu^{2}}{2\upsilon}X.

Elementary analysis of the above function shows that it attains its maximum value of
g(\hat{\delta}_{n},\hat{\mu}_{n},\hat{\upsilon}_{n})=-(\log\hat{\upsilon}_{n}+1)/2

where
\hat{\delta}_{n}
is any real number,
\hat{\mu}_{n}
is as in (
3.7
), and

(7.47)

\hat{\upsilon}_{n}=\frac{1}{Xn}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_{i}-\overline{Y})^{2}.

Thus, in this case the parameter
\delta
is not uniquely estimable via MLE, while estimators of
\mu
and
\upsilon
are the same as those specified by Theorem
3.1
, since the value of
\hat{\upsilon}_{n}
in (
7.47
) is exactly the same as that in (
3.8
) assuming that all the values of
\{X_{i}\}
are equal to the same
X>0
. In particular, this case arises when the sample size is
n=1
, where we additionally have
\hat{\upsilon}_{n}=0
and
g(\hat{\delta}_{n},\hat{\mu}_{n},\hat{\upsilon}_{n})=\infty
. Then the MLEs of
\delta
,
\mu

\upsilon

\delta\neq Y
and setting
\mu=(Y-\delta)/X
, the function

reduces to
-\log\upsilon/2
, with the latter approaching infinity as
\upsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
.

In addition, for
n=2
we have
\sup_{\delta\in\mathbb{R}}w(\delta)=w(\hat{\delta}_{n})=0

, as can be verified directly. Thus, with a sample of two, the MLE of

\hat{\delta}_{n}=\frac{Y_{i}X_{j}-X_{i}Y_{j}}{X_{j}-X_{i}}
