ALKBH5 inhibitors for cancer therapy: molecular docking, challenges and future prospects

Chen Deng, Hai Xu, Jian Pu, Huamei Wei, Wenjin Chen, Yubin Luo, Shengxiao Zhang, Haifeng Jin, Jie Liao, Zhongpeng Fan, Miaomiao Cao, Yangyang Xu, Benshun Hu

Abstract

N6-Methyladenosine (m6A), the most abundant internal modification of eukaryotic mRNA, is dynamically reversed by the FeII/α-ketoglutarate-dependent dioxygenase ALKBH5, a key m6A “eraser” that regulates target mRNA fate through demethylation. ALKBH5 is aberrantly overexpressed in acute myeloid leukemia, glioblastoma, breast cancer and other malignancies, where it sustains oncogenic programs, establishing it as a promising epitranscriptomic drug target. At present, ALKBH5 inhibitor development lags behind that of FTO, with selective agents only recently emerging through covalent inhibitors such as compound 33, 25 and 40, though no clinically available ALKBH5-targeted drug yet exists. This review summarizes the biological functions, structural features, and molecular docking between reported inhibitors and target proteins of ALKBH5. Current challenges, including selectivity over homologous dioxygenases, structural optimization and resistance mechanisms, are critically discussed, alongside future perspectives on structure-guided design, combination regimens and clinical translation. This review provides a medicinal chemistry framework to accelerate the discovery of potent and selective ALKBH5 inhibitors for precision cancer therapy.

Introduction

Among the diverse post-transcriptional modifications occurring in eukaryotic messenger RNA, N6-methyladenosine (m6A) stands out as the most frequently observed internal epigenetic mark, exerting profound influence over the control of gene expression programs (). This reversible RNA modification cycle depends on the coordinated action of writers, erasers, and readers. Methyl group deposition is catalyzed by complexes containing methyltransferase-like 3 and 14 (METTL3/14) heterodimers along with regulatory partners Wilms tumor 1-associated protein (WTAP) and RNA-binding motif protein 15 (RBM15). Conversely, ALKBH5 reverse this process via demethylation. The functional output of m6A marking is then interpreted by YTH domain family (YTHDF), YTH domain-containing (YTHDC), and insulin-like growth factor 2 mRNA-binding protein (IGF2BP) family proteins, whose docking at modified adenosines redirects the post-transcriptional trajectory of target transcripts through altered splicing, export, translation efficiency, and stability (). Through the precise cooperation of these three classes of proteins, as a pervasive epitranscriptomic mark, m6A methylation underpins an elaborate post-transcriptional regulatory architecture that modulates diverse biological programs, spanning from the arbitration of cell fate decisions and the preservation of stem cell pluripotency to developmental patterning, lineage commitment, and neoplastic transformation. Contemporary investigations have increasingly established that quantitative and qualitative perturbations in m6A deposition, alongside dysfunctional operation of its cognate regulatory machinery, serve as critical pathogenic determinants underlying a wide spectrum of human disorders, with oncogenesis and malignant progression representing the most extensively characterized manifestations ().

ALKBH5, as an important member of the AlkB family, serves as a key “eraser” within the m6A modification system, removing m6A modifications from mRNAs through its demethylase activity, thereby regulating the expression of target genes. Numerous studies have shown that ALKBH5 is aberrantly overexpressed in various cancers. In acute myeloid leukemia (AML), Shen et al. discovered that ALKBH5, contrary to previously reported frequent deletion in human AML, is actually abnormally highly expressed, and its elevated expression levels are significantly correlated with poor prognosis in AML patients (). That investigation additionally established that ALKBH5 constitutes an indispensable factor for both leukemogenesis and sustained disease progression, as well as for the propagation of leukemia stem/initiating cells, yet proves non-essential for physiological hematopoietic function (). Mechanistic investigations revealed that ALKBH5 post-transcriptionally regulates key target genes, such as the oncogene transforming acidic coiled-coil-containing protein 3 (TACC3) and the receptor tyrosine kinase AXL by demethylating their mRNAs, enhancing their stability and promoting their expression, thereby driving tumor stem cell self-renewal and malignant proliferation of cancer cells (). Moreover, ALKBH5 has been shown to participate in AML progression and immune evasion through the regulation of additional downstream targets, including inosine triphosphatase (ITPA) and programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) (; ). Anomalous ALKBH5 overexpression extends beyond myeloid leukemia into a range of epithelial-derived cancers. In breast and lung malignancies, for instance, this eraser enzyme enhances metastatic spread and local tissue invasion by operating through RNA modification-dependent processes akin to those characterized in AML (). These findings collectively establish ALKBH5 as a core oncogenic factor and position it as a highly promising therapeutic target for cancer.

Relative to the comparatively mature therapeutic landscape of FTO inhibitors, the discovery and optimization of robust, ALKBH5-selective small-molecule antagonists remain substantially underdeveloped. FTO, as the first identified m6A demethylase, has been targeted by multiple inhibitors that have shown potential in cancer therapy. For example, loratadine, discovered through a drug repurposing strategy, exhibits FTO inhibitory activity in esophageal cancer (), and FTO inhibitors have also demonstrated application prospects in overcoming osimertinib resistance in lung adenocarcinoma (). However, reports on inhibitors specifically targeting ALKBH5 are still limited. Most currently reported ALKBH5 inhibitors are either 2-oxoglutarate (2-OG) analogs or pan-inhibitors that simultaneously target both FTO and ALKBH5, and truly highly selective ALKBH5 inhibitors remain extremely scarce. Recently, the team of Liu Hong and Yang Caiguang at the Shanghai Institute of Materia Medica reported a covalent inhibitor 18 targeting Cys200 of ALKBH5, which exhibits potent ALKBH5 inhibitory activity and favorable selectivity, demonstrating excellent anti-tumor effects in both in vitro and in vivo AML models (). Additionally, Fei et al. reported the salicylaldehyde-based covalent inhibitor compound 25 (), and Wang and colleagues identified a compound 40 as an ALKBH5 inhibitor (). These breakthrough advances indicate that the development of selective ALKBH5 inhibitors is accelerating. Nevertheless, clinically available ALKBH5-targeted drugs are still lacking. The discovery of highly potent and selective ALKBH5 inhibitors is expected not only to provide a novel targeted therapeutic strategy for cancers with overexpression of m6A demethylases but also to serve as indispensable chemical probe tools for in-depth dissection of the biological functions of ALKBH5 in tumorigenesis.

Biological functions of ALKBH5

Functioning as an FeII/α-ketoglutarate (α-KG, also known as 2-oxoglutarate, 2-OG)-dependent oxidative demethylase, ALKBH5 selectively erases m6A epigenetic marks from single-stranded RNA (ssRNA) molecules. This catalytic activity is mediated through its evolutionarily preserved double-stranded β-helix (DSBH) core architecture, complemented by specialized recognition loops that mediate precise engagement with target ribonucleotides (; ). Physiologically, it is indispensable for male spermatogenesis and oocyte meiotic maturation by controlling m6A-dependent mRNA splicing and stability (; ). In the nervous system, ALKBH5 regulates synaptic plasticity and axon regeneration, whereas its dysregulation exacerbates cerebral ischemia reperfusion injury (; ). Cardioprotective roles include promoting cardiomyocyte proliferation and heart regeneration through YTHDF1 demethylation (), conversely, its upregulation in failing hearts and acute myocardial infarction correlates with metabolic reprogramming and aggravated injury (; ). Additionally, hepatic ALKBH5 governs glucose and lipid homeostasis through GCGR and mTORC1 signaling (), and in the immune system, it modulates CD4+ T cell pathogenicity, γδ T cell development, and neutrophil antibacterial migration (; ; ).

Eukaryotic messenger RNAs harbor numerous post-transcriptional modifications, yet none is more abundant than the internal N6-methyladenosine (m6A) residue. This reversible mark is established through the coordinated activity of writer methyltransferases, interpreted by reader proteins that recognize the modification, and reversed by eraser demethylases—three distinct but interdependent enzymatic classes (; ). The methyltransferase complex (MTC) drives m6A installation through a bipartite organizational architecture, the METTL3-METTL14 module provides the catalytic activity, while auxiliary subunits—WTAP, VIRMA, RBM15, and ZC3H13—coordinate RNA substrate engagement and complex scaffolding (). Once deposited, the m6A epigenetic tag is preferentially decoded by a dedicated class of reader effectors, notably the YTH-domain-containing protein family comprising the cytoplasmic paralogs YTHDF1, YTHDF2, and YTHDF3 alongside the nuclear isoforms YTHDC1 and YTHDC2 and the IGF2BP family, which direct the modified transcripts toward alternative splicing, nuclear export, enhanced translation, or regulated decay (; ). As shown in , the erasure of m6A is executed by two FeII/α-ketoglutarate-dependent dioxygenases, and ALKBH5, which oxidatively remove the methyl group to regenerate unmodified adenosine, thereby resetting the epitranscriptomic state and allowing dynamic adaptation to cellular and environmental cues (; ). Disruption of this finely tuned circuitry, through aberrant expression or altered activity of writers, readers, or erasers has been increasingly recognized as a fundamental mechanism underlying malignant transformation, metabolic disorders, and immune dysregulation ().

From an oncological perspective, ALKBH5 assumes a pro-tumorigenic identity in a subset of aggressive cancers. Specifically, in acute myeloid leukemia, glioblastoma, and breast cancer, the enzyme fuels malignant transformation and disease maintenance by actively demethylating key oncogenic messages—namely TACC3, AXL, forkhead box M1 (FOXM1), and homeobox transcription factor NANOG (NANOG)—thereby protecting these transcripts from degradation and ensuring the perpetual renewal and rapid division of the cancer stem cell pool (; ; ; ). Conversely, it acts as a tumor suppressor in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and pancreatic cancer by destabilizing pro-tumorigenic mRNAs such as LYPD1 or activating PER1 and WIF1 to suppress malignant progression (; ). Accumulating data further establish ALKBH5 as a dominant modulator of ferroptosis. Within hypopharyngeal squamous carcinoma and thyroid cancer contexts, the enzyme fosters this regulated necrotic process through the transcriptional or post-transcriptional inhibition of the Nrf2/heme oxygenase-1/glutathione peroxidase 4 defensive axis, yet inhibits ferroptosis in hepatocellular carcinoma and glioblastoma stem cells via non-canonical protein interactions [e.g., circRNA cIARS-mediated BCL-2/BECN1 stabilization ()] or EGFR/SRC-driven nuclear retention that upregulates GCLM and glutathione synthesis (; ; ; ). These divergent functions are further fine-tuned by post-translational modifications, including ROS-induced SUMOylation, KAT8-mediated acetylation, USP36-mediated deubiquitination, and EGFR-activated phosphorylation, which collectively modulate ALKBH5 enzymatic activity, subcellular localization, and substrate specificity (; ; ; ).

ALKBH5 structure

Human ALKBH5 is a 394-residue non-heme FeII/α-ketoglutarate-dependent dioxygenase belonging to the ALKB superfamily (; ). In , its primary structure is organized into three functional segments: an N-terminal alanine-rich region containing a coiled-coil motif that is implicated in nuclear speckle localization (), a central catalytic core (residues 66–292) harboring the canonical FeII-binding HXDXnH motif (His204-Asp206-His266) and the RXnR signature for α-ketoglutarate (2OG) coordination (; ), and a C-terminal intrinsically disordered extension enriched in arginine-serine repeats that likely modulates RNA substrate engagement ().

presents the crystallographic structure of the catalytic domain, revealing a conserved DSBH motif where strands β6, β8, β11, and β13 constitute the primary β-sheet, while β7, β9, β10, and β12 form the secondary counterpart; together these elements provide the active site scaffold (). Within the active site, the catalytic metal center is ligated by a three-residue motif comprising His204, Asp206, and His266. Concurrently, α-ketoglutarate (α-KG, 2-oxoglutarate, 2-OG) within a pocket bounded by the dual β-sheet assembly, while the wider aperture of this groove permits the ingress of single-stranded RNA (ssRNA) substrates (; ). The m6A base is recognized within a pocket comprising Arg130, Tyr139, and His204 (). Three distinctive architectural elements enforce strict selectivity for ssRNA over double-stranded DNA or RNA: (i) the β9-10 extra loop (residues 229–243), which generates steric hindrance against duplex nucleic acids (; ), (ii) an unanticipated Cys230-Cys267 disulfide bond that further occludes the active site from dsDNA/dsRNA access (), and (iii) the nucleotide recognition lid (NRL), subdivided into NRL1 (β2-β3) and NRL2 (β4-β5), which plays a critical role in substrate orientation and catalytic fidelity (; ). Structural comparison reveals that the catalytic chamber of ALKBH5 is considerably more compact than that of FTO. This size differential provides an exploitable structural determinant for achieving selectivity: orthosteric ligands can be tailored to sterically complement the narrower ALKBH5 cavity while clashing with the more expansive FTO active site, thereby discriminating between their respective cofactor and substrate binding domains.

The effect of ALKBH5 in malignant tumors

Functioning as a versatile RNA demethylase, ALKBH5 participates in the pathophysiology of numerous human malignancies and non-malignant conditions alike. highlights its tumor-specific regulatory portfolio: in breast cancer, ovarian carcinoma, glioblastoma, lung cancer, gastrointestinal neoplasms, pancreatic cancer and osteosarcoma, ALKBH5 exerts context-dependent oncogenic or tumor-suppressive functions through the epitranscriptomic control of gene products. Via these downstream effectors, ALKBH5 governs a spectrum of cellular processes encompassing cancer stem cell maintenance, autophagic flux, hypoxic adaptation, and long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) biology, consequently exerting either tumor-suppressive or tumor-promoting effects. Beyond oncology, this demethylase also contributes significantly to pathological conditions such as reproductive dysfunction and aberrant bone mineralization, operating through the perturbation of key signaling cascades including the AKT and NF-κB axes.

By actively stripping m6A modifications from pivotal mRNA substrates, ALKBH5 functions as a principal facilitator of carcinogenesis across multiple solid tumor types, fostering both malignant transformation and sustained neoplastic growth. However, this enzyme acts as both an oncogenic driver in certain malignancies and a tumor suppressor in specific malignant tumors, inhibiting cell proliferation and invasive potential. This functional dichotomy is increasingly attributed to the context-dependent substrate repertoire and spatiotemporal regulatory networks of ALKBH5 ().

A tumor-suppressive identity of ALKBH5 has been uncovered in select carcinoma types, wherein the enzyme exerts post-transcriptional control over transcripts encoding both cancer-promoting and growth-inhibitory factors. In non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), for instance, ALKBH5-driven destabilization of CEMIP mRNA curbs proliferative expansion while simultaneously priming tumor cells for enhanced susceptibility to paclitaxel-induced cell death (). In pancreatic cancer, ALKBH5 promotes WIF-1 transcription and facilitates YTHDF2-dependent PER1 mRNA degradation, collectively suppressing proliferation, colony formation, and migration while improving gemcitabine chemosensitivity (; ). The demethylase also attenuates hepatocellular carcinoma progression by reducing PAQR4 mRNA and protein expression (), and inhibits gastric cancer dissemination through destabilization of PKMYT1 and WRAP53 transcripts (). Furthermore, ALKBH5 confers radiosensitivity in colorectal cancer via circAFF2 modulation (), promotes ferroptosis in thyroid cancer through TIAM1 demethylation, and enhances TLR2 translation to suppress growth and induce apoptosis in hypopharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma (). In osteosarcoma, ALKBH5 increases YTHDF2-dependent degradation of pre-miR-181b-1 and YAP1 to inhibit survival and invasion (; ), whereas in bladder cancer, it enhances CSNK2A1 mRNA stability to restrain malignant progression ().

Conversely, ALKBH5 functions as an oncogenic driver across diverse hematologic and solid tumors by stabilizing pro-tumorigenic mRNAs and disrupting tumor-suppressive pathways. In acute myeloid leukemia and leukemia, ALKBH5 enhances TACC3 () and ITPA () mRNA stability to promote cancer stem cell self-renewal and disease progression, while in multiple myeloma, it stabilizes TRAF1 to support cell survival () and destabilizes SAV1 to facilitate proliferation and neovascularization (). In glioblastoma, ALKBH5 sustains stemness through NANOG and SRY-box transcription factor 2 (SOX2) (; ), promotes cell proliferation and invasion via SPP1 (), and drives radioresistance through upregulation of CHK1, RAD51, FOXM1, and YAP1 () as well as the USP14-ALKBH5 signaling axis (). Solid tumors including renal clear cell carcinoma (), melanoma (; ), glioma (), endometrial cancer (), and cervical cancer () harness ALKBH5 to enhance proliferation, immune evasion, and metastatic dissemination. In non-small cell lung cancer, ALKBH5 stabilizes Janus kinase 2 (JAK2) to induce PD-L1 expression and angiogenesis (), while enhancing DDB2 and XPC mRNA to confer cisplatin resistance (). In the context of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, ALKBH5 actively enhances tumor-mediated immunosuppressive programs, thereby fostering an environment conducive to immune escape () and intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (), drives macrophage recruitment and radioresistance in hepatocellular carcinoma (; ), and supports tumorigenesis and therapeutic resistance in colorectal cancer (; ), gastric cancer (), pancreatic cancer (), triple-negative breast cancer (), and epithelial ovarian cancer ().

Compared with compound 5, modification at the para-position of the benzene ring with small hydrophobic groups improved the potency, as exemplified by 4-chloro (10, IC50 = 2.734 μM) and 4-bromo (7, IC50 = 0.591 μM) (). Notably, relocation of an electron-withdrawing group to the meta-position markedly enhanced the inhibitory activity: the 3-bromo analogue 8 achieved an IC50 of 0.727 μM, whereas the 3-trifluoromethyl derivative 3 emerged as the most potent inhibitor in this series (IC50 = 0.021 μM). As depicted in , molecular docking analysis revealed that all fifteen 1-aryl-1H-pyrazole derivatives (compounds 1–15) adopted a conserved binding orientation within the active pocket of ALKBH5, yet established distinct hydrogen-bonding networks with surrounding residues that rationalize their differential inhibitory potencies. Compound 1 () simultaneously formed hydrogen bonds with Asp206, Ser217, Lys132, and Ser219, establishing a foundational interaction pattern for this series. Compounds 2–4 () maintained critical polar contacts with Arg277, Asp206, His266, Lys132, and Asn193, thereby preserving the core interactions. Compounds 5–8 () continued to engage Arg277, Asn193, Asp206, and Lys132, with compound 6 additionally interacting with Ser217. Notably, compound 9 () displayed the most extensive hydrogen-bonding network among the series, forming multiple polar interactions with Lys132, Asn193, Ser219, Ser217, and Arg277, which likely contributes to its enhanced binding affinity. Compounds 10 and 11 () established hydrogen bonds with Asn193, Arg277, His266, and Lys132. Compounds 12–15 () gained additional stabilization through interactions with Arg277, Lys132, His266, His204, Tyr139, Arg283, and Ser217/Ser219. Collectively, these docking results demonstrate that while Arg277, Asp206, and Lys132 serve as conserved anchor residues for the entire series, the variable hydrogen-bonding engagements with Asn193, His266, Ser217, Ser219, Tyr139, His204, and Arg283 constitute the primary structural determinants modulating the binding strength and inhibitory activity of these ALKBH5 non-covalent inhibitors.

Per Fang et al., a comprehensive series of biochemical and cellular assays was executed to validate the pharmacological properties of compound 3 () (5-hydroxy-1-(3-(trifluoromethyl)phenyl)-1H-pyrazole-3-carboxylic acid), the optimized lead candidate (). Biophysically, compound 3 displayed potent inhibition against ALKBH5 in a fluorescence polarization (FP) assay with an IC50 value of 0.021 ± 0.009 μM, and differential scanning fluorimetry (DSF) confirmed dose-dependent thermal stabilization of the target protein (ΔTm = 8.75 °C) (). Mechanistically, an in vitro dot blot demethylation assay demonstrated that compound 3 efficiently blocked ALKBH5-mediated removal of m6A marks in a concentration-dependent manner. The compound also exhibited high selectivity, showing negligible activity against the paralogue FTO and other AlkB subfamily members (ALKBH1, ALKBH2) in both DSF and FP formats (). Of particular note regarding cellular pharmacology, compound 3 possesses favorable membrane permeability and robust intracellular target engagement, as evidenced by a cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA) showing significant stabilization of ALKBH5 in HepG2 cells compared to DMSO-treated controls (). Furthermore, dot blot analysis of total RNA isolated from HepG2 cells treated with compound 3 revealed a marked, dose-dependent increase in global m6A abundance, confirming that the inhibitor effectively suppresses ALKBH5 demethylase activity in a physiologically relevant intracellular environment. Taken together, these data establish compound 3 as a potent, highly selective, and cell-permeable ALKBH5 inhibitor capable of elevating m6A levels in intact cells, supporting its utility as a chemical probe for exploring the biological functions of ALKBH5 ().

Pyrazolo [1,5-a]pyrimidine derivatives

As shown in , a series of pyrazolo [1,5-a]pyrimidine derivatives (16–21) were designed and synthesized to investigate the structure–activity relationship (SAR) against ALKBH5 (). All compounds share a common pyrazolo [1,5-a]pyrimidine core scaffold, with variations at the C-1 position side chain and the phenyl ring substitution (R1 = H or OH) (). The preliminary SAR analysis revealed that the introduction of a hydroxyl group at the R1 position consistently improved the inhibitory activity across all side-chain types: in , compounds 17 (R1 = -OH, IC50 = 14.44 μM), 19 (IC50 = 7.88 μM), and 21 (IC50 = 2.97 μM) exhibited 1.3-, 1.2-, and 1.6-fold enhanced potency relative to their R1 = H counterparts 16 (IC50 = 18.59 μM), 18 (IC50 = 9.12 μM), and 20 (IC50 = 4.64 μM), respectively (). More significantly, optimization of the C-3 side chain markedly influenced the activity profile. Replacement of the chlorophenyl group (16/17) with ester functionalities (18/19) reduced the IC50 values from double-digit to single-digit micromolar concentrations. Further extension of the ester side chain to incorporate a CF3-containing biphenyl motif (20/21) substantially boosted the ALKBH5 inhibitory potency. Optimization efforts ultimately yielded compound 21 as the most powerful inhibitor within the chemical series. The compound registered an IC50 of 2.97 μM, reflecting a 6.3-fold leap in activity compared with the initial lead compound 16. These results demonstrate that the synergistic modification of the phenyl ring hydroxyl group and the CF3-biphenyl ester side chain at the C-3 position of the pyrazolo [1,5-a]pyrimidine scaffold is a critical strategy for enhancing ALKBH5 inhibitory activity ().

To elucidate the binding mode of pyrazolo [1,5-a]pyrimidine derivatives with ALKBH5 at the molecular level, molecular docking studies were performed using the ALKBH5 crystal structure. As depicted in , compounds 16–21 all bind within the 2-oxoglutarate (2-OG) active site pocket of ALKBH5, yet exhibit distinct interaction patterns dictated by their side-chain architectures. Compound 16 () establishes key hydrogen bonds with ARG-130 (2.6 Å) and ASP-206 (3.1 Å) through its pyrazolo [1,5-a]pyrimidine core and chlorophenyl moiety. The introduction of the phenolic hydroxyl group in 17 () enables an additional hydrogen bond interaction with VAL-202 (2.2 Å) while maintaining contacts with ASP-206 (3.2 Å) and ARG-130 (2.8 Å, 2.6 Å). Compounds 18 () and 19 (), bearing ester side chains, form extended hydrogen-bonding networks with ASP-206, VAL-202, ARG-130, and ARG-277, which correlates with their improved inhibitory potencies. Remarkably, compounds 20 () and 21 (), equipped with the CF3-biphenyl ester side chains, penetrate deeper into the ALKBH5 binding pocket and engage in multiple favorable interactions with pocket residues including ARG-126, VAL-124, GLU-121, HIS-122, TYR-139, and ASP-125. Specifically, 20 forms an extensive hydrogen-bond network with HIS-122 (1.9 Å, 2.6 Å), GLU-121 (2.2 Å), and ASP-125 (3.3–3.5 Å), while 21 forms a strong hydrogen bond with ASP-125 (2.4 Å) alongside hydrophobic contacts with the CF3-biphenyl tail. These docking results rationalize the observed SAR trends: the progressive extension and optimization of the C-1 side chain allow the pyrazolo [1,5-a]pyrimidine derivatives to occupy additional subsites within the ALKBH5 active pocket, thereby establishing more robust intermolecular interactions and significantly enhancing inhibitory efficacy.

In cellular assays, compound 21 () elevated global m6A levels in AML cells, suppressed the proliferation of MOLM-13 and MV4-11 cell lines, achieving IC50 values of 0.45 μM and 1.2 μM, respectively, and showed low toxicity toward normal HEK293 cells and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs). Furthermore, compound 21 induced G0/G1 cell cycle arrest and concentration-dependent apoptosis, mechanistically acting through the ALKBH5-TACC3-c-Myc signaling axis by reducing TACC3 mRNA stability and downstream protein expression. These results collectively establish compound 21 as a potent, selective, and cell-active ALKBH5 inhibitor with a distinct non-2-OG competitive mechanism.

Pyrazolone and benzodioxole derivatives

An extensive high-throughput screening campaign was conducted utilizing the Enamine library of pharmacologically annotated small molecules, two novel hit compounds, Ena15 and Ena21 (; ), were identified as selective inhibitors of ALKBH5 (). As summarized in , Ena15 inhibited ALKBH5 with an IC50 value of 18.3 ± 1.8 μM, whereas Ena21 displayed comparable potency with an IC50 of 15.7 ± 1.0 μM (). Notably, both compounds exhibited divergent selectivity profiles against the homologous demethylase FTO (). At 80 μM, Ena21 showed negligible inhibitory activity toward FTO (36% inhibition), whereas Ena15 not only failed to inhibit FTO but instead enhanced its demethylase activity (−130% inhibition), indicating that both molecules are selective ALKBH5 inhibitors with distinct off-target behaviours ().

Molecular docking simulations were subsequently performed to rationalize the binding behaviors of Eual5 and Eua21 within the ALKBH5 active pocket. As depicted in , the pyrazolone derivative Eual5 anchors in the active site through a network of hydrogen-bonding interactions, specifically engaging ARG-130 (2.3 Å) and TYR-139 (3.3 Å) via its pyrazolone carbonyl and neighboring heteroatoms. Conversely, the benzodioxole derivative Eua21 adopts an extended conformation that projects its aromatic tail toward a distinct subsite, establishing a hydrogen bond with ARG-277 (2.0 Å) through the terminal methoxyphenyl or benzodioxole oxygen atoms (). The divergent interaction profiles of the two chemotypes, Eual5 relying on contacts with the central residues and Eua21 exploiting a peripheral arginine anchor, mirror their comparable yet modest inhibitory potencies. Collectively, these docking analyses reveal that although both scaffolds successfully occupy the ALKBH5 active pocket, the limited number of specific polar interactions and the absence of deep hydrophobic burial indicate substantial room for structure-guided optimization to strengthen ligand-protein affinity.

Imidazobenzoxazine-5-thione derivatives

Starting from the lead compound MV1035, a tricyclic imidazobenzoxazine-5-thione previously shown to inhibit both ALKBH2 and ALKBH5, a series of analogues were designed to dissect the key structural motifs governing activity () (). These findings highlight the ortho-phenol and the propyl/methyl imidazole substituents as indispensable pharmacophoric elements, with compound 22 [2-(4-methyl-5-propyl-1H-imidazole-2-yl)phenol] ()emerging as the most potent analogue in the panel ().

The interaction landscape of imidazobenzoxazine-5-thione derivatives with ALKBH5 was explored through docking calculations. As shown in , the inhibitor adopts a pose in which its thione unit lies within hydrogen-bonding range (2.7 Å) of Arg283, a residue whose guanidinium side chain furnishes a critical electrostatic tether. This polar linkage, together with the snug insertion of the fused benzoxazine–imidazole bicycle into the catalytic cleft, underlies the favorable binding energetics. Surrounding residues contribute additional hydrophobic/van der Waals forces that cradle the planar aromatic surface of the ligand. Moreover, the docking model suggests that substituent variations on the heterocyclic platform alter the degree of shape complementarity toward a nearby hydrophobic microenvironment, thereby modulating inhibitory potency. Such computational evidence aligns with the empirical SAR, pointing to a dual requirement—precise H-bonding to Arg283 and extensive nonpolar burial of the fused ring—for achieving high-affinity ALKBH5 blockade.

Guided by the previously reported putative binding mode of MV1035 with ALKBH5, which partially overlaps the 2-oxoglutarate pocket compound 22 was selected for comprehensive biochemical and cellular validation (). In cell-free demethylase assays, 22 () dose-dependently inhibited ALKBH2 with an approximate IC50 of 25 μM and markedly suppressed ALKBH5 activity, evidenced by a strong, concentration-dependent increase in m6A-RNA signal in dot-blot analyses (10–50 μM). In U87-MG glioblastoma cells, 22 significantly reduced viability in a time- and dose-dependent manner, outperforming the parent MV1035 at 50 μM after 72 h (). These results establish 22 as a simplified yet effective phenylimidazole lead that retains dual ALKBH2/ALKBH5 inhibitory activity, offering a new starting point for the development of glioblastoma-targeted epitranscriptomic agents ().

5-Carboxy-8-hydroxyquinoline derivatives

ALKBH5, an α-KG-dependent demethylase that removes m6A modifications from RNA as part of the “eraser” machinery, is pharmacologically blocked by the small synthetic compound IOX1 (). By selectively blocking the enzymatic activity of ALKBH5, IOX1 restores global m6A methylation levels and subsequently modulates downstream metabolic reprogramming (). In the context of acute myocardial infarction (AMI), IOX1 was identified as a potential therapeutic agent to counteract ALKBH5-overexpression-induced suppression of tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle dehydrogenases and dysregulation of associated amino acid substrates (). To enable targeted delivery to the infarct region, IOX1 was encapsulated within a surface-modified bioengineered ferritin nanocage (HSSS, HAfFtO-ST-SC-Sf) to generate the nanocomplex HSSS-I, though free IOX1 was also evaluated independently for its pharmacological efficacy ().

The ALKBH5 inhibitory activity of IOX1 () was investigated through in vitro cellular assays and in vivo animal studies (). To model I/R pathology, H9C2 cardiomyocytes and rat aortic endothelial cells (RAOEC) were exposed to H2O2 or subjected to hypoxia followed by reoxygenation. Under these conditions, IOX1 treatment effectively counteracted the decline in m6A RNA methylation markers induced by simulated ischemia/reperfusion and normalized the operational capacity of essential TCA cycle enzymes such as fumarase, isocitrate dehydrogenase, and malate dehydrogenase, and normalized the altered concentrations of related amino acid metabolites (). Furthermore, IOX1 markedly improved cell viability, attenuated apoptosis, and reduced the cell death ratio (). In a Sprague-Dawley rat AMI model established by left anterior descending coronary artery ligation and reperfusion, intravenous administration of IOX1 (10 mg/kg, caudal vein, once daily for 14 days) substantially decreased serum levels of the myocardial injury markers creatine kinase-MB (CK-MB) and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), cardiac contractile and diastolic performance were significantly rescued following IOX1 treatment, manifesting as improved left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and left ventricular fractional shortening (LVFS) measurements and a favorable shift in the E/A waveform ratio. The hemodynamic profile was concurrently stabilized, featuring optimized LVSP, reduced ventricular filling pressure (LVEDP), and superior pressure derivative kinetics (±dP/dt). Tissue-level protection was further demonstrated by contracted infarct dimensions, attenuated fibrotic remodeling, and diminished apoptotic burden, as independently verified through Evans blue-TTC viability staining, Masson’s trichrome connective tissue staining, and TUNEL apoptotic labeling ().

ALKBH5 covalent inhibitor

Salicylaldehyde derivatives

Starting from the reference inhibitor DDO-2728 (; , IC50 = 4.06 μM), a scaffold-hopping strategy was pursued in which the triazole-trifluoromethylphenyl fragment was replaced by a picolinic acid-sulfonamide motif, affording compound 23 (). This structural truncation, however, proved detrimental to potency (IC50 = 7.44 μM), indicating that the abbreviated framework alone could not sustain robust target engagement (). Subsequent installation of an ortho-hydroxy and formyl group on the phenyl ring of 23 to generate the salicylaldehyde pharmacophore in compound 24 restored inhibitory efficacy (IC50 = 3.40 μM), representing an approximately 2.2-fold gain over 23 and underscoring the critical role of the salicylaldehyde signature in affinity enhancement (). Expanding the aromatic surface from a phenyl to a naphthalene core in compound 25 furnished the most potent analogue within this chemotype (IC50 = 1.43 μM), improving activity by roughly 2.8-fold relative to the parental DDO-2728 (). Conversely, merely repositioning the hydroxy and formyl substituents around the naphthalene scaffold in regioisomer 26 triggered a severe collapse in activity (IC50 = 8.56 μM), despite retention of identical functional elements. Collectively, these data reveal that the geometric deployment of the salicylaldehyde motif, rather than its mere presence, governs high-affinity ALKBH5 blockade ().

To dissect the atomic underpinnings of inhibition, docking simulations were conducted with representative members of the series. As illustrated in , compound 25 settled into a favorable pose within the ALKBH5 active pocket: the phenolic -OH engaged in hydrogen bonding with TYR-139 (2.2 Å) and ASN-193 (1.9 Å), whereas the aldehyde oxygen simultaneously anchored to the histidine dyad HIS-204 (2.2 Å) and HIS-266 (2.5 Å). The naphthalene nucleus, in turn, nestled against TYR-195 (2.5 Å) and ARG-283 (2.4 Å and 3.1 Å), exploiting both π–π stacking and extensive van der Waals contacts that collectively rationalize its superior potency. By contrast, the altered spatial arrangement in compound 26 disrupted this interaction network, it could only sustain tenuous hydrogen-bond contacts with LYS-132 (2.6 Å), TYR-139 (2.0 Å), and ARG-283 (2.3 Å and 2.5 Å), while forfeiting the crucial histidine-mediated anchoring entirely, an observation that accounts for its markedly attenuated binding. Compound 24, carrying the phenyl-based salicylaldehyde warhead, retained hydrogen-bonding capacity toward ASN-193 (2.0 Å), ARG-277 (2.2 Å), and ASP-206 (2.4 Å), yet lacked the extended conjugation and hydrophobic bulk of the naphthalene variant, resulting in intermediate activity bracketed between 25 and 26. Taken together, the computational evidence points to a dual recognition mechanism: hydrogen-bond tethering of the salicylaldehyde group to the catalytic histidine pair (HIS-204 and HIS-266), coupled with deep hydrophobic accommodation of the naphthalene ring, serves as the structural cornerstone for potent ALKBH5 inhibition within this derivative class.

The optimized covalent inhibitor 25 () was extensively characterized by molecular docking and cellular assays (). Covalent docking against ALKBH5 revealed that the aldehyde group of 25 forms an imine bond with Lys132. Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) confirmed direct binding with a Kd of 2.80 ± 0.55 μM (). Drug affinity responsive target stability (DARTS) and cellular thermal shift assays (CETSA) in MOLM-13 cells further validated target engagement in both cell lysates and living cells (). Mass spectrometry and HPLC-MS/MS unequivocally demonstrated covalent modification of Lys132, while the K132E mutant showed markedly reduced binding and inhibition, corroborating Lys132 as the specific covalent target. 25 exhibited excellent selectivity for ALKBH5 (FP IC50 = 0.49 ± 0.05 μM) over FTO (IC50 = 14.16 ± 1.68 μM) and ALKBH3 (IC50 = 11.71 ± 0.12 μM), and its inhibitory activity was independent of 2-OG concentration (). Functionally, 25 elevated cellular m6A levels in MV4-11 cells, downregulated the ALKBH5-AXL signalling axis (reducing AXL mRNA and protein abundance), promoted G1-stage growth cessation, preventing further cell division, and promoted apoptosis in MOLM-13 cells, underscoring its potential as a selective chemical probe for ALKBH5 ().

Oxindole derivatives

Encouraged by the promising inhibitory activity of the virtual screening hit AG-690/34437009, a focused library of oxindole derivatives (; ; ) was rationally designed and synthesized to systematically probe the structure-activity relationships (SAR) and enhance ALKBH5 inhibitory potency (). Removal of the phenolic hydroxyl group afforded 27 (IC50 = 5.027 μM), which exhibited enhanced potency relative to the parent compound. SAR studies revealed that the 3-bromo-5-nitrophenyl motif is indispensable for activity: Relocating the bromo substituent from the 3- to the 4-position (28, IC50 = 8.532 μM) diminished activity, indicating that the 3-bromo group is optimal for engaging L129 via a second hydrogen bond. Replacement of the 4-chlorophenyl ring with a methoxyl group at the 3- or 4-position yielded 29 (IC50 = 5.608 μM) and 30 (IC50 = 3.848 μM), respectively (). Finally, substitution of the nitro and bromo groups with cyano and fluoro atoms (31 and 32) resulted in reduced activity (IC50 = 6.126 and 8.663 μM, respectively), underscoring the superiority of the 3-bromo-5-nitro combination. Collectively, 30 emerged as the most potent inhibitor within this series ().

As depicted in , the oxindole scaffold is consistently anchored in the metal-binding site through a conserved hydrogen-bonding network that mimics the endogenous α-ketoglutarate cofactor. The C2 carbonyl oxygen of the oxindole lactam acts as a hydrogen-bond acceptor to the side-chain amide of Asn193 (2.0–2.6 Å) and/or the carboxylate of Asp206 (2.3–3.6 Å), while the indole NH donates a hydrogen bond to Asp206 or Arg283 (2.2–2.8 Å), thereby locking the inhibitor in a productive orientation. The 3-aryl substituent extends into a hydrophobic sub-pocket lined by Tyr139, His266, and Arg283, where π-π stacking and van der Waals contacts provide substantial binding energy. Notably, the more potent analogues bear additional polar functionalities on the terminal phenyl ring that engage in auxiliary hydrogen-bonding interactions with Ser217, Ser219, Thr254, or Glu153 (2.1–3.1 Å), effectively bridging the ligand to the solvent-exposed loop region and enhancing affinity. Conversely, analogues lacking these distal polar groups or carrying sterically demanding substituents fail to establish these supplementary contacts, resulting in weaker docking scores and reduced enzymatic inhibition, which is consistent with their attenuated biological activity. Overall, the docking analysis underscores that optimal ALKBH5 inhibition by oxindole derivatives relies on the synergistic contribution of three key elements: (i) oxindole core-mediated metal-cofactor mimicry through Asn193/Asp206, (ii) hydrophobic complementarity within the Tyr139/His266/Arg283 pocket, and (iii) specific polar interactions at the rim of the active site facilitated by Ser217 and Thr254.

Maleimide derivatives

Multiple maleimide-based compounds were synthesized and pharmacologically profiled as prospective ALKBH5 antagonists, encompassing two structural subclasses: as shown in , N-arylethyl maleimides 33–38 and 3,4-diaryl maleimides 39–41 (; ) (; ). In the N-arylethyl series, biochemical assessment by fluorescence polarization (FP) assay identified compound 36, bearing a 4-hydroxyphenylethyl substituent on the maleimide nitrogen, as the analogue with an IC50 of 1.1 μM against ALKBH5 (; ). This activity markedly surpassed its regioisomeric counterparts 34 (2-chloro, IC50 = 4.53 μM) and 35 (3-chloro, IC50 = 0.89 μM), as well as the 2-methoxy derivative 33 (IC50 = 0.62 μM), highlighting the pivotal contribution of the para-hydroxyl group to inhibitory potency (; ). The 2-(2-hydroxyethoxy)phenyl analogue 38 displayed moderate activity (IC50 = 1.76 μM), whereas the ortho-substituted ester-bearing derivative 37 was the least active within this subset (IC50 = 0.79 μM), suggesting that bulky or strongly electron-withdrawing ortho substituents are detrimental to optimal binding (; ). In the 3,4-diaryl maleimide series, compound 40 emerged as the strongest inhibitor with an IC50 of 2.32 μM, outperforming analogues 39 (IC50 = 5.344 μM) and 41 (IC50 = 9.57 μM). Collectively, these structure–activity relationships demonstrate that a para-hydroxyl-decorated N-arylethyl side chain and an appropriately substituted 3,4-diaryl maleimide scaffold are favorable structural motifs for achieving potent ALKBH5 inhibition (; ).

Molecular docking studies were used to rationalize the observed SAR trends and elucidate the binding modes of maleimide derivatives 33–41 within the ALKBH5 catalytic pocket (). Across the series, the maleimide carbonyl core was consistently anchored via a conserved hydrogen-bonding network involving Tyr139, Asn193, and Arg283. Notably, the inhibitor 36 (IC50 = 1.1 μM) established supplementary polar interactions with Ser217 (2.2 Å) and Arg277 (2.1 Å) mediated by its para-hydroxyl group, thereby reinforcing ligand anchoring beyond the primary maleimide-protein contacts. By contrast, the less active ortho-substituted analogues 33 and 37 engaged only the canonical triad plus a distal contact with His266 (2.8 Å) or Asn193 (2.2 Å), respectively, lacking the additional edge-to-face stabilization afforded by the para-hydroxyl motif. Within the 3,4-diaryl maleimide subset, the superior activity of compound 40 (IC50 = 2.32 μM) correlated with its capacity to form dual hydrogen bonds with Arg283 (1.9 and 2.0 Å) alongside interactions with Tyr139 (2.7 Å) and Asn193 (2.3 Å), whereas the weakest analogue 41 (IC50 = 9.57 μM) forfeited one of these Arg283 contacts and presented a more restricted interaction network limited to Asn193 (2.7 Å) and Arg283 (2.1 Å). Taken together, the docking analyses reveal that inhibitory potency across the maleimide series is governed by the extent of hydrogen-bonding complementarity with the catalytic pocket, particularly the ability to engage auxiliary residues such as Ser217 and Arg277 in addition to the central Tyr139/Asn193/Arg283 anchor.

Challenges in the development of ALKBH5 inhibitors

Hurdles of selectivity

As members of the FeII/α-KG-dependent oxygenase family, ALKBH5 and FTO exhibit a shared structural blueprint centered on the canonical DSBH fold. This conserved topology incorporates the functionally essential His-Asp-His triad for metal ion ligation and a dedicated 2-oxoglutarate (2-OG) binding cavity (; ). Despite sharing the same superfamily, ALKBH5 possesses a markedly more compact catalytic pocket than its paralog FTO, providing a structural basis for selective orthosteric inhibition (), the high topological similarity between their catalytic pockets remains the principal source of off-target risk. Most reported ALKBH5 inhibitors are either 2-OG analogs or pan-inhibitors that simultaneously target both FTO and ALKBH5, whereas truly selective small molecules are extremely scarce (). Recent progress has demonstrated that covalent strategies targeting ALKBH5-unique residues, such as the maleimide derivative 33 binding Cys200 () or the salicylaldehyde-based compound 25 forming an imine with Lys132 () can achieve excellent selectivity over FTO and ALKBH3. Likewise, structure-guided optimization of maleimide derivatives has furnished potent and selective ALKBH5 blockers by engaging non-catalytic cysteine residues (). Nevertheless, how to systematically exploit these subtle structural differences, such as the nucleotide recognition lid (NRL) and the Cys230-Cys267 disulfide bond to finely discriminate ALKBH5 from FTO while retaining potent target engagement remains an unresolved challenge at the interface of structural biology and medicinal chemistry.

Optimization of druggability

The transition from lead compounds to clinical candidates is frequently impeded by suboptimal metabolic stability, limited oral bioavailability, and potential systemic toxicity. For instance, although IOX1 effectively inhibits ALKBH5 and restores global m6A levels, its encapsulation within surface-modified bioengineered ferritin nanocages (HSSS) was required to achieve targeted delivery to the infarct region, implicitly indicating unfavorable pharmacokinetic properties of the free compound (). Similarly, the optimized covalent inhibitor compound 25 exhibits high binding affinity (Kd = 2.80 ± 0.55 μM by ITC) and favorable selectivity for ALKBH5 over FTO and ALKBH3 (; ). However, the metabolic lability of its aldehyde warhead and the risk of off-target covalent modification necessitate rigorous preclinical safety profiling. It is also noteworthy that ALKBH5 is indispensable for male spermatogenesis and oocyte meiotic maturation (; ), implying that chronic systemic inhibition may elicit unanticipated adverse effects such as reproductive toxicity. Consequently, candidate drugs must be equipped with robust tumor-targeting delivery strategies or localized administration routes to widen the therapeutic window, further complicating the druggability optimization landscape.

To safely administer ALKBH5 inhibitors systemically, three parallel risk-mitigation strategies should be pursued. (i) Tumor-targeted delivery systems: As exemplified by the ferritin nanocage encapsulation of IOX1 (HSSS-I) for myocardial infarction (), nanoparticle-based or antibody-drug conjugate platforms could restrict ALKBH5 inhibition to the tumor microenvironment, minimizing systemic exposure and reproductive toxicity. (ii) PROTAC-mediated targeted degradation: Unlike occupancy-driven inhibitors, PROTACs achieve catalytic target elimination and may allow lower effective doses, reducing off-tissue effects. The established FTO-PROTAC framework () provides a practical blueprint for developing ALKBH5-specific degraders, which could selectively degrade ALKBH5 in tumor cells while sparing normal tissues. (iii) Inducible or localized administration routes: For solid tumors, intratumoral injection or hypoxia-activated prodrugs could confine ALKBH5 inhibition to the tumor niche, avoiding the physiological requirements of ALKBH5 in testis and ovary ().

Mechanisms of resistance

Long-term ALKBH5 inhibition is anticipated to provoke adaptive escape through three convergent mechanisms. First, target-dependent alterations. Tumor cells may acquire resistance mutations at the catalytic site or covalent-target residues (Lys132, Cys200) that weaken inhibitor binding. More importantly, post-translational modifications function as adaptive molecular switches: USP36-mediated deubiquitination stabilizes ALKBH5 protein to compensate for pharmacological blockade, whereas KAT8-driven acetylation and EGFR-activated phosphorylation dynamically modulate enzymatic activity and substrate specificity (; ; ). Second, epitranscriptomic bypass and metabolic reprogramming. Cancer cells can counteract by elevating the paralogue demethylase FTO or rewiring methyltransferase complexes to maintain m6A homeostasis (). Additionally, tumors may circumvent ALKBH5 dependency by activating compensatory metabolic routes—such as shifting from ALKBH5-regulated GLUT4-mediated glycolysis to alternative energy pathways—to sustain proliferation and therapeutic evasion (). Third, microenvironment-driven heterogeneity and immune evasion. Intratumoral hypoxia transcriptionally upregulates FTO, which elevates PD-L1 and blunts T-cell attack, creating an immune-evasive niche that persists despite ALKBH5 suppression (). In glioblastoma, radiation-responsive activation of the MST4-USP14 axis stabilizes ALKBH5 and enhances DNA repair, indicating that DNA-damage therapies may inadvertently select for resistant clones (). Collectively, these multi-layered mechanisms underscore that monotherapy will likely face rapid adaptive escape, necessitating combination strategies that concurrently neutralize compensatory nodes.

Future prospects for the development of ALKBH5 inhibitors

Development of novel inhibitors

The continuous elucidation of ALKBH5 crystal structures has furnished atomic-level templates for structure-guided inhibitor design. The evolutionarily preserved double-stranded β-helix (DSBH) topology is complemented by distinctive structural motifs, including the nucleotide-binding lid and a cysteine bridge connecting Cys230 and Cys267, provides druggable pockets that can be exploited to discriminate ALKBH5 from its paralog FTO (). Recent successes in integrating fluorescence polarization-based high-throughput screening with rational SAR optimization, exemplified by the development of 1-aryl-1H-pyrazole derivatives and pyrazolo [1,5-a]pyrimidine scaffolds, demonstrate that iterative medicinal chemistry campaigns can deliver sub-micromolar, selective ALKBH5 inhibitors (; ). Moreover, the emergence of covalent inhibitors targeting non-catalytic cysteine or lysine residues has opened new chemical space for achieving exquisite selectivity (; ; ). Looking forward, artificial intelligence-driven generative models and deep-learning-based affinity prediction are expected to accelerate hit-to-lead optimization beyond traditional SAR exploration. The development of bifunctional degraders such as PROTACs, as well as dual-target inhibitors simultaneously engaging ALKBH5 and complementary epigenetic nodes, represents a promising next-generation strategy that may overcome the limitations of conventional occupancy-driven inhibition.

Combination therapeutic strategies

Given the pleiotropic role of ALKBH5 in driving therapy resistance, monotherapy with ALKBH5 inhibitors may face rapid adaptive escape; therefore, rational combination regimens are essential. Preclinical evidence strongly supports co-administration with cytotoxic chemotherapy: ALKBH5 inhibition destabilizes oncogenic transcripts such as TACC3 and ITPA in acute myeloid leukemia (), restores sensitivity to paclitaxel in non-small cell lung cancer (), enhances gemcitabine efficacy in pancreatic cancer (), overcomes cisplatin resistance in gastric and epithelial ovarian cancers (; ), and resensitizes triple-negative breast cancer to doxorubicin. Furthermore, ALKBH5-mediated radioresistance in glioblastoma and hepatocellular carcinoma indicates that ALKBH5 blockade may function as an effective radiosensitizer when combined with ionizing radiation (). Notably, ALKBH5 regulates PD-L1 expression and immune evasion across multiple malignancies, including non-small cell lung cancer, intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, and glioma (), and modulates the tumor immune microenvironment to influence anti-PD-1 therapy response. Consequently, combining ALKBH5 inhibitors with immune checkpoint blockade holds substantial potential to convert immunologically “cold” tumors into “hot” ones, thereby achieving durable antitumor immunity.

Clinical translation

Despite robust preclinical efficacy, no ALKBH5-targeted agent has yet entered human clinical trials, underscoring the urgent need to bridge the gap between bench and bedside. The clinical development trajectory of epigenetic demethylase inhibitors offers valuable guidance. Given the context-dependent dichotomy of ALKBH5 function—oncogenic in acute myeloid leukemia, glioblastoma, and breast cancer, yet tumor-suppressive in hepatocellular carcinoma and pancreatic cancer ()—blanket systemic inhibition carries inherent risks of paradoxically promoting tumor growth in specific malignancies. To mitigate this, we propose a biomarker-guided precision enrollment strategy analogous to HER2-targeted therapy or PD-L1 companion diagnostics. Specifically, ALKBH5 expression levels and m6A landscape signatures should serve as predictive biomarkers for patient stratification. Tumors with confirmed ALKBH5 overexpression and concomitant m6A hypomethylation (e.g., AML with elevated TACC3/AXL/ITPA, glioblastoma with NANOG/FOXM1 upregulation) represent the primary target population (; ; ). Conversely, malignancies where ALKBH5 acts as a tumor suppressor (e.g., HCC with PAQR4 destabilization, pancreatic cancer with PER1 activation) should be excluded from monotherapy trials (; ). Furthermore, dynamic m6A monitoring during treatment could function as a pharmacodynamic biomarker, ensuring on-target engagement without exceeding thresholds that would disrupt physiological spermatogenesis or oocyte maturation (). For instance, the LSD1 inhibitor LH-1802, discovered through structure-based optimization, has successfully advanced into Phase I clinical trials for relapsed or refractory acute myeloid leukemia and myelodysplastic syndromes, demonstrating that targeted demethylase inhibitors can achieve acceptable safety profiles in hematologic indications (). This precedent suggests that ALKBH5 inhibitors should initially prioritize hematologic malignancies, where ALKBH5 oncogenic dependency is well established and patient stratification via biomarker-driven enrichment is most feasible, before expanding into solid tumor indications. Moreover, given the indispensable physiological roles of ALKBH5 in male spermatogenesis and oocyte meiotic maturation (; ), early-phase clinical trials must incorporate rigorous reproductive toxicity monitoring, pharmacokinetic–pharmacodynamic modeling, and dose-escalation designs to ensure an adequate therapeutic index. Ultimately, the establishment of standardized m6A biomarkers and the execution of well-designed Phase I/II studies will be pivotal in realizing the clinical potential of ALKBH5-directed epitranscriptomic therapy.

Comparative therapeutic landscape: ALKBH5 versus METTL3 inhibition

It is instructive to contextualize ALKBH5-directed “eraser” inhibition against parallel efforts targeting METTL3, the catalytic “writer” of m6A. METTL3 is frequently overexpressed in AML, where it drives leukemogenesis by stabilizing oncogenic transcripts (e.g., MYC, BCL2, MDM2) (). Three classes of METTL3 modulators have emerged: substrate-competitive inhibitors (e.g., STM2457, UZH2), allosteric inhibitors (e.g., CDIBA, Eltrombopag), and PROTAC degraders (e.g., STC-15, KH12) ().

These two epitranscriptomic strategies face distinct challenges. METTL3 competitive inhibitors target the conserved S-adenosylmethionine (SAM)-binding pocket, risking cross-reactivity with other methyltransferases (), whereas recent ALKBH5 covalent inhibitors (e.g., compounds 25, 33) exploit unique non-catalytic residues (Lys132, Cys200) and a more compact active site, achieving superior selectivity over FTO (; ). Conversely, both METTL3 and ALKBH5 inhibitors may disrupt normal physiological functions—METTL3 inhibition impairs erythropoiesis (), while ALKBH5 loss affects spermatogenesis and oocyte maturation (; )—necessitating careful dosing. Resistance mechanisms also converge: compensatory upregulation of FTO or METTL14 may respectively bypass ALKBH5 or METTL3 blockade (; ).

Clinically, METTL3-targeted agents have advanced further: STC-15 has entered Phase I trials, and STM2457 shows robust anti-leukemia efficacy (; ). In contrast, no ALKBH5 inhibitor has yet reached clinical evaluation. However, ALKBH5 inhibitors offer unique advantages in targeting cancer stem cell self-renewal, immune evasion (via PD-L1 regulation), and glioblastoma stemness (; ; ). Thus, “writer” and “eraser” inhibition represent complementary pillars of m6A-targeted oncology, and lessons from the METTL3 clinical trajectory—biomarker-driven stratification and combination with chemotherapy or venetoclax—should inform future ALKBH5 drug development.

Conclusion

This review systematically summarizes the research advances of ALKBH5 as an m6A demethylase in tumor epitranscriptomic regulation and the current status of small-molecule inhibitor development, elucidating its core value as a precision therapeutic target in the RNA epigenetic landscape. ALKBH5 exerts oncogenic driving effects in acute myeloid leukemia, glioblastoma, and breast cancer by demethylating and stabilizing target mRNAs such as FOXM1, and NANOG, in an opposing pathological setting, the enzyme operates as a growth-inhibitory factor in both hepatocellular carcinoma and pancreatic neoplasms. Leveraging unique architectural features revealed by ALKBH5 crystal structures, including the double-stranded β-helix (DSBH) fold, the nucleotide recognition lid, and the Cys230-Cys267 disulfide bond, researchers have successfully developed multiple potent inhibitors: non-covalent chemotypes such as 1-aryl-1H-pyrazole derivatives (IC50 = 0.021 μM), pyrazolo [1,5-a]pyrimidine scaffolds, and IOX1, and covalent inhibitors achieving exquisite selectivity by targeting Cys200 or Lys132, as exemplified by the maleimide derivative compound 33, the salicylaldehyde-based compound 25 (FP IC50 = 0.49 μM), and oxindole derivatives. Nevertheless, critical bottlenecks constraining clinical translation persist, including off-target risks arising from the high structural homology between ALKBH5 and FTO, the metabolic lability of certain lead compounds and potential reproductive toxicity, and adaptive resistance mechanisms involving USP36-mediated deubiquitinative upregulation or compensatory m6A pathway rewiring. Looking forward, artificial intelligence-assisted drug design, degradationproteolysis-targeting chimera (PROTAC)-mediated targeted degradation, ALKBH5/ALKBH2 dual-target inhibitors, and combination strategies with chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or immune checkpoint blockade are expected to offer novel avenues for overcoming drug resistance and widening the therapeutic window. Drawing on the successful advancement of the LSD1 inhibitor LH-1802 into Phase I clinical trials, ALKBH5 inhibitors should prioritize biomarker-guided clinical validation in hematologic malignancies, accompanied by the establishment of standardized m6A dynamic monitoring systems. In conclusion, the in-depth development of ALKBH5 inhibitors not only holds promise for delivering precision epitranscriptome-targeted therapeutic regimens for malignant tumors but also provides indispensable chemical probes for dissecting the biological functions of ALKBH5, carrying significant scientific importance and broad clinical application prospects. Crucially, the clinical application of ALKBH5 inhibitors must transcend a ‘one-size-fits-all’ paradigm. Rigorous patient stratification based on ALKBH5 expression status, tumor-specific m6A signatures, and integrated biomarker monitoring—coupled with advanced drug delivery technologies—will be indispensable to harness the therapeutic potential of ALKBH5 inhibition while averting context-dependent adverse outcomes, including the inadvertent promotion of tumor growth in ALKBH5 tumor-suppressive malignancies.
