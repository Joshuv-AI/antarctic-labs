Adult-Lifespan Segment-Specific Normative Atlas of Cervical Spinal Cord Morphology, With Exploratory Application in Degenerative Cervical Myelopathy
Authors: Shuo Duan, Tianhua Rong, Duo Zhang, Guanmei Cao, Lei Wang, Bingxuan Wu, Baining Zhang, Yechuan Mei, Yi Lu, Baoge Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo establish an adult-lifespan segment-specific normative atlas of cervical spinal cord morphometry as a physiologically adjusted reference and to explore its application in degenerative cervical myelopathy (DCM). MethodsWe included 829 healthy volunteers and 210 surgically treated DCM patients undergoing cervical magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) from 2019 to 2023. Follow-up MRI was obtained in a stratified random sample of 207 healthy volunteers and 42 patients. Axial T2-weighted images at C2–3 to C6–7 were segmented semiautomatically. Cross-sectional area (CSA) was the primary morphometric measure; right-left and anteroposterior diameters (APDs) were complementary measures. In healthy volunteers, sex-stratified, level-specific models including age, age², magnetic field strength, and scanner manufacturer were used to construct the atlas and compute z-scores. In DCM, z-scores were summarized as CompMinZ and NonCompMinZ and explored in relation to clinical outcomes. ResultsIn healthy volunteers, CSA showed a nonlinear pattern, with smaller values at older ages. Longitudinal changes were age dependent: CSA, right-left diameter, and APD increased in younger adults, were stable in midlife, and declined in older adults. In DCM, z-scores were most negative at compressed levels, while noncompressed segments also frequently fell below the normative mean. Z-score metrics showed exploratory associations with neurological status and postoperative outcomes. In the postoperative subset, CompMinZ showed the strongest association with recovery rate, whereas maximum spinal cord compression was not significantly associated with recovery rate. ConclusionAn adult-lifespan, segment-specific normative atlas of cervical spinal cord morphometry provides a physiologically adjusted reference for interpreting cervical cord morphologic variation and deviation, with exploratory application in surgically treated DCM.

INTRODUCTION

Degenerative cervical myelopathy (DCM) is a leading cause of nontraumatic spinal cord dysfunction in adults and commonly presents with gait disturbance, impaired hand dexterity, and limb numbness and weakness, resulting in substantial functional limitation [1-3]. Increasing evidence suggests that neurologic impairment in DCM cannot be explained by focal mechanical compression alone, but reflects the combined effects of extrinsic compression and secondary intramedullary injury, including demyelination, axonal loss, and chronic tissue loss involving both gray and white matter [4-6]. Accordingly, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-based assessment of spinal cord injury has become increasingly important in DCM, not only for identifying the site of compression, but also for characterizing the structural consequences of the disease [7-9].

Among morphologic measures available on routine clinical MRI, spinal cord cross-sectional area (CSA) is one of the most practical because it can be derived directly from axial images and generally shows acceptable reproducibility [10]. Prior studies have suggested that smaller cervical CSA is associated with greater neurologic impairment [11-13]. Studies in DCM have also shown that reduced cervical CSA is associated with worse neurologic function, including lower Japanese Orthopaedic Association (JOA) scores [14,15]. However, absolute CSA values are difficult to interpret at the individual level because they vary systematically across cervical levels and are strongly influenced by age and sex [10,16,17]. In DCM, interpretation of CSA is more complex. Focal compression can distort the cord locally, and adjacent segments may also be affected, so the measured CSA may not reflect compression alone. In addition, differences in MRI sequences and acquisition parameters may influence cord boundary definition and contribute to measurement variability, which can complicate direct comparison across individuals, time points, and scanners [18]. Even in healthy adults, age-related morphometric changes are usually gradual, so a single measurement may still fall within the normal range unless interpreted against an appropriate reference. As a result, CSA alone is of limited value as a standardized marker of disease-related morphologic change.

To improve the comparability of cervical spinal cord morphometry, several normalization strategies have been proposed, including use of the pontomedullary junction as a spatial reference to reduce level dependence, adjustment for physiologic covariates (e.g., sex, cord length, and brain-derived metrics), and template-based automated pipelines that report morphometric measures in a common standardized space [19-22]. These approaches have advanced quantitative spinal cord imaging, but they do not fully resolve a practical problem in DCM: how to determine whether a measured cord abnormality exceeds what would be expected from normal variation across age, sex, and segmental level. In routine clinical practice, cervical MRI is interpreted primarily at the disc level, and a physiologically adjusted, segment-specific reference remains lacking. As a result, even when morphometric measurements are geometrically standardized, their interpretation at the individual patient level remains challenging in degenerative cervical cord compression.

Against this background, we aimed to establish a segmentspecific, age-, sex-, and acquisition-adjusted normative atlas of cervical spinal cord CSA across the adult lifespan, supported by longitudinal follow-up in a subset of healthy volunteers. This framework was intended to provide a physiologically adjusted reference for distinguishing normal adult variation from disease-related morphologic deviation at the individual level. We then explored its application in surgically treated DCM by quantifying segment-level deviation patterns and examining their relationships with baseline neurologic status and postoperative outcomes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1. Participants and Clinical Assessment

This study was approved by the institutional review board (IRB) of Beijing Tiantan Hospital, Capital Medical University (IRB No. KY2020-073-02). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. This was a single-center observational study of healthy volunteers and patients with DCM who underwent cervical spine operation at our institution between January 2019 and December 2023. Restricting enrollment to surgical cases provided a clinically homogeneous group and made postoperative imaging follow-up feasible.

For longitudinal evaluation, between January 2024 and January 2025, a stratified random subset of previously enrolled healthy volunteers and surgically treated DCM patients was selected for follow-up cervical MRI. To ensure demographic representativeness, participants were selected through stratified random sampling based on age and sex, with a computer-generated random sequence applied within each stratum. Follow-up imaging in healthy volunteers was used to characterize temporal morphometric changes across adulthood, while in patients with DCM it enabled assessment of postoperative cord morphology relative to the preoperative baseline. All DCM patients had undergone preoperative neurological evaluation with the JOA score, and those who returned for follow-up imaging were reassessed clinically at the same visit, from which the JOA recovery rate was derived.

2. Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Healthy volunteers were eligible if they were 20 to 80 years of age, had no myelopathic symptoms or signs, no significant neck pain within the preceding 3 months, and no MRI evidence of cervical spinal cord compression. Patients with DCM were eligible if they were 20 to 80 years of age, had clinical signs and symptoms of myelopathy, and had cervical spinal cord compression on MRI consistent with the diagnosis of DCM. To maintain a relatively homogeneous surgical DCM cohort, only patients who subsequently underwent surgical treatment were included in the patient group.

Exclusion criteria for both groups were prior cervical spine surgery, traumatic spinal cord injury, spinal infection, spinal or intramedullary tumor, ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament and/or ligamentum flavum, inflammatory myelopathy, and inadequate image quality or substantial artifacts precluding reliable morphometric assessment. Healthy volunteers were also excluded if MRI showed any evidence of cervical cord compression, whereas patients were excluded if cord compression was attributable to nondegenerative causes.

3. MRI Acquisition and Morphometric Measures

All cervical MRI examinations were performed at our institution using 1.5-T or 3.0-T scanners from multiple vendors under routine clinical acquisition conditions. Axial T2-weighted images were obtained at intervertebral disc levels from C2–3 to C6–7. At each level, 3 axial slices were prescribed according to a predefined protocol, corresponding to the inferior endplate of the cranial vertebra, the mid-disc level, and the superior endplate of the caudal vertebra. Imaging planes were planned to be as perpendicular as possible to the spinal cord centerline and as parallel as possible to the adjacent endplates (Fig. 1).

All axial T2-weighted images were stored in DICOM format and converted to NIfTI format for analysis. To improve boundary delineation on routine axial images, super-resolution reconstruction was applied before segmentation while preserving the original physical image dimensions through image geometry metadata [23]. Semiautomatic spinal cord segmentation was performed using the Spinal Cord Toolbox (ver. 7.2) [19] to generate spinal cord masks. All masks were reviewed by 2 senior physicians (1 spine surgeon and 1 radiologist, each with more than 10 years of experience), with manual correction performed when necessary, particularly at compressed levels. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus, and consensus masks were used for subsequent analyses.

Morphometric parameters derived from the consensus masks included CSA, anteroposterior diameter (APD), and right-left diameter (RLD) (Fig. 1). For healthy volunteers and noncompressed DCM segments, segment-level values were calculated as the mean of the 3 predefined axial slices at each disc level to represent overall segmental cord morphology and reduce slicelevel variability. For compressed DCM segments, the single slice showing the greatest focal compression was used to capture the maximal local morphologic deviation at the site of mechanical compression. To quantify the potential influence of this sliceselection difference, we performed a sensitivity analysis in the healthy-volunteer cohort by comparing the 3-slice average CSA with the minimum single-slice CSA at each disc level from C2–3 to C6–7. The relative difference was calculated as (CSA_threeslice mean − CSA_single-slice minimum)/CSA_three-slice mean×100%. For each segment in DCM, compression severity was graded independently by the 2 senior physicians as 0 (no compression), 1 (mild compression), or 2 (severe compression), and disagreements were resolved by consensus [15].

To assess CSA measurement reliability, all available paired independent physician measurements and finalized corrected measurements used in the main analysis were compared. Agreement was evaluated using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) based on a 2-way mixed-effects absolute-agreement model, with mean absolute difference and root mean square error used to quantify measurement discrepancy.

4. Normative Modeling of CSA and Z-Score-Based Phenotypes

To standardize morphometric assessment in DCM while accounting for physiological and acquisition-related variation, we constructed a segment-specific normative atlas of cervical spinal cord CSA using the healthy-volunteer cohort. For each disc level from C2–3 to C6–7 and for each sex, CSA was modeled as a function of age, age [2], magnetic field strength, and scanner manufacturer. This approach provided an expected mean CSA for a given segment and individual profile, together with the residual standard deviation (SD) of the healthy reference distribution.

For each segment in patients with DCM, CSA was converted to a z-score using the difference between the observed CSA and the corresponding model-predicted mean, divided by the residual SD of the reference model. Negative z-scores indicated CSA values below the expected reference mean. For comparison with a conventional geometric compression metric, maximum spinal cord compression (MSCC) was also calculated. MSCC was represented by the CSA compression ratio, calculated as the minimum CSA at compressed levels divided by the mean CSA of noncompressed levels; lower values indicate greater compression severity.

To summarize segment-level deviation patterns in DCM, we defined 2 z-score-based phenotypes. CompMinZ was defined as the minimum z-score among compressed segments and was intended to reflect the greatest local morphometric deviation at sites of mechanical compression. NonCompMinZ was defined as the minimum z-score among noncompressed segments and was intended to reflect morphometric deviation outside the compressed levels.

5. Clinical Outcome and Postoperative Follow-up Assessment

Clinical neurological status was assessed using the JOA score. Preoperative JOA was available for the full DCM cohort, whereas postoperative JOA was assessed in the subset of patients who completed postoperative follow-up. The JOA recovery rate was calculated as (postoperative JOA−preoperative JOA)/(17−preoperative JOA), with higher values indicating greater neurological recovery.

For patients with paired postoperative MRI, the last available follow-up scan was used for longitudinal morphometric assessment. CSA z-scores were calculated using the same normative atlas framework as the preoperative scans. To evaluate postoperative morphometric evolution at the cervical cord level, CSA z-scores were compared between preoperative and last follow-up MRI across all cervical disc levels from C2–3 to C6–7. This longitudinal analysis was not restricted to surgically decompressed levels, but was intended to characterize whole-cervical-cord morphometric changes over time in the postoperative follow-up subset. Because the number of patients with paired postoperative MRI was limited, postoperative morphometric and clinical outcome analyses were considered exploratory.

6. Statistical Analysis

Associations between atlas-based z-score metrics and clinical outcomes were assessed using Spearman correlation analysis. In the full DCM cohort, CompMinZ and NonCompMinZ were correlated with preoperative JOA score. In the postoperative follow-up subset, CompMinZ, NonCompMinZ, and MSCC were correlated with postoperative JOA score and JOA recovery rate. Patients were also stratified according to a prespecified preoperative JOA threshold, and key imaging metrics were compared between severity groups. To further assess whether atlasbased metrics provided explanatory information beyond MSCC, exploratory hierarchical ordinary least-squares regression analyses were performed. For preoperative JOA score in the full DCM cohort, the base model included age, sex, and MSCC, and the expanded model additionally included NonCompMinZ. For postoperative JOA score and JOA recovery rate in the postoperative follow-up subset, the base models included preoperative JOA score and MSCC, and the expanded models additionally included CompMinZ. Model improvement was evaluated using adjusted R2, Akaike information criterion (AIC), and nested model F-change tests. Continuous variables were standardized before ordinary least-squares regression analyses, whereas sex was retained as a binary variable. For each regression model, analyses were performed using complete cases with available data for all variables included in that model. Because of the limited postoperative follow-up sample size, postoperative regression analyses were considered exploratory. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

1. Adult-Lifespan Trajectories and Segment-Specific Normative Atlas in Healthy Volunteers

A total of 829 healthy volunteers were included in the healthy reference cohort (mean age, 45.97±14.24 years), including 335 men (44.67±14.11 years) and 494 women (46.85±14.28 years). Demographic characteristics stratified by age band and sex are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. After requiring complete morphometric measurements and acquisition metadata for hardware-adjusted normative modeling, 827 healthy volunteers were included in the final normative atlas analysis.

Cervical spinal cord CSA in healthy volunteers demonstrated a nonlinear trajectory across the adult lifespan (Fig. 2). In men, CSA displayed a turning point around age 40, after which values tended to decline. In women, the trajectory was comparatively flatter, with a less pronounced inflection—underscoring the need for age- and sex-adjustment when interpreting individual-level cord morphology. Supplementary Table 2 summarizes descriptive age- and sex-stratified CSA values and mean−2 SD reference thresholds for each cervical level. To visualize agerelated morphologic variation, we generated mean axial cervical cord templates for male and female healthy volunteers within 3 age groups (20–39, 40–59, and ≥60 years) (Fig. 3).

Independent physician CSA measurements showed excellent agreement with the finalized corrected CSA measurements. Segment-specific analyses showed consistently high agreement, with ICCs exceeding 0.94 across all cervical segments (Supplementary Table 3). These findings support the reliability of the CSA measurement workflow.

Using the cross-sectional healthy volunteer cohort, we fitted segment- and sex-specific regression models to estimate the expected spinal cord CSA at each disc level from C2–3 to C6–7. In the revised hardware-adjusted framework, CSA was modeled as a function of age, age2, magnetic field strength, and scanner manufacturer. These models provided individualized predicted CSA values and residual standard deviations for z-score computation. The fitted curves supported nonlinear adult-lifespan trajectories of CSA, with sex-specific patterns and smaller values at older ages. Supplementary Table 4 presents the model fit comparison between the base normative models including age and age2 and the expanded hardware-adjusted models additionally including magnetic field strength and scanner manufacturer. Reported metrics include R2, adjusted R2, AIC, ΔAIC, F-change p-values, and residual SDs used for patient-level z-score calculation. Supplementary Table 5 presents the hardware-related regression coefficients, including magnetic field strength and scanner manufacturer effects.

2. Longitudinal Morphometric Changes in Healthy Volunteers

Of the 209 healthy volunteers who completed repeat MRI, 2 were excluded due to inadequate image quality, and the remaining 207 were included in longitudinal analyses. The mean followup interval was 29.26±13.61 months. Overall, annualized morphometric changes were small across all cervical levels, consistent with the expectation that year-to-year variation in absolute CSA is modest at the individual level and supports the rationale for reference-based rather than absolute interpretation. Sex-specific sample sizes and mean ages at baseline and follow-up within each decade are summarized in Supplementary Table 6.

Age-stage-dependent patterns were nonetheless detectable at the group level (Fig. 4). Among younger participants (20–39 years), CSA, RLD, and APD tended to increase across segments over the follow-up interval, with men showing larger annualized gains than women. This pattern shifted in the middle-aged group (40–59 years): men showed a tendency toward CSA and APD decline, whereas women remained largely stable or showed a slight upward trend, and RLD changed relatively little in either sex. In participants aged 60 years or older, CSA, RLD, and APD declined more consistently across segments in both sexes, regardless of the level examined.

3. Exploratory Deviation Mapping in Surgically Treated DCM

The DCM cohort included 210 surgically treated patients, comprising 143 men (mean age, 56.70±10.00 years) and 67 women (60.07±9.45 years). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 1, compression most frequently involved C4–5 and C5–6, with C5–6 accounting for the largest number of severe compressions (grade 2). Segmental z-score distributions are shown in Fig. 5. Segment-level CSA z-scores were most negative at compressed segments, while noncompressed segments also commonly fell below the normative mean. These findings indicate that mor-phologic deviation in DCM was not limited to the maximally compressed level, although the magnitude of deviation varied across segments and individuals.

4. Exploratory Associations With Baseline Neurologic Status

Based on preoperative JOA score, 93 patients were classified as severe (JOA ≤11) and 117 as mild to moderate (JOA ≥12). In the full preoperative DCM cohort, CompMinZ did not differ significantly between severity groups and was not significantly correlated with preoperative JOA (Supplementary Fig. 2). In contrast, NonCompMinZ showed a weak but significant positive correlation with preoperative JOA, indicating that less negative deviation outside compressed segments was associated with better baseline neurologic status (Supplementary Fig. 3). These findings suggest that extracompression morphometric deviation may be related to baseline neurologic status, although the association was modest.

5. Exploratory Postoperative Follow-up Analyses

Among the 42 patients with paired postoperative MRI, the mean follow-up interval was 20.88±12.98 months. CSA z-scores were compared between preoperative and last follow-up scans across all cervical levels from C2–3 to C6–7, rather than only at decompressed surgical levels. At the last follow-up, CSA z-scores remained below the healthy reference level across most segments and were overall lower than preoperative values, with significant decreases at C2–3, C3–4, and C6–7 but not at C4–5 or C5–6 (Supplementary Fig. 4). These findings suggest that cervical cord morphometric deviation may persist or progress at several levels during postoperative follow-up.

Mean postoperative JOA score at final follow-up was 14.00± 1.99, compared with 11.44±2.54 preoperatively, and the mean JOA recovery rate was 0.48±0.21. In correlation analyses within the postoperative follow-up subset, CompMinZ showed the strongest association with JOA recovery rate, whereas NonComp-MinZ showed a positive but nonsignificant trend. By comparison, MSCC was not significantly associated with JOA recovery rate. For postoperative JOA, all 3-imaging metrics were significantly associated with outcome, with CompMinZ demonstrating the strongest relationship (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Hierarchical regression analyses further supported the incremental value of atlas-based metrics beyond MSCC. In the full preoperative DCM cohort, adding NonCompMinZ modestly improved model fit for preoperative JOA. In the postoperative follow-up subset, adding CompMinZ improved model fit for both postoperative JOA and JOA recovery rate, with the largest improvement observed for recovery rate. Detailed model results are presented in Supplementary Table 7. Given the relatively small postoperative cohort, these postoperative morphometric and clinical association analyses should be interpreted as exploratory.

DISCUSSION

In DCM, imaging assessment is primarily focused on the level and severity of cord compression, because surgical management is directed at decompression of the responsible segment. However, compression geometry alone does not fully account for the heterogeneity of neurologic impairment and postoperative recovery across patients with apparently similar imaging findings. In this study, we established a segment-specific normative atlas of cervical spinal cord CSA across the adult lifespan using a large healthy-volunteer cohort, with longitudinal follow-up supporting the cross-sectional patterns. This framework permits cervical cord morphology to be interpreted relative to expected variation across age, sex, and segmental level, rather than by absolute measurements alone. We then explored its application in surgically treated DCM, where deviation outside compressed levels showed a weak association with baseline neuro-logic status, whereas maximal deviation at compressed levels was more strongly associated with postoperative recovery.

Cervical spinal cord morphometry is influenced by segmental anatomy and physiological factors such as age and sex, which limits the interpretability of raw cord CSA values at the individual level [10,22,24]. In our healthy cohort, CSA followed a nonlinear trajectory across the adult lifespan, with relatively larger values in midadulthood and smaller values at older ages. This pattern was more pronounced in men, whereas women showed a flatter trajectory. Longitudinal follow-up provided supportive evidence that morphometric change over time is generally modest but not uniform across age stages. Together, these findings indicate that the same absolute CSA value may have different biological meaning depending on segment, age, and sex, and support the use of an age-, sex-, and segment-adjusted reference for patient-level interpretation of cervical cord morphometry.

Several normalization strategies have been proposed to improve the comparability of spinal cord morphometrics, including use of the pontomedullary junction as a spatial reference to mitigate level dependence, adjustment for physiologic covariates (e.g., sex, cord length, and brain-derived measures), and template-based automated pipelines that report morphometrics in a standardized space [19-22]. Most approaches, however, have focused primarily on descriptive morphometry or normalization of absolute measurements, and their clinical meaning in diseased spines—particularly DCM—remains incompletely defined. Consequently, a large-sample, physiologically adjusted cervical reference spanning multiple segments is still needed to support patient-level interpretation of deviation patterns in DCM. The main contribution of the present study is not simply the measurement or normalization of cervical cord CSA, but the establishment of an age-, sex-, segment-, and acquisition-adjusted deviation-based interpretive framework. By converting observed CSA into segment-specific z-scores, this framework allows patient-level interpretation of whether cord morphology deviates from individualized normative expectations. This approach further enables distinction between local compressed-level deviation and extracompression deviation, providing a clinically interpretable way to map local and nonlocal cord vulnerability in DCM.

In the DCM cohort, morphologic deviation was not confined to the compressed level. Although the most negative z-scores were observed at compressed segments, noncompressed segments also commonly fell below the normative mean. This finding suggests that cervical cord morphologic abnormality in DCM may extend beyond the site of maximal mechanical deformation [25-28]. In the full preoperative DCM cohort, NonCompMinZ showed a weak association with baseline neurological status, whereas CompMinZ did not show a comparable association. This pattern suggests that compressed-level and noncompressedlevel deviations may reflect different aspects of cord involvement. CompMinZ may primarily capture the local burden of tissue compromise at mechanically compressed levels, whereas NonCompMinZ may reflect extracompression morphometric deviation related to broader cord vulnerability. This extracompression deviation may reflect secondary changes along the spinal cord, such as Wallerian-like degeneration or depletion of global neural reserve, rather than a purely focal effect of mechanical compression. From this perspective, NonCompMinZ may capture part of the cumulative burden of chronic cord injury beyond the site of maximal compression [25,27]. However, these associations were modest, and the present data do not support interpretation of either NonCompMinZ or CompMinZ as a stand-alone marker of baseline neurological severity. Moreover, a sensitivity analysis in healthy volunteers suggested that using the minimum single-slice CSA instead of the three-slice average may introduce only a small negative bias, making sliceselection differences unlikely to fully account for the marked negative z-score deviations observed in compressed DCM segments. These exploratory findings require further validation in larger cohorts.

In contrast to baseline neurologic status, CompMinZ showed a stronger relationship with postoperative recovery measures in our cohort. In the correlation analyses, CompMinZ demonstrated the strongest association with both postoperative JOA and JOA recovery rate, whereas NonCompMinZ showed only a positive but nonsignificant trend with recovery rate and MSCC was not significantly associated with recovery. One possible explanation is that the depth of morphologic deviation at the compressed level may reflect the local burden of tissue compromise more directly than baseline clinical severity, which is influenced by multiple factors [29-32]. The paired follow-up MRI analysis provided an additional longitudinal perspective. This analysis was not restricted to surgically decompressed levels, but compared CSA z-scores across all cervical levels between preoperative and last follow-up MRI. Z-scores remained below the normative reference level across most segments and were lower at several levels at follow-up, suggesting that postoperative morphometric evolution may not simply reflect local decompression-related recovery. Instead, DCM-related cord remodeling may persist or further evolve over time at the whole-cervical-cord level. Because the postoperative cohort was relatively small and no nonoperative comparison group was available, these postoperative associations and longitudinal MRI findings should be interpreted as exploratory and should not be taken as proof of a causal relationship between decompression and morphometric change.

MSCC remains a useful descriptor of geometric compression, but the degree of focal narrowing does not necessarily parallel the extent of underlying tissue injury [28]. Chronic cord compression may impose different histopathologic burdens across patients with similar compression geometry, contributing to heterogeneity in baseline neurologic status and postoperative recovery [33,34]. In this study, MSCC was represented by a CSA compression ratio and therefore primarily reflected the geometry of focal cord narrowing, with lower values indicating more severe compression. However, this measure does not indicate whether the observed cord morphology is abnormal relative to age-, sex-, segment-, and acquisition-adjusted reference expectations. Consistent with the correlation findings, the hierarchical regression analyses further supported the complementary value of atlasbased deviation metrics beyond MSCC. In the full DCM cohort, NonCompMinZ provided modest but significant incremental explanatory value for preoperative JOA beyond age, sex, and MSCC, suggesting that morphometric deviation outside the compressed levels may be related to baseline neurological status. In the postoperative follow-up subset, adding CompMinZ to models that included preoperative JOA and MSCC improved model fit for 2 postoperative outcomes: the final postoperative JOA score and the JOA recovery rate, with the largest improvement observed for recovery rate. These findings suggest that compressed-level atlas-based deviation may provide outcomerelevant information beyond baseline neurological status and focal compression geometry, although this interpretation remains exploratory. Importantly, these results do not imply that MSCC should be replaced in routine clinical evaluation. Rather, atlas-based z-score metrics may provide complementary information by quantifying local and extracompression cord vulnerability relative to individualized reference expectations. Because the present study was exploratory in nature and the postoperative follow-up cohort was relatively small, broader clinical application of atlas-based deviation mapping will require external validation in larger prospective cohorts.

There were also several limitations in this study. First, all imaging data were obtained at a single center. Although the study included a large healthy-volunteer cohort and MRI examinations from multiple scanners, the normative atlas established here still requires further multicenter validation in independent cohorts. Second, all patients in the DCM cohort underwent surgical treatment, whereas patients managed conservatively were not included. This likely biased the sample toward more advanced disease, and whether similar deviation patterns are present in patients with cervical cord compression who are treated nonoperatively remains to be determined. Third, cervical MRI in both healthy volunteers and patients was performed on scanners from 4 manufacturers. Although magnetic field strength and scanner manufacturer were incorporated into the normative models, residual acquisition-related variability cannot be fully excluded. Fourth, the postoperative follow-up cohort was relatively small, so outcome-related associations should be regarded as preliminary. Fifth, compressed segments in DCM were characterized using the single most affected slice, whereas healthy volunteers and noncompressed segments were characterized using the mean of 3 slices. Although the sensitivity analysis in healthy volunteers suggested that this strategy introduced only a small negative bias, it may still have slightly increased the apparent deviation at compressed levels. Finally, because the study was based on conventional axial T2-weighted imaging and did not include diffusion imaging, magnetization transfer imaging, or other quantitative contrasts, it was not possible to further clarify the mechanisms underlying interindividual differences in morphologic deviation.

CONCLUSION

A segment-specific, age-, sex-, and acquisition-adjusted normative atlas of cervical spinal cord CSA across adulthood was established, with longitudinal follow-up supporting the crosssectional patterns. This atlas provides a physiologically adjusted reference framework for interpreting cervical cord morphologic variation related to normal aging and DCM. In exploratory analyses of surgically treated DCM, atlas-based deviation measures showed distinct associations with baseline neurologic status and postoperative outcomes. These findings support atlasbased deviation mapping as a standardized framework for morphologic assessment in DCM, although broader clinical application will require validation in larger multicenter prospective cohorts.
