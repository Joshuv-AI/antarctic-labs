 Summary of Research Results:

 Task 1: Ink Stability, and Coating Specifications, for Production Implementation (SOPO
    Subtask 1.1.3).

 Subtask 1.1 The Contractor and the Participant will conduct ink characterization studies to
 understand the colloidal phenomena influencing ink stability and settling rate. Due to their high
 density, low porosity and poor stabilization, PGM-based catalyst particles are prone to
 agglomeration and sedimentation. Over the time of a production run, poor ink quality can lead to
 variations in loading, and catalyst layer composition. Moreover, pumping, filtration and coating
 quality can be adversely affected. Colloidal dispersions of continually decreasing particle size
 lend themselves to increasing rates of agglomeration following milling, thus directly influencing
 the particle settling rate. Steric, electrostatic or electro-steric stabilization techniques will be
 considered for improving stabilization. This understanding will be used to develop improved ink
 formulations with suitability for production timelines. First iterations will target a loading of 0.5-
 0.6 mg/cm2 while we qualify even lower loadings for durability. Additional lower loaded
 products will be evaluated for manufacturability with the final loading selected based on ability
 to manufacture at scale. (SOPO Subtask 1.3.1).

 Subtask 1.1 Ink Characterization Studies Results:

 NLR established an ink preparation process that was crucial for conducting ink investigations
 towards optimizing ink design for roll-to-roll coating processes. Initial studies focused on
 evaluating particle size and zeta potential (a measure of particle surface charge) of dilute inks as
 a function of solvent composition (normal-propanol/water ratio, A/W), particle type (referred to
 here as Particle 1 and 2), and ionomer/catalyst ratio (I/C) to gain insights into the underlying
 interparticle interaction mechanisms. Preliminary particle size measurements using dynamic light
 scattering (DLS) on Plug catalyst inks prepared by ultrasonication (bath) mixing process,
 suitable for mixing dilute inks, resulted in poor data quality.

 To improve the ink mixing process, we have analyzed the particle size distribution as a function
 of different mixing parameters, including sonicator type. Increasing the bath sonication time to
 80 minutes resulted in no clear improvement in measurement quality. Next, a probe sonicator
 was analyzed to determine if greater particle size reduction could be achieved. Probe sonication
 has much higher energy intensity compared to a bath sonicator. As shown in Figures 1c & 1d,
 using the probe sonicator in combination with the bath sonicator led to a clear improvement in
 the measurement quality, as signified by a more consistent intensity-size profile between runs
 and a smaller polydispersity index.

 3

 Fig. 1. DLS intensity-size curves of Particle 1 (a & c) and Particle 2 (b & d) inks comparing bath
 only (a & b) and a combination bath and probe sonication (d & c) mixing methods.

 The effect of probe sonication cycles (duration) on size distribution was analyzed to optimize the
 mixing time. The inks were subjected to 30-seconds of probe sonication followed by a 30-second
 rest period in each cycle. As shown in Figure 2 (2a, 2b, 2d and 2e), increasing the sonication
 cycles from 5 to 20 led to significant improvement in particle size distribution and in the
 measurement quality. The particles were less polydisperse (indicated by the lower polydispersity
 index PDI values) and the intensity-size distribution curves are more consistent between runs at
 higher sonication cycles.

 Fig. 2. DLS intensity-size curves of Particle 1 (a - c) and Particle 2 (d - f) inks comparing the effect
 of probe sonication cycles/duration and alcohol/water (A/W) ratio on particle size distribution.
 Each probe sonication cycle constitutes 30-seconds probe sonication followed by 30-seconds
 rest time.

 4

 In addition to mixing parameters, the impact of solvent composition (A/W) on particle
 agglomeration was also analyzed. As shown in Figure 2 (2b, 2c, 2e and 2f), decreasing the A/W
 ratio led to a decrease in the average particle size. This observation is consistent with the
 sedimentation behavior of concentrated inks observed by Plug Power, Inc.

 For Particle 1 inks, NLR analyzed the effect of ionomer concentration (ionomer-to-catalyst (I/C)
 ratio) on zeta potential and agglomerate size of catalyst inks at different water-to-alcohol ratios
 (A/W) to gain insights into their stability behavior. Figure 1a compares the catalyst z-average
 diameter (Zavg) of Particle 1 as a function of I/C at two different A/W ratios measured using
 DLS. At high A/W, Zavg values of inks without ionomer are larger than that of inks with ionomer
 but show a large variability. This suggests inks without ionomer are likely more agglomerated,
 likely leading to the observed variability, whereas the inks with ionomer were more stable
 against agglomeration. The Zavg values of inks with ionomer show a minimal change with
 increasing I/C, indicating no significant variation in the agglomerated structure of inks, at least
 within the measured time scales.

 The corresponding zeta potential values of Particle 1 inks as a function of I/C are shown in
 Figure 1c. In all cases, the zeta potential values are negative in sign, indicating the Particle 1
 surface was negatively charged. The zeta potential magnitude increased with increasing I/C
 (negative in sign) up to 0.25. As the ionomer is negatively charged, this increase in zeta potential
 with I/C indicates an increase in the amount of ionomer adsorption on the particle surface,
 increasing the surface charge of the particles. Ionomer adsorption is known to generally enhance
 the stability of catalyst particles via electrostatic repulsions or steric hindrance mechanisms.
 Therefore, the enhanced stability in inks with ionomer (or reduced agglomeration) observed from
 the DLS data is likely due to ionomer adsorption increasing repulsion between particles.

 For I/C > 0.05, further examination of their sedimentation behavior, capturing agglomeration
 kinetics differences over long durations would more clearly reveal whether an increase in
 ionomer adsorption with I/C is further increasing the ink stability, which remains unclear from
 the DLS data. Additionally, for I/C above 0.25, the trend in the zeta potential values was not
 clear due to large variability in the values and requires additional investigation. One possible
 explanation is the presence of excess free ionomer, expected beyond a certain I/C when the free
 surface area of catalyst particle is saturated by ionomer coverage, influencing the measurement
 quality. Further examination of ink ionomer adsorption magnitudes as a function of I/C may help
 explain the variability at high I/Cs.

 The particle Zavg as a function of I/C at low A/W is compared with high A/W in Figure 3a. For
 low A/W the Zavg values of inks without ionomer are lower than at high A/W, indicating lower
 agglomeration. At water-rich solvent compositions, electrostatic repulsions, which are long-
 ranged forces, are expected to be stronger compared to those in alcohol-rich solvent
 compositions due to enhanced dissociation of ionic groups on the particle surface. However, the
 zeta potential magnitudes at low A/W are not significantly larger than at high A/W, and thus do
 not support this hypothesis. This suggests other factors, such as the electric double layer
 (counter-ion environment near the surface) and short-ranged solvent-particle interactions (i.e.,
 van der Waals), influence the interparticle interactions, play a role in the stability differences.

 5

 Furthermore, in contrast to high A/W, the addition of ionomer does not lead to any clear
 variation in their Zavg at low A/W. The Zavg values vary between 181 – 256 nm for I/Cs between
 0.05 to 0.25. However, like at high A/W, the corresponding zeta potential values increase with
 increasing I/C up to 0.25, though the increase is weaker compared to low A/W.

 Fig. 3. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) and zeta potential measurements of Particle 1 (a & c), and
 Particle 2 (b & d) inks represented by z-average particle size diameter (a & b) and zeta potential (c
 & d) at different alcohol-to-water (A/W) ratios. All inks contain 0.1 wt% catalysts. Lighter data sets
 represent replicate measurements.

 The Zavg measurements of a different catalyst ink particle type (Particle 2) as a function of I/C at
 both high and low A/W ratios are shown in Figure 3b. The trends for Particle 2 are qualitatively
 similar to Particle 1 inks. At high A/W, the Zavg decreases with increasing I/C, indicating that
 ionomer was promoting the stability of particles. In contrast, low A/W, the variation in Zavg with
 I/C is minimal with relatively lower Zavg magnitudes compared to high A/W, indicating an
 overall greater stability (lower agglomeration) compared to high A/W. While the Zavg values of
 Particle 2 inks are similar to Particle 1 inks at low A/W for all I/Cs (ranging between 154–205
 nm), their values at high A/W are larger, particularly at low I/Cs (< 0.1 I/C, 173–709 nm). This
 indicates Particle 2 inks are highly agglomerated compared to Particle 1 inks at alcohol-rich
 solvent compositions due to poor solvent-particle interactions. The zeta potential trends are also
 qualitatively similar to Particle 1 inks (Figure 3d). The zeta potential values increase with
 increasing I/C until 0.25, with low A/W inks exhibiting a weaker increase and larger zeta
 potential magnitudes compared to high A/W cases at lower I/Cs (£ 0.1). The increase in zeta
 potential values indicates an increase in ionomer adsorption onto catalyst particles with ionomer
 concentration.

 6

 To complement the zeta potential measurements, which are indicative of the amount of ionomer
 adsorbed onto particles, NLR developed a thermogravimetric analyzer (TGA) protocol based on
 literature to directly quantify the adsorbed ionomer mass. The goal was to quantify both
 adsorbed ionomer on the catalyst surface and free ionomer in the medium, separated through
 centrifugation. The work in this quarter focused on identifying the mass resolution limits of the
 instrument as well as determining any catalyst mass loss percentage (without any ionomer)
 during the temperature ramp which is important for accurate calculation of adsorbed ionomer
 mass. Figure 4a compares measured ionomer mass versus known ionomer mass in dispersions,
 prepared using ionomer powder. The measured mass is in good agreement with known mass
 down to 0.03 mg resolution, allowing us to study ionomer adsorption at a wide I/C range.

 Fig. 4. Thermogravimetric analyzer (TGA) measurements of (a) ionomer dispersions at different
 known concentrations to determine mass resolution limits and (b) Particle 1 inks in water without
 any ionomer to determine Particle 1 mass loss.

 To determine the Particle 1 mass loss, a temperature ramp up to 800°C was performed as shown
 in Figure 4b. The initial particle mass was determined from the measured mass after a hold at
 100°C. The particle mass loss was estimated from the initial particle mass and the final particle
 mass at the end of the temperature ramp, as shown in Figure 4c. The calculated mass loss for
 Particle 1 under nitrogen purging was 2%.

 Subtask 1.2 Another aspect of this task will be developing QC methods for inks. Catalyst inks
 will be formulated with intentional variations in formulation (e.g. +/- 5% catalyst concentration)
 to simulate process variations. The Contractor will use their available analytical tools to assess
 the impact of formulation variations on ink properties (viscosity, surface tension) and coating
 quality. Quantification of the impact of slight formulation variations will be used to guide
 development of QC tools with appropriate sensitivity to detect variations in critical ink properties
 as well as set quality assurance thresholds to ensure ink lots are not unnecessarily scrapped.
 (SOPO Subtask 1.3.2)

 Subtask 1.3 Another key aspect of catalyst ink development is tuning the formulation for the
 coating process. The Participant, in partnership with the Contractor, will conduct work to
 develop a roll-to-roll intermittent process for printing catalyst layers directly on membrane.
 Work will focus on 1) ink properties to minimize membrane impact, 2) ink properties for
 start/stop of printing and 3) alignment of prints. The ink must be formulated to minimize the
 impact on the membrane (example: swelling, delamination etc.) and will be adjusted through
 traditional formulation methods (example: alcohol-water ratio, solids content etc.). Additionally,
 the ink must support the intermittent nature of the printing, requiring tuning (example: viscosity,
 surface tension),) to match the capabilities of the delivery system. The prints on each side of the
 membrane must be well aligned with each other to avoid loss of active area in the overall stack.
 (SOPO Subtask 1.1.1)

 Task 2: Ink Incoming Materials Inspections

 The Participant and the Contractor will develop techniques for assessing the quality of incoming
 raw materials used in the production of inks for coating. This task will have potential to be scaled
 for use during coating or decal transfer production activities. Techniques could include optical
 vision, x-ray fluorescence, and other analytical testing which will measure material composition,
 surface compositions, and chemical reactive properties of input ink components. Outputs from
 this task will include a defined and documented engineering process with the required analytical
 testing and testing protocol. The Participant will procure the required analytical inspection
 equipment for production to characterize inks produced as acceptable for coating to support this
 task.

 Task 2 Results:

 NLR characterized two ionomer dispersions received from Plug using rheology and TGA to
 analyze differences in ionomer composition and dispersion properties. The two dispersions were
 one dispersion as received by Plug from the supplier and the other was this dispersion measured
 after filtration. The viscosity of ionomer dispersions was measured using steady-shear flow
 sweep from 500 to 0.01 s-1 shear rate range. As shown in Figure 5a, both dispersions showed a
 very weak shear thinning response and the measured viscosities are nearly identical. The
 differences in their low-shear rate viscosity values in the Newtonian plateau region below
 approximately 10 s-1, which are 0.140 ± 0.002 Pa·s and 0.142 ± 0.002 Pa·s for unfiltered and
 filtered ionomer dispersions, respectively, are within instrument resolution (< 5 %).

 8

 Fig. 5. a) Steady shear rheology (b - c) thermogravimetric measurements on unfiltered and filtered
 ionomer stock dispersions.

 Similarly, the TGA measurements of mass loss in unfiltered and filtered ionomer dispersions are
 shown in Figure 5b and Figure 5c, respectively. Unfiltered ionomer stocks showed a mass loss of
 78.9 ± 0.1% and filtered ionomer stock showed a mass loss of 78.8 ± 0.1%. The calculated
 ionomer concentrations from the mass loss values were 21.1 ± 0.1% and 21.2 ± 0.1% for
 unfiltered and filtered ionomer dispersions, respectively, which are very close and consistent
 with rheological measurements indicating minimal differences. Both viscosity and composition
 measurements demonstrate no significant differences in ionomer composition between unfiltered
 and filtered ionomer stock dispersions.

 Task 3. Initial On-Web Coating Inspection Systems Evaluations (SOPO Subtask 1.3.1).

 Subtask 3.1 Using coated materials provided by the Participant that are representative of the
 current electrolyzer product that the Participant produces, the Contractor will test methods for
 defect detection. Rolls of membranes with electrodes, with total length exceeding 1,000 meters,
 will be examined using various in-line techniques to assess electrode uniformity and to detect
 potentially critical defects. Possible technique candidates will include optical scanning and
 thermography with reactive gas excitation for layer uniformity and chromatic confocal probe
 testing for catalyst thickness profiling. After data acquisition, threshold-based data processing
 algorithms will be developed and tested to delineate the location of potential defects. Optical
 microscopy inspection on selected areas with most pronounced inhomogeneities will then follow
 to analyze feature structure, morphology, and position in a multilayer structure. For more detail
 or higher magnification study and for exploration of AI-based techniques, samples and the
 Contractor scanning results will be sent to Participant.

 Subtask 3.1 Results:

 The initial on-web coating inspection systems evaluation task focused on developing a method to
 detect bubbles in Plug’s anode coated on a transparent substrate. Given this objective, NLR
 explored the use of optical reflectance imaging to identify the bubble defects. Optical reflectance
 imaging was conducted at 1,200 dpi resolution on a approximately 11”x9” section of anode and
 observed that bubbles are clearly captured in the image (see Fig. 6). This led to the conclusion
 that very likely an automatic bubble defect detection algorithm can be developed to facilitate in-
 line inspection based on the high-resolution reflectance image acquisition. Thus, NLR

 9

 recommends that further efforts to develop in-line bubble detection capabilities include the
 development of a defect detection algorithm for optical reflectance images of anode material.

 It was determined that it was essential to test Plug MEAs with real manufacturing irregularities

 Task 4. Analytical Characterizations of Defects (SOPO Subtask 1.3.2).

 The Participant will perform ex-situ microscopy and spectroscopy characterization of
 intentionally introduced and actual defects, labeled by the Contractor, to provide in-depth
 understanding of the defect nature in terms of microstructure, compositions and morphology.

 Task 4 Results:

 Work on analytical characterization of defects included characterizing bubble defects in Plug’s
 MEA material in order to inform the development of an inline quality-control method for their
 detection. To learn more about the structure of the bubbles, NLR first used optical profilometry
 and optical microscopy. The profilometer scanning was effective in detecting the presence of
 bubbles and providing surface topography data that indicated that on a microscale, the features
 contain points located below and above the surface. The heights of three bubbles were measured
 with the profilometer, with the highest bubble measuring 53 μm above the surrounding area.
 Microscopy images taken of the bubbles indicated that some fraction of the bubble feature may
 be transparent. It is currently unknown how accurately the optical profilometer can conduct
 measurements on transparent materials.

 To work towards validating height measurements obtained by optical methods, and to further
 understand the structure of the bubbles, NLR developed the capability to use focused ion beam-
 scanning electron imaging (FIB-SEM) to cross-section anode bubbles and then image them.
 NLR used a Gallium FIB-SEM to mill a roughly 233 μm x 40 μm area deep enough to expose
 the depth of the bubble. The face of the milled area was then imaged using SEM. From the image
 of the cross section, it appeared that there is empty volume underneath the bubble (see Figure 7).
 Additionally, the very top of the bubble seemed to be covered by a very thin layer, and the
 thickness of the layer significantly increases the farther from the center we move.

 In this initial step, NLR developed the capability to cross-section and image bubbles in
 electrodes and gained basic information on the structure of an anode bubble. NLR recommends
 that future work cross-sectioning anode bubbles be done such that the milled area can expose

 11

 both the inside of the bubble and the surrounding (nominal) electrode. This would allow for a
 direct comparison of the bubble with a nominal electrode.

 Fig. 7. SEM image of cross section of bubble defect using image stitching.

 The other focus for work on analytical characterization of defects was investigating the coating
 uniformity of Plug’s additive coating layers (ACL). With the objective of developing an inline
 method to measure the thickness of ACL layers, NLR evaluated the correlation between additive
 loading and optical transmission of 1 and 2 ACLs on transparent substrate. If optical
 transmittance correlates with additive loading, then optical transmission imaging could be a
 viable approach for predicting ACL thickness, assuming a uniform additive density within
 layers.
 An optical transmission image was taken of the substrate coated with 1 ACL and 2 ACLs.
 Several 1.5 cm x 1.5 cm regions of both 1 and 2 ACL were then analyzed with X-ray
 fluorescence (XRF). The average additive loading for each region was determined and plotted
 against the corresponding average transmittance value for that area. It was found that there was a
 strong correlation between transmittance and loading at the centimeter scale. The signal is strong
 enough that the 1 ACL is distinguishable from 2 ACL by transmission alone. More investigation
 is needed to determine the uniformity of the transmittance and loading at the sub-centimeter
 scale and whether the correlation between transmittance and loading holds true at that scale.
 NLR also investigated some circular features of higher transmittance that were observed on the
 optical image. These circular features were predominant in the 2 ACL region, each typically less
 than a couple millimeters in XY area. We used optical profilometry on a couple regions with
 many of these features to determine if they correlate with any significant surface topography
 changes. We observed that many of these regions with higher optical transmittance coincide with
 lower heights on the profilometry map, suggesting local depressions or pits in the ACL. These
 profilometry results are consistent with our hypothesis that optical transmittance imaging is
 sensitive to ACL regions of lower areal density. NLR recommends that future work include
 establishing a correlation between additive loading and optical transmittance on the sub-
 centimeter scale.

 Task 6. Three Layer (Two Catalyst Layers and Membrane) Assembly Defect Detection
    (SOPO Subtask 1.3.4).

 A catalyst coated membrane with both anode and cathode is a three-layer structure which can
 contain critical defects in any of the layers, between the layers or on subassembly surfaces. To
 image and reveal the internal structure of such multi-layer product, the Contractor will
 experiment with optical and X-ray transmission methods, which have a potential to be
 implemented in a high-volume, roll-to-roll, or roll-to-piece environment. A part of this task will
 include inspection process definition for pre and post application of the second electrode. In this
 case a few, relatively fast, proof-of-the-concept type experiments will need to be performed to
 determine feasibility and value of each proposed method. In the case of optical transmission
 imaging, the Contractor will utilize its experimental setups specifically designed to image highly
 opaque materials at equivalent to production rate feed rates. The main objective of this task is to
 verify the possibility of having one QC inspection point for the three-layer structure without a
 need to monitor all prior subassembly fabrication steps. To develop these techniques and
 methodologies, a range of defects will be intentionally introduced in fabricated CCMs/MEAs.
 The Contractor will apply their existing detection systems and methods, as well as develop new
 approaches using these well-defined defects. The in-line detection data willx1 be provided to
 Participant to develop machine learning methods for data classification, segmentation, and
 analysis.

 Task 7. Defect Catalog Verification

 Task 10 Results:

 In accordance with Plug’s current research and development (R&D) priorities, NLR’s work
 included developing a quality control method for the fine mesh screens that are embedded in
 Plug’s anode packs. Plug observed the quality of the fine mesh screens received from its vendor
 can vary over time; some fine mesh screens were found to have holes with smaller opening
 widths compared to a nominal fine mesh screen. The openings restrict flow through the screen,
 therefore, it is necessary to check the screens and ensure the size of the openings will not
 decrease flow through the screen below specification.

 Plug proposed exploring computed tomography (CT) to evaluate the quality of the fine screens
 after they are embedded in the anode pack. This work was conducted on one fine screen with
 nominal opening sizes (sample S1L1) and one fine screen with opening sizes that are below
 specification (sample S2L1). Through optical microscopy, it was found that the walls of the
 openings for both the reference and defective screens have complex shapes, with numerous
 protruding features on both sides of each opening. These features appear to be excess material—
 referred to as burrs—introduced by the manufacturing process. We also observed that the walls
 are far from vertical, indicating that a large difference between the volume fraction and shadow
 fraction is likely. The burrs are undesirable because they may impede flow through the meshes.

 When using CT scanning to visualize cross sections of the fine mesh screens (see Figure 8), the
 burrs could be resolved well when a voxel size of 4 μm was used. Although this voxel size limits
 the field of view to roughly a 4 x 4 x 4 mm volume, the mesh openings were seen to be very
 consistent in shape and size, so scanning this volume should be sufficient. Thus, if using CT
 scanning to image the fine mesh screens, NLR recommends using a voxel size of no greater than
 4 μm to capture the protruding features that would impede flow through the meshes.

 NLR also recommends exploring options to perform quality-control checks on the screens before
 they are embedded in the anode packs, rather than after. Once the screens are embedded in the
 anode pack and CT scanning is needed to image them, assessing screen quality becomes
 expensive—especially because higher image quality increases scan time. Moreover, CT scanning
 is more susceptible to image artifacts, and the data processing can be intensive. Evaluating
 unbound fine screens is simpler and more cost-effective, as it can be done with an optical
 microscope for individual part evaluation or with a 2D vision system with a backlight for quick
 evaluation of many parts.
