STABILITY 2: A protocol for a randomised trial in orthopaedic surgery to determine whether anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction using a patellar or quadriceps tendon autograft with or without lateral extra-articular tenodesis is more effective at reducing the incidence of recurrent instability in young, active athletes
Authors: Dianne M Bryant, Alan Getgood, James Irrgang, Volker Musahl, Jinhui Ma, Trevor B Birmingham, Andrew Sprague, Alexandra B Gil, David W Holdsworth, Stephany Pritchett, Jacquelyn Marsh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAnterior cruciate ligament reconstruction (ACLR) is complicated by high failure rates in young, active individuals, which is associated with worse outcomes and higher rates of osteoarthritis (OA). Failure of ACLR limits activity, reduces quality of life and substantially increases socio-economic costs. Thus, strategies to reduce ACLR failure are imperative. In the STABILITY 1 trial, we showed that adding a lateral extra-articular tenodesis (LET) to ACLR with a hamstring tendon (HT) graft provided greater stability than HT alone (Relative Risk Reduction (RRR)=0.38; 95% CI 0.21 to 0.52; p<0001). Recent meta-analyses suggest patellar tendon (bone patellar tendon bone (BPTB)) grafts provide better stability than HT although with greater donor site morbidity. Recently, the quadriceps tendon (QT) graft has become popular, claiming similar stability to BPTB without donor site morbidity. However, no high-quality studies have shown it to be better and no study has determined if the reduced rate of ACLR failure seen with the addition of a LET to a HT graft is still present when using stronger, stiffer BPTB or QT grafts. Therefore, this study will evaluate whether in patients at high risk of rerupture, graft type (QT, BPTB, HT) with or without a LET offers a greater reduction in rate of clinical failure, better performance-based function and patient-reported symptoms, function and quality of life or higher rates of return to sport 2 years after ACLR. And does graft type±LET affect muscle strength recovery or increase the risk of donor site morbidity or lateral compartment articular cartilage degeneration? Do females, who are more often quadriceps dominant in their landing biomechanics, experience better outcomes if their ACLR uses a BPTB and/or QT autograft compared with a HT+LET autograft? Finally, is a particular graft type±LET a more cost-effective approach to ACLR?Methods and analysisAcross 31 centres (9 Canadian, 8 European, 14 USA), we will randomly assign 1292 patients with an ACL rupture who are at high risk of failure to either QT (±LET) or BPTB (±LET). Eligible participants will be 14–25 years of age, with at least two of the following: (1) Grade 2 pivot shift or greater; (2) returning to high-risk pivoting sport; (3) generalised ligamentous laxity; or (4) knee recurvatum of 10 degrees or more. Surgeons with similar expertise and no preference for either graft type will use traditional randomisation; two centres will use an expertise-based approach. Outcomes include ACLR clinical failure, patient-reported outcomes that assess symptoms, activity, participation and quality of life, measures of impaired range of motion and muscle function (quadriceps and hamstring strength), performance-based measures of physical function (hop tests, drop vertical jump), return to preinjury sports, adverse outcomes, intervention-related donor site morbidity and complications. Clinical failure and performance-based outcomes will be assessed by blinded evaluators over 2 years following ACLR. Analyses will combine STABILITY 2 and STABILITY 1 data in a mixed effects model where fixed effects include study, graft type, meniscal repair status and sex, while time, patient and surgeon are random effects variables.Ethics and disseminationEthics approval was granted for each centre prior to beginning recruitment. If successful, the results of this study will inform the optimal treatment of ACL rupture in young athletes, and women specifically, who are at risk for persistent rotational laxity/instability and graft rerupture. Reducing the risk of ACLR clinical failure will reduce the need for revision ACLR and the associated decreased quality of life and socio-economic burden that occurs because of ongoing knee instability and the increased risk for OA.Trial registration numberhttps://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03935750.Keywords: Orthopaedic sports trauma, Knee, Randomized Controlled Trial

Introduction

Anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) rupture is one of the most common musculoskeletal injuries in adolescents and young adults. Age-specific patterns of ACL rupture differ by sex, with peak incidence between 19 and 25 years in males (241.0/100 000 person-years) and between 14 and 18 years in females (227.6/100 000 person years).1 However, relative to the number of individuals playing sport, the incidence of ACL injury is two to four times higher in females.2–5 Epidemiological studies have shown that approximately 2000 patients undergo ACL reconstruction (ACLR) annually in Ontario,6 with over 250 000 performed annually in the USA.7 Although surgery is recommended for those wishing to return to sport, several studies have reported a substantial reduction in quality of life (QOL) and subsequent socio-economic burden as a result of ongoing knee instability,8 failure of the reconstruction, need for revision surgery and development of post-traumatic osteoarthritis (PTOA).9 Up to 30% of individuals under the age of 20 years suffer a reinjury to the reconstructed ACL or injury to the contralateral ACL.10 11 Revision ACLR is associated with deterioration of the articular surface of the joint, increased meniscal tears and meniscal volume loss (increasing the risk of PTOA), a need for repeat surgeries and reduced function and QOL.12 13 Therefore, strategies to reduce ACLR failure, particularly in young active individuals, are critical to improving short and long-term outcomes.

ACLR failure is multifactorial, with four broad categories of factors associated with failure: traumatic reinjury, poor biological healing, insufficient rehabilitation (poor neuromuscular conditioning,14 proprioception and no sport-specific training) and surgical technique.15 The surgical method chosen to reconstruct the ligament and re-establish knee stability can impact all of these risk factors and provides an opportunity to improve the chances of a favourable outcome.16 Two surgical strategies continue to be a substantial topic of interest for surgeons trying to address persistent rotational laxity and failed ACLR. The first is surgery to address concomitant injury to the anterolateral complex (ALC), which includes the iliotibial band (ITB), anterolateral ligament and lateral meniscus.17 An anterolateral reconstruction such as lateral extra-articular tenodesis (LET) may provide greater stability,18 yet surgery-induced lateral compartment osteoarthritis (OA) is a concern given potential overconstraint of the joint.19 New evidence from our group20–22 and others23 24 suggests the ALC structures play a key role in controlling knee stability and that ACLR alone may not be adequate to control rotation.25 We also published a systematic review that showed augmentation of ACLR with LET improved stability rates.26 Most convincing is that we completed an international multicentre randomised clinical trial (RCT) (STABILITY 1) comparing ACLR with hamstring tendon (HT) autograft with or without a LET in 618 young patients (<25 years old) at high risk of ACLR clinical failure that demonstrated the addition of a LET significantly reduces the risk of clinical instability (Relative Risk Reduction (RRR)=0.38, 95% CI 0.21 to 0.52, p<0.0001) and graft failure (RRR=0.67, 95% CI 0.36 to 0.83, p<0.001).27

The second surgical strategy to reduce ACLR clinical failure is graft choice. Surgeons in North America moved away from LET when one study suggested a well performed ACLR with a bone patellar tendon bone (BPTB) autograft negated the need for an anterolateral back up procedure. Unfortunately, this study was underpowered, poorly controlled and retrospective.19 However, the idea that graft choice could overcome anterolateral biomechanical deficiencies has been the basis of substantial study over the past 20 years.28–35 Although most agree that BPTB has lower failure rates, less laxity and limited loss of knee flexion compared with HT, BPTB grafts are associated with patellofemoral crepitance, kneeling pain, patellar fracture and loss of knee extension35 even though most studies fail to demonstrate a difference in patient-reported outcomes (PROs). In 2016, Mohtadi et al observed a 22% prevalence of persistent abnormal rotational laxity in HT grafts compared with 16% in BPTB grafts.30 A second RCT by the same group comparing HT and BPTB ACLR36 showed that HT resulted in a higher traumatic failure rate and a greater number of patients presenting with persistent rotational laxity. However, with only 17 failures in the study, it was drastically underpowered.

Recently, quadriceps tendon (QT) grafts have become popular though the evidence supporting its use is weak.37 Cavaignac et al compared 2-year postoperative outcomes in a non-randomised cohort of 95 patients who received either a QT or HT autograft and found no differences in PROs, although there was an improvement in objective stability rates in the QT group.38 In a small, prospective cohort study in 80 patients, Runer et al compared QT to HT, and reported no difference in PROs, stability, pain or function between the two groups.39 A recent meta-analysis of five retrospective studies comparing 452 BPTB grafts to 354 QT grafts reported no difference in failure,40 however, the small number of events (11 total) suggests the study was grossly underpowered. Stability rates and patient outcomes were also similar, yet fewer patients with the QT graft reported about substantial donor site issues. Therefore, a well-designed, adequately powered RCT is necessary to definitively determine the autograft choice for young, high-risk individuals.

In general, only 63% of individuals with an ACLR return to previous levels of activity.41 In STABILITY 1, although 86% returned to a sport, only 46% who played a high-risk sport before their injury returned to a high-risk sport at a high level.42 Few studies have compared graft type with respect to functional requirements for return to sport. Functional performance depends on neuromuscular training and consists of quantity of movement, including muscle strength (concentric and eccentric) and hop tests,43 and quality of movement,44 including dynamic knee flexion and valgus when landing from a jump.45 46 Functional performance is important for both ACL reinjury prevention and rehabilitation47 48; however, a systematic review by Engelen-van Melick et al found that few, if any, high level studies comparing graft types include both quantity and quality of movement.44 Therefore, functional performance must be an integral component of future studies.

Why is a trial needed now? STABILITY 1 was the first adequately powered RCT to study the effects of a surgical intervention in ACLR in a young, high-risk population. We showed that adding a LET to a HT ACLR provided greater stability than HT ACLR alone (RRR=0.38; 95% CI 0.21 to 0.52; p<0001).27 In 2024, we cochaired a consensus meeting of experts across six out of seven continents, 18 countries, on the use of lateral extra-articular procedures in ACLR where there was 100% consensus that, ‘In young, active patients (≤25 years) undergoing ACLR with HT, a lateral extra-articular procedure is strongly recommended to reduce graft failure’49 citing STABILITY 1.49

However, many surgeons believe that a well performed BPTB or QT graft alone will provide adequate stability to the knee (will not require LET),19 and that the QT results in less donor site morbidity than BPTB even though no properly powered study has evaluated this claim, or whether the reduced rate of ACLR failure seen with HT+LET is still present when using a BPTB or QT graft for ACLR without a LET. STABILITY 2 will directly address these two issues.

Further, since STABILITY 1, there has been an increase in surgeons who use LET to augment the ACLR.50 51 Critically, the quadriceps dominance theory of ACL injury proposes that ACL injuries are caused by overactive knee extensor muscles (quadriceps), leading to excessive anterior tibial force on the ACL, particularly during deceleration and landing. Since females tend to be quadriceps dominant in their landing biomechanics compared with males, and several small studies claim that athletes undergoing HT autograft ACLR have greater extensor (quadriceps) strength compared with greater flexion (hamstring) strength for BPTB or QT graft ACLR, we need to understand if harvesting the HT for the ACLR (which further contributes to quad dominance) should ever be an option for females.

Because the eligibility criteria and outcomes for STABILITY 2 are identical to STABILITY 1 (n=618),52 combining data from both studies will provide sufficient power to determine whether HT+LET offers better outcomes than BPTB or QT and if, in females, an HT+LET (the best HT option) offers similar stability to BPTB and QT (approximately half of each sample are female).

Methods: patient and public involvement, trial design

Patient and public involvement

A patient was featured in a recruitment video and 10 participants from at least five countries will participate in our dissemination plan for patients, parents, athletic trainers and coaches.

Trial design

This is a parallel-groups, international, multicentre, randomised trial where individual participants are randomly assigned to graft type (BPTB or QT) and the addition of a LET or not in a 1:1:1:1 ratio. The study uses a superiority framework to address sample size requirements and will use a superiority framework to interpret the results.

Methods: participants, interventions and outcomes

Trial setting

This is an international trial with 70 participating orthopaedic surgeons across 31 centres throughout Canada (9 centres), the USA (14 centres) and Europe (8 centres). Canadian sites are located in the following provinces: British Columbia, Alberta (2), Manitoba, Ontario (4) and Nova Scotia. Centres in the USA are located in the following states: Pennsylvania, Michigan, New Mexico, California (2), Kentucky (2), North Carolina, Minnesota (2), Virginia, New York, Louisiana and Florida. Countries in Europe with participating centres include Germany (2), Sweden, Norway, UK (2), Denmark and Ireland.

Eligibility criteria

Subjects deemed eligible for the study will have an ACL deficient knee, be 14–25 years old, skeletally mature (ie, closed epiphysial growth plates will be confirmed on standard of care knee radiographs for all study subjects) and have two or more of the following factors that are associated with a high risk of graft failure: participate in a competitive pivoting sport (defined as sports that include cutting and pivoting activities such as basketball, American football, soccer, lacrosse, volleyball, tennis/squash, handball, downhill skiing); or have a pivot shift of grade 2 or greater; generalised ligamentous laxity (Beighton score of ≥4) and/or genu recurvatum >10 degrees.

Individuals will be excluded from the study if they have had a previous ACLR on either knee, partial ACL injury (defined as one bundle ACL tear requiring reconstruction/augmentation of the torn bundle with no surgery required for the intact bundle), multiple ligament injury (two or more ligaments requiring surgery), symptomatic articular cartilage defect requiring treatment other than debridement, >3 degrees of asymmetric varus, inflammatory arthropathy, pregnant or are unable to provide consent.

Interventions and comparators

Efforts to standardise the surgical protocol

We held an investigators’ meeting prior to trial commencement to ensure that surgeon participants would perform the interventions in a standardised fashion to limit the degree of variability within and across study centres. All surgeons participating in the trial were experienced at performing ACL reconstruction. To standardise the procedures for ACLR, we reviewed and demonstrated the procedures for harvest of the BPTB and QT tendon graft, anatomic placement of the femoral and tibial tunnels and methods of graft fixation. To standardise the procedure for the addition of a LET, we reviewed together the procedures for harvesting the ITB, location of the femoral placement of the graft and methods of fixation. To ensure competency in performing a LET and QT autograft harvest, all surgeons were required to complete at least ten of each procedure prior to randomising their first participant.

Standardised surgical protocol

Briefly, all patients underwent anatomic ACLR. All ACLRs were performed in a similar manner across sites. Surgeons used a BPTB or QT autograft, located in an anatomic position within the femoral and tibial attachment sites, with consistent graft fixation. A priori, each surgeon indicated their preferred method of harvesting the QT graft (patellar bone plug or soft tissue only) and continued to use that method for all their participants randomised to QT ACLR. For patients randomised to receive LET, surgeons made an oblique skin incision (approx. 5 cm) between the lateral epicondyle and Gerdy’s tubercle. A 1 cm wide × 8 cm long strip of ITB was fashioned, leaving the Gerdy’s tubercle attachment intact. The graft was tunnelled under the fibular collateral ligament (FCL) and attached to the femur with a Richards’ staple (Smith and Nephew), just anterior to the intermuscular septum, proximal to the femoral insertion of the FCL. Fixation was performed with the knee at 60–700 flexion in neutral rotation. Minimal tension (<20 N) was applied to the graft. The free end was then looped back onto itself and sutured using a No. 1 vicryl. See online supplemental file 3 for a detailed description of the surgical protocol.

Standardised rehabilitation protocol

Regardless of group allocation, all patients’ physical therapists will be provided with the same postoperative rehabilitation protocol and a set of standardised instructions from the surgeon. Focus is placed on early ROM and weight bearing as tolerated. The span of the rehabilitation protocol is 12 months, and it includes three criterion-based phases: tissue protection, motor control and functional optimisation (see online supplemental file 4 for detailed description of the standardised rehabilitation protocol).

Outcomes

The primary outcome for this study is clinical failure of the ACLR over the first 2 postoperative years.27 Clinical failure of the ACLR is operationally defined as a composite of rotational laxity defined as mild asymmetrical pivot shift (grade 1) detected at two or more follow-up visits OR moderate or severe (grade 2 or 3) asymmetric pivot shift at any visit, OR MRI or arthroscopic evidence of graft rupture. The pivot shift test has been reported by Scholten et al53 as the most specific of all clinical ACL tests (with a specificity of 0.97–0.99 and sensitivity of 0.18–0.48). While Kocher et al54 did not find any significant relationships between patient-reported knee instability with anterior laxity of the knee (Lachman or KT-1000 tests), they did find significant relationships between knee instability defined as partial (p=0.01) or full giving way (p=0.01) of the knee with the pivot shift test. Graft rupture is defined as a tear of the graft confirmed either by MRI or arthroscopic examination.

Secondary outcomes include PROs that assess symptoms, activity, participation and QOL, measures of impaired ROM and muscle function (quadriceps and hamstring strength), performance-based measures of physical function (hop tests, drop vertical jump (DVJ)), return to preinjury sports, adverse outcomes, intervention-related donor site morbidity and complications. We will collect preoperative data for all questionnaires, ROM, muscle strength and the results of imaging procedures performed for clinical purposes (eg, standing flexion radiographs and MRI results).

PROs will include a combination of disease-specific and region-specific measures of symptoms, activity, participation and QOL as follows:

Disease-Specific Patient reported outcomes

The ACL Quality of Life Questionnaire55 assesses physical symptoms, occupational concerns, recreational activities, lifestyle and social and emotional aspects of ACL injury. Each item has a 100 mm visual analogue scale (VAS) response option, with labelled anchors at 0 mm (eg, extremely difficult) and 100 mm (eg, not difficult at all). Scores are calculated by converting the average of each of the five domain scores to a total average score out of 100% where 100% represents the best possible score.

Knee-Specific Patient reported outcomes

The knee-specific PROs will include the International Knee Documentation Committee Subjective Knee Form (IKDC-SKF) and the Knee injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Survey (KOOS).

The IKDC-SKF is an 18-item questionnaire querying symptoms, function and sports activities.56 57 The items are summed and transformed to a score that ranges from 0 to 100 with 100 representing no symptoms or limitations with function and sports activities. The KOOS consists of 42 items in five domains that separately measure pain, other symptoms, function in daily living, function in sports/recreation and knee-related QOL.58 59 Domain scores represent the sum of all items in the domain standardised to a score from 0 to 100 (worst to best). Both the IKDC-SKF and KOOS are being used because each is more familiar in different parts of the world and thus, including both will broaden the interpretability of the results.

Patient-Reported change and acceptability of health status

Participants used the Global Rating of Change to rate the change in the status of their knee since surgery on a 15-point ordinal scale.60 61 Response options include: ‘Better’, ‘About the same’ and ‘Worse’. If ‘better’ is selected, we ask the participant how much better and provide a series of responses. If ‘About the same’ is selected, the questionnaire is complete. If ‘worse’ is selected, we ask the participant how much worse and provide a series of responses. This questionnaire is collected at the postoperative visits starting at the 3-month visit.

Participants also completed the Patient Acceptable Symptom State Question62 that asks the participant ‘Taking into account all the activity you have during your daily life, your level of pain and also your activity limitations and participation restrictions, do you consider the current state of your knee satisfactory?’ Response options include Yes or No. This questionnaire is collected at the baseline visit and postoperative visits starting at the 3-month visit.

Measures of impaired range of motion and muscle function

A blinded assessor will measure passive and active knee extension and active-assisted knee flexion with a goniometer. For passive knee extension, the patient will lie supine on the examination table with a bolster under the heels with the quadriceps and hamstrings relaxed to assure full passive extension of the knee. For active-assisted knee flexion, the patient will be seated on the examination table with both legs extended and instructed to perform active-assisted knee flexion by placing one hand under their thigh to initiate flexion and then clasp both hands just below the tibial tuberosity. The side-to-side difference in ROM will be determined and interpreted based on IKDC guidelines.63 64

To assess quadriceps and hamstring strength bilaterally we will use a computerised isokinetic dynamometer using methods previously shown to be reliable and valid.65 66 Briefly, the patient will wear a Tubigrip sleeve on the operative limb to conceal group allocation. Isokinetic measurements will be performed at 90 degrees/s because we are interested in peak torque and power measurements rather than endurance and fatigability. To assess strength, quadriceps and hamstring limb symmetry indices (LSIs) will be calculated as the ratio of peak torque of the ACL reconstructed knee to peak torque of the contralateral normal knee multiplied times 100. We will also calculate the hamstring to quadriceps ratio for the reconstructed and contralateral knees.67 68 We will present these ratios by group by visit but expect that early between-group differences will reflect issues related to donor site morbidity that will resolve by 24 months postoperatively.

Not all sites have access to an isokinetic dynamometer therefore we will also collect isometric quadriceps and hamstring strength using a crane scale (ie, strain gauge) that has been shown to provide a reliable measure of muscle strength after ACL reconstruction.69 70 71 Additionally, isometric thigh strength will be collected prior to surgery on both the ACL-injured and contralateral normal knees. To measure isometric quadriceps and hamstring strength, the participant will be seated with their knee flexed to 90° with one end of the crane gauge securely attached to the participant using a padded ankle strap and the other end attached to an unmovable object. As the participant straightens or bends the knee, the device will record maximal force output in kilograms. Use of the crane gauge will allow for enhanced stabilisation during the test, which is necessary to reliably and accurately measure isometric quadriceps and hamstring strength.

Performance-Based measures of physical function

Performance-based tests of the participant’s physical function will include hop tests72 and the DVJ tests to assess dynamic knee flexion and valgus. When possible, strength testing (isokinetic and/or isometric) should be performed prior to the performance-based test to ensure that the participant is appropriate for the hop tests. Participants should display ≥80% quadriceps LSI to be cleared for the hop tests.72 If it is not possible to perform strength testing prior to the hop tests, the blinded examiner with a clinical background should use their clinical judgement in determining whether it is safe to proceed with the hop tests.

The series of four hop tests (single hop for distance, triple hop for distance, triple cross over hop and timed 10-metre hop) are proxies for neuromuscular control, strength and confidence in the limb. The hop tests are one of the most common functional outcomes used in ACL research and return to sports decisions.71 73 74 Participants will perform a series of four hop tests using methods previously shown to be reliable and valid following ACL reconstruction.73 The hop tests will be conducted by a trained physical therapist, kinesiologist or research assistant who is blinded to the operative procedures via Tubigrip worn over the participant’s operative knee. For each hop test, we will present results as a LSI,72 which expresses test performance of the operative limb as a percentage of the non-operative limb. A higher LSI indicates a higher level of function for the operative limb. The LSI for each hop test as well as the average LSI of the four hop tests will be used for data analysis.

The DVJ test, which mimics the physical demands of competitive jumping sports like basketball or volleyball,67 75 will be used to assess dynamic valgus collapse of the knee that is associated with risk of ACL injury.67 68 75 The test is particularly suitable for patients who are preparing for return to sport after ACLR because it allows for a highly relevant evaluation of knee stability during sport specific movements. Recent studies have shown that measures of dynamic knee valgus during a DVJ test effectively demonstrate differences between healthy and ACLR knees,76 77 and knees reconstructed with HT versus BPTB grafts.78

In sites that have a three-dimensional motion capture system synchronised with floor-mounted force plates, the DVJ is measured using methods previously shown to be reliable and valid.79 Although the angles and moments at the hip, knee and ankle will be analysed bilaterally, the peak knee abduction angle and moment will be used in the statistical analysis as they predict future injury68 and are highly reliable using our procedures.79

At all sites, the DVJ will also be assessed using the study-supplied Microsoft Kinect V.2 and ACL-Gold software to measure frontal plane kinematics. Dynamic valgus of the lower extremity is operationally defined as the ratio of the distance between the knees to the distance between the ankles. This technology has been shown to be a reliable method of calculating frontal plane moments and has been shown to have a very high correlation with three-dimensional optical marker based motion analysis systems.80 81

To perform the DVJ, participants will stand on a box approximately 30 cm in height with the balls of each foot off the edge of the box. A Microsoft Kinect V.2 sensor is placed 3.4 m away from the box, mounted on a 1-metre-high tripod. The Kinect sensor is connected to a Windows based computer with the ACL-Gold software. The participant drops off the box, landing on both feet and then performs a maximum vertical jump as quickly as possible, landing in the same spot as the initial landing. The participant then takes a few steps forward, which triggers the automated data collection. The results are then automatically populated in a results screen in the system. The participant will perform three DVJs with the average measurement of dynamic valgus of the lower extremity recorded.

Return to activity measures

The Marx Activity Rating Scale will be used to measure return to activity.82 It is a four-item scale where individuals rate how often they are able to perform each activity (eg, running, cutting, decelerating and pivoting). One point is allocated for each response category to create a score that ranges from 4 to 16 points, with 16 representing the highest level of activity.

Psychological readiness for return to sport will be measured using the Anterior Cruciate Ligament-Return to Sport after Injury scale.83 The scale was developed to quantify psychological factors associated with return to sport (RTS). This scale includes 12 items measured on a 0 to 10 VAS and was developed based on three components correlated to RTS in the literature: emotions, confidence in performance and risk appraisal. It has been shown to be a valid tool to assess psychological readiness for RTS, with studies showing that psychological and physical readiness are different constructs that may require different time frames for full recovery.84

We will also record sports and level of participation prior to injury and postoperatively to determine whether participant returns to his/her previous level of activity, and if not, why not. Levels of activity included elite, varsity, competitive, recreational or non-organised sport which defined level of play and frequency of game, practice or training, which was included on the patient-reported Case Report Form (CRF).

Donor site morbidity and adverse events

We will assess donor site morbidity by determining the presence of anterior kneeling pain and sensory disturbance secondary to the graft site skin incision. Anterior kneeling pain will be assessed by asking the participants to rate their pain using an 11-point (0–10) numeric pain rating scale while they kneel on a hard floor. Sensory disturbance will be assessed via light touch to regions around the graft skin incision and anterolateral tibia and will be rated as absent, mild, moderate or severe.

To determine whether the addition of LET to ACLR contributes to the development of lateral compartment articular cartilage degeneration and overall knee OA, the Western University site will assess the articular cartilage in the lateral compartment of the knee, using quantitative MRI (qMRI). Specifically, T1rho and T2 relaxation times are sensitive to changes in collagen and proteoglycan content of articular cartilage,85 86 typically observed following ACL injury.87 88 Increases in T1rho or T2 relaxation time have been shown to be predictive of the development of OA.89 Participants will undergo MRI at baseline and 2 years postoperatively. Baseline measurement provides the opportunity to control for lateral compartment injury severity at time of ACL injury. Scans will be performed using a 3T Siemens Magnetom Trio magnet, and a 15-channel Siemens PRISMA knee coil (Siemens Medical Solutions, New Jersey, USA). qMRI pulse sequences consist of a Sagittal Multi-Echo Spin Echo T2 Mapping sequence and a 16-shot Gradient Echo T1rho Mapping sequence. T1rho and T2 relaxation maps are generated using software developed in-house by fitting image intensities of the T1rho and T2 weighted images pixel-by-pixel to the equation S(TE) ∝ exp(-TE/T2) using a Levenberg-Marquardt monoexponential fitting algorithm. Load-bearing central, anterior and posterior regions of medial and lateral articular cartilage of the femur and tibia are manually segmented and analysed using 3D Slicer software. The reader is blind to scan order and to group.

All centres will perform anteroposterior and lateral view plain radiographs preoperatively and at 2 years postoperatively. Lateral compartment joint space narrowing (sign of OA) and tibial slope (a covariate) will be measured using software demonstrated to be reliable and valid.

All complications (intraoperative and postoperative) will be recorded and classified based on their severity, relatedness, expectedness and seriousness (see Harm section for definitions of each rating and online supplemental file 2) for a description of the Data Safety and Monitoring Board (DSMB) and Internal Adverse Event Adjudication Committee). Secondary surveillance on failures will be completed by the participant at each follow-up visit to capture any self-reported problems with their surgical knee. This will act as a secondary check to ensure we are capturing all of our adverse events (AEs) including graft failures.

Cost-Effectiveness measures

Patients who experience an event that is at least possibly related to graft type or LET will report ER visits, hospitalisations, specialist and rehab appointments, tests, procedures, medications and time from work or caregiving. To report quality-adjusted life years we will use the utility value from the European Quality of Life Scale (Euro-QoL-3L).90 The EuroQoL comprises two sections, the EQ-5D index and the EQ-5D VAS. The EQ-5D index is a 5-item standardised generic measure of health-related QOL that includes the domains of mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain and discomfort and anxiety and depression. Each item is scored using a 3-point response scale and each combination of response choices describes a health state (243 unique health states). Each health state can be converted to a utility value from 0 (worst) to 1.0 (best) using a scoring formula. The EQ-5D VAS is a 0 (worst) to 100 (best) scale that assesses patient-perceived health status.

Harms

Participants in this study will undergo ACLR surgery as part of their standard of care treatment. The surgery will be performed by surgeons who are experienced in reconstructing structures of the knee. The risks associated with the study including the risks of surgery, radiation exposure and temporary pain are no greater than what would be expected if the individual did not participate in the study because the surgery, radiographs and clinical tests like the pivot shift test and measurement of ROM are part of routine care for patients undergoing an ACLR. See online supplemental file 5 for a detailed list and anticipated rate of expected AEs.

Classification and adjudication of adverse events

At each follow-up clinic and research visit, the clinical research assistant (CRA) will question the participant about the occurrence of any AEs since the last visit. In collaboration with the site Principal Investigator (PI), each event is reported by the CRA using the Adverse Event Report CRF that queries date of onset, date of first knowledge, details of the event and any related tests and treatments. Additionally, the CRA includes the rating of the severity (mild, moderate, severe, life threatening/disabling, fatal), the relatedness (unrelated, unlikely related, possibly related, probably related, definitely related), expectedness and whether the event qualifies as a serious AE (SAE). An event qualifies as a SAE if, in the view of either the investigator or sponsor, it results in any of the following outcomes: death, a life-threatening AE, inpatient hospitalisation or prolongation of existing hospitalisation, a persistent or significant incapacity or substantial disruption of the ability to conduct normal life functions, or a congenital anomaly/birth defect. Important medical events that may not result in death, be life-threatening or require hospitalisation may be considered serious when, based on appropriate medical judgement, they may jeopardise the participant and may require medical or surgical intervention to prevent one of the outcomes listed in this definition. Examples of such medical events include allergic bronchospasm requiring intensive treatment in an emergency room or at home, blood dyscrasias or convulsions that do not result in inpatient hospitalisation, or the development of drug dependency or drug abuse. A surveillance and alert system was created to ensure immediate reporting of SAEs to the study medical safety officer (see online supplemental file 2) for the roles and responsibilities of the Internal and External Adverse Event Adjudication Committee).

In addition to the SAE surveillance system, the Internal Adverse Event Adjudication Committee, blind to group allocation, adjudicates weekly the information provided on all Adverse Event Report forms and submits any requests for reclassification or additional information to the site team. All AEs and SAEs queries will be monitored until completion. Specifically for SAEs, once free of queries, the adjudicated SAE Report form is sent electronically to the safety officer of the DSMB (see online supplemental file 2). AEs are reported to the DSMB biannually in an Open report as running list and as aggregated tallies by centre and overall, and in the closed report further subdivided into blinded groups (A–D). Decisions by the DSMB to unblind the groups must be justified and by majority vote.

Potential risks associated with privacy and confidentiality

This study will collect data from or about the participant using paper-based forms. These paper-based forms will only include a participant’s unique database ID number. It will not include the participant’s name. The file that links the participant’s name with the unique database ID number is stored separate from the study data. All paper documents are kept in a locked filing cabinet in a locked room that is accessible only to individuals at each site who are part of the research team.

In addition, participants can opt into direct data entry for PROs and CRAs can collect data using electronic CRFs by logging into the study database using their unique username and strong password. The study electronic study data is managed by a company called EmPower Health Research (www.empowerhealthresearch.ca). To reduce the risk of a breach of privacy, data travels in a scrambled format (Secure Sockets Layer (SSL)) to an encrypted server that is secured by a professional hosting company with extremely high standards of physical and virtual security. However, even with this high level of security, there is always a remote chance that study data could be accessed or ‘hacked’. Therefore, the collection of sensitive information from participants is limited to the minimum amount necessary to achieve the aims of the research.

The primary outcome is measured at 3, 6, 12 and 24 months postoperatively. PROs, ROM and isometric strength are measured preoperatively and at 6 weeks and 3, 6, 12 and 24 months after surgery, which is consistent with regular clinical practice patterns. Muscle strength and hop tests are performed 6, 12 and 24 months postoperatively and the DVJ will be assessed 6 and 12 months postoperatively. Radiographs and MRI occur preoperatively and at 24 months postoperatively (see figure 1).

Sample size

For the primary research question

The absolute risk of clinical failure of the ACLR (as defined by either graft rupture/symptomatic instability requiring revision ACLR surgery or persistent rotational laxity as measured by an asymmetrical positive pivot shift compared with the contralateral side) is estimated to range from 25–35%.40 87 88 We consider a relative reduction in the rate clinical failure of ACLR of at least 40% by 24 months after surgery to merit a change in practice (ie, of sufficient magnitude to warrant the additional costs of adding a LET). Since our primary interest is in determining the main effect of graft choice and whether the effect of LET varies by graft choice, the focus will be on the following comparisons: (1) HT+LET versus HT (already shown by STABILITY 1), (2) BPTB+LET versus BPTB, (3) QT+LET versus QT, (4) BPTB versus HT+LET, (5) QT versus HT+LET and (6) BPTP+LET versus QT+LET.

With 210 patients per group and a type I error rate of 1%, we would have 80% power to detect a HR of 0.56 (ie, 44% clinical failure risk reduction when comparing the LET versus no LET condition) assuming the clinical failure rate is 33% (the average rate of failure in STABILITY 1). A small type I error rate of 1% was used to reduce the risk of a type I error due to the multiple comparisons based on the Bonferroni method to achieve an overall type I error rate of 5%. Even if there is an intra-cluster/surgeon correlation coefficient as large as 0.02, (1) the average number of surgeons per site is three given the number of surgeons at each site ranges from 1 to 4, and (2) the average number of patients per surgeon is 22, we will need 281 patients per group to account for the clustering effect. To reduce the risk of losing precision from patients withdrawn and lost to follow-up, an additional 15% of patients will be recruited (attrition was 5% from STABILITY 1), for a total of 323 participants per group or 1292 patients total (or 1910 when STABILITY 1 and STABILITY 2 data are combined).

For the Sex-based research question

Preliminary results of STABILITY 1 suggested that HT+LET is superior to HT ACLR alone and is associated with an increased odds of failure compared with HT+LET for both males (OR=2.53, 95% CI 1.42 to 4.51) and females (OR=1.76, 95% CI 1.05 to 2.96).31 Given these results, and because females tend to be quadriceps dominant in their landing biomechanics compared with males, and use of a HT graft is currently the most common method of ACLR, we need to understand whether harvesting the HT (which may further contribute to quadriceps dominance) should ever be a first-line option for females. Thus, STABILITY 2 will compare failure between HT+LET and other graft options (BPTB or QT) for males and females separately.

Among the 309 patients per group, we assume half will be female (51.5% of STABILITY 1 participants were female). Thus, we expect to have 159 females in each of the HT+LET, BPTB and QT groups. Given the failure rate of 29% for females when treated with HT+LET (based on the result from STABILITY 1), the minimum detectable OR will be 2.1 with a power of 80% at the significance level of 0.05. Given the failure rate of 21% for males when treated with HT+LET (based on the result from STABILITY 1), we will be able to detect an OR of 2.4 with a power of 80% at the significance level of 0.05. According to the rule of thumb on magnitudes of effect sizes by Cohen,89 an OR of 2.1 or 2.4 is considered to be as small (1.5) to medium (3.5) effect size, that is, with the sample size of 309 (159 females and 150 males) per group, we will have a power of 80% to detect a small to medium treatment effect for males and females separately at the significance level of 0.05. See online supplemental file 6 for sample size considerations for secondary research questions.

Methods: assignment of interventions

Randomisation

The randomisation scheme will be generated using the web-based application available through the EmPower data management centre (www.empowerhealthresearch.ca). This system requires entry of the patient’s date of birth, database ID and responses to all stratification questions prior to group allocation to protect concealment of allocation. The randomisation table is not available to surgeons or CRAs.

Randomisation will be stratified by surgeon, participant sex and status of the menisci (normal/repaired vs meniscectomy) in permuted mixed block sizes to increase the probability that any differences in outcome attributable to these factors are equally dispersed between treatment groups. Each site will either use traditional or expertise-based randomisation. At sites participating in traditional randomisation, participants will be randomised to graft type and LET or no LET in the operating room following diagnostic arthroscopy and final confirmation of eligibility criteria.

The potential for expertise bias exists around performing the graft harvest for a BPTB or QT autograft and thus, for sites participating in an expertise-based scheme, randomisation to graft type (and hence to the surgeon performing that graft type) will occur after initial eligibility is confirmed by a neutral third physician and consent for participation is granted. Randomisation to LET or no LET will take place in the operating room following diagnostic arthroscopy and final confirmation of eligibility criteria.

Methods for protecting against sources of bias

Selection bias: We partially determine eligibility prior to surgery. Patients then undergo arthroscopy to confirm eligibility in the operating room. On confirmation, randomisation occurs thereby reducing the risk of selection bias via post randomisation exclusions. Eligible postrandomisation exclusions (for expertise-based sites) include conditions present prior to randomisation but not discovered until surgery (eg, partial ACL rupture where ACLR is not performed). We will analyse participants in the group to which they were randomised regardless of graft received or adherence to protocols. Given that STABILITY 1 followed the same protocols as STABILITY 2, and they are being performed immediately in series, important changes in ancillary care and surgeon expertise are unlikely. Consequently, analyses that combine data from STABILITY 1 and STABILITY 2 are unlikely to suffer significant selection biases.

Performance bias: It is not possible to blind the surgeon to group allocation however, to reduce the risk of performance bias, all surgeons met and agreed on a standardised surgical and rehabilitation protocol.

Detection bias: It is not possible to blind the patient (different incision patterns) which could contribute to detection bias for PROs; however, we are able to blind other outcome assessors. To accomplish this, participants will wear a Tubigrip sleeve over their operative knee to conceal incisions. A blinded surgeon or experienced physiotherapist will conduct the assessment of the primary outcome at each visit, as was done in STABILITY 1. Assessors for other performance-based outcomes like ROM, strength, hop and DVJ tests will also be blinded to group allocation using this same approach.

Methods: data collection, management and analysis

Data collection methods

The CRA will collect complete contact information from the participant and two individuals who do not reside with the participant but are likely to maintain contact should the individual relocate. This information will be collected at the time of enrolment in the study. Additionally, participants will be asked to update their contact information at each follow-up visit.

Participants wishing to complete PROs through direct data entry are provided a unique username and must set up a strong password. Providing the option to patients for direct data entry allows the CRAs to collect patient reported data when participants cannot physically attend a follow-up visit (eg, vacation). The software records the date, time and user information in the audit log so that the data submitted through direct data entry can serve as a primary data source. The audit log also tracks initial data values, updated data values and reasons for changes made to updated data values.

Similarly, CRAs can elect to complete data collection directly online if they are registered in the database under a unique username and password. Any hard copy CRFs are stored at the site within research folders labelled by participant and serve as source documents. Other source documents uploaded into the database include the operative report from the official health record and de-identified plain radiographs.

To aid the CRA with tracking participant visits, the data management software provides a participant tracker report that the CRAs can generate for their site. The report provides a list of participants by row, and each column represents a visit. Each cell provides the status of the visit as complete, incomplete, overdue or missed. Missing or incomplete forms are listed within each cell. Upcoming visits display the date the visit window opens and the date the visit window closes. The report can be limited to those with an overdue visit or with a visit coming up within a defined number of days as specified by the CRA. This feature assists the site with planning and tracking participant visits.

In addition, the data management software will send automatic email reminders to the CRA of an upcoming follow-up visit prior to the visit, on the date that the visit window opens, the ideal date and a few days after the ideal date if the CRFs remain incomplete. The CRA and site investigator are notified if the visit still remains incomplete 7 days prior to the final visit window date. The CRA can also click on a link within the participant’s dashboard to send them a reminder link at any time.

The data management software also sends an automatic email to participants (who have opted into this feature) regarding upcoming and overdue appointments. Participants will be contacted via email 1 week prior to the follow-up due date, at the due date and up to three times after the due date. If the participant does not respond to the third contact to their preferred contact, phone calls to the preferred and alternative contacts will be made by the CRA and by the surgeon until the participant completes the follow-up visit or withdraws their consent. Data collected up to the date of withdrawal will be retained for analysis.

We have implemented several strategies to promote retention including (1) collecting complete contact information from the patient and two individuals likely to maintain contact should the patient relocate, and update this information at each visit, (2) provide a letter of information detailing the purpose of the study, the importance of patient individual participation, attendance at follow-up visits and related compensation, (3) provide a list of follow-up appointments at the first follow-up visit so that patients can plan accordingly, (4) generate a secure login link for patients to complete questionnaires online, (5) send automatic, system-generated email reminders to the CRA and participant of upcoming and overdue appointments, (6) facilitate follow-up at a different site for patients who have relocated, (7) having the surgeon phone patients who miss a visit, (8) provide study-wide monthly reports of the retention rate at 6-month, 12-month and 24-month visits by centre and overall, (9) meet with any centre demonstrating <90% follow-up, (10) provide a ‘recovery report’ to patients at the end of the study that illustrates their recovery and (11) renumerate participants US$50 for completion of each of the screening/baseline, 6-month, 12-month and 24-month postoperative visits and US$20 for completion of each of the 6-week and 3-month postoperative visits. Participants that complete all study visits will also be provided with an additional US$50 payment bonus.

Data management

Electronic CRFs within the study database include specifications of type of data by element (string, numeric, date, checkbox, etc), labels by variable and element values and labels which automatically produce a data field map for the study data analysts. If applicable, numeric elements include defined minimum and maximum values and are designated as an integer or with the number of acceptable decimal places. The database has programmed within form skip logic, mutually exclusive versus check all that apply limitations and hide and show capabilities for visit-specific requirements. The database also includes between form logic that defines when visit-specific and event-specific CRFs are ordered (eg, protocol deviation, missed visit, adverse event).

The Quality Control Committee (QCC) will proactively electronically monitor participant recruitment and retention using the web-based data management system that can generate several reports. The participant tracker (described above) can be generated study-wide and missing data reports by participant or site and can be shared with the site CRA and site investigator for discussion, encouragement and resolution (if possible). The database also generates a by site and overall study recruitment curve illustrating the projected and actual recruitment by month, study flow diagram to show why patients are being excluded as well as recruitment and retention efforts, an AE report and withdrawal report (see online supplemental file 2 for membership and mandate of the QCC).

In addition, site remuneration for data collection is dependent on complete and query-free CRFs per participant by visit. EmPower provides monthly reports of visit completion and quarterly reports of corresponding site and patient remuneration by site and to the lead institution responsible for issuing site payments. In addition, a monthly report of the proportion of completed visits (6 months, 12 months, 24 months) by site, colour-coded by completeness (green ≥95%, yellow 80–94%, red <80%) will be distributed electronically and discussed at Investigator meetings.

Statistical methods

The data collected through this study will be pooled with the data from STABILITY 1 for analysis (n~1800). We will provide a descriptive summary of participants in each treatment group at each visit using means and SD for normally distributed data, median and IQR for continuous but not normally distributed data, and count and percentage for categorical data.

Statistical analysis for primary and sex-based research question

We will use a mixed-effects logistic regression using the primary composite outcome of ACLR clinical failure measured over time as the dependent variable to estimate the effects for treatment group (HT+LET, HT, BPTB+LET, BPTB, QT+LET and QT), time and the time by treatment group interaction, with meniscal repair status, age, sex, surgeon (or surgeon pair for expertise-based sites) and tibial slope as time-independent covariates and contralateral injury, post ACLR meniscus tear/retear, ipsilateral limb exposure (in hours), time since returning to sport and time since discharge from physical therapy as time-dependent covariates and surgeon as a random effect. Time to return to sport and to discharge from physical therapy are considered as covariates in the model since we expect that participants who return to sport earlier or cease to attend physical therapy earlier are more likely to experience the outcome. Both contralateral injury (yes/no) and meniscus tear/retear (yes/no) collected at each study visit will be included in the model as a time-varying covariate since the occurrence of contralateral injury and meniscus tear will alter (ie, eliminate or reduce) the risk of experiencing graft failure over the next few months while the patient recovers. Following sufficient recovery time, the patient will resume activities and again be at risk of sustaining the primary outcome (graft failure). Exposure time will be defined as the total number of hours spent playing sports since the surgery. The greater the exposure the greater the risk of graft failure. Since graft failure will occur only once during the follow-up period, the outcome data will be treated as missing after the failure occurs. Given these data are not missing at random, we will use the maximum conditional likelihood approach proposed by Skrondal and Rabe-Hesketh91 to provide an unbiased estimate of the treatment effect. Within the model, we will calculate the OR, 95% CIs and p values for the six prespecified intergroup contrasts and for change in rate of failure within each group over time.

As a second means to evaluate the primary outcome, we will conduct a time-to-event analysis to investigate the effects of surgical procedures on the hazard of graft failure. The ‘survival’ time is defined as the time from randomisation to graft failure with data censored at the time of loss to follow-up or at the end of the study, whichever occurs first. We will use a multivariable Cox frailty model with random intercepts to account for site clustering, meniscal repair status, age, sex, surgeon as time-independent covariates and contralateral injury, meniscus tear/retear, ipsilateral limb exposure, return to sport (yes/no) and discharge from physical therapy (yes/no) as time-dependent covariates. Within this model, we will calculate the HR, 95% CIs and p values for the six prespecified between-group contrasts.

To address the sex-based question, we will repeat the primary analysis for males and females separately and provide the odds of failure with a BPTB or QT graft compared with an HT+LET graft. See online supplemental file 6 for a detailed description of the statistical plan of analysis to address secondary and health economic analysis research questions.

Handling of missing data

For missing data (missing secondary outcomes or time-dependent covariates), we will evaluate whether data are missing completely at random by comparing the available data (especially at baseline) for those with and without missing data at follow-up. Multiple imputation using full conditional specification method (FCS) (also called the multivariate imputation by chained equations), which is widely applied for the arbitrary missing pattern, will be used for imputing the missing data. In particular, FCS logistic regression method will be used for imputing binary data and FCS regression method will be used for imputing continuous data. We will set the maximum number of iterations as 20 and generate 25 imputation datasets to ensure reliable inference.92 Complete case analysis will also be conducted as a sensitivity analysis to assess the impact of missing data on the estimation of intervention effect. For the primary outcome (ie, graft failure), since graft failure will occur only once during the follow-up period, the outcome data will be treated as missing after the failure occurs. Given the data are missing not at random, we will use the maximum conditional likelihood approach to handle it as described above in the analysis for the primary research question. We will also conduct the time-to-event analysis which does concern itself with data (either observed or missed) after the occurrence of graft failure. All the above analyses will be implemented using SAS V.9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina, USA).

Methods: Monitoring

Data monitoring committee

The DSMB will be convened by the National Institute of Arthritis and Musculoskeletal and Skin Diseases (NIAMS) to review and monitor the data and safety of the study. Prior to the start of recruitment, the DSMB will review the research protocol, informed consent documents and plans for data and safety monitoring and, if acceptable, approve the study to initiate enrolment. Afterwards, the DSMB will meet at least twice annually to monitor and evaluate the progress of the trial; consider factors external to the study that may have an impact on the safety of the participants or the ethics of the trial; review clinical research site performance; protect safety of study participants; report on safety and progress of trial; consider risk-benefit ratios; monitor confidentiality of the trial data; and make recommendations to the sponsor (NIAMS) regarding continuation, termination or other modifications of the trial. An emergency meeting of the DSMB may be called if there are any interim concerns. The DSMB Chair will write a report after each meeting, summarising the study status and outlining any concerns. DSMB members will include experts in orthopaedic surgery, clinical epidemiology and biostatistics (see online supplemental file 2 for details about DSMB membership and operations). There are no interim analyses or stopping rules planned for this trial.

Trial monitoring

The Clinical Coordinating Center at the University of Pittsburgh in collaboration with Western University will be responsible for the CMP under the coleadership of ABG, who will serve as quality control lead, and Stacey Wanlin, project coordinator for this trial. We will publish the results in open access journals and present at international scientific conferences (International Society of Arthroscopy, Knee Surgery and Orthopeadic Sports medicine (ISAKOS), European Society of Sports Traumatology, Knee Surgery and Arthroscopy (ESSKA), American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS), American Orthopaedic Society for Sports Medicine (AOSSM), Canadian Orthopaedic Association (COA), etc) as we did for STABILITY 1.
