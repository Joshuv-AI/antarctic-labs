Executive Summary
Via comparisons to experiments and high-fidelity simulations, we develop an accurate conductivity model
for weakly-ionized N2 gas near atmospheric pressures. Section 1 develops the fluid theory from first-
principles. In Section 2, we compare this model to data from swarm experiments — noting excellent agree-
ment. Next, Section 3 treats the dynamic problem of relativistic electron beam propagation in N2 . Once
more, we observe great agreement between the fluid prescription and published results from a Boltzmann
solver. Finally, we conclude in Section 4.

1 Formulating a Response Model
Nitrogen gas may display a dielectric response to an ionizing source. This source may be the impact ion-
ization of fast particles, or the electron avalanche ionization that results from the application of a strong
electric field to the medium. Using first-principles, we formulate the required rate equations and constitutive
relations. We begin with the generalized Ohm’s law. Unless otherwise noted, the cgs system of units will be
used throughout this report.

Hence, our conductivity model may be improved upon by accounting for the velocity-dependence of the
effective electron-neutral collisional cross-section for momentum transfer. Moreover, we ought to know the
proper form of f0 ; as it may not be a Maxwellian — even in steady-state.
Several effects may cause a steady-state distribution to deviate from a Maxwellian. For example, electric
fields may bias the secondary electrons along the field direction, and excitation collisions may also distort
the distribution near resonances.
To accommodate these effects, we employ a python implementation of the two-term Boltzmann solver,
BOLSIG [25]. BOLOS [26], as it is called, fully neglects electron-(ion/electron) collisions. BOLOS requires
cross-sections for all plasma electron interactions. We obtain these from LxCat [27, 28]. These include
elastic scattering and key vibrational modes.
Next, we solve for the steady-state distribution functions across a wide range of electric field strengths.
Then, given the steady-state electron temperature, we construct a kinetic (correction) coefficient, gσ (Te ).
Using gσ , we modify the electron-neutral resistivity; to wit

                                              ηen → gσ (Te )ηen .                                          (42)

Figure 3 shows gσ vs. the electron temperature. Plotted alongside the BOLOS result (solid, “blue” curve)
is the equivalent for a Maxwellian (dashed, “green” curve). Notice that the Maxwellian gσ is not constant;
contra the approximation used in Eq. (5). This variation with electron temperature is a consequence of the
(irregular) velocity-dependence of the effective momentum transfer cross-section, σen (v).
Interestingly, while the two curves differ in Fig. 3 — particularly at higher electron energies (Te > 10 eV)
— they are on the same order at lower energies. This suggests that the Maxwellian approximation is not
terribly inaccurate at these temperatures.
Note: the kinetic coefficient for the electron-ion resistivity, which also corrects for electron-electron colli-
sions, is known for a fully ionized (weakly-coupled) plasma [29]. It is constant across temperature, and its
value is ≈ 0.51 for Zi = 1.

2 Comparison to Swarm Experiments
As already noted, a sufficiently strong field causes the dielectric breakdown of the gas. The liberated elec-
trons may then be accelerated to high energies by the field, generating further ionizations — a process known
as the Townsend avalanche (discharge). Then, the resulting plasma may drive a return current in response to
the applied field. The associated drift velocity of the plasma electrons may be measured to infer the electron
avalanche rate, mobility, etc.
Our model equations may be applied to this problem; thus providing a means of validation via comparisons
to empirical results for the steady-state drift velocity, temperature, etc.
To proceed, we require formulae for the electron avalanche rate, and the secondary electron birth energy (or
characteristic energy). We obtain these quantities, as functions of the applied electric field, from BOLOS.
The generalized loss function, however, is not a direct output of BOLOS. Rather, we obtain RN2 by inspect-
ing Eq. (22), which shows that the loss function is given by

What is particularly encouraging, however, is the excellent agreement between BOLOS and the experimental

3 1 MeV Beam Verification Study
Slinker et al. [30] solved a linearized Boltzmann equation for the secondary electrons in weakly-ionized N2 .
Electron-ion collisions were neglected, and spatial homogeneity was assumed. Also note that recombination
was neglected in the model, as it does not play a dynamical role over the considered time-scale.

4 Concluding Remarks
We showed that a simplified hydrodynamic model to describe the dielectric response of weakly-ionized N2
plasma to a beam/E-field source — as detailed in Section 1 — agrees fairly well with experiment (Section
2) and time-dependent Boltzmann simulations (Section 3). Given the uncertainties in the relevant cross-
sections, this level of agreement likely proves sufficient for most purposes.
Furthermore, in Sections 1.2 and 1.6, we demonstrated that space charge effects and the displacement cur-
rent may often be neglected — which greatly simplifies the field solve. We simply require high conductivity;
i.e., 4πaσ/c ≫ 1. However, on a short enough time-scale (prior to sufficient ionization), these effects may
be important.
Moreover, we stress the importance of the excitation-type energy losses suffered by the secondary electrons.
We found that a generalized loss term, RN2 is essential to accurately predict the secondary electron temper-
ature. In particular, vibrational excitation cooling of N2 molecules by secondary electrons is a key effect.
Nevertheless, despite this rich physics, Fig. 2 showed that the effective electron-neutral collision frequency
for momentum transfer is largely insensitive to the electron temperature in the realistic range for near atmo-
spheric plasmas (∼ 1-10 eV). For this reason, it may be sufficient to treat the secondary electron temperature
as a constant for the purposes of calculating the background’s constitutive relations, or to subsume the de-
pendence in a mobility (which nominally depends upon the electric field alone).
