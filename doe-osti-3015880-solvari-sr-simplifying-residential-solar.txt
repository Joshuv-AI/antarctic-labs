 Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

 Accelerated weathering and material compatibility testing for polymeric and asphaltic materials.

 NLR will be testing the durability of the polymeric materials proposed for use in the supporting
 structures designed by TESCI solar. This will include the bare materials and coupons attached to
 asphalt shingles. The exposure will be in damp heat and separately heat, humidity and UV light,
 all followed by mechanical evaluation.

 1

 Modification 1 is an extension of the first round of testing with both a continuation of some of
 the same testing and addition of new testing methods and materials. The testing of materials in
 the condition of A3 looking at mechanical durability will be continued looking at the materials
 used in the mounting brackets. We will be adding in testing of the silicone adhesive to the
 module and of the module itself. For the mounting brackets, testing will also be conducted at
 multiple temperatures and humidity levels to allow for extrapolation to the field.

 CRADA benefit to DOE, Participant, and US Taxpayer:

 • Uses the laboratory’s core competencies.

 Summary of Research Results:

 Purpose:

 The purpose of this CRADA is to reduce the risk of the polymeric component choice through
 accelerated weathering testing. NREL will evaluate 3 different polymeric materials intended to
 be used as mounting materials and two different asphalt shingle types. Materials and coupon
 samples will be exposed to damp heat (85°C and 85% RH) or to a combination of heat, humidity,
 and UV radiation. Afterwards, NREL will then test mechanical strength over time to predict best
 long-term performer. The coupon samples attached to asphalt shingles will be used to validate
 material compatibility between the mount polymers, flashing tape, and asphalt shingles. The
 results will be used to down select the most durable materials.

 Statement of Work:

 The participant, TESCI Solar, will be responsible for getting samples ready for testing to NREL
 (Tasks 1 through 4). These are expected to be completed within the first month or two of the
 project. A range of expected numbers of samples is given to account for bad tests that must be
 repeated or additional evaluation cycles if desired. The dogbone samples will be construction
 according to ASTM D638 Type I, and the Izod impact samples will be 2.5”×1”×0.5”. The
 coupon samples for tensile and Izod impact testing will be attached to asphalt shingles with the
 intention that the composite materials will be removed just prior to evaluation. It is also desirable
 to evaluate the adhesive properties of the asphalt shingle to the composites after accelerated
 exposure. The exact form of these samples will be determined through a few trial experiments in
 the first month of the project. The flexible asphalt side of the lap shear samples will be attached
 to a rigid surface prior to testing.

 Once received by NREL, the samples will be exposed to either the condition A3 of IEC 62788-7-
 2 (0.8 W/m²/nm at 340 nm, 65°C, 20% RH, 85°C black panel temperature using a Xenon arc
 lamp compatible with ASTM D7869) or damp heat (85°C and 85% RH). Samples will be
 removed at the intervals specified in task 2 for all exposures and will include at least 3 replicates.
 After all exposures and tests are completed, additional material level evaluations will be
 performed as desired to evaluate any issues we see (Task 5). Lastly, a final report will be written
 presenting the results of this study.

 Introduction:
 The company Technology, Engineering & Consulting for the Solar Industry (TECSI) is
 developing a photovoltaic (PV) system designed for rapid installation on asphalt shingle
 residential rooftops. The technology has been spun off into the company Solvari,
 www.solvarisolar.com, and is now manufacturing and selling the modules.

 The modules are constructed as frameless modules and instead use small “feet” at the corners
 and sides of the modules to mount them to the roof. The feet have a flange with two screw holes
 each to secure them to the roof. These feet utilize a roofing adhesive to seal up the screw holes to
 prevent water ingress. Once mounted on the rooftop, a leveling screw is used to adjust the height
 of the panel to adjust for variations in the roof curvature. A handle is also included on the center
 and back of the modules to allow for easy handling. This drastically simplified installation
 process enabling installation speeds for up to 1 kW every 10 minutes.

 Figure 1. Backside image of a module with integrated microinverter and mounting feet.

 This design is also technology agnostic and can be used with crystallin Si or thin film
 technologies, or with glass/glass or glass/polymer-back sheet modules. The only requirement is
 that the modules arrive at Solvari without out a frame.

 With this design, the primary questions being addressed by this research are for the mechanical
 strength and durability of the material used for the feet, the strength, sealing capability, and
 durability of the roof flashing tape, and the adhesion strength of the adhesive used on the feet.
 For this purpose, we evaluated, 5 different materials for the feet, 2 flashing tapes, and two
 different shingles. Tests included exposure to ultraviolet light under IEC 62788-7-2 condition
 A3, and to heat and humidity (RH) in the dark at 85°C/85% RH, 85°C/25% RH, 65°C/25% RH,
 105°C/25% RH, and 85°C/50% RH. Evaluation methods included Izod impact testing, lap shear
 testing, and tensile strength testing.

 Experimental Methods:
 Materials:
 The shingles were standard residential asphalt shingles and were distinguished only as being
 black or brown. No significant characteristics of construction of the shingles was available to us.
 Similarly, the flashing tapes were simply distinguished as being either black or grey with very
 little known about them besides the fact that they are typical materials used in the roofing
 industry. The flashing tapes are only known to contain materials such as a polyolefin elastomer,
 carbon black, Talc, Ca(OH)₂, Mg(OH)₂, and other non-disclosed components.

 For the feet, five materials were used and designated as “L”, “S”, “B1”, “B2”, and “B3”. These
 are composed of various mixtures with base polymers of polycarbonate, polyphenylene ether,
 polystyrene, polyamide-6, polyethylene terephthalate, and polybutylene terephthalate. All these
 materials were pigmented with carbon black for it’s antioxidative and UV absorbing abilities as a
 stabilizer. Beyond just the basic material base composition, we were not told more about its
 composition.

 The adhesives evaluated for adhering the feet to the module were silicone based designated as T1
 and T2. T1 is a neutral condensation cure silicone commonly used in the PV industry for
 adhering junction boxes to the backs of modules. According to the manufacturer’s MSDS, it
 “may” release methanol, ethanol, or methyl-ethyl ketoxime. We presume that these statements
 are misleading and the material is simply an oxime condensation cure silicone elastomer. T2 is a
 fast curing two-part silicone adhesive commonly used in the PV industry for adhering the frame
 to a module.

 Flashing Tape Adhesion:
 The durability of the flashing tape adhesion strength to the feet to the roofing shingles was
 evaluated using a lap shear configuration. For consistency, the multi-layer shingles were cut
 apart into single layer pieces. Only the outer surface of the shingles were used for the adhesion
 testing. Because the feet materials were obtained in the form of a dogbone sample for testing or
 as a sheet that was cut into dogbone samples, the dogbone samples were cut into two pieces to

 Izod Testing:
 Izod testing is used to determine the impact resistance or toughness of a material and helps to
 evaluate how well a material can absorb energy when subjected to a sudden force or impact. In
 an Izod test, the amount of energy needed to break a sample is determined as the reduction in the
 swing height of the hammer arm. Figure 11 shows a typical apparatus for Izod impact testing.

 Figure 11. Schematic Image of an Izod testing apparatus.

 In an Izod test, a notched specimen is clamped vertically in a vise, and a weighted pendulum is
 dropped from a specified height. The pendulum strikes the specimen at the notch height but on
 the opposite side, and the energy absorbed by the material during fracture is calculated from the
 reduction in the final height of the swing arm. This energy represents the materials’ impact
 resistance. For this test, we used a 5 lb Izod pendulum.

 Some of the Izod samples came to us as bars and we had the machine shop us a custom drill bit
 in order to cut out the notch. The type S material came in sheets, and we were able to use a water
 jet cutter to cut out the samples. In both cases, the radius of the curvature of the tip is within the
 ASTM D256-10 standard compliance.

 The Izod sample dimensions are 2.5” L x 1/2” W x1/8” T in accordance with ASTM D256. The
 notch radius is 0.25±0.05 mm. The following figure below shows the dimension design
 qualifications considered for the sizing of the Izod samples.

 dimensions but were only about 3.18 mm thick.

 Tensile Strength Testing:
 Tension testing is where material is loaded until a predetermined deformation level, or the
 sample fails. This test provides load and displacement data to find material properties such as the
 modulus of elasticity, toughness and maximum tensile strength. The sample shape for tensile
 testing is a dog bone and all samples were 7” L × ¾” W × 1/8” Tall. Tensile testing was
 performed using an Instron 5900 Universal Testing Machine using 5 kN pneumatic side action
 grips and serrated grips to ensure a secure hold on the specimens. The grips were connected to
 compressed air set to 55 psi, generating approximately 8 kN of clamping force. A universal joint
 was used to maintain alignment throughout the test and to allow for mobility in loading each
 sample. Strain data was collected using an Instron Advanced Video Extensometer, while the
 Bluehill 3 software controlled the machine and recorded the test data. Each specimen was dog
 bone-shaped with a thickness of 3.18 mm and a width of 13 mm. The grip-to-grip length was set
 to 115 mm, and a preload of 1 N was applied to the sample before testing commenced.

 Prior to testing, each specimen was prepared by applying reference marks using a stencil and
 white paint marker. These marks were used both to ensure consistent grip placement and to serve
 as displacement reference points for the video extensometer. We marked each sample to an
 approximate, uniform size. After marking, the sample was mounted in the pneumatic grips and
 carefully aligned using the universal joint to ensure it was as straight as possible. Figure 17
 shows the sample marking and the stencil for making the marks.

 23

 Figure 17. Image of dog bone sample marking tool (bottom) alongside a marked dog bone sample
 (top).

 Once the sample was secured, the test was initiated using Bluehill 3 software, which
 automatically applied the 1 N preload and extended the sample at a constant rate of 5 mm/min.
 Throughout the test, the video extensometer tracked the displacement of the reference points,
 providing the strain data. The system recorded load and displacement data, which was then
 exported for further analysis. The raw data was processed using an Excel macro, which
 automatically calculated the stress and strain for the test. Figure 18 includes an example of

 able to determine the change in displacement due to it losing its reference point. These are
 examples why certain data points have been classified as “bad data” and excluded from the data

 net of no effect at 65°C. For some of the measurements between 85°C and 105°C, the rate of
 degradation switches between positive and negative making an Arrhenius fit impossible,
 Figure 55. For the Izod test there is an increase in the measured values with time for exposure at

 Discussion:
 Material B2 for ETB, and B3 for Izod strength are estimated to have a lifetime of 8 to 24 years in
 hot and severe climates. However, materials S is only predicted to have a lifetime of 2 to 3 years
 for Izod strength. The analysis of the other material properties were not extrapolated, but this
 was in a large part because the degradation was too small to measure and the degradation may
 have had active mechanisms with both positive and negative effects. In Figure 62 the
 degradation rate expressed as the relative percent per hour, is plotted for the different materials
 and the different mechanical properties. Looking at the combined set of exposures can give one a
 sense of the relative degradation rate. While the exposures at 85°C/85% RH and 105°C/25% RH
 provide the largest signal, these exposure conditions are very high and not necessarily good
 predictors of performance. The best predictor would be the experiments at 65°C/25% RH but the
 signal strength is often very poor and it is often not the lowest degradation rate as would be
 expected. Amongst the various properties, a consistent trend between the properties is not seen,
 and the lower lifetime for the Izod strength of material S may be an anomalous result.

 Conclusion:
 In these experiments, material candidate L was eliminated because of an interaction with the
 flashing tapes tested. With respect to lap shear testing, the flashing tapes did not behave
 significantly differently with the feet materials tested. For the silicone material, T1 and T2, most
 of the lap shear failure was cohesive which is consistent with there not being a significant or
 consistent difference between the adhesion to different substrate materials. However, T1 did
 have some lower results in adhesion to glass when the failure mode switched to an adhesive
 mode.

 Tensile testing showed greater changes in material S in UV exposure as compared to damp heat.
 Materials B1 and B3 showed increases in Izod breaking energy upon exposure to UV light, but
 B2 showed a moderate decrease in breaking energy.

 The thermal extrapolation experiments with Material B2 for ETB, and B3 for Izod strength are
 estimated to have a lifetime of 8 to 24 years in hot and severe climates. However, materials S is
 only predicted to have a lifetime of 2 to 3 years for Izod strength. Amongst the various
 properties, a consistent trend between the properties is not seen, and the lower lifetime for the
 Izod strength of material S may be an anomalous result.
