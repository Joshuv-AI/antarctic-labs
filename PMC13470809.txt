Influence of Water–Cement Ratio on WPU-Modified Ultrafine Cement Grouting Materials and Reinforcement Mechanism for Crushed Sandstone
Authors: Xiang Cheng, Chaoyu Tian, Yanfen Wang, Guangming Zhao, Yian Chen, Bofang Zhou, Xiangrui Meng, Yingjie Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To balance slurry workability for deep-mine grouting and the load-bearing capacity of grouted crushed-sandstone, a waterborne polyurethane (WPU)-modified ultrafine cement composite grouting material was successfully developed using ultrafine Portland cement blended with a polycarboxylate superplasticizer, an expansive agent, and an accelerator. Macro- and microscale characterizations were conducted to clarify the influence of w/c (0.40∼0.60) on slurry properties, mechanical performance, hydration kinetics, and microstructure. Load-bearing behavior and failure modes were further evaluated via crushed sandstone cementation tests. The results show that the addition of 0.5 wt % WPU shortens the initial setting time, reduces volumetric shrinkage, and enhances mechanical strength. With increasing w/c, fluidity and setting time increase markedly, whereas mechanical strength first increases and then decreases. Optimum performance is achieved at a w/c of 0.45, where the 1 d compressive and flexural strengths increase by 22.87 and 4.3%, respectively, relative to the control sample. In the grouting reinforcement test, the 28 d peak strength of the WPU-modified grouted crushed-sandstone specimen at a w/c of 0.45 reaches 20.09 MPa, which is 38.55% higher than that of the control sample, indicating improved load-bearing capacity and elastic deformation capability. XRD, FTIR, SEM, TG, and isothermal calorimetry collectively suggest that the w/c can modulate interfacial coverage and bridging effects of WPU by regulating the water state and pore structure, thereby improving matrix continuity. Accordingly, a triple-coupled mechanism involving hydration-kinetics regulation, interfacial cooperative enhancement, and constrained pore-structure evolution is proposed.

Introduction

In recent years, as shallow coal resources in China have gradually been depleted, coal mining activities have increasingly extended to greater depths. Statistics show that among China’s proven coal reserves, only 19.97% are located at depths shallower than 1000 m, whereas the proportions at depths of 1000–2000 m and 2000–4000 m reach 21.08 and 58.95%, respectively. The surrounding rock environment of deep roadways is characterized by high in situ stress, intense mining disturbances, pronounced rheological behavior, and soft rock properties, which induce large surrounding-rock deformation and deep microcrack propagation, thereby posing serious threats to coal mine safety. As an efficient support technique for coal mine roadways, grouting technology plays a critical role in maintaining roadway stability by filling fractures and pores, enhancing structural integrity and load-bearing capacity, and reducing the risk of deformation and failure. However, under such complicated mining conditions, fracture closure, seepage-channel contraction, and slurry rheology jointly affect diffusion behavior and reinforcement efficiency of grouting materials. Consequently, the injectability and diffusion radius of conventional grouting materials are insufficient to meet reinforcement requirements. −

At present, grouting materials used in mining are mainly divided into cement-based grouting materials and chemical grouting materials. Among them, Portland cement has become the most widely used grouting material in the coal mining industry due to its good durability, low cost, high stone strength, and wide availability of raw materials. Nevertheless, traditional cement grouting materials are often associated with slow setting, high bleeding, and insufficient early strength, and when particle size is relatively large, filtration clogging readily occurs in microfractures, limiting their application in deep microfracture permeation grouting. Therefore, ultrafine grinding and the incorporation of composite admixtures have become important approaches for improving the comprehensive performance of cement-based grouting materials to satisfy diverse construction conditions in coal mining. Compared with inorganic additives, polymer modifiers, owing to their low slurry viscosity, good injectability, and ability to penetrate microfractures, have become ideal external additives for modifying cement-based grouting materials. Waterborne polyurethane (WPU) modified cement-based composites, as a novel type of organic–inorganic composite material, have been widely applied in many fields due to their excellent performance and environmental friendliness. Fan et al. found that the incorporation of an appropriate amount of waterborne polyurethane can improve the mechanical properties, durability, and microstructure of concrete. Lee , constructed polyurethane-modified composite concrete using two-step and three-step casting methods. The results showed that damping performance and tensile performance were substantially enhanced. Tang et al. found that polyurethane can accelerate the hydration rate of ordinary Portland cement and enhance the strength of the hardened matrix. Ahmad et al. repaired existing cracks using polymer-modified cement mortar, which observed that the load-bearing capacity of the repaired cracks was greatly improved. Moreover, previous studies have shown that grouting material diffusion and filling behavior in fractured rock are strongly affected by fracture aperture, stress state, seepage conditions, and slurry rheology, which further influence bonding at the rock–grouting material interface, permeability reduction, and the mechanical response of grouted consolidated bodies under stress conditions. − However, most related studies have focused on conventional mortar or concrete systems, and for ultrafine cement grouting systems, especially the interaction mechanisms at the sandstone–grouting material interface involving ultrafine cement particles, polymer phases, hydration products, and crushed sandstone aggregates remain insufficiently clarified. , In addition, the water–cement ratio (w/c), as a key parameter governing slurry rheology, bleeding, filtration effects, and polymer film continuity, still lacks a consensus regarding its influence on the hydration process and product evolution of WPU-modified ultrafine cement.

Based on this background, this study aims to develop a WPU-modified ultrafine cement grouting material for mine surrounding rock reinforcement and related underground engineering grouting fields. Ultrafine Portland cement was used as the cementitious phase, and WPU, expansive agent, accelerator, and polycarboxylate superplasticizer (PCE) were incorporated to construct an organic–inorganic composite grouting material system. The effects of w/c on bleeding behavior, fluidity, setting time, volumetric stability, and mechanical properties were systematically investigated. The correlations among different performance parameters were clarified, and the evolution of microstructure and hydration products was characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD), Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), and scanning electron microscopy (SEM). In addition, grouting reinforcement tests using crushed sandstone aggregates were conducted to evaluate the load-bearing capacity of grouted crushed-sandstone specimens and the bonding performance at the sandstone–grouting material interface under controlled laboratory conditions. The obtained results provide laboratory evidence for material design, mix proportion optimization, and reinforcement-mechanism analysis.

Materials and Methods

Raw Materials

Ultrafine Portland cement (P.O 32.5) was used as the primary cementitious material and was supplied by New Special Materials Group Co., Ltd. The cement exhibited a median particle size (D50) of 13.61 μm, which is beneficial for grouting material injectability and the filling of fine fractures in broken rock masses. As shown in Figure a, the cement mainly consisted of CaO and SiO2, with smaller amounts of Al2O3 and Fe2O3. The XRD pattern in Figure b confirmed the presence of characteristic clinker phases of ordinary Portland cement, and the main physical properties are summarized in Table . A polycarboxylate-based superplasticizer with a D50 of 5.71 μm was incorporated to improve slurry fluidity at low w/c. The expansive agent supplied by Henan Mingzhu Chemical Co., Ltd. primarily consisted of CaO (70.8%) and had a D50 of 37.14 μm. It was used to compensate for hydration shrinkage and enhance the volumetric stability of the hardened grouting material. The accelerator (J85 type), provided by Huaibei Xinda New Building Materials Co., Ltd., was mainly composed of sodium aluminate and had a D50 of 50.77 μm. It was employed to shorten the setting time and promote early age strength development. Waterborne polyurethane (WPU, model F0410), supplied by Shenzhen Jitian Chemical Co., Ltd., was used as an organic modifier to improve the toughness, crack resistance, and interfacial bonding performance of the composite grouting material. The WPU was supplied as a gel-like emulsion with a solid content of 35% ± 5%, pH of 4–6, and viscosity of 800–1200 mPa·s. Mix Proportions and Specimen Preparation

The experimental program is presented in Table . Based on previous studies and preliminary optimization tests, the dosages of WPU, expansive agent, accelerator, and PCE were fixed in this study to isolate the effect of w/c. The experimental groups were designed with w/c as the primary variable, ranging from 0.40 to 0.60. In addition, a WPU-free control group (A0) was prepared at a w/c of 0.45. Ultrafine cement, expansive agent, accelerator, and PCE were weighed separately and dry-mixed in a powder mixer for 4 min. An appropriate amount of WPU was then dispersed in mixing water and added to the dry mixture, followed by high-speed mixing for 3 min to obtain a homogeneous slurry without visible sediment, thereby preparing the WPU-modified ultrafine cement composite slurry. The slurry was cast into molds (40 mm × 40 mm × 160 mm) and demolded after 24 h. The demolded specimens were cured in a standard curing chamber (20 ± 2 °C and 99% relative humidity) for 1, 3, 7, and 28 d.

Slurry Properties Testing

According to GB/T 1346–2011, the initial and final setting times of WPU-modified ultrafine cement were determined using a Vicat apparatus. For bleeding-rate measurement, 50 mL of the composite slurry was poured into a graduated cylinder and allowed to stand for 60 min. The volumes of the supernatant water and underlying slurry were recorded, and their volume ratio, expressed as a percentage, was defined as the bleeding rate. For fluidity testing, the slurry was poured into a truncated conical mold (60 mm × 70 mm × 100 mm; height × upper inner diameter × lower inner diameter). After leveling the surface, the mold was lifted smoothly, and the slurry was allowed to spread freely until flow ceased. The final spread diameter was measured as the fluidity index. After demolding and curing for 28 d, specimen dimensions were measured using a vernier caliper, and volumetric stability was evaluated based on the volume change rate.

Mechanical Strength Testing

In accordance with GB/T 17671-2021, specimens at designated curing ages were placed vertically on the supporting cylinders of a cement flexural testing machine (DKZ-5000, Xiyi Building Materials Instrument Factory; maximum capacity 11.50 MPa) and loaded at a constant rate until fracture, and the flexural strength was recorded. The two fractured halves (80 mm × 40 mm × 40 mm) obtained after the flexural test were then subjected to compressive strength testing using an automatic constant-stress cement testing machine (YES-300, Shanghai Yingsong Industrial and Mining Equipment Instruments; maximum capacity 300 kN; loading rate 0.50 kN/s).

Microstructural Characterization

Mineral phases and their contents in the hardened specimens were semiquantitatively analyzed using an X-ray diffractometer (XRD 6000, Shimadzu, Japan) with a 10 wt % TiO2 internal standard. XRD measurements were conducted with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 A) over a 2θ range of 5∼80° at a scanning rate of 5°/min. Surface functional groups of hardened specimens were characterized by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy over a wavenumber range of 500–4000 cm–1. The microstructural morphology of hardened specimens was examined using SEM (S-3000N, Hitachi, Japan).

Grouted Crushed Sandstone Test

Figure illustrates the preparation procedure for grouted crushed-sandstone specimens. The crushed sandstone aggregates were prepared by sieving the source sandstone. The source sandstone was mainly composed of SiO2, CaCO3, and dolomite [CaMg­(CO3)2], with a density of 2.25 g·cm–3 and a porosity of 11.67%. Raw materials were weighed according to the mix proportions listed in Table , thoroughly mixed, and injected into cylindrical molds (50 mm × 100 mm) containing 200 g of crushed sandstone aggregates with particle sizes of 10–20 mm. After curing in a standard curing chamber for 24 h, the specimens were demolded and further cured for 14 d. The uniaxial compressive strength and microstructural morphology of the grouted crushed-sandstone specimens were evaluated using an RMT universal testing machine and SEM. Images of the grouted crushed-sandstone specimens at different fracture stages were selected, and surface cracks and pores were binarized for failure-mode analysis.

Results and Discussion

Slurry Properties

Figure illustrates the fluidity of WPU-modified ultrafine cement composite grouting materials at different w/c ratios. Compared with the control group A0, as the w/c increased from 0.4 to 0.6, the variation rates of fluidity for each group are −52.5, −6.5, 0.3, 21.6, and 27.4%, respectively. At a w/c of 0.45, the incorporation of WPU exerts a certain weakening effect on fluidity. It can be attributed that the enhancement of interparticle interactions by WPU leads to an increase in the apparent viscosity of the slurry, consequently resulting in higher yield stress and shear resistance. − With increasing w/c, the overall fluidity of the WPU-modified groups increases. The fluidity is relatively low at a w/c of 0.40. When the w/c reaches 0.45 or higher, the fluidity increases markedly. This is mainly attributed to the increase in liquid-phase content and the reduction in solid-phase volume fraction, which decrease the apparent viscosity of the slurry and interparticle flow resistance. , Meanwhile, the viscosity-enhancing effect of WPU on the slurry gradually weakens with increasing w/c. Although higher fluidity facilitates the filling and diffusion of grouting materials in fractures with small apertures and poor connectivity, it is not a parameter for which larger values are unconditionally preferable. An appropriate w/c should be determined in conjunction with bleeding, segregation, and strength of hardened specimens.

Fluidity of WPU-modified ultrafine cement grouting materials at different w/c.

As a suspension-type particulate system, cement slurry is prone to bleeding and segregation during placement. In general, a cement slurry can be considered stable when its bleeding rate is below 5% after a prescribed standing period. Figure presents the bleeding rates of WPU-modified ultrafine cement composite grouting materials at different w/c ratios. As shown in the figure, the bleeding rates of slurries A0 to A5 are 2, 0.1, 1, 2, 2.4, and 4%, respectively. At a w/c of 0.45, WPU exhibits a noticeable bleeding-reduction effect. WPU forms a semicontinuous three-dimensional polymer network within the slurry via bridging flocculation and film formation. This network increases the apparent viscosity and enhances particle suspension stability. Consequently, it disrupts the connectivity and reduces the effective cross-sectional area of upward water-migration pathways, thereby suppressing bleeding. − In addition, with increasing w/c, the bleeding rate of the WPU-modified groups exhibits an upward trend. When the w/c reaches 0.6, the bleeding rate of A5 is 4%, indicating a good slurry stability. As w/c increases, the free-water content rises, and excess water is unfavorable for formation of the WPU three-dimensional network. The synergistic effect of these factors leads to a gradual increase in bleeding rate.

Bleeding rate of WPU-modified ultrafine cement grouting materials at different w/c ratios.

Figure shows the setting time of WPU-modified ultrafine cement composite grouting materials at different w/c ratios. Taking A0 as the reference, when the w/c increases from 0.4 to 0.6, the relative variation rates of the initial setting time for WPU-modified groups are −50.7, −47.8, −44.9, −8.7, and 163.8%, while the relative variation rates of the final setting time are −26.4, −17.9, 11.3, 114.2, and 184.0%, respectively. It indicates a pronounced dependence of the WPU modification effect on w/c. WPU effectively promotes slurry setting at a w/c of 0.45, mainly because the hydrophilicity of WPU converts part of the free water into bound water. It can reduce the effective w/c, enabling the slurry to form a load-bearing skeleton at an earlier stage. Moreover, the setting time increases with increasing w/c. When the w/c reaches 0.6, the overall setting time exceeds that of A0. This indicates that the accelerating effect of WPU is masked by the retarding effect induced by higher w/c. Under high w/c conditions, the reduced interparticle contact and a decreased number of nucleation sites lower the effective concentrations of accelerators and expansive agents. It hinders the timely formation of a continuous skeleton by early hydration products, thus delaying setting time. Meanwhile, the number density of WPU latex particles and interfacial coverage decrease, weakening the ability of interfacial interactions to regulate the local hydration environment and ion migration. −

Setting time of WPU-modified ultrafine cement grouting materials at different w/c ratios.

Figure presents the volume change ratio of WPU-modified ultrafine cement specimens after 28 d of curing at different w/c ratios. The volume change ratios of hardened specimens A0 to A5 are −0.19, −0.06, −0.13, −0.19, −0.25, and −0.38%, respectively. At a w/c of 0.45, the shrinkage magnitude of the modified group A2 is reduced by approximately 31.57% compared with A0, indicating that the incorporation of 0.5 wt % WPU effectively inhibits shrinkage. WPU forms localized films on cement particles and hydration products, thereby covering pore walls and bridging interfacial voids in the matrix. This increases resistance to outward water migration and slows moisture loss. − The volumetric shrinkage increases with increasing w/c, and the shrinkage of A5 is approximately 6.3 times that of A1. This behavior is mainly attributed to the increased total evaporable water at higher w/c ratios, which is commonly accompanied by higher porosity and pore connectivity, thereby intensifying shrinkage induced by moisture loss. , In addition, a reduced volume fraction of WPU is unfavorable for particle coalescence and film formation, weakening the water resistance and flexible restraint.

Volume change ratio of WPU-modified ultrafine cement hardened specimens at 28 d.

Thermogravimetric Analysis and Isothermal Calorimetry

Figure shows the TG and DTG curves of hardened specimens A0, A2, and A5 cured for 28 d. The three specimens exhibit generally consistent mass-loss stages and characteristic peak positions over the temperature range of 30∼900 °C, which indicates similar main thermal decomposition pathways. The mass-loss process can be divided into four temperature intervals. The mass loss near 30∼60 °C corresponds to the evaporation of free water and physically adsorbed water. The broad mass-loss peak in the range of 60∼200 °C is mainly attributed to the dehydration of hydration products such as C–S–H gel and ettringite (AFt). A pronounced mass-loss peak appears at approximately 440 °C, corresponding to the dehydroxylation of CH. The major mass-loss peak in the range of 600∼800 °C is associated with the decarbonation of CaCO3 and the release of CO2. In the low-temperature dehydration region, the dehydration peak of A2 shifts slightly toward higher temperatures relative to A0, and the TG residual mass within 30∼200 °C is marginally higher. These features suggest that the addition of WPU delays low-temperature water loss to some extent. This behavior may be related to water adsorption and hydrogen-bonding interactions introduced by WPU. It promotes partial conversion of free water to more strongly bound water and increases the energy required for thermal removal. Consequently, the dehydration rate decreases and the peak shifts to higher temperature.

(a) TG curves of WPU-modified ultrafine cement, (b) corresponding DTG curves.

Meanwhile, WPU forms polymer films and network structures within the matrix after hardening, producing interfacial coating and bonding effects that hinder water migration and evaporation. In contrast, specimen A5 at high w/c exhibits more pronounced mass loss in the 30∼200 °C range, consistent with a higher fraction of free and weakly bound water. This indicates that low-temperature dehydration behavior is strongly influenced by variations in w/c. The CH dehydroxylation and CaCO3 decarbonation peaks intensify with increasing WPU dosage and w/c. This trend can be mainly attributed to enhanced thermal decomposition contributions from CH and carbonate-related phases. Compared with A0, A5 exhibits more pronounced mass loss in the CaCO3 decomposition region, whereas A2 shows only a slight increase. This difference can be mainly attributed to the increase in w/c to 0.6, which exerts a more pronounced influence on carbonate-related phase transformations than incorporation of 0.5% WPU.

Figure presents the heat flow and cumulative heat release curves of specimens A0, A2, and A5 within 48 h. The hydration process of Portland cement generally undergoes the preinduction period, induction period, acceleration period, deceleration period, and steady period. As shown in Figure a, the main heat-flow peak of A0 appears at 18.2 h. After incorporating 0.5% WPU, the heat flow slightly decreases during the preinduction period for A2, while the main heat-flow peak advances to 7.2 h. This phenomenon can be explained by the synergistic effects of interfacial hindrance and heterogeneous nucleation. On the one hand, WPU latex particles adsorb onto cement particle surfaces and form localized films. It may reduce the early dissolution rate or increase interfacial diffusion resistance, leading to a slight reduction in heat release during the preinduction period. On the other hand, latex particles and their film phases provide additional heterogeneous nucleation interfaces, while simultaneously reducing the effective free water fraction and increasing local ionic activity. It enables the system to reach the supersaturation condition required for C–S–H nucleation at an earlier stage, and thus promotes an earlier onset of the acceleration period. In contrast, A5 maintains a higher heat flow during the induction period, and its main heat-flow peak is delayed to 23.1 h. A higher w/c increases the liquid-phase volume fraction and enhances ionic dilution. It prolongs the time required for Ca2+ and dissolved silicate species to reach supersaturation for C–S–H nucleation. In addition, an increased interparticle spacing weakens the formation of early structure and connected skeleton. Moreover, the number density of latex particles per unit volume decreases under high w/c conditions. It can result in a lower density of heterogeneous nucleation sites, which may further amplify the delay of the main peak. As shown in Figure b, after entering the acceleration period, the heat flow of A2 remains higher than that of A0, and the cumulative heat release at 48 h reaches 111.1% of A0. It indicates that a low dosage of WPU can alter early hydration kinetics through interfacial regulation and heterogeneous nucleation, thereby increasing the degree of hydration within 48 h. The cumulative heat release of A5 is lower than that of A0, suggesting that the ionic dilution and delayed structural development dominate at early ages under high w/c conditions, resulting in suppression of the overall hydration degree.

(a) Heat flow curves within 48 h; (b) cumulative heat release curves within 48 h.

Mechanical Properties

Figure a,b present the compressive strength and the corresponding three-dimensional response surface of WPU-modified ultrafine cement specimens at different w/c ratios, respectively. At a w/c of 0.45, the compressive strength of A2 increased by 22.87, 6.86, 17.13, and 0.77% at different curing ages compared with A0. These results indicate that 0.5% WPU contributes to early age compressive strength at w/c of 0.45. This improvement can be attributed to the provision of heterogeneous nucleation interfaces and regulation of interfacial deposition behavior by WPU, thereby promoting more uniform deposition and continuity of early hydration products. The response surface exhibits a nonlinear trend, with the strength peak concentrated near w/c of 0.45 at 28 d and decreasing as w/c deviates to either side. At 28 d, the compressive strengths of the other groups differed from that of A2 by −6.5% (A1), −19.4% (A3), −29.4% (A4), and −35.9% (A5), indicating that within the optimal w/c range, increasing w/c is associated with more pronounced strength attenuation. When w/c < 0.45, insufficient free water can restrict ionic transport and dissolution–precipitation, thereby retarding hydration product formation and early structural development. When w/c > 0.45, the strengths of A3∼A5 at all curing ages tend to be lower than those of A2, and the response surface shows a distinct concave feature in this interval. This behavior can be mainly attributed to increased pore water and aggravated pore defects that weaken skeletal continuity, together with limited compensation from later-stage hydration for pore deterioration. In addition, dilution can hinder WPU coalescence and film formation, thereby weakening its reinforcing effect. Collectively, these factors contribute to a decrease in compressive strength with increasing w/c.

(a) Compressive strength of WPU-modified ultrafine cement at different w/c ratios and curing ages, (b) three-dimensional response surface of compressive strength.

Figure a,b present the flexural strength and the corresponding three-dimensional response surfaces of WPU-modified hardened specimens at different w/c ratios. At w/c of 0.45, the flexural strength of A2 is higher than that of A0 by 4.3, 3.9, 3.8, and 2.2% at the respective curing ages. Compared with compressive strength, the overall enhancement in flexural strength is relatively limited, indicating that WPU mainly inhibits defect propagation associated with bending failure to a modest extent. The flexural-strength response surface shows an overall trend similar to that of compressive strength, with the peak likewise concentrated near w/c of 0.45 and 28 d. Relative to A2, the 28 d flexural strength varies by −5.8% (A1), −18.1% (A3), −32.8% (A4), and −46.2% (A5) as w/c changes, demonstrating that within the optimal w/c range, increasing w/c is associated with more pronounced strength attenuation. When w/c > 0.45, the increased pore volume fraction reduces the effective load-bearing cross-section and decreases the local tensile capacity of the matrix, thereby facilitating crack initiation and coalescence. Meanwhile, dilution disrupts WPU particle coalescence and reduces film continuity, weakening the crack-bridging effect under flexural loading and consequently further diminishing flexural load-bearing capacity.

(a) Flexural strength of WPU-modified ultrafine cement at different w/c ratios and curing ages, (b) three-dimensional response surface of flexural strength.

Pearson correlation analysis was used to examine linear relationships among variables, and statistical significance was indicated by asterisks at p ≤ 0.05, as shown in Figure . The absolute correlation coefficients between w/c and the measured properties are all greater than 0.85, indicating strong linear associations. Among workability-related indices, fluidity, bleeding rate, and setting time generally exhibit strong positive correlations with each other. Three indices show moderate to relatively strong negative correlations with strength, indicating that increasing mixing water to improve fluidity is accompanied by a loss in strength. The correlation coefficients between w/c and the volume change ratio range from −0.89 to −1.00, demonstrating a strong negative correlation. In addition, the correlation coefficients between volume change ratio and mechanical strength range from 0.86 to 0.94. It indicates that suppressing autogenous shrinkage facilitates formation of a dense skeleton and microcrack closure, thereby enhancing both flexural and compressive strengths. The correlation coefficients between flexural and compressive strengths at different curing ages are close to 1. The finding suggests that mechanical properties at different ages respond consistently to changes in mix proportions. Overall, the performance indices show high linear correlations, indicating strong statistical interrelationships within the system.

Microstructural Characteristics

Figure a presents the XRD patterns of WPU-modified ultrafine cement specimens at 28 d, with TiO2 as an internal standard. Qualitative analysis indicates that the detected phases mainly include CaCO3, ettringite (AFt), CH, residual clinker phases, and an amorphous fraction largely associated with C–S–H-type hydrates. The peaks of C2S, C3S, and C3A are primarily attributed to residual unhydrated clinker phases. The CaCO3 may originate from carbonate-bearing raw materials and from the partial carbonation of CH formed during cement and expansive-agent hydration. Minor amounts of AFm, together with aluminate carbonate phases such as Hc and Mc, suggest aluminate-phase transformation and reactions involving carbonate ions in the system.

XRD patterns and semiquantitative analysis of WPU-modified ultrafine cement samples at different w/c ratios (a, b).

Based on the Rietveld full-pattern refinement results with TiO2 as an internal standard, as shown in Figure b, comparison between A0 and A2 at w/c of 0.45 shows that incorporation of 0.5% WPU reduces the AFt content from 6.51 to 5.21%. This result suggests that WPU may influence ettringite crystal growth by coating particle surfaces or regulating ionic activity in the pore solution. As w/c increases from 0.40 to 0.60, the phase assemblage exhibits a clear evolution. The amorphous phase content increases from 71.09 to 75.31%, exceeding 60% in all groups, which mainly originates from C–S–H gel. Correspondingly, the overall content of unhydrated clinker phases decreases, with C2S decreasing from 5.41 to 2.60% (a reduction of approximately 51.9%) and C3S showing a similar downward trend, while the CH content increases from 1.32 to 2.05%. These findings indicate a higher degree of hydration at higher w/c ratios. In addition, the CaCO3 content increases from 11.75 to 13.68%, which can be attributed to improved pore connectivity that facilitates CO2 diffusion and thereby enhances carbonation, consistent with the thermogravimetric analysis results. The AFt content continuously decreases from 5.95 to 3.96%, indicating that under the combined effects of higher w/c and enhanced carbonation, the overall content of sulfoaluminate hydrate phases declines.

Figure shows the FTIR spectra of WPU-modified ultrafine cement specimens cured for 28 d at different w/c ratios. The peak positions of all spectra are essentially identical, and no new characteristic peaks are observed. It indicates that variations in w/c do not alter the types of main hydration products, and the chemical environment is jointly governed by inorganic hydration products and organic components from WPU. The absorption peaks at 458 and 974 cm–1 are attributed to Si–O–Si bending and Si–O stretching vibrations, respectively, reflecting the silicate framework of C–S–H gel. The band near 537 cm–1 is mainly associated with silicate-framework bending vibrations and may result from superposition of residual clinker silicate phases and hydrated silicate gel. The absorption band near 1116 cm–1 is dominated by asymmetric Si–O stretching vibrations, which reflect changes in the degree of silicate-chain polymerization and may partially overlap with S–O stretching vibrations of sulfate groups. Carbonate-related absorptions appear near 1428, 875, and 713 cm–1, corresponding to asymmetric stretching and bending vibrations of CO3 2–. It is indicated that the samples have a certain degree of carbonation. The O–H stretching vibration of CH is observed at 3642 cm–1, as well as a broad band at 3440 cm–1 and a weak band at 1640 cm–1 corresponding to O–H stretching and H–O–H bending vibrations of water molecules, indicating the presence of interlayer or adsorbed water. The C–H stretching vibration at 2956 cm–1 is a characteristic signal of polyurethane segments, which demonstrates that WPU is retained in the hardened matrix.

As shown in Figure , analysis of the relative areas of the fitted peaks indicates that WPU incorporation and variation in w/c jointly regulate the structural characteristics of silicate chains. At w/c of 0.45, A2 exhibits slight increases in the proportions of Q3 and Q4 compared with A0, whereas the proportion of Q2 remains essentially unchanged, indicating that a low dosage of WPU promotes a tendency toward higher silicate-chain polymerization. As w/c increases from 0.40 to 0.60, the proportion of Q2 decreases, whereas those of Q2–3 and Q3 increase simultaneously, accompanied by an increase in the relative area fraction of CO3 2– related peaks. These results indicate a shift in spectral contributions toward higher wavenumbers and enhanced carbonate absorption, suggesting that higher w/c ratios promote a higher degree of hydration while concomitantly intensifying carbonation effects. The average degree of polymerization (DOP) of each group was calculated using DOP=1.5AQ1−2+2AQ2+2.5AQ2−3+3AQ3+4AQ4AQ1−2+AQ2+AQ2−3+AQ3+AQ4 , yielding values of 2.248, 2.228, 2.255, 2.262, 2.283, and 2.311, respectively. The DOP generally increased with WPU incorporation and increasing w/c. In conjunction with the mechanical performance results, the increase in the average degree of polymerization of C–S–H does not fully correspond to mechanical improvement. Instead, pore-structure deterioration and interfacial weakening may be the primary causes of the decline in mechanical performance.

Grouting Reinforcement Test

To evaluate the bonding load-bearing capacity of WPU-modified ultrafine cement grouting material for crushed sandstone aggregates in mining grouting reinforcement scenarios, the complete uniaxial compressive stress–strain curves of grouted crushed-sandstone specimens were obtained, as shown in Figure . After WPU incorporation, A2 exhibits a higher slope and a wider elastic-stage range than A0, indicating enhanced deformation resistance during early loading. The peak strength increases from 16.57 to 20.09 MPa, corresponding to an increase of approximately 21.24%, whereas A2 shows a rapid decline in postpeak load-bearing capacity. These results may be attributed to the densification of the cemented matrix by WPU and the strengthened bonding between hydration products and crushed sandstone aggregates. In addition, It has an inhibitory effect on the connection of microcracks, thereby improving prepeak stiffness and ultimate load-bearing capacity.

Compressive stress–strain curves of grouted crushed-sandstone specimens.

Regarding the effect of w/c, as w/c increases from 0.40 to 0.60, the compaction characteristics gradually weaken. The higher fluidity of slurries facilitates filling of macroscopic construction voids between aggregates, allowing the compaction process to be completed more rapidly at the initial loading stage. The slope of the elastic stage decreases with increasing w/c, reflecting a reduction in the equivalent modulus, which originates from the lower solid-phase volume fraction and reduced matrix densification. Under high w/c conditions, formation of bleeding channels and shrinkage-induced debonding further exacerbates stiffness degradation. With increasing w/c, the nonlinear damage development stage exhibits a widened, plateau-like prepeak nonlinear region, indicating increased apparent ductility and prepeak deformation adaptability. This behavior is mainly attributed to enhanced damage dispersion induced by higher w/c. The peak strength first increases and then decreases, with A2 being the optimal group in terms of peak strength. By A5, the peak strength decreases to 9.13 MPa, corresponding to a reduction of approximately 54.55%, indicating that pore structure deterioration and interfacial weakening dominate the reduction in load-bearing capacity at high w/c. Postpeak load-bearing behavior is characterized by a gradual decrease in the postpeak descending rate, leading to improved stress adaptability and enhanced energy dissipation capacity.

As shown in Figure , binarized failure morphologies under uniaxial compression indicate that crack evolution is strongly dependent on the microstructural characteristics of grouted crushed-sandstone specimens. In Figure , T, S, and TS denote tensile failure, shear failure, and tensile-shear composite failure, respectively. Primary cracks preferentially initiate at macroscopic voids or locally weak regions and propagate along discontinuities in the load-bearing skeleton and stress concentration paths, ultimately coalescing into macroscopic failure bands. Specimens in group A0 exhibit the most complex crack networks, in which vertically penetrating cracks coexist with multiple oblique secondary cracks, accompanied by pronounced crack branching. This pattern represents a typical composite failure mode involving tensile splitting and shear sliding and reflects pronounced matrix heterogeneity, whereby damage accumulates simultaneously at multiple strength-weak points, leading to divergent crack paths and extensive branching. In contrast, the failure mode of A2 is characterized by penetration of a limited number of macroscopic primary cracks. Suppression of branched cracks and concentration of through-going crack paths indicate that WPU modification enhances crack resistance and deformation compatibility of the matrix, promotes stress localization within the main failure band, and results in a shear-dominated failure mode. However, at the same WPU dosage, further increases in w/c lead to redispersed crack distributions, with increased crack numbers and more uniform spatial distributions. Accordingly, the dominant failure mechanism shifts from shear-dominated to longitudinal splitting-dominated, accompanied by a marked reduction in the concentration of penetrating primary cracks. This evolution is associated with pore defect accumulation and skeleton stiffness degradation induced by high w/c ratios. Under compressive loading, the loosened matrix is unable to effectively restrain lateral expansion, thereby triggering multipoint tensile splitting and ultimately resulting in a relatively gradual failure process characterized by columnar splitting.

Mechanism Analysis

Figure illustrates the influence of w/c on the microstructure evolution and mechanical mechanism of WPU-modified ultrafine cement. At the molecular level, polar groups in WPU can form hydrogen bonds with water molecules and Si–OH groups on C–S–H surfaces, promoting WPU interfacial adsorption and bound-water stabilization. This favors local water retention and early hydration-product deposition. As capillary water is gradually consumed during hydration, WPU latex particles coalesce into localized covering and bridging structures, thereby improving crack-propagation resistance. At low w/c, a high solid-phase volume fraction is associated with increased slurry apparent viscosity and yield stress. However, the high-viscosity rheological state tends to impair fluidity and restrict air release, which tends to promote formation of closed pores and particle agglomeration defects. In addition, insufficient available water tends to impose combined constraints of mass-transfer resistance and internal water deficiency on hydration of C3S and C2S, thereby limiting development of mechanical performance.

Influence mechanism of w/c on WPU-modified ultrafine cement grouting material (a–c).

As w/c increases to an appropriate range, the supply of reactive water becomes sufficient to promote clinker dissolution and hydration progression, accompanied by a reduction in unhydrated phases and an increase in CH formation. The in situ carbonate products may provide partial pore filling and crack-tip blunting effects. Within this range, hydration-water availability and slurry fluidity tend to be relatively coordinated, enabling sustained C–S–H gel formation and development of a more compact packing structure. Meanwhile, WPU tends to distribute more uniformly on hydration-product surfaces, allowing its coverage and bridging toughening effects to act more effectively and suppress development of connected capillary pores. These coupled effects are associated with a relatively favorable microstructural state and mechanical response for the composite system.

However, as the w/c further increases, it becomes difficult for the low-volume fraction WPU to establish a continuous polymer film in the system. The coverage morphology of WPU will shift from relatively continuous to discrete bridging and point-like distribution, weakening the toughening effect. At the same time, the increase in free water will lead to an increase in capillary pores in the hardened matrix and enhanced connectivity. Cracks can easily initiate at the holes and weak interfaces and propagate along the connected pore channels. The overall structure of the matrix shifts from relatively dense to lose, resulting in a weakening of mechanical properties.

Conclusions

A WPU-modified ultrafine cement composite grouting material with rapid setting, slight expansion, and enhanced strength and toughness is developed for mining grouting reinforcement. The effects of w/c on its workability, mechanical performance, hydration behavior, microstructure, and grouting reinforcement performance are evaluated. The main conclusions are summarized as follows.(1)The w/c plays a dominant role in the workability and development of the hardened structure of WPU-modified ultrafine cement grouting material. As the w/c increases from 0.40 to 0.60, fluidity increases, while the bleeding rate, setting time, and volumetric shrinkage also increase. Mechanical strength shows a rise-then-fall trend. Considering workability, volumetric stability, and mechanical performance, the optimal w/c is 0.45.(2)At a w/c of 0.45, 0.5 wt % WPU improves the early age performance of the ultrafine cement system. Compared with the WPU-free control group, the initial and final setting times are shortened by 47.80 and 17.92%, respectively. The bleeding rate decreases to 1.0%, and the 28 d volume change ratio is reduced by 31.57%. The 1 d compressive strength of A2 is 22.87% higher than that of the control, but this gain decreases at later ages. The flexural strength at all curing ages remains higher than that of the control.(3)Isothermal calorimetry and TGA results show that WPU affects the early hydration process and thermal stability of the hardened specimens. At a w/c of 0.45, the main heat-flow peak appears approximately 11 h earlier than that of the control, and the thermal decomposition behavior tends to be more stable. XRD, FTIR, and SEM analyses show that the hardened specimen at a w/c of 0.45 has the most compact microstructure. With increasing w/c, the features related to clinker hydration and C–S–H silicate-chain polymerization become more pronounced, but pore defects and matrix looseness also increase.(4)At a w/c of 0.45, the grouted crushed-sandstone specimen shows the best load-bearing performance, with a 38.55% increase in 28 d peak compressive strength and improved elastic deformation characteristics. The strength deterioration is more pronounced when the w/c exceeds 0.45 than when it is lower than 0.45. SEM observations show that A2 has narrower interfacial gaps, improved interface continuity, and a denser grouting-material matrix, whereas excessive w/c leads to more open gaps and loose porous features.

In addition, long-term durability under harsh mine water environments is not directly evaluated. Therefore, further stress–seepage coupled grouting tests, durability evaluation, and field validation are needed before direct application to deep mine roadway reinforcement.
