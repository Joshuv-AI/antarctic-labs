Introduction
 The Department of Energy (DOE) issued a Request for Information (RFI) in December
2025 inviting public comments regarding partnerships for transformational Artificial Intelligence
(AI) models. DOE is establishing the Genesis Mission Consortium, a public-private partnership
platform to curate the trove of scientific data available across the National Laboratory complex
and develop self-improving AI models that utilize this data. This RFI solicited feedback from
industry, nonprofit organizations, universities, independent research organizations and other
stakeholders. Specifically, the RFI asked three questions on (1) mobilizing DOE National
Laboratories to curate the scientific data in a responsible and privacy-preserving manner, (2) the
extent to which existing general-purpose AI models can be leveraged and which scientific
disciplines are priorities for such model development, and (3) mechanisms by which these AI
models can be provided to scientific communities.
 DOE received over 200 submissions, including both proprietary and nonproprietary
information. This document summarizes the responses from the nonproprietary
information-bearing submissions. These submissions were made by a total of 194 unique
responders spanning across businesses, universities, nonprofit organizations, research institutes
and laboratories as well as a variety of other contributors, including individual contributions.
Two-thirds of the submissions were made by businesses, spanning from small businesses to
Fortune 100 firms. The figure below shows the breakdown of the submissions by institution
types. The rest of this document outlines a summary of the response to the three questions asked
in the RFI.

Response to Question 1
Methods for mobilizing National Labs to form public-private partnerships for data
curation

• Governance: Establish clear governance and coordination mechanisms—such as cross-lab
 steering committees or central consortium frameworks—to unify efforts and coordinate lab-
 led domain-specific efforts across the DOE complex.
• Standards: Automate and standardize data curation practices and infrastructure by
 developing common schemas, metadata requirements, and auditable data readiness pipelines,
 aligning with FAIR principles.

• Incentives: Incentivize participation from both National Laboratories and industry partners
 by associating funding with measurable milestones, recognizing data producers, and offering
 early access to curated datasets and co-development opportunities.
• Federation: Implement a federated data curation model where National Laboratories retain
 stewardship of their data while adhering to central standards and exposing AI-ready datasets
 through secure interfaces.

• Infrastructure: Invest in tools and infrastructure that transform raw data into AI-ready
 formats, focusing on analytics-ready datasets with clear provenance, uncertainty, and
 reproducibility.
Methods for anonymizing and desensitizing data or using privacy-preserving AI training
methods
• Enclaves: Prioritize confidential computing and secure enclaves, where data processing and
 AI training occur within secure environments and only non-sensitive artifacts are published.
• Federated learning: Utilize federated learning frameworks that allow models to be trained
 across distributed datasets without sharing raw data, only model updates or gradients.

• Noise: Employ differential privacy techniques to add calibrated noise to datasets or model
 outputs, ensuring that aggregate results conceal sensitive individual data and prevent re-
 identification risks.
• Synthetic data: Generate high-fidelity synthetic data based on real scientific data to allow
 model development and testing without exposing proprietary or sensitive information.
• Tiers: Implement a layered strategy for data desensitization, including tiered access with
 explicit rules and auditing, automated detection and redaction, field-level encryption, and
 dynamic masking.

Response to Question 2
Methods to fine-tune general purpose models and combine them with AI models trained in
scientific and engineering data

• Orchestration: Employ a hybrid architecture where general-purpose models act as
 orchestrators for high-level reasoning, while specialized scientific models handle domain-
 specific tasks through tool-calling and API-based integration.
• Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT): PEFT techniques, such as LoRA 1, can reduce
 computational costs and training time while maintaining the benefits of transfer learning and
 serving domain-specific queries.

• Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG): RAG 2 is also a primary method for
 incorporating curated DOE knowledge and context, especially for lower-stakes tasks, which
 enables updates at the data layer and ensures explicit citation provenance.

• Updates: Regularly update models with new experimental, simulation, and literature data,
 guided by evaluation frameworks that assess scientific utility and reproducibility. Avoid full

• Feedback: The development of AI models should actively drive data curation and
 generation, with observed model limitations informing targeted curation of existing datasets
 and the generation of new synthetic data.

Science and engineering applications that can benefit most from self-improving models, the
data needed to build and update these models, and best use cases for these models.

• Applications: Self-improving AI models are prioritized in a broad range of scientific and
 engineering domains, including materials science and chemistry, energy systems and grid;
 earth sciences (including critical minerals); nuclear and fusion science; advanced computing
 (including quantum computing and microelectronics) and AI; advanced manufacturing;
 biosciences and health; national security and infrastructure; physics (including astrophysics,
 cosmology, and particle physics); and other areas. The histogram in Figure (2) breaks down
 the more than 250 application topics from the responses into these categories.
• Data: While existing DOE datasets provide a foundational starting point, there is a strong
 consensus that the continuous improvement and self-correction of AI models will heavily
 depend on the collection and generation of new data. This new data will often be guided by
 model feedback, targeted experiments, high-throughput simulations, and the need to fill
 critical gaps, especially for rare phenomena, extreme conditions, or emerging fields. The
 responses were evenly split between the need for using preexisting data (68 responses) and
 the need for incorporating new data (72 responses).
• Best-Use Cases: The submissions outlined more than 290 best-use cases, broadly
 categorized under automation, innovation, analytics, modeling, optimization, resilience,
 control, and operation. The histogram in Figure (3) represents the frequency of each of these
 best use cases while also outlining the relevance of these use cases to the three goals of the
 Genesis Mission: energy, national security, and discovery science.

Response to Question 3
Best mechanisms for DOE to provide the AI models to the scientific community

• Data: A tiered hybrid data architecture with a strong emphasis on data readiness and
 governance is crucial. This would create a federated AI Data Foundry with lab nodes for
 ingestion and curation, alongside a centralized catalog/registry for discovery and versioning.

• Governance: The platform will implement robust security and governance frameworks,
 mandate confidential computing, and provide diverse access lanes and compute allocations
 with streamlined proposal processes for academic researchers. Cloud usage through this

 interface should be FedRAMP compliant, auditable, and integrated with DOE identity and
 access management.

• Platform: The DOE should deliver AI models through a federated "Science Cloud Interface"
 with a serverless inference layer providing an Inference-as-a-Service API. This platform
 should also offer workflows as an integrated research infrastructure, training and evaluation
 code, weights, datasets, benchmarks, and documentation under permissive open-source
 licenses. This platform should feature a central discovery layer and a distributed execution
 layer.

• Public-Private Partnership: The DOE should establish a flexible, inclusive public-private
 consortium to encourage broad participation from small and medium-sized innovators,
 ensuring broad access and responsible data stewardship for AI models. This consortium
 should implement a three-layered hybrid multi-cloud architecture, combine DOE's High-
 Performance Computing resources with commercial cloud scalability, and prioritize a
 modular, tiered architecture for model integration and delivery.

Response to Considerations
In the RFI document, the responders were also asked to consider a few topics while framing the
answers to the questions. Some responders addressed both the questions and the additional
considerations. This section lists the additional suggestions that appeared in response to the
considerations.

Best mechanisms for DOE to structure and incentivize partnerships between DOE National
Laboratories and other partners

 • Prioritize flexible and inclusive consortium structures that lower barriers to entry for
 small businesses and independent innovators, avoiding one-size-fits-all requirements.
 Allow joint intellectual property rights and streamline commercialization when feasible.

 • Provide priority pilot slots and early access to non-sensitive outputs for investors,
 alongside competitive grant opportunities and access to DOE data and computing
 resources for academic partners. Recognize external contributions such as DOIs for
 dataset and citation credits.
Best mechanisms to develop governance models for shared data, AI models, and computing
infrastructure, ensuring compliance with legal, regulatory, and privacy standards

 • Implement a tiered governance model with a DOE Oversight Board, an Executive
 Steering Committee, and technical Working Groups, supported by a real-time, role-based
 automated dashboard.

 • Governance frameworks must include clear policies for data access, model use, lifecycle,
 and attribution, along with security by design, tiered access, desensitization, consortium-
 wide safety certification, and compute-to-data for restricted tiers.
 • Compliance can be enforced through standard onboarding packages; auditable release
 gates; continuous operational controls for Research Technology, and Economic Security
 (RTES) requirements; and automated tests for conformance and sensitive data.

Best mechanism to prepare and curate scientific data at scale for AI training using existing
scientific data sets and developing approaches to building and maintaining Findable,
Accessible, Interoperable, and Reusable (FAIR), AI-ready data repositories

 • Maintaining FAIR, AI-ready repositories involves a hybrid approach with a central
 metadata catalog and distributed storage/compute, coupled with consistent dataset cards
 and provenance. Establish cross-laboratory Data Broker and Standards (DBS) teams or an
 ‘AI-Ready Data Corps’.

 • DOE should implement an AI-ready curation pipeline with common schemas,
 standardized ingest processes, and quality gates for completeness, consistency, and bias
 screening.

 • Data cleaning should take place on edge nodes or storage controllers, while separating
 and preserving the data from different stages of cleaning.

 • Introduce new embedding and tokenization schemes, with explicit versioning and
 documentation of these choices. Leverage modern query frameworks for automated
 preprocessing.

Best mechanism to balance the benefits and challenges of using federated or distributed
versus centralized data repositories

 • A hybrid architecture is widely recommended, where sensitive datasets remain local to
 National Laboratories, while discoverability (metadata) and evaluation harness packaging
 are centralized.

 • Federated models are particularly beneficial for sensitive or proprietary data, allowing
 models to be trained without centralizing raw data, which helps maintain compliance and
 trust.

 • Centralized storage should be reserved for low-sensitivity datasets where lifecycle cost
 and access speed justify it, or for core datasets, while federated approaches handle
 domain-specific data or data requiring local control.

How can DOE’s development of AI models best drive the curation of existing DOE data
and the generation of new data?

 • AI models can drive curation by identifying benchmark gaps and data quality issues,
 which in turn indicate missing data modalities or coverage, leading to targeted curation
 efforts.

 • AI models can make the pipeline self-improving by employing techniques such as
 adaptive data capture and providing assistance in tasks such as data cleaning, annotation,
 and provenance tracking.
 • The development of AI models can motivate the creation of new data by highlighting the
 need for specific datasets or data types that are currently lacking., This will ensure new
 data generation, by new experiments or generative surrogates, is targeted and addresses
 identified needs.

How can DOE best leverage its Other Transaction Authority (OTA) under 42 U.S.C.
§7256(a), (g)?
 • DOE should use OTA for rapid pilots and modular scaling by outcomes by tying awards
 to measurable deliverables and acceptance criteria. This will allow for quick iteration and
 adaptation in AI development.
 • OTAs can deploy modular 'model agreements' for data use, privacy-preserving
 computation, evaluation access, and IP expectations, which will reduce bespoke
 negotiation overhead and preserve appropriate protections for sensitive contributions.

 • OTA flexibility allows for tailored IP and data rights arrangements that encourage
 private-sector participation while protecting government interests. Flexible agreements
 under the OTA can provide paths for rapid commercialization.
What lessons learned from existing AI consortia or other public-private consortia can DOE
incorporate into this consortium effort?

 • Successful AI consortia establish a neutral backbone with enforceable standards, publish
 shared benchmarks and evaluation harnesses, mandate provenance/versioning, and fund
 sustained operations.

 • Lessons learned emphasize the importance of clear governance, sustained funding, and
 independent evaluation. These lessons also suggest that a technical governance layer—
 placed above the research organizations—should enforce management related actions.

 • Flexibility and interoperability are key to success, as seen in existing partnerships, with
 databases supporting diverse data types and dynamic queries reducing barriers to
 collaboration and driving model development.

What advantages or disadvantages might incorporated consortia, Focused Research
Organizations, or other less-traditional awardee structures have in the context of this
consortium effort? In addition to DOE, what kinds of entities might provide investment in
such awardees, and what kinds of consideration might such investors receive?
 • Incorporated consortia and Focused Research Organizations (FROs) introduce agility and
 mission focused execution and potentially faster prototyping. They can also provide a
 clearer corporate structure for managing large AI programs and addressing concerns
 across diverse scientific domains.
 • Advantages include faster hiring and execution, clearer accountability, and more flexible
 partnerships. Potential disadvantages include increased bureaucracy, inefficient
 coordination across independent National Laboratories, and the risk of governance
 capturing—where the regulatory body is dominated by the interest of the group they are
 meant to regulate—or misalignment of priorities.

 • Beyond DOE, investment could come from U.S. industry consortia members,
 philanthropy, state/regional entities, venture capital firms, and mission-aligned funds.
 Investors might receive priority pilot slots, early access to non-sensitive outputs,
 intellectual property rights, or governance roles.
