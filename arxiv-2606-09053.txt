Perturbation method for non-convex variational problems
H. Belcheva, N. Zlateva

Abstract
For a variational problem in a Banach space with quadratic kinetic term and a lower semicontinuous potential, existence of a minimizer is restored by replacing the norm of the space with an equivalent one, arbitrarily close to the original. The form of the problem is preserved; only the norm is perturbed. This extends a result of Ivanov and Zlateva from the convex to the lower semicontinuous case.



Key words:
variational problem, existence of solutions, per-turbation method, integral functional

AMS Subject Classification:
46N10, 35A15, 49J45, 90C48

1
Introduction

Let
(X,\|\cdot\|)
be a Banach space. We consider the variational problem

(1)

\left(P_{\|\cdot\|}\right)\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\inf\int_{0}^{1}\left(\|v(t)\|^{2}+f(u(t))\right)dt,\\[12.0pt]
\displaystyle u(t)=\int_{0}^{t}v(s)\,ds,\quad v\in\mathbb{L}_{2}([0,1],X),\end{array}\right.

where
f:X\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is a proper, lower semicontinuous and bounded below function. This problem can be read as minimizing the action of curve
u
starting at the origin, with quadratic kinetic term and potential
f
. When
X
is finite dimensional, the operator
v(t)\mapsto u(t)=\int_{0}^{t}v(s)\,ds
is compact, so weakly convergent minimizing sequences become strongly convergent on the trajectory side, and the direct method of calculus of variations
[
1
,
2
,
7
]
delivers a solution. When
X
is infinite dimensional this fails: the operator is no longer compact, weak convergence of
v_{n}
does not imply norm convergence of
u_{n}
, and even simple choices of
f
may leave
(P_{\|\cdot\|})
without a minimizer.

The standard remedy is a variational principle, for example that of Ekeland, to perturb the functional by an arbitrarily small term and recover existence. The price is that the perturbed functional no longer has the original form of
(P_{\|\cdot\|})
. An alternative approach, developed by Ivanov and Zlateva
[
9
,
10
]
, is to perturb only the integrand of the integral functional, thus preserving the structure of the problem. For convex
f
, this leads to the following result, easily derivable from
[
9
, Theorem 1.1]
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
(X,\|\cdot\|)
be a Banach space. Let
f:X\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be a convex lower semicontinuous function such that
0\in\mathrm{dom\,}f
, and
f\geq k\|\cdot\|
for some constant
k>0
. Consider the optimization problem
\left(P_{\|\cdot\|}\right)
defined by (
1
).

For each
\varepsilon>0
there is an equivalent norm
|\cdot|
on
X
such that

\|\cdot\|\leq|\cdot|\leq(1+\varepsilon)\|\cdot\|

and the corresponding problem
\left(P_{|\cdot|}\right)
has a solution.

The conclusion is striking: existence is restored not by adding a perturbation term to the functional, but by replacing the norm on
X
with an equivalent one, arbitrarily close to the original. The problem
(P_{|\cdot|})
has
exactly the same form
as
(P_{\|\cdot\|})
– a quadratic kinetic term plus the same potential
f
along the trajectory – it is merely posed in
(X,|\cdot|)
instead of
(X,\|\cdot\|)
.

The convexity assumption on
f
, however, is restrictive. The aim of the present work is to remove it. Combining techniques from
[
9
,
10
,
14
]
with a tailored variational principle in the spirit of Deville–Godefroy–Zizler, we extend the above theorem to the case of a merely lower semicontinuous
f
, see Theorem
6.1
.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
fixes notations, recalls the Bochner integral setting, and discusses why the direct method fails in infinite dimensions. In Section
3
we introduce an intermediate topology
\tau
on
Y=\mathbb{L}_{2}([0,1],X)
, which is finer than the weak topology but coarser than the norm topology, and prove a generalized Cantor Lemma adapted to it. Section
4
sets up the perturbation space of squared continuous seminorms on
X
. In Section
5
we adapt the variational principle of Deville–Godefroy–Zizler
[
4
,
5
]
to our setting, see Theorem
5.1
. Section
6
contains the main result, Theorem
6.1
. Section
7
outlines directions for further research.

2
Preliminaries and notations

Let us consider first the finite-dimensional case of the problem
\left(P_{\|\cdot\|}\right)
, that is when
X
is a finite dimensional Euclidean space
E
, and
\|\cdot\|
is the Euclidean norm.
For
v\in\mathbb{L}_{2}([0,1],E)
denote

\varphi(v)(t):=\int_{0}^{t}v(s)\,ds.

Considered as an operator from
\mathbb{L}_{2}([0,1],E)
into itself,
\varphi
is a compact linear operator, so it maps weakly convergent sequences into strongly convergent ones. Let
\{v_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a minimizing sequence to the problem

\left(P_{\|\cdot\|}\right)\quad\inf\int_{0}^{1}(\|v(t)\|^{2}+f(\varphi(v)(t)))\,dt.

Since
\{v_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
will be bounded, and since the unit ball of
\mathbb{L}_{2}([0,1],E)
is weakly compact, there will be a subsequence, that we will denote again by
\{v_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
, such that
v_{n}
weakly converges to some
\bar{v}
, as
n\rightarrow\infty
, written as
v_{n}\to_{w}\bar{v}
. Then
\varphi(v_{n})
norm converges to
\varphi(\bar{v})
, as
n\rightarrow\infty
, written as

\varphi(v_{n})\rightarrow\varphi(\bar{v})
,
and
\bar{v}
is a solution to
(P_{\|\cdot\|})
.

The above illustrates the so-called direct method of calculus of variations, see e.g.
[
1
,
2
,
7
]
. It works, because the value function is weakly lower semicontinuous.

In the case of a general Banach space
X
, however, and even for a Hilbert space
H
, the operator
\varphi
is not weak-to-norm continuous.
Indeed, let for example
H=\ell_{2}
, and

v_{n}(t)=e_{n},\quad\forall t\in[0,1],

where
\{e_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is the canonical basis of
\ell_{2}
.
So,
v_{n}\to_{w}0
, but

\varphi(v_{n})(t)=te_{n},\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N},\ \forall t\in[0,1],

and, since

\|e_{i}-e_{j}\|=\sqrt{2},\quad\forall i\neq j,

we have

\|\varphi(v_{i})-\varphi(v_{j})\|^{2}=2/3,\quad\forall i\neq j,

hence the set
\left\{\varphi\left(v_{n}\right):n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
is discrete.

We work in a Banach space
(X,\|\cdot\|)
and we will denote
Y:=\mathbb{L}_{2}([0,1],X)
.

All integrals are considered in Bochner sense, see
[
6
, Chapter II]
.
More precisely, see e.g.
[
8
, p. 8]
, a function
s:[0,1]\to X
is
simple
if

s(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\chi_{A_{i}}(t),

for some
c_{i}\in X
and some Lebesgue measurable sets
A_{i}\subset[0,1]
, where
\chi_{A}
is the
characteristic function
of
A
:

\chi_{A}(t):=\begin{cases}1,&t\in A,\\
0,&t\notin A.\end{cases}

A function
v:[0,1]\to X
is
strongly measurable
if there is a sequence
\{s_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
of simple functions such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\|v(t)-s_{n}(t)\|=0,\text{ for almost all }t\in[0,1].

A strongly measurable function
v
is
Bochner integrable
if there exists a sequence
\{s_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
of simple functions such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{0}^{1}\|v(t)-s_{n}(t)\|\,dt=0,

see
[
8
, Definition 1.2.1]
. The integral of
v
is then the limit of the integrals of the
s_{n}
’s while for a simple function
s
the definition of integral is clear:

\int_{0}^{1}s(t)\,dt=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}m(A_{i}),

where
m
denotes the Lebesgue measure.

Alternatively, by
[
8
, Proposition 1.2.2]
, a strongly measurable function
v
is Bochner integrable if and only if

\int_{0}^{1}\|v(t)\|\,dt<\infty.

The space
Y
then consists of those Bochner integrable functions
v
for which

\|v\|_{\mathbb{L}_{2}}^{2}:=\int_{0}^{1}\|v(t)\|^{2}\,dt<\infty,

see
[
8
, Definition 1.2.15]
.

We will also use some auxiliary results.

Lemma 2.1

(
[
8
, Lemma 1.2.19]
)
.

The simple functions are dense in
Y
.

The linear operator
\varphi:\mathbb{L}_{2}([0,1],X)\to\mathbb{L}_{2}([0,1],X)

retains the following important property.

Lemma 2.2
.

The bounded linear operator
\varphi:\mathbb{L}_{2}([0,1],X)\to\mathbb{L}_{2}([0,1],X)
defined as

(2)

\varphi(v)(t):=\int_{0}^{t}v(s)\,ds,\quad\forall v\in\mathbb{L}_{2}([0,1],X)

is sequentially weak-to-norm closed.

Proof.

Note that for each
v\in Y
the function
t\to\varphi(v)(t)

is continuous. Let

v_{n}\to_{w}v
(i.e. in the weak topology in
Y
) and
\varphi(v_{n})\to\overline{u}
(i.e. in the norm topology in
Y
).
We have to show that
\overline{u}=\varphi(\bar{v})
.

Fix an arbitrary
x^{*}\in X^{*}
and an arbitrary
t\in[0,1]
. The linear functional

v\to\int_{0}^{t}\langle x^{*},v(s)\rangle\,ds

belongs to
Y^{*}
, so from the weak convergence of
v_{n}
to
\bar{v}
, we have that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{0}^{t}\langle x^{*},v_{n}(s)\rangle\,ds=\int_{0}^{t}\langle x^{*},\bar{v}(s)\rangle\,ds,

or,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left\langle x^{*},\int_{0}^{t}v_{n}(s)\,ds\right\rangle=\left\langle x^{*},\int_{0}^{t}\bar{v}(s)\,ds\right\rangle.

Therefore,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\langle x^{*},\varphi(v_{n})(t)\rangle=\langle x^{*},\varphi(\bar{v})(t)\rangle,\quad\forall x^{*}\in X^{*},\ \forall t\in[0,1],

and,

(3)

\varphi(v_{n})(t)\to_{w}\varphi(\bar{v})(t),\text{ as }n\to\infty,\quad\forall t\in[0,1].

But the norm convergence of
\varphi(v_{n})
to
\overline{u}
in
Y
, that is,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{0}^{1}\|\varphi(v_{n})(t)-\overline{u}(t)\|^{2}\,dt=0,

and the continuity of the functions
\varphi(v_{n})
, and
\overline{u}
give

\varphi(v_{n})(t)\to\overline{u}(t),\text{ as }n\to\infty,\quad\forall t\in[0,1].

The latter and (
3
) yield the desired conclusion
\overline{u}=\varphi(\bar{v})
.
∎

The function
f:X\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is called lower semicontinuous if its
epigraph

\mathrm{epi\,}f:=\{(x,r)\in X\times\mathbb{R}:f(x)\leq r\}
is a closed set, and
proper
if its
domain

\mathrm{dom\,}f:=\{x\in X:f(x)<\infty\}
is non-empty.

Let
f:X\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be a fixed proper, lower semicontinuous and bounded below function. We will assume without loss of generality that
f\geq 0
.

Let
P
denotes the closed cone of all continuous semi-norms on
X
squared. That is, each
p\in P
is positive, convex, continuous and
2
-homogeneous function:

p(tx)=t^{2}p(x),\quad\forall x\in X,\ \forall t\in\mathbb{R}.

With the metric induced by the uniform convergence on the unit ball
B_{X}
of
X
,

\bar{\rho}(p_{1},p_{2}):=\|{p_{1}}-{p_{2}}\|_{\infty}:=\sup_{x\in B_{X}}|{p_{1}(x)}-{p_{2}(x)}|,

the cone
P
turns into a complete metric space
(P,\bar{\rho})
.

Consider for
p\in P
the function
H_{p}:\mathbb{L}_{2}([0,1],X)\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
defined as

H_{p}(v):=K_{p}(v)+F(v),

where

K_{p}(v):=\int_{0}^{1}\|v(t)\|^{2}\,dt+\int_{0}^{1}p(v(t))\,dt,\text{ and }F(v):=\int_{0}^{1}f(\varphi(v)(t))\,dt.

Obviously, we mean the (integrals of) squared seminorms
p
as perturbations of the main problem
(P_{\|\cdot\|})
which might be rewritten in the form

(4)

(P_{\|\cdot\|})\qquad\inf_{v\in Y}\ \int_{0}^{1}\left(\|v(t)\|^{2}+f(\varphi(v)(t))\right)\,dt.

For simplicity we also write
K:=K_{0}
, and
H:=H_{0}
.
In our notation the problem
(P_{\|\cdot\|})
is equivalent to:

\inf_{v\in Y}H(v).

We aim to show that for each
\varepsilon>0
there is
p\in P
such that
\bar{\rho}(p,0)=\|p\|_{\infty}<\varepsilon
, and
H_{p}
attains minimum.

Another auxiliary result we shall use in the sequel is the well-known Fatou Lemma.

Lemma 2.3

(
[
12
, Theorem 1.28]
)
.

Let
g_{n}:[0,1]\to[0,+\infty]
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
be strongly measurable functions. Then

\int_{0}^{1}\liminf_{n\to\infty}g_{n}(t)\,dt\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int_{0}^{1}g_{n}(t)\,dt.

From the Fatou Lemma it easily follows that the functions
F
,
K_{p}
, and therefore,
H_{p}
are norm lower semicontinuous for each
p\in P
. To see this for e.g.
F
, fix
v\in Y
and a sequence
\{v_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset Y
such that

v_{n}\to v\text{ and }\lim_{n\to\infty}F(v_{n})=\liminf_{w\to v}F(w).

Then
v_{n}\to_{w}v
and from Lemma
2.2
it holds that

\varphi(v_{n})\to\varphi(v)
.
The latter spells

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{0}^{1}\|\varphi(v)(t)-\varphi(v_{n})(t)\|^{2}\,dt=0,

which implies that for a subsequence, still denoted
\{v_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
, see
[
12
, Theorem 3.12]
,

\varphi(v_{n})(t)\to\varphi(v)(t),\text{ for almost all }t\in[0,1].

Set

g(t):=f(\varphi(v)(t))
,
g_{n}(t):=f(\varphi(v_{n})(t))
.
Since
f
is norm lower semicontinuous,

\liminf_{n\to\infty}g_{n}(t)\geq g(t),\text{ for almost all }t\in[0,1].

By the Fatou Lemma,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}F(v_{n})

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{0}^{1}g_{n}(t)\,dt

\displaystyle\geq\int_{0}^{1}\liminf_{n\to\infty}g_{n}(t)\,dt\geq\int_{0}^{1}g(t)\,dt

\displaystyle=F(v),

which means that
F
is norm lower semicontinuous at
v
.

In fact the function
K_{p}
is even weakly lower semicontinuous, because it is convex, but
F
is not and that is why we need to introduce an intermediate topology in
Y
.

3
The topology
\tau

Apart from the norm and weak topologies on
Y:=\mathbb{L}_{2}([0,1],X)
we consider also an intermediate linear topology
\tau
on
Y
, which is the product of the weak and norm topologies on the graph of the linear operator
\varphi
defined by (
2
). The graph of
\varphi
is a closed set in
(Y,\sigma(Y^{*},Y))\times(Y,\|\cdot\|_{\mathbb{L}_{2}})
, hence we can identify the latter with
Y
via the mapping
v\mapsto(v,\varphi(v))
. Since we are working with sequences, what we need in most cases is:

v_{n}\to_{\tau}v\iff v_{n}\to_{w}v,\text{ and }\varphi(v_{n})\to\varphi(v).

Clearly,
\tau
is stronger than the weak topology on
Y
and weaker than the norm topology.

Proposition 3.1
.

For each
p\in P
the function
H_{p}
is sequentially
\tau
lower semicontinuous.

Proof.

Let us fix
p\in P
.
We have already noted that
K_{p}
is weakly lower semicontinuous, and hence
\tau
lower semicontinuous. To check that
F
is sequentially
\tau
lower semicontinuous, let
v_{n}\to_{\tau}v
, as
n\to\infty
. In particular,
\varphi(v_{n})\to\varphi(v)
as
n\to\infty
, and as in Section
2
, we see that

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}F(v_{n})\geq F(v)
.
∎

Proposition 3.2
.

A subset
A\subset Y
is sequentially
\tau
-compact if and only if
A
is weakly-compact and
\varphi(A)
is norm-compact.

In particular, if
X_{1}
is a finite-dimensional subspace of
X
and

Y_{1}:=\mathbb{L}_{2}([0,1],X_{1})
,
then
B_{Y_{1}}
is sequentially
\tau
-compact.

Proof.

Let
A\subset Y
be a sequentially
\tau
-compact set, and let the sequence
\{v_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset A
be arbitrary. Since
A
is sequentially
\tau
-compact, there is a subsequence
\{v_{n_{k}}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
such that

v_{n_{k}}\to_{\tau}v\in A\text{ as }k\to\infty.

In particular,
v_{n_{k}}\to_{w}v
, as
k\to\infty
and
A
is sequentially weakly compact, which is the same as weakly compact by the Eberlein-Šmulian theorem, cf. e.g.
[
13
, Theorem 3.28]
.

To see that
\varphi(A)
is norm-compact, let
\{u_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset\varphi(A)
be arbitrary. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
choose
w_{n}\in A
such that
\varphi(w_{n})=u_{n}
. Since
A
is sequentially
\tau
-compact, there is a subsequence
\{w_{n_{k}}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
and
w\in A
such that
w_{n_{k}}\to_{\tau}w
, as
k\to\infty
. In particular,
\varphi(w_{n_{k}})\to\varphi(w)
in the norm of
Y
, that is,
u_{n_{k}}\to\varphi(w)\in\varphi(A)
, as
k\to\infty
. Hence
\varphi(A)
is sequentially norm-compact, and therefore norm-compact.

On the other hand, let
A\subset Y
be such that
A
is weakly compact and
\varphi(A)
is norm-compact. Let the sequence
\{v_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset A
be arbitrary. Since
A
is weakly compact, and thus sequentially weakly compact by the Eberlein-Šmulian theorem, there is a subsequence
\{v_{n_{k}}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
such that
v_{n_{k}}\to_{w}v\in A
, as
k\to\infty
. Since
\varphi(A)
is norm-compact, there is a further subsequence
\{v_{n_{k_{i}}}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
such that
\varphi(v_{n_{k_{i}}})\to\bar{v}\in\varphi(A)
, as
i\to\infty
. Since the graph of
\varphi
is sequentially closed in the product of the weak and norm topologies, see Lemma
2.2
, we have
\bar{v}=\varphi(v)
and, therefore,
v_{n_{k_{i}}}\to_{\tau}v
, as
i\to\infty
. Hence,
A
is sequentially
\tau
-compact.

Let now
X_{1}\subset X
be a finite-dimensional subspace, and
Y_{1}:=\mathbb{L}_{2}([0,1],X_{1})
. Since
Y_{1}
is reflexive,
B_{Y_{1}}
is weakly-compact, i.e.
\sigma(Y_{1}^{*},Y_{1})
-compact. The
Y^{*}
-weak topology on
Y_{1}
when considered as a subspace of
Y
, that is the topology
\sigma(Y^{*},Y_{1})
, is weaker than its own
Y_{1}^{*}
-weak topology
\sigma(Y_{1}^{*},Y_{1})
, by the Hahn-Banach Theorem
[
13
, Theorem 3.3]
. Therefore,
B_{Y_{1}}
is
\sigma(Y^{*},Y)
-compact, that is, weakly compact in
Y
. Also, since
\mathrm{dim\,}X_{1}<\infty
, by the Arzelà-Ascoli Theorem
[
13
, Theorem A.5]
the restriction of
\varphi
on its invariant subspace
Y_{1}
is compact, so
\varphi(B_{Y_{1}})
is norm-compact. From the already established characterization, it follows that
B_{Y_{1}}
is sequentially
\tau
-compact.
∎

Kuratowski
[
11
]
proved a generalization of the Cantor Lemma in terms of the
\alpha
measure of non-compactness he defined. This line was extended by De Blasi
[
3
]
who defined measure
\beta
of weak non-compactness of a set in a Banach space. The following result is another generalization of the Cantor Lemma.

Lemma 3.3

(
Generalized Cantor Lemma
)
.

Let
(X,\bar{\tau})
be a topological space and let
d
be a sequentially
\bar{\tau}
lower semicontinuous metric on
X
such that
(X,d)
is a complete metric space and, moreover,

(5)

\forall x\in U\in\bar{\tau}\ \exists\varepsilon>0,V\in\bar{\tau}:\ x\in V\text{ and }V_{\varepsilon}\subset U,

where
V_{\varepsilon}:=\{x\in X:\ d(x,y)\leq\varepsilon
for some
y\in V\}
.

Let for a set
A\subset X
,
\bar{\mu}(A)
denotes the measure of
\bar{\tau}
non-compactness of
A
, that is

\bar{\mu}(A):=\inf\{\varepsilon:\ A\subset C_{\varepsilon},\text{ for some sequentially }\bar{\tau}\text{-compact set }C\}.

If the sets
A_{n}\subset X
are non-empty, nested, i.e.
A_{n+1}\subset A_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, sequentially
\bar{\tau}
-closed, and such that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\bar{\mu}(A_{n})=0
,
then

\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}\neq\varnothing.

Proof.

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, fix

x_{n}\in A_{n}
.

Because the sets
A_{n}
are nested and sequentially
\bar{\tau}
closed, it is enough to show that the sequence
\{x_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
has a
\bar{\tau}
-convergent subsequence. To find the latter, we will perform a Cantor diagonalization procedure.

Let
\varepsilon_{n}>0
be such that

\varepsilon_{n+1}<\varepsilon_{n},\ \forall n\in\mathbb{N},\text{ and }\lim_{n\to\infty}\varepsilon_{n}=0,

and for some sequentially
\bar{\tau}
-compact sets
C_{n}
,
A_{n}\subset(C_{n})_{\varepsilon_{n}}
.

Let

y_{n}^{(1)}\in C_{1}
be such that
d(x_{n},y_{n}^{(1)})\leq\varepsilon_{1}
.

Since
C_{1}
is sequentially
\bar{\tau}
-compact, there is
\bar{\tau}
-convergent subsequence of
\{y_{n}^{(1)}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
. That is, there is strictly increasing function
\sigma_{1}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
such that

y_{\sigma_{1}(n)}^{(1)}\to_{\bar{\tau}}z_{1}\text{ as }n\to\infty.

We choose subsequence of subsequence by induction. Let
\{y_{\sigma_{k}(n)}^{(k)}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset C_{k}
be such that

y_{\sigma_{k}(n)}^{(k)}\to_{\bar{\tau}}z_{k}\text{ as }n\to\infty.

Since
\{x_{\sigma_{k}(n)}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is a subsequence of
\{x_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
, we have that

x_{\sigma_{k}(n)}\in A_{k+1}\subset(C_{k+1})_{\varepsilon_{k+1}},\quad\forall n\geq k+1.

So, there are

\{y_{\sigma_{k}(n)}^{(k+1)}\}_{n=k+1}^{\infty}\in C_{k+1}\text{ such that }d(x_{\sigma_{k}(n)},y_{\sigma_{k}(n)}^{(k+1)})\leq\varepsilon_{k+1}.

Since
C_{k+1}
is sequentially
\bar{\tau}
-compact, there is a
\bar{\tau}
-convergent subsequence of
\{y_{\sigma_{k}(n)}^{(k+1)}\}_{n=k+1}^{\infty}
, that is, there is a strictly increasing function
\nu_{k}
from
k+1,k+2,\ldots
into itself such that for

\sigma_{k+1}(n):=\sigma_{k}(\nu_{k}(n))

we have that

y_{\sigma_{k+1}(n)}^{(k+1)}\to_{\bar{\tau}}z_{k+1}\text{ as }n\to\infty,

and so on ….

We claim that the sequence
\{z_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
is convergent in the metric
d
. Indeed, for any
j>k
, because of the inclusion
\{\sigma_{j}(n):\ n\in\mathbb{N}\}\subset\{\sigma_{k}(n):\ n\in\mathbb{N}\}
, we have that

d(x_{\sigma_{j}(n)},y_{\sigma_{j}(n)}^{(k)})\leq\varepsilon_{k}.

But also, by construction,

d(x_{\sigma_{j}(n)},y_{\sigma_{j}(n)}^{(j)})\leq\varepsilon_{j}<\varepsilon_{k}.

So, by the triangle inequality,
d(y_{\sigma_{j}(n)}^{(k)},y_{\sigma_{j}(n)}^{(j)})\leq 2\varepsilon_{k}
.

Using that

y_{\sigma_{j}(n)}^{(k)}\to_{\bar{\tau}}z_{k}
, and
y_{\sigma_{j}(n)}^{(j)}\to_{\bar{\tau}}z_{j}
, as
n\to\infty
,
and the
\bar{\tau}
lower semicontinuity of the metric
d
, we get

d(z_{j},z_{k})\leq 2\varepsilon_{k},\quad\forall j\geq k,

which means that the sequence
\{z_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
is a
d
-Cauchy sequence, and because
(X,d)
is complete, we get a
z
such that

d(z_{k},z)\to 0\text{ as }k\to\infty.

Let
U\in\bar{\tau}
be an arbitrary neighbourhood of
z
, that is,
z\in U
. Let
V\in\bar{\tau}
and
\varepsilon>0
be such that
z\in V
and
V_{\varepsilon}\subset U
, see (
5
). Let
j\in\mathbb{N}
be so large that

\varepsilon_{j}<\varepsilon\text{ and }z_{j}\in V.

Now, since
\{\sigma_{k}(k):k\in\mathbb{N},\ k\geq j\}\subset\{\sigma_{j}(n):n\in\mathbb{N}\}
,
and since
y_{\sigma_{j}(n)}^{(j)}\to_{\bar{\tau}}z_{j}
, as
n\to\infty
, there is
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that

y^{(j)}_{\sigma_{k}(k)}\in V,\quad\forall k>N.

But
d(x_{\sigma_{k}(k)},y^{(j)}_{\sigma_{k}(k)})\leq\varepsilon_{j}<\varepsilon
, therefore

x_{\sigma_{k}(k)}\in V_{\varepsilon}\subset U,\quad\forall k>N.

This means that
\{x_{\sigma_{k}(k)}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}

\bar{\tau}
-converges to
z
, and the proof is completed.
∎

We will use the following particular case of the generalized Cantor Lemma for the topology
\tau
.

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
S
be a nonempty closed set in
Y
.
If the sets
A_{n}\subset S
are non-empty, nested, i.e.
A_{n+1}\subset A_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, sequentially
\tau
-closed, and such that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu(A_{n})=0
,
where
\mu(A)
is the measure of
\tau
non-compactness of
A
, that is

(6)

\mu(A):=\inf\{\varepsilon:\ A\subset C_{\varepsilon},\text{ for some sequentially }\tau\text{-compact set }C\},

then

\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}\neq\varnothing.

Proof.

We consider the topological space
(S,\tau)
and take
d
to be the metric on
S
induced by the norm in
Y
. Thus
(S,\|\cdot\|_{\mathbb{L}_{2}})
is a complete metric space. Since the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbb{L}_{2}}
is a convex and continuous function, it is weakly lower semicontinuous and, therefore, sequentially
\tau
lower semicontinuous, and so is the metric
d
on
S
.

Condition (
5
) holds because
\tau
is a linear topology on
Y
. Indeed, let
x\in U\in\tau
. By continuity of addition at
(x,0)
, there exists a
\tau
-open neighbourhood
V
of
x
and a
\tau
-open neighbourhood
W
of
0
such that
V+W\subset U
. Since
\tau
is weaker than the norm topology,
W
is norm-open, and being a neighbourhood of
0
, contains some
\varepsilon B_{Y}
with
\varepsilon>0
. Then
V_{\varepsilon}=V+\varepsilon B_{Y}\subset V+W\subset U
. Obviously, taking intersections with
S
changes nothing.

From the generalized Cantor Lemma the claim follows.
∎

4
Perturbation space

To construct a perturbation space on
Y
, we “lift” each function
P\ni p:X\to\mathbb{R}
to a function
\mathfrak{p}:Y\to\mathbb{R}
by

\mathfrak{p}(v):=\int_{0}^{1}p(v(t))\,dt.

We consider the lifted cone

\mathcal{P}:=\{\mathfrak{p}:\ p\in P\}

with the lifted metric

\rho(\mathfrak{p}_{1},\mathfrak{p}_{2}):=\bar{\rho}(p_{1},p_{2}),

so
(\mathcal{P},\rho)
is a complete metric space.

We will show that
(\mathcal{P},\rho)
is a perturbation space on any ball
rB_{Y}
, where
B_{Y}
is the unit ball of
Y
, with respect to the measure
\mu
of
\tau
-non-compactness defined by (
6
).

For a definition of a perturbation space on a set, see
[
14
, Definition 2]
.

To this end we will prove first some auxiliary results.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and
v\in Y
be arbitrary. There exists a finite dimensional
X_{1}\subset X
such that

d(v,Y_{1})<\varepsilon,\text{ where }Y_{1}=\mathbb{L}_{2}([0,1],X_{1}).

Proof.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
and
v\in Y
. From Lemma
2.1
there is a simple function

s(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\chi_{A_{i}}(t),

such that
\|v-s\|_{Y}<\varepsilon
. Let

X_{1}:=\mathrm{span}\,\{c_{i}:\ i=1,\ldots,n\}
,
and
Y_{1}:=\mathbb{L}_{2}([0,1],X_{1})
. Clearly
s\in Y_{1}
, so
d(v,Y_{1})\leq\|v-s\|_{Y}<\varepsilon
.
∎

Lemma 4.2

(
[
9
, Lemma 2.5]
)
.

Let
X_{1}
be a finite-dimensional subspace of
X
and
Y_{1}=\mathbb{L}_{2}([0,1],X_{1})
. Then for any
v\in Y
,

d^{2}(v,Y_{1})=\int_{0}^{1}d^{2}(v(t),X_{1})\,dt.

Proposition 4.3
.

({\cal{P}},\rho)
is a complete metric space. For any set
S=rB_{Y}
,
r>0
, the metric
\rho
dominates the uniform convergence on
S
, i.e.

\sup_{v\in S}\mathfrak{p}(v)\leq r^{2}\rho(\mathfrak{p},0).

Proof.

({\cal{P}},\rho)
is a complete metric space because
(P,\bar{\rho})
is a complete metric space.

Fix
r>0
and let
S=rB_{Y}
. Let
\mathfrak{p}\in{\cal{P}}
be arbitrary, and let
\displaystyle\mathfrak{p}(v):=\int_{0}^{1}p(v(t))\,dt
for some
p\in P
. We have that

\displaystyle\mathfrak{p}(v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}p(v(t))\,dt=\int_{0}^{1}\|v(t)\|^{2}p\left(\frac{v(t)}{\|v(t)\|}\right)\,dt

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\|v(t)\|^{2}\bar{\rho}(p,0)\,dt=\bar{\rho}(p,0)\int_{0}^{1}\|v(t)\|^{2}\,dt=\rho(\mathfrak{p},0)\|v\|^{2}.

Therefore,

\sup_{v\in rB_{Y}}\mathfrak{p}(v)\leq\rho(\mathfrak{p},0)\sup_{v\in rB_{Y}}\|v\|^{2}=r^{2}\rho(\mathfrak{p},0).

and the proof is completed.
∎

Proposition 4.4
.

For every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\delta>0
such that for each
v_{0}\in Y
there exists
\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}
(depending on
v_{0}
) such that

\displaystyle(a)

\displaystyle\rho(\mathfrak{p},0)<\varepsilon,

\displaystyle(b)

\displaystyle v_{0}\in\delta\argmin\mathfrak{p},

\displaystyle(c)

\displaystyle\mu((3\delta)\argmin\mathfrak{p})<\varepsilon.

Proof.

Obviously,
\min\mathfrak{p}=\mathfrak{p}(0)=0
for any
\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}
, so

\delta\argmin\mathfrak{p}=\{v\in Y:\ \mathfrak{p}(v)\leq\delta\}.

Fix an
\varepsilon>0
and then fix a
\delta>0
such that

\delta<\varepsilon^{3}/12
. Fix
v_{0}\in Y
.
Then fix an
a>0
such that

\varepsilon/2>a>6\delta/\varepsilon^{2}
, and fix some
b\in(0,\varepsilon/2)
such that

b\|v_{0}\|^{2}<\delta/2
.

From Lemma
4.1
we can find a finite-dimensional subspace
X_{1}\subset X
such that for
Y_{1}=\mathbb{L}_{2}([0,1],X_{1})
,

d(v_{0},Y_{1})<\sqrt{\frac{\delta}{2a}}.

Denote

C:=2\sqrt{3\delta/b}B_{Y_{1}}
.
From Proposition
3.2
, the set
C
is sequentially
\tau
-compact.

Define
p\in P
as

p(x):=ad^{2}(x,X_{1})+b\|x\|^{2}.

From Lemma
4.2
we have for the corresponding to it function
\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}
that

\mathfrak{p}(v)=ad^{2}(v,Y_{1})+b\|v\|^{2}.

Hence,
\rho(\mathfrak{p},0)=\bar{\rho}(p,0)=\sup p(B_{X})\leq a+b<\varepsilon
and
(a)
is verified.

Since

\mathfrak{p}(v_{0})=ad^{2}(v_{0},Y_{1})+b\|v_{0}\|^{2}<a\frac{\delta}{2a}+\frac{\delta}{2}=\delta,

we have that
v_{0}\in\delta\argmin\mathfrak{p}
and
(b)
holds.

Let now
v\in(3\delta)\argmin\mathfrak{p}
be arbitrary. That is,
\mathfrak{p}(v)\leq 3\delta
. The latter implies that

ad^{2}(v,Y_{1})\leq 3\delta
, as well as
b\|v\|^{2}\leq 3\delta
.
Hence,
v\in\sqrt{3\delta/b}B_{Y}
. We claim that

d(v,Y_{1})=d(v,C).

Indeed, since
C\subset Y_{1}
, we have
d(v,Y_{1})\leq d(v,C)
. For the opposite inequality, let
w_{0}\in Y_{1}
be the nearest point to
v
in
Y_{1}
, i.e.
d(v,Y_{1})=\|v-w_{0}\|
. If
w_{0}\notin C
, then
\|w_{0}\|>2\sqrt{3\delta/b}
, so

\|v-w_{0}\|\geq\|w_{0}\|-\|v\|>2\sqrt{3\delta/b}-\sqrt{3\delta/b}=\sqrt{3\delta/b},

contradicting
v\in\sqrt{3\delta/b}\,B_{Y}
. Hence
w_{0}\in C
, and
d(v,C)\leq\|v-w_{0}\|=d(v,Y_{1})
.

Using that

d^{2}(v,Y_{1})\leq 3\delta/a<\varepsilon^{2}
, thus
d(v,Y_{1})\leq\varepsilon
and
d(v,Y_{1})=d(v,C)
we obtain that
v\in C_{\varepsilon}
.
Since
v\in(3\delta)\argmin\mathfrak{p}
was arbitrary,
(3\delta)\argmin\mathfrak{p}\subset C_{\varepsilon}

and
(c)
holds.
∎

Propositions
4.3
and
4.4
show that the complete metric space
(\mathcal{P},\rho)
exhibits all features of a perturbation space on each ball in
Y
with respect to the measure
\mu
of
\tau
non-compactness, see
[
14
, Definition 2]
. Perturbation spaces with respect to the Kuratowski measure of non-compactness
\alpha
(see
[
10
, Definition 4.2]
,
[
14
, Definition 2]
) or with respect to De Blasi measure of weak non-compactness
\beta
(see
[
9
, Definition 3.1]
) are used for establishing variational principles in the corresponding papers
[
10
,
14
,
9
]
. However, the results therein cannot be directly transferred to our setting.

To get an appropriate variational principle in our settings, we need also the following simple version of
[
9
, Lemma 2.3]
: if
S\subseteq Y
is a non-empty set,
\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{g}:Y\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
are functions bounded below on
S
, and
\delta>0
is arbitrary, then

(7)

\Omega_{\mathfrak{f}}^{S}(\delta)\cap\Omega_{\mathfrak{g}}^{S}(\delta)\neq\varnothing\Rightarrow\Omega_{\mathfrak{f}+\mathfrak{g}}^{S}(\delta)\subset\Omega_{\mathfrak{f}}^{S}(3\delta)\cap\Omega_{\mathfrak{g}}^{S}(3\delta).

Here for a function
\mathfrak{f}:Y\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
we denote the
\delta\argmin
of
\mathfrak{f}
on
S
by

\Omega_{\mathfrak{f}}^{S}(\delta):=\{v\in S:\mathfrak{f}(v)\leq\inf\mathfrak{f}+\delta\}.

5
Variational principle

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
(X,\|\cdot\|)
be a Banach space, and let
Y=\mathbb{L}_{2}([0,1],X)
. Let
f:X\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be a lower semicontinuous and bounded below function such that
0\in\mathrm{dom\,}f
.

Then for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists
p\in P
such that
\|p\|_{\infty}<\varepsilon
and

H_{p}
attains its minimum on
Y
.

Proof.

Let us assume without loss of generality that
f
is bounded below on
X
by zero.

Since for any
p\in P
,

H_{p}(v)=\int_{0}^{1}(\|v(t)\|^{2}+p(v(t))+f(\varphi(v)(t)))\,dt,

for
v\equiv 0
, we get

H_{p}(0)=\int_{0}^{1}f(\varphi(0))\,dt=\int_{0}^{1}f(0)\,dt=f(0).

Therefore,

\inf_{v\in Y}H_{p}(v)\leq H_{p}(0)=f(0).

Fix
r>0
such that
r^{2}\geq f(0)+1
, and set
S:=rB_{Y}
.
Then,

(8)

H_{p}(v)=\int_{0}^{1}(\|v(t)\|^{2}+p(v(t))+f(\varphi(v)(t)))\,dt\geq\int_{0}^{1}\|v(t)\|^{2}\,dt=\|v\|_{Y}^{2}.

Note first that
\inf_{v\in S}H_{p}(v)=\inf_{v\in Y}H_{p}(v)
: any
v\not\in S
satisfies
\|v\|^{2}>r^{2}\geq f(0)+1\geq\inf_{v\in Y}H_{p}(v)+1
, hence
H_{p}(v)>\inf_{v\in Y}H_{p}(v)+1
, so
\displaystyle\inf_{v\in Y\setminus S}H_{p}(v)\geq\inf_{v\in Y}H_{p}(v)+1
and the infimum on
Y
is equal to the infimum on
S
.

Moreover, for
0\leq\delta<1
and any
v\not\in S
we have
H_{p}(v)>\inf_{v\in Y}H_{p}(v)+1>\inf_{v\in Y}H_{p}(v)+\delta
, so
v\not\in\delta\argmin H_{p}
; that is,
\delta\argmin H_{p}\subset S
. Combining,
\Omega^{S}_{H_{p}}(\delta)\subset\delta\argmin H_{p}
for all
p\in P
: if
v_{0}\in S
with
H_{p}(v_{0})\leq\inf_{v\in S}H_{p}(v)+\delta=\inf_{v\in Y}H_{p}(v)+\delta
, then
v_{0}\in\delta\argmin H_{p}
. In particular, if
H_{p}
attains its minimum on
S
at some
\bar{v}
, then
H_{p}
attains its minimum on
Y
at the same
\bar{v}
.

Now, consider for
n\in\mathbb{N}
the subset
D_{n}
of
\cal{P}
defined by

D_{n}:=\left\{\mathfrak{p}\in{\cal{P}}:\exists t>0:\ \mu\left(\Omega_{H_{p}}^{S}(t)\right)<\frac{1}{n}\right\}.

We will show that
D_{n}
is a dense and open set in
(\cal{P},\rho)
.

Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
\mathfrak{p}\in D_{n}
be arbitrary and let
\beta>0
be such that

\mu\left(\Omega_{H_{p}}^{S}(3\beta)\right)<\frac{1}{n}.

For any
\mathfrak{h}\in\cal{P}
such that
\rho(\mathfrak{h},0)<\beta/2r^{2}
, and any
v_{1},v_{2}\in S
, by Proposition
4.3
we have

\mathfrak{h}(v_{1})-\mathfrak{h}(v_{2})\leq 2\sup_{v\in S}\mathfrak{h}(v)\leq 2r^{2}\rho(\mathfrak{h},0)<\beta
, therefore,
\Omega_{\mathfrak{h}}^{S}(\beta)=S
. From (
7
) for
H_{p}
and
\mathfrak{h}
it follows that

\Omega^{S}_{H_{p+h}}(\beta)\subset\Omega_{H_{p}}^{S}(3\beta),

thus
\mu(\Omega^{S}_{H_{p+h}}(\beta))<1/n
and
\mathfrak{p}+\mathfrak{h}\in D_{n}
. So,
D_{n}
is open.

Let now
\mathfrak{h}\in\cal{P}
be arbitrary. Fix an arbitrary
\varepsilon\in(0,1/n)
.
Let
\delta>0
be provided by Proposition
4.4
. Fix

v_{0}\in\Omega_{H_{h}}^{S}(\delta),

and let
\mathfrak{p}\in\cal{P}
satisfy the conclusion of the Proposition
4.4
for
v_{0}
. Then Proposition
4.4
(b)
implies that
v_{0}\in\Omega_{\mathfrak{p}}^{S}(\delta)
. From (
7
) it follows that

\Omega_{H_{h+p}}^{S}(\delta)\subset\Omega_{\mathfrak{p}}^{S}(3\delta).

Since
\min_{Y}\mathfrak{p}=\mathfrak{p}(0)=0
and
0\in S
, we have
\Omega^{S}_{\mathfrak{p}}(3\delta)=\{v\in S:\mathfrak{p}(v)\leq 3\delta\}\subset(3\delta)\argmin\mathfrak{p}
, so by monotonicity of
\mu
and Proposition
4.4
(c)
,
\mu(\Omega^{S}_{\mathfrak{p}}(3\delta))\leq\mu((3\delta)\argmin\mathfrak{p})<\varepsilon
. Therefore
\mu(\Omega_{H_{h+p}}^{S}(\delta))<\varepsilon
.
As
\varepsilon<1/n
, this means that
\mathfrak{h}+\mathfrak{p}\in D_{n}
. By Proposition
4.4
(a)
,
\rho(\mathfrak{p},0)<\varepsilon
, and the distance from
\mathfrak{h}
to
D_{n}
is smaller than
\varepsilon
. In other words,
D_{n}
is dense in
(\cal{P},\rho)
.

Sets
D_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
are open dense sets in the complete metric space
({\cal{P}},\rho)
. By Baire Category Theorem, the set
\displaystyle D:=\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}D_{n}
is a dense set.

Let us fix
\mathfrak{p}\in D
. Then for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
there is
t_{n}>0
such that
\mu(\Omega_{H_{p}}^{S}(t_{n}))<1/n
. Take a sequence
\{t_{n}^{\prime}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
with
0<t_{n}^{\prime}\leq t_{n}
that decreases monotonically to zero. Then
\mu(\Omega_{H_{p}}^{S}(t_{n}^{\prime}))<1/n
. The sets
A_{n}=\Omega_{H_{p}}^{S}(t_{n}^{\prime})
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
are nested sets with
\mu(A_{n})<1/n
, which, because of the sequential
\tau
lower semicontinuity of
H_{p}
, are sequentially
\tau
-closed.

From Corollary
3.4
applied to
(S,\tau)
with the metric
d
induced by the norm in
Y
for the sets
A_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, it follows that the set
\displaystyle A:=\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}A_{n}\neq\varnothing
. Let
\bar{v}\in A
. This means that
\bar{v}\in S
and
\displaystyle H_{p}(\bar{v})\leq\inf_{v\in S}H_{p}(v)+t_{n}^{\prime}
, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Hence
H_{p}(\bar{v})=\min_{v\in S}H_{p}(v)=\min_{v\in Y}H_{p}(v)
, that is,
H_{p}
attains its minimum on
Y
.

To conclude, observe that since
D
is dense in
({\cal{P}},\rho)
, for a given
\varepsilon>0
one can find a
\mathfrak{p}\in D
such that
\varepsilon>\rho(\mathfrak{p},0)=\bar{\rho}(p,0)=\|p\|_{\infty}
. The proof is completed.
∎

6
Main result

Here is the promised extension of Theorem
1.1
.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
(X,\|\cdot\|)
be a Banach space. Let
f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
be lower semicontinuous, bounded below and such that
0\in\mathrm{dom\,}f
.
Consider the optimization problem
\left(P_{\|\cdot\|}\right)
defined by (
1
).

For each
\varepsilon>0
there is an equivalent norm
|\cdot|
on
X
such that

(9)

\|\cdot\|\leq|\cdot|\leq(1+\varepsilon)\|\cdot\|

and the corresponding problem
\left(P_{|\cdot|}\right)
has a solution.

Proof.

Fix arbitrary
\varepsilon>0
. From Theorem
5.1
it follows that there exists a squared continuous seminorm
p
on
X
such that
\|p\|_{\infty}<\varepsilon
and

H_{p}
attains minimum on
Y
.

Setting
|\cdot|:=\sqrt{\|\cdot\|^{2}+p(\cdot)}
one gets a norm such that the problem
(P_{|\cdot|})
has a solution.

Since for all
x\in X
,
\|x\|\leq\sqrt{\|x\|^{2}+p(x)}=|x|
, and for
x\neq 0
,

|x|=\sqrt{\|x\|^{2}+p(x)}=\sqrt{\|x\|^{2}+\|x\|^{2}p\left(\frac{x}{\|x\|}\right)}\leq(\sqrt{1+\varepsilon})\|x\|\leq(1+\varepsilon)\|x\|,

the estimate (
9
) holds.
∎

7
Concluding remarks

It might be interesting to apply our technique to the general case of the problem (
4
), namely

\min_{v\in Y}I(v),

where
\displaystyle I(v)=\int_{0}^{1}G(v(t),u(t),t)\,dt
with
u(t)=\varphi(v)(t)
.

Our method requires sequential
\tau
lower semicontinuity of
I
, so it would be good to have sufficient conditions for it in terms of
G
.

Of course, even better if
I
is weakly lower semicontinuous. Provided
X
is finite dimensional, under which conditions on
G
the value function
I
is weakly lower semicontinuous? The classical answer (Tonelli, covered in
[
2
,
7
]
): For
I(v)=\int_{0}^{1}G(v(t),u(t),t)\,dt
with
u=\varphi(v)
, weak lower semicontinuity typically requires
G
to be lower semicontinuous in
(v,u)
, and convex in
v
(the “velocity” variable) with a growth/coercivity condition on it. So, our case
G(v,u,t)=\|v\|^{2}+f(u)
is favorable:
\|v\|^{2}
is convex in
v
, and
f(u)
depends only on “position” variable
u
, so only lower semicontinuity of
f
is needed. When
X
is an infinite dimensional space even with convexity in
v
, weak convergence
v_{n}\to_{w}v
does not ensure that
\varphi(v_{n})\to\varphi(v)
in norm as
\varphi
is not compact. This is exactly why
\tau
was needed – it forces the norm convergence of
\varphi(v_{n})
by definition.

Therefore, in the case of infinite dimensional
X
, some conditions to check in future research are, for example,
G
lower semicontinuous in
(v,u)
, convex in
v
, with growth conditions. Would these allow perturbed minimization?
