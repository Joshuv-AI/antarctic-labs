ABSTRACT
This SAND report summarizes work supported by an Engineering Sciences Research Foundation
(ESRF) Lab Directed Research and Development (LDRD) project entitled “Beyond Magic Barrels:
Digital manufacturing for crystallization, process development and optimization of explosive
materials.” This SAND report is written in two parts with Part 1 discusses recrystallization of our
explosive exemplar and Part 2 summarizing our work with recrystallization of resveratrol. We have
studied resveratrol recrystallization with a multiscale approach combining experiments, modeling
and simulation. At the single crystal scale, microscopy experiments illuminate crystal time-dependent
growth rates using advanced image analysis. Bench scale experiments were carried out to look at
growth of multiple particles in a small reactor creating thousands of particles and analyzing the
results with microscopy and CT. For the modeling we combine kinetic Monte Carlo (kMC) models
with subscale information from density functional theory (DFT) or molecular dynamics. This work
is discussed in Part 1 and can also be found in a paper from the project discussing a coarse-grained
kMC model specifically developed for resveratrol. For well-mixed systems, we have population
balance equations (PBE) linked with species mass conservation forming a set of ordinary differential
equations that can be solved quickly. For more complicated geometries, such as the vat
crystallization used throughout the complex, a coupled computational fluid dynamic (CFD)/PBE
method was developed to account for gradients in temperature and concentration and differences in
crystallization rates throughout the domain. These simulations are more complex and require high
performance computing. We present results for two cases: 5% seed fast cool with parameters fit to
the well-mixed case and 5% seed slow cool using the same parameters. We show reasonable
agreement with experiments though are particles are significantly larger than the experiments.

1. INTRODUCTION AND BACKGROUND
This SAND report summarizes work supported by an Engineering Sciences Research Foundation
(ESRF) Laboratory Directed Research and Development (LDRD) project entitled “Beyond Magic
Barrels: Digital manufacturing for crystallization, process development and optimization of
explosive materials.” This SAND report is written in two parts where Part 1 discusses
recrystallization of the explosive exemplar and Part 2 summarizes our work with recrystallization of
the resveratrol exemplar.

Fundamental understanding of nucleation and growth mechanisms [1][5] during crystallization could
impact industries from biology, to geochemistry, nanomanufacturing, and pharma [9]. However, the
theoretical framework for predicting these phenomena is still lacking, especially for systems that
follow nonclassical pathways to crystal formation (Figure 1) [1][5]. Classical nucleation theory
assumes that crystals can grow only from monomer additions, while other growth mechanisms are

Figure 1. Figure taken from Fu et al., 2022 [1] showing the classical and nonclassical pathways
 to crystal formation

possible including agglomeration of an amorphous phase that then crystalizes or aggregation of mesoscale
crystals, or “mesocrystals,” that then aggregate to form larger crystals. Foundational theory and predictive
models of crystallization of small organic molecules from solution is current area of research.

In this SAND report, we study the recrystallization of resveratrol, a common antioxidant that has
also been used as chemotherapy [2][3], and has a similar symmetry, stacking and hydrogen bonding
as our energetic exemplar. The structure and crystal morphology of resveratrol is shown in Figure 2.
We think the resveratrol stacks the aromatics in the longer direction of the needle, leaving the
oxygens to interact in the thinner direction. We assume “oriented attachment” aggregation models
that consider the preferential orientation and addition of crystals to form plate-like or needle-like
structures [11][12].

 10

Figure 2. Chemical structure of trans-resveratrol (left) and images of typical crystals (right)

In this project, we have studied resveratrol recrystallization with a multiscale approach combining
experiments, modeling and simulation (Figure 3). At the single crystal scale, microscopy experiments
illuminate crystal time-dependent growth rates using advanced image analysis. Bench scale
experiments were carried out to look at growth of multiple particles in a small reactor creating
thousands of particles with microscopy and CT. For the modeling we combine kinetic Monte
Carlo (kMC) models with subscale information from density functional theory (DFT) or molecular
dynamics. This work is discussed in Part 1 and can also be found in a paper from the project
discussing a coarse-grained kMC model specifically developed for resveratrol [4]. For well-mixed
systems, we have population balance equations (PBE) linked with species mass conservation
forming a set of ordinary differential equations that can be solved fairly quickly [13][14][15][16]. For
more complicated geometries, such as the vat crystallization used throughout the complex, a
coupled computational fluid dynamic (CFD)/PBE method was developed to take into account
gradients in temperature and concentration and differences in rates throughout the domain. These
simulations are more complex and require high performance computing.

2. RESVERATROL EXPERIMENTS FOR CRYSTAL GROWTH AND
 CRYSTAL SIZE DISTRIBUTION

2.1. Single Crystal Growth Observations
The growth of resveratrol was imaged optically over time over a varying set of temperature and
concentration conditions in order to understand single crystal growth patterns and habits.

2.1.1. Single Crystal Growth Microscopy: Stagnant Growth
To image single crystal growth in a stagnant liquid, solutions were quenched below their solubility
limit and crystal formation observed using an optical microscope. Solutions were prepared by
dissolving >99.0% pure (GC) resveratrol powder (Sigma Aldrich) into an ethanol (Fischer Scientific):
deionized water solution (2:1). Two initial concentrations were of interest: 10 wt%, and 9 wt%.
Solutions were mixed at room temperature and heated to 65 °C in a closed container in an oven to
allow complete dissolution to occur without evaporation. Concentrations were chosen to match those
of our research partners at Purdue, who mapped a solubility curve for this solvent-solute system,
noting that the solution has high solubility while also undergoing primary nucleation of visible crystals
over laboratory acceptable timeframes and temperature ranges [23].

Once fully dissolved, approximately 30 minutes, low-volume quantities (~0.565 mL) of solution were
injected into SecureSeal™ Hybridization Chambers (Grace Biolabs) adhered to standard, clear glass
microscope slides via transfer pipettes. These commercially available wells could be sealed completely
to prevent evaporation of the solution, simplifying the experiment (Figure 4).

 Figure 4. SecureSeal hybridization chamber used for limiting evaporation during experimental
 observations. Gaskets adhere to standard glass slides to form a sealed well.

 13

  Figure 5. Crystal growth of 10 wt% resveratrol in an ethanol: water solution imaged over time
 after quenching from 60 oC to 20 oC

The solution-filled chambers were then transferred to a dial-controlled hotplate stationed near the
digital microscope and was set to, and monitored at, 65 °C to ensure the mixtures remained in solution
until quenching and imaging. Quenching was achieved with the use of a custom, temperature-
controlled microscope stage which was regulated by a JULABO refrigerated circulator. Once the stage
reached the desired quench temperature, the solution-filled chambers were quickly transferred to the
stage and imaging was started. Although the temperature was not monitored during the experiments
due to the inaccessibility of the sealed solution, it was estimated from separate thermocouple
measurements that the temperature reached the stage temperature within 30 s.

Example images are shown below in Figure 5 for the higher solution concentration, 10 wt% quenched
to 20 oC. Almost immediately, crystals nucleate in the solution and begin to grow in length. Capturing
the initial nucleation event was near impossible; although the solution was visibly clear and not
intentionally seeded, by the time imaging could be started there was always visible nucleation as shown
in Figure 5, t=0. Noticeable twinning of crystals initiates after a minute of growth, and nucleation
seems to decrease almost immediately. Particles grow with an elongated needle-like habit until
approximately 4 minutes, where three-dimensional star-shaped crystals start to dominate. Additional
arms of the star shapes are added in time, each originating from the center of the crystal. Figure 6 and
Figure 7 display an additional image of the star-shaped crystals that form at additional magnifications
and longer growth times.

To reduce the three-dimensional crystal growth habit, a second solution concentration was prepared
with 10% less resveratrol powder (0.09 g resveratrol / 1.0 g total mixture)). The reduced concentration gave a
reduced supersaturation as the solution was quenched to desired temperatures. Although the
preparation, transferring, handling, and image capturing procedures remained identical, the reduction
in resveratrol proved to provide much less densely populated crystal growth with separated single
crystal needles.

Several timelapse videos were recorded by programming the digital microscope to take an image every
15 seconds, as shown in Figure 8 and Figure 9. These timelapse videos were later post-processed to
analyze the crystal growth as a function of time at multiple steady state temperatures. It was observed
that the clearest images and subsequent timelapse videos were produced while quenching at 30 °C and
40 °C, while the crystallization at 20 °C appeared more distorted. This could be due to several factors,
one of which may be the greater temperature gradient between the 65 °C hot plate and the stage.

At reduced concentration, needle-like crystals grow with an extremely large aspect ratio. After an
initial width is established, the width stays approximately constant, whereas the length continues to
evolve over several minutes of growth.

2.2. Crystal dimension extraction procedure
Crystal samples showing clear single crystal growth (Figure 8-Figure 9) were examined to explore the
role of solution temperature on crystal growth. Using image processing software (OpenCV2) on the
static images from the timelapse video, the temporal length, width, and aspect ratio of the crystals
were captured throughout the growth of the crystal.
To capture the evolution of crystal dimensions over time, the time-lapse video was decomposed into
a sequence of individual frames. Each frame was preprocessed by converting it to grayscale
(intensity range 0–255), followed by the application of a Gaussian filter with a kernel size of (11,11)
to reduce noise and smooth the image, thereby facilitating crystal detection. Crystal regions were
distinguished from the background solution based on intensity contrast. Specifically, an intensity
threshold 𝐼𝑐 was applied, where pixels with Gaussian-smoothed intensities greater than or equal to
𝐼𝑐 = 230 were classified as part of the crystal. The corresponding pixel coordinates were then
extracted to quantify and analyze crystal dimensions. This procedure was repeated for all frames in
the time-lapse series. To gather the length and width dimensions of each crystal, each segmented
crystal was extracted as a series of points from the image (See Figure 10), where a coordinate
transform was conducted to align each grain axis with along the X and Y axis respectively. With this,
accurately measured dimensions for the length and width were gathered.

Like Figure 11, the temporal variation in crystal width for quench temperatures of 20 °C, 30 °C, and
40 °C is presented in Figure 12. In contrast to the length growth trends, increasing solution
temperature significantly suppresses the width growth rate. Specifically, the width growth rate at 20
°C is approximately 13 times greater than that at 40 °C. This indicates that crystal growth is both
face-specific and temperature-dependent: at higher quench temperatures, growth is strongly favored
in the length direction over the width direction. To further illustrate this effect, the aspect ratio
(L/W) was plotted as a function of time. Crystals quenched at lower temperatures maintain a
relatively low aspect ratio (~4), which gradually decreases during growth. In contrast, crystals
quenched at higher temperatures exhibit a pronounced increase in aspect ratio, confirming that
growth along the length direction is substantially greater than along the width direction.

2.3. Study of Resveratrol crystallization: Examination of crystal length, width,
 and height after 5 days
Resveratrol was dissolved in an ethanol-water solution (2:1 ratio of ethanol to water) prepared by
combining 40 g of ethanol and 20.01 g of deionized water. A 20 g portion of this solution was
transferred to a container, followed by the addition of 2 g of resveratrol, resulting in a resveratrol-to-
solution ratio of 0.1 g per 1 g. The mixture was then heated in an oven at 70°C for 24 hours,
ensuring complete solubility of the resveratrol. Subsequently, the solution was transferred to a
chamber maintained at 20°C and left undisturbed for 5 days.
Cystal samples were taken out of the solution and placed into a Bruker SKYSCAN
1272 microcomputed tomography (microCT or μCT) scanner, a nondestructive imaging tool [21] is
used to construct a high-resolution three-dimensional (3D) model of the crystal population. The
resolution of the microCT machine is 0.45 𝜇𝑚. The constructed three-dimensional model of the
crystal population was analyzed in Dragonfly software, Version 2024.1 [22], where individual crystals
were segmented from the population via a watershed transform.

2.4. Crystal dimensions and aspect ratio
To determine the aspect ratios of individual crystals, it is necessary to measure their length, width,
and height dimensions. A widely used method for calculating aspect ratios involves computation of
the eigenvalues of the moment of inertia tensor for each crystal. In this approach, the smallest
eigenvalue corresponds to the longest axis of the crystal, while the largest eigenvalue represents the
shortest axis. However, a notable limitation of this method is that it only provides the ratio of the

 21

longest to the shortest axis, leaving the length of the intermediate axis undetermined. Therefore, to
gather the length, width, and height dimensions of each crystal, each segmented crystal was exported
from Dragonfly as a mesh file, where a coordinate transform was conducted to align each grain axis
with along the X, Y, Z axis respectively (Figure 14). With this, accurately measured dimensions for
the length, width, and height were gathered.

3. CONTINUUM MODELING OF CRYSTAL SIZE DISTRIBUTION
At the continuum-scale, a coupled fluid-thermal-reacting computational fluid dynamic (CFD)-
population balance model (PBM) has been developed based on the method of moments, to predict
crystal size distributions in Goma [27], an open source code developed at Sandia and University of
New Mexico, following a recent framework developed for bubble populations in polyurethane
foams [6]. Although nucleation, growth, and break up were implemented at the beginning of the
project, nonspherical particles with anisotropic crystal growth and cuboid shapes presented
challenges. We ended up assuming that the crystals grow isotropically and nucleate and grow while
keeping the same aspect ratios, thus only one length scale needs to be tracked. The shape was
needle-like with three different length scales, width, length, and height as seen in the experimental
section. A scale factor is used in the population balance equations to keep track of the geometry
similar to work from Zhang and Doherty for two-dimensional growth modeling [8]. The model is
implemented using the quadrature method of moments (QMOM) [17][24][25].

3.3.2. The finite element method, initial, and boundary conditions
The final system of 11 equations consisting of the conservation of mass (1), momentum (3), energy
(1), species (2), and moment equations (4) is discretized using the finite element method. The
discretized system is then solved using the open-source software Goma [27]. A combination of Q1
and Q2 elements is used for the various equations. Mass, species, pressure, and moment equations
are solved using Q1 elements, and the remaining equations are solved on Q2 elements.

 30

For pressure stabilization, when we use a lower order velocity interpolant, we use the Galerkin least
square pressure stabilization with local scaling, combined with a lagged scaling, which converges
faster for decoupled systems. The implicit Euler method is used for integration in time for all the
equations excluding 𝑌0 and 𝑌1 for which we use a semi-implicit Taylor-Galerkin time integration
and the moment equations, which are integrated using an explicit Euler because they require the
adaptive Wheeler algorithm to be performed during the solution.

For computational efficiency we solve a segregated system of four matrices: 1) mass and
momentum, 2) species, 3) energy, and lastly the 4) moment equations. To solve the resulting linear
systems, we use Krylov-based iterative solvers, GMRES/LU for the first three systems, and a
parallel direct solver, Mumps, for the moment equations in Petsc solver package [26].

3.5. Conclusions for coupled CFD/PBE

We have presented a coupled computational fluid dynamics/population balance equation method
for modeling recrystallization in vats where there are large gradients in temperature and
concentrations and the additional polydispersity in crystal size compared to a well-mixed system.
Average results for the fast cool and the slow cool cases for 5% seed are shown in Figure 38. The
slow cooled case has more particles, indicating that it had more time for nucleation than the fast
cooled case. Both cases have there maximum pdf at a particle size of about 10m.

Figure 38. Comparison of fast cool and slow cool case for 5% seed experiments. Slow cool
experiment has more particles and a slightly smaller maxima than the fast cooled case.

For future work, we would like to improve the model by testing against additional data sets. We
would like to explore the stability of our solution, expand the population balance model to capture
missing physics that contribute to the observed polydispersity, including potentially more complex
nucleation, both primary and secondary nucleation, or perhaps a more complex growth model. It
may may sense to add in breakage as well, since we are considering a mixing system with local high
gradients in shear rates. Improvements to our PBE model will also be investigated, for instance, we
may need to track two or three length scales instead of just one. The length growth may depend on
the length itself. These new requirements for a more complex morphology model would also require
improvement to the quadrature method of moments algorithm with more advanced ways of finding
and evolving the integration point perhaps using a submatrix solve for the location of the quadrature
points and weights.

4. A KINETICALLY INFORMED THERMODYNAMIC (KIT) DESIGN
 FRAMEWORK FOR UNDERSTANDING THE CRYSTALLIZATION
 CHARACTERISTICS OF RESVERATROL

This paper was submitted to CrystEngComm, published, by the Royal Society of Chemistry, in July
2025. No reviews have yet been received. The authors are Monika Neal, Álmos Orosz, Rekha Rao,
and Zoltán K. Nagy.

4.1. Abstract
Crystallization is a crucial process in the production of solid materials across various industries, where
it not only purifies the compound but also defines essential particle attributes. These particle-specific
critical quality attributes (CQAs), including crystal size distribution, morphology, and purity, directly
impact the quality and efficiency of downstream operations, such as filtration, drying, and formulation.
The ability to precisely control these attributes during crystallization is essential for ensuring consistent
product quality, process efficiency, and regulatory compliance. Many process parameters can influence
CQAs; the solvent is often the first variable investigated when designing a crystallization process, and
selection is typically guided by thermodynamic criteria such as solubility and theoretical yield.
However, as development advances to larger scales, kinetic limitations such as agglomeration or poor
crystal habit often emerge, driven by factors like local supersaturation, mixing inefficiencies, and
particle–particle interactions. These behaviors are difficult to predict from solubility data alone and
require experimental insight to effectively manage. This study presents a kinetically informed
thermodynamic (KIT) design framework for solvent screening to help mitigate these issues. The KIT
design framework enhances early decision-making by combining traditional thermodynamic screening
with straightforward kinetic assessments using small-scale cooling crystallization experiments.
Through qualitative and semi-quantitative observations of nucleation, growth, agglomeration, and
aspect ratio, the framework helps prioritize solvents that not only meet yield targets but also support
desirable kinetic behavior. This integrated approach improves the reliability of solvent selection,
reduces the likelihood of scale-up challenges, and minimizes the need for extensive late-stage process
troubleshooting.

5. TWO-DIMENSIONAL POPULATION BALANCE MODELING (2D-PBM)
 AND MACHINE LEARNING-BASED MULTI-OBJECTIVE
 OPTIMIZATION FOR THE COOLING CRYSTALLIZATION PROCESS
 OF RESVERATROL
This paper was submitted to AICHE Journal in March 2025. It has received favorable reviews, and a
revised version was resubmitted to the journal. The authors are Álmos Orosz, Monika Neal, Rekha
Rao, Botond Szilágyi, and Zoltán K. Nagy.

5.1. Abstract
Crystallization is a critical process in the pharmaceutical industry, influencing the purity of active
pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs) and the efficiency of downstream processes. By controlling crystal
attributes such as size, shape, morphology, and polymorphism, the quality and processability of
pharmaceutical products can be significantly improved. However, achieving optimal conditions
through experimentation alone can be challenging for complex crystallization systems. This study
explores the application of a two-dimensional Population Balance Model (2D-PBM) to simulate and
optimize a cooling crystallization process. In the study, resveratrol is used as a model API
crystallized in a water-ethanol solvent mixture. Experimental data were collected using advanced
techniques such as Focused Beam Reflectance Measurement (FBRM) and, UV/Vis spectroscopy,
and the 2D crystal size data was measured through microscopy imaging. A design of experiments
(DoE) consisting of 6 calibrations and 1 validation experiments were executed to generate data for
model building. Upon parameter estimation, a well-calibrated mathematical model was obtained with
good prediction capability and with parameters having narrow confidence intervals. The calibrated
2D-PBM was utilized for multi-objective optimization (MOO) in two scenarios: (1) maximizing yield
and minimizing batch time while meeting quality constraints, and (2) minimizing crystal aspect ratio
while maximizing the median size of the crystal size distribution (CSD). MOO1 revealed minimal
trade-offs, achieving high yield while maintaining quality constraints. MOO2 demonstrated an
inherent dependency between crystal size and aspect ratio, where decreasing aspect ratio required
sacrificing crystal size or yield. A hybrid simulation approach integrating first-principal models and
machine-learning (ML)-based regression/classification models is proposed and utilized to address
computational challenges. This framework effectively predicted Pareto-optimal solutions, while
reducing simulation time by over 5000-fold, and providing an innovative approach, e.g., hybrid
optimization or model-predictive control applications. The study highlights the potential of
combining advanced, but computationally costly modeling and machine learning techniques to
optimize complex pharmaceutical crystallization processes efficiently, rapidly, and with a well-
controlled precision.

7.2. Conclusions and Next Steps

In this project, we have developed experimental methods and models for understanding the
recrystallization of resveratrol, an important pharmaceutical that forms needle-like structures with
three different length scales. The model was compared to experimental results for crystallization in
an EasyMax and was able to capture the trends for a 5% seed case with fast and slow cooling. We do
plan to continue to improve the model with algorithmic changes to make it more predictive
including other length scales.

As this LDRD concludes, we do have a follow-on project with many of the team continuing the
collaboration with a DREA2M LDRD on automation for processing energetic materials. We also
have an ASC PSI project on reactor modeling for metal particle formation, which also involves
CFD/PBE for morphology prediction. These metal particles are formed in large vats with poor
mixing and large temperature and concentration gradients. We hope to continue our collaborations
with Purdue and FSU/FAMU through and are working on proposal calls and the possibility of
writing joint proposals where possible. We are also looking at working on possible CRADAs with
industrial partners in pharma and consumer products such as GSK or P&G.
