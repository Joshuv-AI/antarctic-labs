Abstract
 The Zettawatt-Equivalent Ultrashort pulse laser System (ZEUS) is presently operational at the
Gerard Mourou Center for Ultrafast Optical Science (CUOS) at the University of Michigan. ZEUS is a
significant upgrade of the previous high power laser systems at CUOS and consists of two beamlines that
operate in perfect synchronization. The 500 TW beamline became operational in 2023, 2 PW operation
started in 2025 and full 3 PW power levels will be available in 2026. It is presently the highest power
laser system in the US. In this grant the high field science group at CUOS has leveraged this unique high
power laser facility to investigate laser wake field acceleration (LWFA) in ultra-high power laser plasma
interactions and have shown how this can scale for future electron–positron colliders at high energy. The
dual beam experimental configuration enables flexibility for many frontier experiments in laser-driven
acceleration research, in particular, enabling extended channelling/acceleration experiments, positron
generation/acceleration experiments and proof-of-principle transverse pumping “dephasingless” electron
acceleration experiment and theory.
 LWFA may be able to miniaturize particle accelerators for high energy physics and also enable
new sources of ultrafast, extreme brightness and precise x-rays for a wide variety of applications. In laser
wake field acceleration, an electron bunch “surfs” on the electron plasma wave (the “wake field”)
generated by the ponderomotive force of an intense laser. The plasma wave has a strong longitudinal
electric field that stays in phase with the relativistic driver. A relativistic charged particle may, therefore,
remain in phase with the accelerating field over long distances and gain ultra-relativistic energies. The
accelerating electric field strength that the plasma wave can support can be many orders of magnitude
higher than that of conventional accelerators, which makes laser wakefield acceleration an exciting
prospect as an advanced accelerator concept.
 In this research project we have investigated the scaling of this mechanism to laser powers of 2
PW and have measured the x-ray emission and radio frequency emission resulting from the acceleration
process. We have also performed theoretical investigation of mechanisms to scale laser driven accelerators
to much higher energy using dephasingless acceleration processes.

Introduction

 The Center for Ultrafast Optical Science has been a leader in the development of ultra-high
power laser technology related to the construction of HERCULES. This laser has reached record
intensities using adaptive optics to achieve near diffraction limited focal spots. In order to make
intensities > 1022 W/cm2 possible using dense targets, the laser contrast (the ratio of the peak
intensity to the pre-pulse intensity) has been improved to 11 orders of magnitude, in order to restrict
the generation of pre-plasma. Several technological advances such as the use of a high energy
regenerative amplifier, the “extraction during pumping” method of Ti:Sapphire pumping,
cryogenic cooling of the Ti:Sapphire crystal, and relay imaging have also been developed.
 The Zettawatt-Equivalent Ultrashort pulse laser System (ZEUS) project has recently been
completed at the Center for Ultrafast Optical Science (CUOS) at the University of Michigan – as
funded by the NSF. ZEUS is a significant upgrade of the previous HERCULES 300 TeraWatt (TW)
laser system and consists of two beamlines that are designed to operate simultaneously and in
perfect synchronization. It will also be able to operate in a single beam 3 Petawatt (PW) mode
which will make it the highest power laser system in the US. The 500 TW beamline was operational

 The first LWFA experiments in target area 1 were conducted in the summer of 2024 and

LASER WAKEFIELD ACCELERATION AT 3 PETAWATTS

 Plasma-based particle accelerators have proven to be an attractive technology for their
ability to accelerate particles to ultra-relativistic energies in short distances. Their multi-GV/m
(109) accelerating gradients provide a promise to cheaper and smaller, “tabletop” particle
accelerators for x-ray production, relativistic electron, ion and neutron generation, medical
radiotherapy, material imaging, compact free electron lasers, and even exploration of non-linear
quantum electrodynamics. Beginning almost two decades ago with the demonstration of quasi-
monoenergetic particle acceleration steady progress has been made in enhancing electron beam
quality, increasing beam energy, minimizing relative energy spreads, moving towards high
repetition rates, and achieving long-term operational stability.
 In the basic configuration for Laser Wakefield Acceleration, a high intensity pulse,
typically ~ 1019 W/cm2, is focused into an optically transparent (underdense) plasma. At high
intensities, the effective light pressure, or ponderomotive force, pushes electrons away from the
most intense regions of the laser. This pushing leads to a charge separation between electrons and
comparatively immobile background ions, which in the low-intensity case can be treated as a
simple harmonic oscillator leading to the generation of electron plasma waves. This regime is
defined as the “linear wakefield regime” and is quantified via the normalized vector potential of
the laser field:

Where 𝑒 is the fundamental electron charge, 𝑨 is the vector potential, 𝑚e the mass of an electron,
and c is the speed of light. When the value of 𝑎o =1, electrons are capable of gaining their rest mass
energy (i.e., achieving relativistic energies) within one optical cycle of the laser. As the value 𝑎o
>1, electrons in the plasma are expelled radially outwards from the laser focal region at relativistic
speeds leaving behind a completely void region in its wake, where only the heavier positively
charged ions remain.

 This void takes the shape of a relativistic plasma wave or plasma “bubble” co-moving with the
laser group velocity. The charge separation leads to conditions suitable for an efficient high

gradient charged particle accelerator, with transverse confining fields and longitudinal accelerating
fields as demonstrated in Fig. 6. This high intensity laser wakefield regime is known as the
“bubble” or “blowout” regime and has been a key area of interest for its demonstration to
consistently accelerate quality quasi-monoenergetic particles to GeV energies. Various regimes of
these laser driven wakefield accelerators exist depending on the matching of laser and plasma
parameters.
 Even with LWFA’s success in demonstrating few-femtosecond electron bunches, GeV
electron energies, nano-Coulomb charge, low transverse emittance, and high repetition rates –
much work is still ongoing as these milestones have yet to be achieved simultaneously.

Radiation Shielding

Figure 8: Axonometric view of shielding wall design

 The expected laser power and intensity from the ZEUS system means that significant radiation
shielding is required in the target areas. However, because only a limited number of shots will be
produced at 1-5 Hz due to burst mode operation and as 3 PW operation will be performed at 1
shot/minute, this will consequently limit the total amount of radiation produced. Limiting the total
number of shots (i.e., determining the maximum number of high dose shots per day as well as the

maximum number of high dose shots per year) has proven to be an effective method for limiting
radiation generation in the past – since after more than ten years of HERCULES operation (at 0.1
Hz) we have only measured significant radiation doses inside the interaction chambers.
 There has never been a measurable dose outside of the present radiation shielding during this
time. In ZEUS the radiation shielding wall design consists of 24 inch thick high-density concrete
walls, which surrounds the new target chambers. In addition, the shielding walls have been
extended at varying heights above the laboratory (the high bay) to protect occupants in the
mezzanine floor office areas and stairways from radiation exposure (see Figure 9). In addition to
the structural radiation protection, we use local lead shielding (for x-rays) lined with borated plastic
(for neutrons) around the target chamber. This design has been simulated using the Monte-Carlo
code FLUKA and has been found to enable safe operation. A final optimized shielding design will
be established in consultation with the University of Michigan Radiological Safety Office after
completion of commissioning shots in 2024. Because of induced radioactivity we have determined
that a less than 15 minute delay is required after a high dose shot before researchers can be allowed
back into the experimental area.
 It should be emphasized that CUOS and the University of Michigan have significant experience
in dealing with laser produced radiation hazards and we have a high degree of confidence in our
ability to predict, monitor and handle these sources. It is anticipated that no user of the facility will
be required to be classified as a radiation worker.

Diagnostic Development – Gamma Ray Spectrometer

 Diagnostics for angularly resolved measurements of X-ray betatron radiation from laser
wakefield acceleration experiments at 3 PW in ZEUS have been developed. In particular, we use
a filter pack as shown in Figure 13 below. The filter pack is made of multiple repeating columns
with 11 filters in each column. The filters are varying thicknesses of aluminum or copper that will
absorb photon energies differently based on the attenuation curve of each material. Because of
these differences, a particular incident photon energy spectrum will create a unique transmission
pattern through the filters.
 From this transmission pattern, we can determine the critical energy of the synchrotron like x-
ray spectrum that would have produced it. This can be done column by column to find the critical
energy at different angles as the x-rays are streaked across the camera.

Figure 13: (Left) Color-coded filter pack schematic listing the thickness, position and material of
each filter. Each column is identical and contains aluminum and copper filters. (Right) Physical
version of the filter pack used in experiments.

The photon beam has an uneven spatial intensity distribution which needs to be flattened before
we can extract transmission values. We have worked out an algorithm that does the following:
1. Retrieves background values from the spaces between the filter columns
2. Interpolates across the whole image to retrieve the intensity profile
3. Divides the original image pixelwise by this background profile

background.

 After the background has been flattened, the transmission values through each filter are

EMP (Electro-magnetic Pulses) characterization from Laser Wakefield Accelerators

Figure 15: Image plate of LWFA electron beam with annular electron spray (annular ring of
electrons is about 10 degrees)

 Measurements of Radio Frequency (RF) emission may be a useful diagnostic for electron
dynamics in laser-plasma accelerators. Such radiation can also be detrimental as it is a significant
source of noise for other diagnostics as a disruptive electromagnetic pulse (EMP). Consequently,
it is important to understand its effects and mitigation strategies for 3PW experiments on the ZEUS
laser facility. According to most theoretical models for RF emission in laser plasma experiments,

the electric and magnetic fields are directly proportional to the charge emitted by the target. For
solid targets, this has been experimentally verified, but a significant gap in the literature remains
for RF emission from experiments with gaseous targets.
 In laser wakefield acceleration (LWFA), lasers are used in with low-density targets to accelerate
electrons to a beam. However, recent work at CUOS has demonstrated that the lasers in LWFA
experiments make not only a beam of electrons but an annular spray of lower energy electrons as
well (Fig. 15). There is direct evidence of non-beam electrons being ejected from the plasma in
LWFA, which may contribute to the EMP.
 For this project we performed RF measurements from a two-laser ionization injection LWFA
experiment. This is a particular setup for LWFA in which a “driver” laser pulse creates the
wakefield, and the “injector” laser pulse populates (“injects”) the wakefield with high energy
electrons, which then get trapped and accelerated into an electron beam. Varying the delay of the
injector pulse also seemed to control the efficiency of the LWFA mechanism.
 Fig. 16 depicts the setup of the ionization injection experiment performed using the frontend of
the ZEUS laser at the Center for Ultrafast Optical Science (CUOS), which produced about 9J in
39 fs FWHM. The driver pulse was picked off of the center of the laser and was focused with an
f/40 optic for an a0 of 0.71. The injector was the remaining annulus of the laser which was focused
with an f/3.8 optic for an a0 of 1.88. We controlled the time delay between the driver and injector
pulses between shots. The target was a gas cell which was filled with helium gas with nitrogen
dopant ranging from 0% to 5% for some shots.

Fig. 16: Setup for LWFA experiment in ZEUS Target Area 3 (TA3).

 The electron spectrometer consists of a magnet and two scintillating LANEX screens, capturing
electrons from 20 MeV to 300 MeV with divergences less than 20 milliradians. A typical
spectrometer image is shown in Fig. 17. EMP was measured by a “B-dot” probe consisting of
enameled magnet wire soldered to the end of coaxial cable. This probe was placed 35 centimeters
away from the target.

Fig. 17: Typical electron spectrometer image for an electron beam.

 The y-axes of the subfigures in Fig. 18 is the integral of the power spectrum of the oscilloscope
trace from the B-dot probe. We used this quantity to measure the overall strength of the EMP.

 A few significant results are as follows. First, the EMP depends on the relative delay between
the driver and injector beam pulses. Secondly, introducing a wave plate changed the optimum
injector delay for EMP production (Fig. 18a). This may correspond to the optical path difference
induced by including the wave plate. Thirdly, doping the helium with nitrogen tended to decrease
the EMP (Fig. 18b). This is in contrast to the electron beam, which had increased strength when
the helium was doped with nitrogen. Fourthly, higher gas pressure led to higher EMP (Fig. 18c).
This is to be expected as a higher electron density would lead to more charge being ejected from
the plasma.

EMP comparison to bremsstrahlung and electron beam signals
 The relationships between the EMP, bremsstrahlung, and electron beam strength are drawn in
Fig. 19. First it is important to verify the positive correlation between the bremsstrahlung signal
and the electron beam signal (Fig. 19a). Beam electrons between 20 MeV and 300 MeV should
emit x-rays when they are stopped by the stainless steel walls of the chamber. When there is more
electron charge, there should be more x-rays, and this seems to be the case.

Fig. 19. Scatter plots which represent the relationships between (a) bremsstrahlung and electron
beam strength, (b) EMP and electron beam strength, and (c) EMP and bremsstrahlung. The
bremsstrahlung is quantified by the peak voltage from the PMT signal, the electron beam strength
is quantified by the integrated LANEX scintillation signal, and the EMP is quantified by the
integrated B-dot signal. All figures represent the same population of shots.

 Second there is a surprising result in that when there is little to-no electron beam present, the
EMP ranges from its weakest to its very strongest (the vertical bar of points on the left side of Fig.
19a). This suggests that the relativistic electron beam is not the primary source of electrons which
cause EMP. There must be a low-energy (below 20 MeV) or high-divergence (greater than 20
milliradians) population of electrons which escape the plasma and cause an EMP. A further look
at Fig. 19b suggests that increasing the electron beam strength raises the lower bound on the
number of “EMP-electrons” which are produced.
 Thirdly, from Fig. 19c we have verification that the EMP is indeed from a population of
electrons which are leaving the plasma. The strongest bremsstrahlung signals certainly come from
the electrons of the LWFA beam. But when the beam is not present, there is still a bremsstrahlung
signal which has a small but strong correlation with the EMP strength. This suggests that the EMP
electrons are low energy but still fast enough to create measurable bremsstrahlung radiation.

In summary for Fig. 19, the main result is that there is a population of low-energy electrons and/or
high-divergence electrons which are not captured by the electron spectrometer but do contribute
to the EMP and bremsstrahlung signals.

Control of transverse density gradients in Laser Wakefield Accelerators
 Laser wakefield accelerators (LWFA) may prove to be a promising alternative to large scale
radio frequency accelerators due to their capability for generating multi-GeV electron beams, as
well as its success in practical applications in various science frontiers, such as advanced light
sources including compact X-ray free electron lasers (XFEL) and strong field quantum
electrodynamics. At present, a significant effort has been put into boosting the electron energy
gain towards the TeV regime for future electron-positron colliders. However, the maximum energy
gain of a LWFA is only on the order of ≤10 GeV limited by the so called “3D” problem, i.e.,
diffraction, depletion and dephasing. Therefore, to achieve TeV energy gain, multi-stage LWFA
and dephasingless LWFA have been proposed. In either case, it is essential to precisely diagnose

the electron acceleration process, especially the dephasing point, to fully utilize the maximum
electron energy gain.
 So far, the diagnosis of electron spectrum is primarily done with magnetic electron
spectrometers located downstream of the accelerator as planned in ZEUS. This is a limitation in
that the electron spectrum was measured after the whole acceleration process, providing no
information with regard to the electron beam energy evolution. Attempts for such evolution
measurements have been made in multi-shot mode by either varying the acceleration length in an
invasive way or by inferring from the Thomson scattering spectrum in which the tunable colliding
location was varied inside the plasma. Such multi-shot methods rely on the stability and the
reproducibility of the LWFA, which is still difficult to obtain. There is still no single-shot method
of measuring the electron energy evolution.
 Here we describe the development of a noninvasive single-shot method to reconstruct the
electron acceleration process via the streaking concept of the betatron X-rays in LWFA
experiments. This is made possible due to the fact that the measurable properties of betatron X-
rays can be completely determined by the electron energy given known plasma parameters. As we
know that during the acceleration in LWFA, electrons perform transverse betatron oscillations due
to the inherent transverse focusing force of the plasma wakefield and produce synchrotron-like
betatron radiation.
 Thus, the properties of the betatron X-rays can be correlated with the electron properties. Thus,
the betatron radiation properties are completely determined by the electron energy γ and its
betatron oscillation amplitude rβ given known plasma density. Note that since the betatron
oscillation amplitude scales with electron energy, with a known plasma density distribution, the
betatron properties will be determined only by the electron energy. This means if we can measure
the temporal evolution of the betatron critical energy, we should be able to reconstruct the temporal
evolution of the electron acceleration process.
 Similar to the idea of the synchrotron light source device in which electron bunch at different
locations of its circular trajectory emits at different angle, we previously proposed to use a curved
LWFA to convert the temporal evolution of the betatron X-ray into the angular distribution, i.e.,
the so called betatron “streaking”. Such a curved LWFA is achieved by launching the laser pulse
into a plasma with a transverse density gradient in which the laser pulse front tilt develops due to
the transverse phase velocity gradient and eventually leading to a parabolic trajectory. With
Particle-in-Cell (PIC) simulations, we demonstrated the diagnosis of electron acceleration process
in single shot.
 Here, we report on the preliminary experimental demonstration of single shot diagnosis of
electron acceleration dynamics using the same method. In our experiment, LWFA was achieved
with a two-stage 3D printed gas jet where the first stage was the injector with localized ionization
injection and the second stage was the accelerator which also provides the desired transverse
density gradient on its edges to realize the curved LWFA. With the curved LWFA, we observed
simultaneously the steering of the laser driver, accelerated electron beam as well as the betatronX-
ray. The spectrum of the betatron X-rays was resolved angularly with which the dephasing length
(and potentially the electron acceleration process) was reconstructed Steering of the laser, electron
and betatron X-ray beams
 A laser pulse with a peak power of 100 TW was focused onto a two-stage gas jet target. The
first stage was designed to provide localized ionization injection to minimize the energy spread of
the electron beam. The second stage further accelerate the injected electron beam and provides a
linear density gradient at its edges.

 As mentioned above, a plasma with a transverse density gradient will cause a curved curved
trajectory of a laser pulse due to the gradient of the transverse laser phase velocity. Such curved
trajectories were observed in the experiment via the Thomson scattering light.

Figure 22: Example of beam steering with a transverse density gradient plasma. (a) and (b), laser
trajectories and the X-ray beam profile when the laser is propagating in the middle (uniform
density) of the gas jet, respectively.

 Fig. 22(a) and (b) demonstrates the comparison of the laser trajectories in a straight LWFA and
a curved LWFA by moving the target in transverse. In Fig. 22(a), the laser was propagated in a
uniform density region so that the laser trajectories are parallel to the edge of the nozzle.
Meanwhile, the betatron X-rays were centered on the detector, as shown in Fig. 22(b).
 While, when the target was moved to the very edge of the nozzle where a quasi-linear density
gradient can be found (see an example in Fig. 22(c)), the laser pulse was steered gradually during
the propagation sideways to form clear curved trajectories. The maximum deviation at the end of
the propagation was about 300 μm, which agrees with PIC simulations with similar laser and
plasma parameters. As a consequence, the betatron X-rays were shifted to the same direction by
approximately 10 mrad, as shown in Fig. 22(c).
 Quantitative steering of the beams is presented in Figure 23. We investigated such steering in
two scenarios. In the first case, the nozzles are orthogonal to the laser propagation. Therefore,
when scanning the transverse location of the target, the laser pulse would experience regions with
either uniform plasma density or with linear density gradient. Consequently, the laser
displacement, the betatron X-ray beam and the electron beam pointing all demonstrate a plateau
followed by a linear transition, as shown in Fig. 23(a-c). In the second case, there is a small angle
of few tens of mrad between the laser incidence angle and the long edge of the second gas nozzle.
The laser will always experience a transverse density gradient regardless of the initial transverse
location. Therefore, the plateaus observed in the first case disappeared, all three beams were
steered consistently, as we can see from Fig. 23(d-f).

Elongated X-ray profile due to the density gradient
 The transverse density gradient also affects the betatron X-ray spatial distribution dramatically.
In Fig. 24, we compared the X-ray beam profile in three cases, including the two cases discussed
above with the two stage gas jets, as in Fig.24(b) and (c) and the case of using agas cell as the
target in Fig. 24(a). The gas cell target has the advantage of providing homogeneous plasma
density distribution to avoid unstable acceleration processes. The X-ray beam profile in this case
would be smooth and approximately isotropic in the transverse direction. The slight elliptical shape

Mitigating Spatiotemporal Couplings on ZEUS
 A key concept driving progress in high intensity lasers and wakefield acceleration is the ability
to concentrate the maximum amount of laser energy into the smallest 3-dimensional (x, y, t) space-
time possible. Historically, this feat has been approached in a two-step sequential campaign: 1)
temporal compression of the laser in time and 2) ensuring a spatially uniform optical wavefront
suitable for focusing in space. This approach encourages a space-time separable viewpoint of the
laser electric field:
                                     𝐸(𝑥, 𝑦, 𝑡) = 𝐸(𝑥, 𝑦) ∗ 𝐸(𝑡)
where 𝐸 represents the electric field, 𝑡 is time, and 𝑥, 𝑦 the spatial coordinates. Until recent years,
this framework has proven adequate with the use of chirped pulse amplification and compression
via reflective gratings for controlling the temporal profile, alongside adaptive optics (i.e.,
deformable mirrors) for spatial control which has allowed for the realization of TW Class laser

systems. But as high-power lasers increase in power and subsequently their beam sizes, so does
the relative contribution of so called “spatio-temporal couplings” (STCs).
 These couplings can best be summarized as spectral properties (whether in amplutide or
phase) of the laser which are spatially dependent, that when Fourier transformed into real space
manifest themself as temporal (intensity and phase) variations across the beam profile. These
couplings almost always lead to detrimental effects of increasing the pulse duration and reducing
the pulse focus-ability. On the other hand, they do provide a unique method of controlling the
properties of light and therefore laser-plasma interactions.
 For brevity we will only introduce the lowest order coupling which leads to the greatest
reduction in on target intensity outside of the well-known Pulse Front Tilt, that is Pulse Front
Curvature (PFC). In this STC, the duration of the pulse is constant across the beam, but the arrival
time at focus varies quadratically with the radial position. This STC has a relatively commonplace
source, simple chromatic lenses - often used within beam expanders during high-power pulse
amplification. From the temporal viewpoint, the outer edges of the pulse travel through a different
amount of glass than regions near the

 The PFC spatio-temporal coupling mentioned above has been investigated during the

beam. This was estimated to contribute more than a 100 fs of PFC; thus artificially stretching the
pulse in time and reducing the on-target peak intensity.

 This work is expected to continue in several ways. The most evident is the need to
characterize the spatio-temporal beam profile on ZEUS, including the above-mentioned PFC. The
field of pulse metrology and more specifically high-power pulse metrology is an extremely rich
and complex area which extends beyond the present work. In short, the ultimate goal of this
metrology is single-shot, full spatio-temporal reconstruction of the pulse profile. Attempting to
measure such a 3dminesional space of fs timescale on 2-dimensional detectors is far from trivial.
 We have also explored advanced pulse measuring techniques. At this time, we have decided
to take a simple first step and use a Swamp Optics GRENOUILLE to probe the nearfield beam at
several positions along the transverse profile. This technique will be capable of capturing the first-
order STCs of pulse front tilt and pulse front curvature. As the repetition rate of ZEUS (1 shot/min)
prevents the practical implementation of other multi-shot techniques, options for measuring higher
order STCs is limited. We have begun investigation of implementing STRIPED-FISH a
wavelength-multiplexed, digital-holographic technique for single-shot spatial and temporal pulse
reconstruction in the nearfield; the logistics and practicality of its role on ZEUS are still under
review. In addition to pulse measurements, continued efforts are underway in pulse modeling. We
plan to implement the spatial-spectral modeling of this work into the pulse amplification modeling
and observe the resulting spectral-radial pulse structure. The intent is this insight could be coupled
and realized as adjustments within the laser compressor to prevent spatial walk-off of the pulse
spectral contributions which manifests itself as pulse front tilt and higher order STCs in the spatial-
temporal domain.
 Finally, it is of interest to perform LWFA experiments and simulations of the role of PFC
in electron acceleration. The use of spatially and temporally structured foci has been of extreme
interest in novel concepts of electron acceleration and radiation generation with techniques such
as flying-focus. These techniques remain difficult to implement, but perhaps the use of pulse
structure garnered from uncompensated PFC coupled to spectral chirp via second order group
delay dispersion (GDD) and third order dispersion (TOD) could lead to favorable acceleration
dynamics. At present, work is underway to implement these pulse profiles in the quasi-cylindrical
particle-in-cell code FBPIC experiments on ZEUS.

Controlled electron injection via wake shaping from co-propagating laser pulses

 As alluded to above, work is ongoing to improve LWFA parameters for both industrial and
scientific applications. Of particular note is the seminal question, “How do you get quality
electrons in a micron-sized bubble moving at nearly the speed of light?”. This question sets the
framework for the injection problem. In using LWFA for quasi-monoenergetic electron
acceleration, this manifests itself in the goal to inject a single electron bunch on-axis into the
leading plasma wave or “bubble” in the nonlinear blowout regime, where electrons then observe a
flat accelerating field along the bunch duration. Over the years, many techniques have been
proposed including self-injection, ionization injection, shock injection, density down-ramp
injection, multi-laser injection, and more. Though some techniques have proven more robust than
others, work continues on the topic.

 Based on previous experiments on the HERCULES laser and observations that
demonstrated two identical co-propagating beams could lower the self-injection threshold, here
we propose a new technique for electron injection utilizing multiple, independent co-propagating
laser pulses. It is recognized that the spatio-temporal pulse structure at focus plays a significant
role in the wakefield evolution and subsequent electron beam dynamics. This is supported by work
which investigated the use of PFT to steer electron beams. Typically, this manipulation of the laser
is unfavorable as it immediately results in the degradation of the pulse parameters (i.e. ,pulse
duration, intensity, transverse profile) and creates complex spatial-spectral phase contributions
which leads to largely unpredictable long term laser evolution and plasma wave dynamics.
Furthermore, such drastic alterations for inducing pulse-shaping effects within the laser chain (as
intentionally misaligning optics) are risky endeavors which may lead to laser hot spots and
eventually damaged optics. To get around these challenges, we have numerically investigated the
use of two co-propagating laser pulses to create a simple ad-hoc spatio-temporal coupling in the
far-field which leads to dynamic shaping of the plasma wave structure. Manipulating these
structures allows for the controlled injection and steering of electrons merely by varying relative
laser pulse delays, intensity, spatial displacement, etc.
 In the scheme, a relatively short, tightly focused, low energy “satellite” pulse is co-
propagated (parallel in the forward direction) alongside a much larger, and more energetic
“driving” laser. The main pulse acts as the driver for the wakefield for the duration of the electron
accelerating phase. The satellite is focused off-axis from this driver and creates a small perturbation
in the initial plasma bubble structure. As the pulses propagate further into the plasma, the
relativistic non-linear plasma response begins to couple the lasers together due to gradients in the
plasma index of refraction50,51. This coupling adds a temporal dependance to the plasma bubble
structure which has been shown to induce electron injection.
 To investigate the plausibility of the scheme, we ran a number of 2D and fully 3D particle-in-
cell (PIC) simulations using the OSIRIS framework. Given the vast number of parameters which
could be varied over the two pulses, most of the results presented here are from 2D simulations
which have a significantly reduced computational load and allow for parameter scanning at
physically relevant numerical resolution.
 The 2D simulations use a primary driving laser pulse of 𝑃=75 TW, gaussian FWHM 𝜏=
31 𝑓𝑠 , gaussian focal spot 𝑤o=12.8 𝜇𝑚, for a normalized vector potential of 𝑎! = 3.67; lying
well within range of high-repetition rate Ti:Sapphire systems. Satellite pulse parameters center on
encompassing the least amount of pulse energy while still creating significant perturbations to the
wakefield; using a pulse with varied power from 𝑃 = 0.3TW → 12 TW in a 5 fs FWHM duration
and focused to 𝑤 = 6.4𝜇𝑚 for a normalized vector potential in the range of 𝑎o = 0.5 → 3.0.
The relative energy between these two pulses is between 0.1% and 3%; within the range of many
laser facilities “probe” beams. The use of the temporally short, tightly focused satellite is multi-
folded. The short temporal nature of the pulse ensures the perturbation is minimal compared to the
large bubble structure and leads to a single injection event. Furthermore, this short duration
corresponds to a low amount of laser energy required and which depletes quickly when compared
to the driver, preventing long term distortion on the driver evolution. In a similar manner the pulse
is tightly focused to achieve high intensities which results in a significant pondermotive push to
electrons on the primary bubble sheath, while subsequently quickly diffracting and leaving the
subsequent electron acceleration to be dominated via nominal driver dynamics and scalings.
 The simulations are executed in a flat-top pre-ionized hydrogen plasma profile at 75% the
self injection threshold (SIT) density. This threshold denotes the point where the temporally and

spatially perfect, single driver pulse no longer induces self-injected electrons from the background
plasma over the duration of the simulation. This constraint yields a slightly “detuned” matching of

subsequently drives an asymmetric plasma wave. The satellite observes the negative transverse
index gradient resulting from the relativistic mass increase and “blown out” region of the driving
pulse, causing it to couple into the driver while shifting the coherent intensity profile centroid

coupling and the ideal wakefield acceleration stage.

satellite pulses to transition between LWFA regimes for the generation of large amounts of betatron

 Further as evident, the parameter space for this scheme is virtually infinite and thus finding

nature observed, the problem is well suited for modern optimization and neural network techniques
which could utilize an ensemble of low-resolution simulations to make informed optimization of
specific parameters. These results may then be feed into high resolution or higher dimensionality
simulations for more physically relevant results.

Transversely pumped acceleration concept
 The key goals of this aspect of our research program are:
(i) to perform systematic numerical modeling of a tranverse pumping dephasingless acceleration
scheme with multiple beams and with proof of principle ZEUS experiments having a traveling
wave.
(ii) to us a tilted phase front traveling wave acceleration scheme with the 3 PW ZEUS laser to test
this scheme.
 Advanced focusing concepts offering spatiotemporal control of a laser’s focal position and
velocity have seen a wide variety of applications. An application of particular interest in high
energy physics involves the use of spatiotemporally controlled lasers to produced a plasma wave
with a phase velocity equal to the speed of light in vacuum, c. This allows for electron acceleration
at higher densities, allowing for a larger accelerating field (since the accelerating fields scale as E
∝ n1/2). This has been investigated in simulations using pulse front tilt and stepped echelons for
electron acceleration and chirped pulses for ion acceleration
 We here propose a new concept of Transversely Pumped Acceleration (TPA), a versatile
accelerator concept that allows for customized tuning of plasma wave phase velocity. The concept
of transversely pumped laser acceleration naturally invites the use of arrays of high repetition rate
fiber laser beams. We demonstrate applications of this proof of principle concept using the 2D
OSIRIS PIC codes
 In one formulation TPA uses multiple arrays of counter-propagating, focused laser
beamlets (alternatively several larger energy beams with tilted phase fronts). The adjacent pulses
within each laser beamlet array are delayed with respect to one another such that the intersection
point of the incoming beamlet arrays on the focal line propagates at a desired velocity. A schematic
of the proposed acceleration concept in shown in Figure 16.
 TPA offers attractive advantages over existing schemes for dephasingless electron
acceleration as well as ion acceleration. Existing dephasingless electron acceleration concepts are
limited when scaling up to obtain electron energies in the TeV range. Using stepped echelons to

impart radial pulse delay onto a laser pulse to be focused by an axiparabola is a way to ensure a
constant wake phase velocity of c. However, in order to reach TeV electron energies, one must
operate the accelerator over several meters, requiring on the order of 10 ns of radial pulse delay.
Another scheme proposes the crossing of two counter-propagating laser pulse with pulse front tilts
to produce an intersection point that travels at c. This concept is limited by the amount of energy
that can be put into the two laser pulses, requiring large and expensive optics to reach high energies.
TPA is not limited by the aforementioned factors and is very straightforward to scale up in length,
and therefore electron energy gained.

Figure 34. Schematic of Transversely Pumped Acceleration scheme in the context of electron
acceleration. Multiple laser beamlet arrays are injected symmetrically onto a line focus in the
center of the plasma column. The direction of particle acceleration is transverse to the direction
of laser beamlet propagation.

 All that must be done is to add more moderate energy laser pulses to the arrays of laser
beamlets. An additional advantage of using many moderate to low energy laser pulses in the TPA
scheme is the ability to operate at a much higher repetition rate, a very desirable trait for research
and medical applications which enables use of machine learning to optimize parameters. Using
many low energy laser beamlets also allows for the use of smaller optics, significantly reducing
cost.
 TPA’s completely transverse geometry also allows for flexibility in the injection of beams
into the accelerating structure. Coherence effects from the incoming lasers in TPA can cause laser
beating which negatively effects the stability of the accelerating structure for both ion and electron
acceleration. In practice, controlling the phase velocity of the plasma wave only requires control
over the injection timing of each set of laser beamlets when the spacing, dx is kept constant.
 For dephasingless electron acceleration, one requires the phase velocity of the plasma wave
to be c. One can use 10 fs pulses such that there is no more overlap of the adjacent laser pulses
due to their short temporal (and therefore spatial) extent. When accelerating electrons with the
TPA scheme, it is important to maintain a quasi-continuous perturbation in the plasma. This is done
by using 50 fs pulses. This allows for a continuous expulsion of electrons from the charge
displacement region in the laser focal volume. If shorter pulses are used, electrons continually leak
into the ”bubble” and the energy spectrum of the electron beam is quite poor. The discrete nature
of the laser beamlets causes regions of lower ponderomotive force that fail to expel electrons.
Cross polarized adjacent laser beamlets are again employed for electron acceleration to avoid
coherence effects.
 In the example case here shown in Figure 34. The a0 of each beamlet is 33.1 (corresponding
to 10 J), The density of the hydrogen plasma is 1019 cm−3. The spacing between laser pulses is
equal to the beam waist and is 3 μm. In 800 μm of acceleration, a peak electron beam energy of 2

GeV is reached, providing promising applications for high energy density physics applications in
the form of TeV colliders. The acceleration gradient in this case is (2.5 TeV/m).
Traditionally, the three primary limitations to electron acceleration are dephasing, depletion, and
defocusing, all of which are bypassed using TPA. Results demonstrated in this work show TPA
could allow for the production of a TeV electron beam in only 40 cm.

 Perhaps the most unique advantage of TPA is the simplicity of scaling the accelerating
structure to longer accelerating distances, one must simply add more laser beamlets. There is no
need for wavefront shaping of a short pulse or precise chirping of a longer laser pulse, both of
which would require very specialized optics that may prove difficult to produce. TPA offers a
straightforward method of tuning plasma wave phase velocity with applications in ion and electron

 TPA could allow for an increase in average power of laser generated particle sources while
simultaneously making them more compact. Low energy lasers can operate at a much higher
repetition rate and average power than high energy systems so it is necessary to combine many
lower energy pulses to increase flux, while maintaining beam quality.
Indeed the basic concept of this scheme can be tested without using many lasers – simply by using
the ZEUS laser beam with a tilted pulse front in a traveling wave scheme (an cylindrical focus).
