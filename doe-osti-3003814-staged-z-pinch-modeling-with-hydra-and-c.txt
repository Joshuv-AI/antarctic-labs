B. Project Scope

This was a collaborative effort between Lawrence Livermore National Security, LLC
(“Contractor”), as manager and operator of Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory
(“LLNL”) and Magneto Inertial Fusion Technologies, Inc. ("MIFTI" or "Participant"), to
develop staged Z-pinch (SZP) modeling with HYDRA and CHICAGO codes.

The contractor performed numerical simulations of the SZP to reproduce and validate Participant’s
MACH2 simulations. Contractor used the state-of-the-art, radiation-magneto-hydrodynamic code
HYDRA, as well as the hybrid particle-in-cell code CHICAGO. This work helped assess the
physical and numerical uncertainties associated with the simulations and the overall fusion
scheme. HYDRA is a well-established and validated code. This work has helped to independently
assess the SZP concept to give confidence to the Participant and to the broader fusion community.

The two tasks, as stated in the original statement of work for the CRADA, are described below.

 Task 1: Simulation Validity Study in HYDRA

 Contractor will perform numerical simulations with the HYDRA code to assess physical and
 numerical uncertainties associated with the simulations and the overall SZP fusion scheme.
 The result of this study will be a comparison with MACH2 simulations run by the Participant
 and with an evaluation of the impact of different numerical choices (e.g., grid resolution) on
 the results.

 Task 2: Extended Simulations in HYDRA and CHICAGO

 Contractor will use more extended physics models, such as the mix model in HYDRA and the
 hybrid-kinetic method in CHICAGO to investigate the impact of including additional physics
 models. For example, Contractor may study interspecies mix between the liner and fusion fuel
 to see if this reduces fusion yield, or the impact of 3D modeling on instability growth. The
 exact topic researched will be informed by the results of Task 1. The results of this task will
 be a report on the impact of the studied physics.

We have completed both tasks with the addition of a no-cost extension of the project. There was
no property acquired during the scope of this work. Analytical scripts were created that were shared
between the Contractor and the Participant.

C. Technical Accomplishments

The technical accomplishments are broken down into the two related tasks.

Task 1: Simulation Validity Study in HYDRA

No radiation case and no ad-hoc energy limits

CRADA TC02390 Page 3

The main purpose of this task was to use the code HYDRA to validate the code MACH2. We were
able to accomplish this to an extent. The codes were validated when using the same equation-of-
state definitions, circuit driver characteristics, and without the use of the radiation transport, and
without the use of ad-hoc temperature/internal energy limits. When radiation transport was used
in both codes, they were not found to agree. Additionally, we found that many of the MACH2
simulations run previously by the Participant used artificial limits on the internal energy in the
code. We did not find these limits to be justified.

Both simulations, HYDRA and MACH2, used a circuit to apply voltage and current to the plasma.
The circuit setup and parameters are shown in Figure 1(a). The voltage, indicated as a circled V,
is applied as a function of time as shown in Figure 1(b). This resulted in a current from the HYDRA
simulation as shown in Figure 1(c), which is found to reach a peak of around 20 MA.

Figure 1: (a) The circuit driver (b) the applied voltage and (c) the resulting current drive used in the
simulations.

The simulations are run in 1D cylindrical geometry. The circuit driver is applied to the simulations
which are initialized with a plasma with the mass density profile shown in Figure 2. The setup has
an inner DT fuel, from R=0 to 2 mm with an initial density of 9.8 mg/cc, surrounded by a silver
liner, from R=2 to 3 mm with an initial density with a 600 mg/cc. All material is initialized to a
temperature of 2 eV.

Figure 2: Radial distribution of initial mass density in the simulations. DT gas compromises R = 0–2 mm,
a silver liner from R = 2–3 mm, and a vacuum region where R > 3 mm.

The simulations were initialized using the same grid resolution. The SESAME equation-of-state
was used in both simulations. To understand the similarity between the two simulations a plot of
the energies in different components was analyzed, as is shown in Figure 3. The energy was
evaluated as the energy in kinetic energy “Ram energy,” and internal energies of the Ag liner and
DT liners. As is shown in the figure, there is reasonable quantitative agreement between the Hydra

and Mach2 simulations. Note that a negative 65 ps offset was applied to the Hydra simulations to
get the timing to agree with Mach2.

The energies in the simulations show the transfer of kinetic energy of the implosion into internal

The spatially and temporally resolved plasma parameters were also investigated, as shown in
Figure 4. He we plot the mass density, ρ, the ion temperature, Ti, total pressure, P, and the magnetic
field in the azimuthal direction, Bθ. Multiple times near stagnation, 128.5 ns, are plotted with both
HYDRA (black) and MACH2 (red) simulations shown. The simulations do not have exactly the
same profiles but are generally in agreement. Note that at some points, for instance around
R = 0.5 mm at 125 ns, the HYDRA simulations have a much hotter temperature. However, the
high temperature occurs at a low density and is found to have little impact on the results.

Based on the reasonable agreement between the HYDRA and MACH2 simulations in both the
total energy partition and the radial profiles, we believe that in the case without the use of the
radiation transport, and without the use of ad-hoc temperature/internal energy limits that the codes
agree.

Including radiation

After the successful comparison of HYDRA and MACH2 without radiation, we turned on the
radiation transport in both codes. HYDRA has many options for radiation transport including
multi-group diffusion and implicit Monte-Carlo. It can also be used in non-thermal equilibrium.
However, MACH2 was limited to single-group diffusion using the thermal equilibrium
assumption, and thus HYDRA was run in this method as well so that the codes were comparing
the same nominal physics.

The mass density as a function of time and radius is shown in Figure 5(a,b) for HYDRA and
MACH2, respectively. Figure 5(c) shows the mass-averaged ion temperature of the fuel as a
function of the liner compression ratio. The two codes show excellent agreement in terms of the
fuel parameters up to around 127 ns as indicated on Figure 5(c).

Figure 5: Mass density as a function of radius and time for (a) HYDRA and (b) MACH2 simulations. The
solid cyan and dashed white curves shows the fuel-liner and liner-vacuum interfaces, respectively. The
uppercase letters (A, B, etc.) point out different times of interested during the compression. Panel c shows
the mass-averaged ion temperature in the fuel as a function of compression ratio. The letters correspond
to the same times as on panels a and b. Dashed line corresponds to an adiabatic index of 4/3.

Comparing the liner and fuel radii as a function of time in Figure 6(a) we see that the liner outer
radius begins to deviate at a slightly earlier time, around 120 ns. Also, looking at the mass-
averaged ion temperature of the fuel, in Figure 6(b), we find peak temperatures of around 1 keV
for HYDRA and 2.5 keV for MACH2. This is a relatively large disagreement.

To better understand the reason for the discrepancy between the two codes, MACH2 was compared
in cases with and without radiation. This comparison is plotted in Figure 7, where the case with
radiation is shown as solid curves and the no radiation case is shown in dashes. As with all
MACH2 simulation in this work, both simulations were set up and run by the MIFTI team; they
were analyzed by LLNL.

 Figure 7: Comparison of energies in MACH2 simulations as a function of time, with (solid curves) and
 without (dashed curves) radiation physics turned on in the code.

What we found was that there is more energy in the case with radiation included. This added a
total of 700 kJ to the total energy, 500 kJ of this was into the internal energy of the liner and 50 kJ
from internal energy of the fuel. Radiation should reduce the total energy in the system. It should
reduce the internal energy of the liner. These odd results caused us to question the implementation
of radiation in these particular MACH2 simulations. We do not know if this was an issue with the
input deck or with the code itself, however for this work we could not provide adequate agreement
between HYDRA and MACH2 in the case with radiation on. This increased energy in the system
is consistent with the higher ion temperature seen in MACH2 compared to HYDRA. Thus, this
issue with radiation is likely as important factor in that discrepancy.

Ad-hoc internal energy caps

An important finding during our work was the impact of ad-hoc internal energy caps used in the
line on the liner in MACH2. The MIFTI team started using these caps as way to prevent high-
temperature spikes in low density regions outside of the main implosion. However, when looking
at the comparison between HYDRA and MACH2, we found very large impacts of these caps on
the neutron yield. Essentially, by limiting the internal energy of the liner, the compressibility of
the liner increases, and it is possible to achieve unphysically large compression ratios. Previous
work by MIFTI used such caps in their simulations, and thus the compression ratios and yields
achieved in those simulations are doubtful. Another rationale for the use of caps is to limit the
heating of the liner due to reduction of heat transport caused by the higher magnetic fields.
However, we also investigated turning off heat conduction completely, even this dramatic change
did not have the same impact as adding energy caps. Our understanding is that magnetic flux
limited heat transport is now available in MACH2, and this is a preferable solution to using ad-
hoc energy caps.

In Figure 6(a) we show plots of neutron yield as a function of time for different values of the liner
internal energy cap, in Figure 6(b) we show the total yield as a function of the cap. We find that
by imposing a cap on the internal energy, the yield is significantly increased from 4.3 MJ at a cap
of 500e6 to 11 MJ at 6e6. The fact that a cap at 5e6 causes a lower yield (1.6 MJ) may be due to
numerical instabilities, because it was not possible to run with a lower cap than 5e6. We stress that
the rationale behind using ad-hoc internal energy caps is not clear. Thus, while this is an interesting
numerical study, we do not see any reason that such cap should be utilized as a reflection of reality.

Figure 8: (a) Yield as a function of time in MACH2 simulations for different levels of internal energy caps
 as described in the legend. (b) Yield as a function of internal energy cap in the liner.

Summary

To summarize this task, we have found agreement between the HYDRA and MACH2 codes for
staged Z-pinch implosions in the case without radiation and without ad-hoc limits to the liner
internal energy. If radiation transport is utilized in MACH2 we find that there are inconsistencies
in the energy that do not seem to be physically appropriate. We found that applying internal energy

caps on the liner had a large effect on the simulations, but we do not believe this to be a physically
realistic manner to use the code.

Task 2: Extended Simulations in HYDRA and CHICAGO

Liner material scan

Simulations were also run to determine the impact of different liner materials on the effectiveness
of the implosion. These simulations used a different setup, instead of a solid liner, a gas puff
geometry was utilized. In this study we varied the material of the liner while keeping the mass
density of the liner constant. The neutron yield as a function of the liner atomic number is plotted
in Figure 7. Here there is a strong dependence of the neutron yield with the liner material. The
higher atomic numbers perform better. We found that this was due to the increased compressibility
of the liner due to both the ability of the liner to store more internal energy and the ability of the
liner to radiate more at higher atomic numbers,

Alpha particle deposition modeling

We also investigated the kinetic behavior of alpha deposition using the particle-in-cell (PIC)
simulation code Chicago. By using the PIC method, we can follow the alpha particles individually
as they travel through the fuel and liner material. This is important as it allows us to understand
the impact of both Coulomb scattering and the particle deflection via the magnetic field.

For this simulation study, we began with the parameters of a staged Z-pinch simulation at the point
of stagnation. These plasma profiles (density, temperature, magnetic field) were transferred to the
PIC simulations. Figure 8(a,b,c) shows the electron density, magnetic field and electron
temperature profiles used in the PIC simulations, respectively. We note that the simulation used in
this case was taken from a run with a temperature cap on the liner. This is the reason for the
unphysically low electron temperature in the liner.

CRADA TC02390 Page 10

Figure 10: (a) Electron density, (b) magnetic field and (c) electron temperature profiles extracted at peak
 compression in HYDRA and used for the Chicago PIC simulations.

Alpha particles are injected into the simulations with a kinematically-accurate energy spectrum
based on the ion temperature from the HYDRA simulations. The initial alpha density profile is
shown in Figure 9(a). To study the effects of electronic stopping and magnetic field inhibition, we
ran three different simulations. The first simulation investigated the alpha transport with the
nominal plasma parameters. As shown in Figure 9(b), most alpha particles are confined to within
the fuel; and 73% of the initial alpha energy is depositing in the fuel. In the second case, the
magnetic field was removed from the simulation to identify its role in suppression of alpha
transport. By removing the magnetic field, as shown in Figure 9(c), we find that the alpha particles
are no longer confined within the liner; only 62% of the initial alpha energy is depositing in the
fuel. This shows the importance of the field on keeping the alpha particles within the fuel. A similar
result was found in a simulation without a liner or a magnetic field in which 60% of the alpha
energy was deposited into the fuel. The remaining alpha energy was carried out of the simulations
boundaries with the escaping particles. In the final case, the liner material was removed to
investigate the role of scattering from the liner. In this case, shown in Figure 9(d), many of the
alpha particles are still trapped within the fuel and 100% of the initial alpha energy is deposited in
the fuel. The reason that this number is higher than the nominal case, because no collisional energy
deposition could occur in the liner and the magnetic field was sufficiently strong to confine the
alphas in the fuel volume.

 Figure 11: Alpha particle densities at (a) initialization of the simulation and after 50 ps for the (a)
 nominal case, (b) the case without a magnetic field and (c) the case without a liner.

The PIC simulations show the importance of the magnetic field in achieving alpha heating an
confinement within the fuel. This conclusion may differ depending on the implosion parameters,
especially the fuel density and strength of the magnetic field along the liner-fuel interface.

D. Expected Economic Impact

The work in this project has helped to increase the effectiveness of modeling the staged Z-pinch
(SZP) concept, which is the core technology of MIFTI. Improvements to the modeling of the SZP
will improve design process for MIFTI and enable them to execute better experiments. These
improvements will better position MIFTI to engage with investors and raise capital to improve
continue to grow the company and improve their scientific foundation.

E. Participant Contribution

The participant was given access to Livermore Computing (LC) High Performance Computing
(HPC) systems. The participant used this access to run HYDRA themselves. This allowed the
research to proceed more rapidly with multiple users investigating different implosion scenarios.
Additionally, the participants ran all MACH2 simulations using their own company computing
resources. This allowed for cross-comparison between HYDRA and MACH2 simulations.
