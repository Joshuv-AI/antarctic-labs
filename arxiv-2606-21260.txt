Subsampling for supervised learning in reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces
Eyal Vayness & Maxime Sangnier

Abstract
In the era of big data, subsampling became a common practice in statistical learning. By selecting a subgroup of individuals based on which the learner is trained, subsampling aims at reducing the computational cost and time of the estimation step, and ideally leads to a decrease of its energy consumption and carbon footprint. This work focuses on a nonparametric setting, in which the hypotheses set lies in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space, and the estimator is a minimizer of an empirical risk reweighted à la Horvitz-Thompson. By studying the asymptotic properties of this estimator, we reveal an optimal subsampling scheme (regarding the trace of the covariance operator) and show that it can be used via plug-in. A numerical study on synthetic and real-world datasets shows the practicability and the benefit of the proposed approach.

Introduction

As of the last couple decades, the overwhelming data accumulation in various domains such as medical research, road safety, bioinformatics, physics, genetics and social media, led scientists to rethink standard statistical learning methods.
Beyond the computational barrier (in time and memory) induced by the data deluge and the economic cost of training models on large datasets, the overconsumption of energy resources also became a concerning topic for the scientific community.
With this in mind, it is of interest to reduce the computational cost of learning methods, particularly regarding their training phase, which often involves minimizing an objective function and is more demanding than inference.

A first thought to limit the computational burden of training procedures, is to leverage more efficient optimization algorithms, such as active set strategies
(
24
)
, stochastic gradient methods
(
3
;
7
)
and random coordinate descents
(
39
;
14
)
, to cite only a few.
However, these approaches still work on the whole training set, which may include either redundant or quite uninformative observations.
Data set reduction methods can then be used in order to reduce the effective size of the dataset, while producing only informative datapoints.
Sketching, for instance, is a way to reduce the dataset by generating new individuals, which are linear combinations of the original ones
(
30
;
32
;
47
)
.
Another approach is the coreset technique, which randomly pick points from de dataset in order to uniformly approximate the objective function
(
22
;
29
;
25
;
35
;
5
)
.
Nonuniform subsampling of points can also be implemented thanks to their leverage scores
(
28
;
9
;
49
;
27
)
, which are based on the covariates but not on the target values.
Stepping back, subsampling can also be analyzed for general inclusion probabilities, from the excess risk point of view
(
8
;
18
)

and the estimator asymptotic distribution side
(
44
;
46
;
19
)
.
In the latter case, it is even possible to exhibit an optimal subsampling scheme, which minimizes the trace of the limit covariance matrix.
Several other approaches exist, such as sampling with influence functions
(
41
)
and leveraging minimum enclosing ball from computational geometry
(
42
)
.

At this step, we remark that many methods, such as stochastic optimization and sketching, are task-agnostic, which leaves an area of improvement.
Moreover, if coreset techniques propose data-dependent sampling procedures, these ones are aimed at uniform approximation the objective function and consequently often leverage intractable probabilities
(
29
;
5
)
.
On the contrary, optimal subsampling proposes an easy-to-implement way to extract informative individuals from the original dataset, such that the trace of the asymptotic covariance matrix is minimal
(
46
)
.

In this work, we focus on nonparametric supervised learning, and in particular on kernel methods, where the hypotheses lie in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS)
(
2
)
.
In this case, few of the methods described above can be used (they have been mainly designed for parametric inference), the most notable being sketching
(
50
;
12
;
11
)
and specific approaches tailored for support vector machines, such as minimum enclosing balls
(
42
)
and stochastic subgradient algorithm
(
38
)
.
However, kernel learning can rely on kernel approximation techniques to lighten the numerical training cost,
which consists in replacing either the Gram matrix by a low-rank approximation (for instance, thanks to the Nyström method
(
48
;
10
;
33
)
), or the feature map by a finite-dimensional mapping (thanks to random Fourier features (RFF)
(
31
;
4
;
34
;
26
)
).
Since the computational cost of a kernel method is mainly function of the size of the optimization problem to solve (which is basically represented by the spatial complexity of the design or Gram matrix of the effective dataset),
the appeal of the Nyström method, RFF and sketching is to cut down the computational burden from
O(n^{2})
(for an initial dataset of size
n
) to
O(nm)
, where
m
an approximation parameter (the dimension of the subspace for the Nyström method, the number of random projections for RFF, the number of linear combinations for the sketching approach).
If this is a big gain, we remark that the computational cost still depends on the size
n
of the original dataset, which may be very large in practice.
On the contrary, subsampling
s
datapoints would lead to a
O(s^{2})
cost, which is largely less.

We thus aim at designing a data-dependent subsampling procedure for nonparametric supervised learning in RKHSs.
From the methodological side, we follow the line of research initiated by
44
(then followed by numerous papers including
(
45
;
46
;
19
;
40
)
), which determines the subsampling scheme minimizing the trace of the asymptotic covariance matrix of a M-estimator of an empirical risk reweighted à la Horvitz-Thompson.
From the theoretical side, we study asymptotics in RKHSs with tools coming from functional random probability spaces
(
6
;
21
)
with pieces of elements from martingale theory
(
17
)
.
These two aspects distinguish our work from that by
46
since we place ourself in a particular nonparametric setting and employ different techniques of proof.
The latter are also different from the empirical process techniques used by
16
;
36
for analyzing usual empirical risk minimizers (ERMs).

In
Section

2
, we first revisit asymptotic normality of the ERM in RKHSs, based on the asymptotically finite-dimensional property
(
21
;
43
)
.
This result corroborates the ones presented in
(
16
;
36
)
and comes with a complementary technique of proof.
Then, in
Section

3
, the subsampled risk minimizer (SRM) is presented as well as its convergence and asymptotic distribution.

Section

4
exhibits the optimal subsampling scheme and
Section

5
presents a way to implement it in practice.
Le latter strategy is numerically evaluated on synthetic and real-world datasets in
Section

6
, and favorably compared to state-of-the-art approximation techniques for kernel methods.

Background

Let
Z\in\mathcal{Z}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
be the random variable of interest with unknown distribution.
We focus here on supervised learning and assume that
Z
has a canonical decomposition:
Z=(X,Y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
where
X
is the vector of covariates and
Y
is the response.
Denote
\ell:\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
the loss function associated to the learning task of interest,
and assume that
\ell
is non-negative, convex and twice-differentiable in its first argument.
The setting defined this way suits both binary classification:
\mathcal{Y}=\{\pm 1\}
and for instance
\ell(\hat{y},y)=\log(1+\exp(-y\hat{y}))
for logistic regression (where
\hat{y}\in\mathbb{R}
is a prediction compared to
y\in\mathcal{Y}
);
as well as regression:
\mathcal{Y}=\mathbb{R}
and for example
\ell(\hat{y},y)=\frac{1}{p}|\hat{y}-y|^{p}
for
L^{p}
regression.

The working set of predictors is
\mathbb{H}
, the RKHS induced by some kernel
k:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
(i.e. a symmetric and positive definite function).
In other words,
\mathbb{H}
is the closure of the span of the map by
k
of elements of
\mathcal{X}
, i.e.

\mathbb{H}\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}\overline{\spn\{K_{x}:x\in\mathcal{X}\}},

where
K_{x}=k(x,\cdot)
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
.

Given the previous elements, the population risk function of the learning task considered is:

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:true_{l}oss}{L}:h\in\mathbb{H}\mapsto\mathbb{E}\left[\ell(h(X),Y)\right],

(1)

which could be minimized so as to obtain an optimal predictor.
However two problems arise:
first, the distribution of
Z=(X,Y)
is assumed unknown (such that the expectation in (
1
) cannot be computed);
second, the current assumptions do not ensure existence of a minimizer of
L
in
\mathbb{H}
.
To solve those problems, we turn towards the empirical version of (
1
):

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:empirical_{l}oss}{L}_{n}(h)\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(h(X_{i}),Y_{i}),

(2)

based on a dataset
{\mathcal{D}_{n}}=\{Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}\}=\{(X_{1},Y_{1}),\dots,(X_{n},Y_{n})\}
made of independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables sharing the same distribution as
Z=(X,Y)
,
and add a ridge penalization tuned by some parameter
{\lambda_{0}}>0
for the true risk and by
{\lambda_{n}}>0
(a possibly random parameter) for the empirical one.
The objective functions, namely the true and empirical regularized risks now read:

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:true_{r}isk}{\mathcal{R}}(h)\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}{L}(h)+\frac{{\lambda_{0}}}{2}\|h\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2},

(3)

and

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:empirical_{r}isk}{\mathcal{R}}_{n}(h)\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}{L}_{n}(h)+\frac{{\lambda_{n}}}{2}\|h\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}.

(4)

Let us remark that minimizing
{\mathcal{R}}
(respectively
{\mathcal{R}}_{n}
) is equivalent to minimizing
L
(respectively
L_{n}
) inside a ball of
\mathbb{H}
, the radius of which depending both on the penalization parameter
{\lambda_{0}}
(respectively
{\lambda_{n}}
) and the data (see for example
(
36
)
).
In addition, the framework above offers the chance to take
{\lambda_{n}}\neq{\lambda_{0}}
, which makes sens in practice since penalizing is also a way to prevent overfitting, on the condition that the penalization parameter
{\lambda_{n}}
is tuned with respect to the sample size
n
and the data (roughly speaking, the less the data, the stronger the penalization).

Now, by convexity of the loss
\ell
(with respect to its first argument) and
{\lambda_{0}}>0
, the true regularized risk
{\mathcal{R}}
is lower semi-continuous and strongly convex, and admits a unique minimizer in
\mathbb{H}
, denoted
{h_{0}}

(
6
, Lemma 2.15)
.
The same holds true for the empirical regularized risk
{\mathcal{R}}_{n}
(since
{\lambda_{n}}>0
), the minimizer of which is denoted
{\hat{h}_{n}}\in\mathbb{H}
.

2.1
Assumptions

Before introducing the proposed subsampling estimator, we aim at presenting the asymptotic distribution of
{\hat{h}_{n}}
as a stepping stone to our main result.
This requires a few assumptions, which will be needed all along our analysis.

Assumption 1
.

The kernel
k:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is continuous and bounded: there exists
\kappa>0
such that
\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sqrt{k(x,x)}\leq\kappa
.

Assumption

1
is standard in the analysis of estimators lying in RKHSs (see for instance
(
1
)
).
In particular, the continuity of
k
(jointly with
\mathcal{X}
being separable) leads the RKHS
\mathbb{H}
to be separable
(
6
, Lemma 4.33)
.
This means that there exists a complete orthonormal system
(e_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\mathbb{H}
(i.e.
\|e_{j}\|_{\mathbb{H}}=1
and
\langle e_{j},e_{k}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}=0
for all
j,k\in\mathbb{N}
,
k\neq j
),
which will enable to define the trace of a linear operator in
\mathbb{H}
in
Section

3
.
The bound
\kappa
, as for it, leads to bounded predictors (
\sup_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}|h(\bm{x})|<\infty
, for all
h\in\mathbb{H}
)
(
6
, Lemma 4.28)
,
which helps in obtaining convergence of empirical quantities (see
Appendix

A
).

Henceforth, let
\ell^{\prime}
and
\ell^{\prime\prime}
be respectively the first and second partial derivatives of
\ell
with respect to its first variable.

Assumption 2
.

For all
y\in\mathcal{Y}
and
B>0
, the loss
\ell
and its derivatives,
\ell^{\prime}
and
\ell^{\prime\prime}
, are
\phi_{B}(y)
-Lipschitz in their first variable on
[-B,B]
, i.e. for
k\in\{0,1,2\}
:

|\ell^{(k)}(\hat{y}_{1},y)-\ell^{(k)}(\hat{y}_{2},y)|\leq\phi_{B}(y)|\hat{y}_{1}-\hat{y}_{2}|,

for all
\hat{y}_{1},\hat{y}_{2}\in[-B,B]
.

Assumption 3
.

For all
B>0
and
k\in\{0,1,2\}
,
\mathbb{E}\left[\phi_{B}(Y)^{4}\right]
and
\mathbb{E}\left[\ell^{(k)}(0,Y)^{4}\right]
exists and are finite.

Assumption

2
asks for
\ell
and its derivatives to be locally Lipschitz continuous in their first argument.
For simplicity, we consider the same Lipschitz coefficient
\phi_{B}(\cdot)
for all derivatives.
In particular, if
\ell
is thrice-differentiable, then one may choose, for all
y\in\mathcal{Y}
and
B>0
,
\phi_{B}(y)=\max_{k\in\{1,2,3\}}\sup_{t\in[-B,B]}\ell^{(k)}(t,y)
.

Assumption

3
as for it, is a common moment assumption in asymptotic analyses, except that it is required up to the degree
4
in order to handle the Horvitz-Thompson reweighting introduced in
Section

3
.
In addition,
Assumptions

2
and
3
(with
Assumption

1
) yields that
\ell^{\prime}({h_{0}}(X),Y)K_{X}
and
\ell^{\prime\prime}({h_{0}}(X),Y)K_{X}
have a finite fourth order moment.

At last,
Assumption

4
asks for the convergence of the regularization parameter
{\lambda_{n}}
towards
{\lambda_{0}}
at a rate greater than
1/\sqrt{n}
, which is basically that of
L_{n}(h)
towards
L(h)
, for any
h\in\mathbb{H}
.

Assumption 4
.

The (random) regularization parameter sequence
({\lambda_{n}})_{n>0}
verifies that
{\lambda_{n}}>0
almost surely for all
n>0
and

\sqrt{n}({\lambda_{n}}-{\lambda_{0}})\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}}0.

2.2
Asymptotic normality of ERM

With the assumptions stated above, our first step is to derive gradients of the regularized risks.
Indeed, since
\ell\in\mathscr{C}^{2}
, the empirical regularized risk
{\mathcal{R}}_{n}
is twice-differentiable
and its minimizer
{\hat{h}_{n}}
verifies the following stationarity condition:

\nabla{\mathcal{R}}_{n}({\hat{h}_{n}})=0\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad{\lambda_{n}}{\hat{h}_{n}}=-\nabla{L}_{n}({\hat{h}_{n}}),

(5)

with

\nabla{L}_{n}(h)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell^{\prime}(h(X_{i}),Y_{i})K_{X_{i}},

(6)

for all
h\in\mathbb{H}
.
In addition, letting “
\otimes
” being the outer product in
\mathbb{H}

(i.e. for all
h,f\in\mathbb{H}
,

(h\otimes f)
is a linear operator from
\mathbb{H}
to
\mathbb{H}
such that,
for all
g\in\mathbb{H}
,
(h\otimes f)g\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}\langle f,g\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}h
)
one may also compute the Hessian operators of the empirical risks:

\nabla^{2}{L}_{n}(h)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell^{\prime\prime}(h(X_{i}),Y_{i})K_{X_{i}}\otimes K_{X_{i}}\quad\text{and}\quad\nabla^{2}{\mathcal{R}}_{n}(h)=\nabla^{2}{L}_{n}(h)+{\lambda_{n}}\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{H}},

(7)

where
\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{H}}
is the identity operator in
\mathbb{H}
.

The same applies to the true regularized risk
{\mathcal{R}}
:
according to
Lemma

A.1
, under
Assumptions

1
,
2
and
3
,
{\mathcal{R}}
is twice differentiable at
{h_{0}}
, with:

\nabla{\mathcal{R}}({h_{0}})=\nabla{L}({h_{0}})+{\lambda_{0}}{h_{0}}\quad\text{and}\quad\nabla^{2}{\mathcal{R}}({h_{0}})=\nabla^{2}{L}({h_{0}})+{\lambda_{0}}\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{H}},

where

\nabla{L}({h_{0}})=\mathbb{E}\left[\ell^{\prime}({h_{0}}(X),Y)K_{X}\right]\quad\text{and}\quad\nabla^{2}{L}({h_{0}})=\mathbb{E}\left[\ell^{\prime\prime}({h_{0}}(X),Y)K_{X}\otimes K_{X}\right].

Let us remark that
\nabla^{2}{\mathcal{R}}
is continuous at
{h_{0}}
and that
\nabla^{2}{\mathcal{R}}({h_{0}})
is invertible
(
16
, Lemma A.5)
.

We are now ready to state asymptotic normality of
{\hat{h}_{n}}
.
For this purpose, let us recall that we say that
H
is a Gaussian process in
\mathbb{H}
with mean
0
and covariance operator
\Sigma:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
, denoted
H\sim\mathcal{G}(0,\Sigma)
,
if for any positive integer
m
and
h_{1},\dots,h_{m}\in\mathbb{H}
,
(\langle H,h_{1}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}},\dots,\langle H,h_{m}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}})^{\top}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,(\langle\Sigma h_{i},h_{j}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}})_{1\leq i,j\leq m})
.

Theorem 2.1

(Asymptotic normality of
{\hat{h}_{n}}
)
.

Under
Assumptions

1
,
2
,
3
and
4
, one has

\sqrt{n}\nabla^{2}{\mathcal{R}}({h_{0}})({\hat{h}_{n}}-{h_{0}})\overset{\mathrm{d}}{\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{G}(0,\Sigma),

with
\Sigma\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}\mathbb{E}\left[\ell^{\prime}({h_{0}}(X),Y)^{2}K_{X}\otimes K_{X}\right]-\nabla{L}({h_{0}})\otimes\nabla{L}({h_{0}})
.

The proof of
Theorem

2.1
(see
Section

A.2
) is close to that in a parametric model, thanks to a Cramér-Wold-type result.
The latter holds for, given assumptions on the considered RKHS
\mathbb{H}
, random sequences are asymptotically finite-dimensional in the sense of
(
43
, Chapter 1.8)
or
(
21
, Theorem 7.7.5)
.

Asymptotic properties of the ERM in the non-parametric framework of RKHSs are known.
Main results include consistency
(
6
)
,
outlining of a limit Gaussian process
(
16
)
,
and asymptotic normality in a ball of
\mathbb{H}

(
36
)
.

Theorem

2.1
is similar to a result by
16
;
15
,
but the proofs differ since
16
;

make use of a functional delta method on the empirical process.

Theorem

3
Asymptotics of subsampling

We now introduce, in this section, the two considered subsampling strategies (with and without replacement),
along with the corresponding estimators, nicknamed SRM in both cases.
We then prove asymptotic normality of both estimators with general subsampling schemes,
and discuss in the next section the choice of the latter.

Let
\Delta_{n-1}=\{\bm{x}\in(0,1)^{n},\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}=1\}
be the set of probability vectors of size
n
,
and consider
{\bm{\pi}}_{n}\in\Delta_{n-1}
a subsampling probability (SSP) vector, which may ultimately depend on the dataset
{\mathcal{D}_{n}}
.
A subsampling scheme of size
s_{n}\leq n
consists in randomly picking points
Z_{i}
in the dataset
{\mathcal{D}_{n}}
with inclusion probabilities
\pi_{n,i}
.
More formally, we consider, in the context of this paper, the two following subsampling strategies: one with replacement and another without.

Subsampling with replacement

In subsampling
with
replacement (also called Multinomial subsampling),
one draws independently
s_{n}
point indices:

I_{n,1},\dots,I_{n,s_{n}}|{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\overset{{{}}{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\pi_{n,i}\delta_{\{i\}},

where
\delta_{\{i\}}
is the Dirac measure at
i
.
Since the choice is with replacement, each point
Z_{i}
appears
\gamma_{n,i}=\sum_{j=1}^{s_{n}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{I_{n,j}=i\}}
times in the subsampled dataset,
with
\gamma_{n,i}
being referred to as the multiplicity or the number of occurrences of
Z_{i}
(which may be null if
Z_{i}
is not picked).
In this situation

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline qu:gamma_{m}ultinomial}(\gamma_{n,1},\dots,\gamma_{n,n})|{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\sim\text{Mul}(s_{n},{\bm{\pi}}_{n}),

(8)

(Multinomial distribution with parameters
s_{n}
and
{\bm{\pi}}_{n}
)
and the subsample size (counting multiplicities) is equal to
s_{n}
:
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{n,i}=s_{n}
almost surely.

Subsampling without replacement

In subsampling
without
replacement (or Poisson subsampling),
ones draws independently for each point
Z_{i}
:

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline qu:gamma_{p}oisson}\gamma_{n,i}|{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}\text{Ber}(s_{n}\pi_{n,i}),

(9)

(Bernoulli distribution with parameter
s_{n}\pi_{n,i}
)
and decides that it should enter the subsampled dataset when the multiplicity (or inclusion indicator) verifies
\gamma_{n,i}=1
.
This is possible assuming that
\pi_{n,i}\leq s_{n}^{-1}
for all
n>0
and
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,
and in this situation, the subsample size is in average equal to
s_{n}
:
\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{n,i}|{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\right]=s_{n}
.

In both situation, the subsampling schemes can be summarized as incorporating the
i
-th observation
Z_{i}
,
\gamma_{n,i}
times to the subsampled dataset, where
\gamma_{n,i}\in\mathbb{N}
is a random variable such that
\mathbb{E}\left[\gamma_{n,i}\middle|{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\right]=s_{n}\pi_{n,i}
.
The subsamples are then the
Z_{i}
s such that
\gamma_{n,i}>0
, for
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,
and we define the subsampled and regularized risks as follows:

\displaystyle L^{*}_{s_{n}}(h)\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\ell(h(X_{i}),Y_{i})}{s_{n}\pi_{n,i}}\gamma_{n,i},\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:subsample_{l}oss}

(10)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}^{*}_{s_{n}}(h)\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}L^{*}_{s_{n}}(h)+\frac{{\lambda_{s_{n}}^{*}}}{2}\|h\|^{2}_{\mathbb{H}},\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:subsample_{r}isk}

(11)

where
{\lambda_{s_{n}}^{*}}>0
is a (possibly random) regularization parameter.
Let also
{h^{*}_{s_{n}}}
be the minimizer of
{\mathcal{R}}^{*}_{s_{n}}
.
By Fermat’s rule, one has:

-{\lambda_{s_{n}}^{*}}{h^{*}_{s_{n}}}=\nabla{L}^{*}_{s_{n}}({h^{*}_{s_{n}}}).

(12)

The inverse probability weighting à la Horvitz-Thompson of
Equation

10
is a mean to produce unbiased estimations of the empirical and true risks (
Equations

2
and
1
):

\mathbb{E}\left[{L}^{*}_{s_{n}}(h)\middle|{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\right]={L}_{n}(h)
and
\mathbb{E}\left[{L}^{*}_{s_{n}}(h)\right]={L}(h)
.
This is a critical point in order to get limit results, such as consistency and asymptotic normality.
However, this is not enough.
First, the regularization parameter
{\lambda_{s_{n}}^{*}}
has to converge to
{\lambda_{0}}
(for consistency),
and with a rate at least
1/\sqrt{s_{n}}
, for asymptotic normality.
This is formalized by
’
‣
Section

3
below.

Assumption
4
’
.

The (random) regularization parameter sequence
(\lambda^{*}_{s_{n}})_{n>0}
verifies that
{\lambda_{s_{n}}^{*}}>0
almost surely for all
n>0
and

\sqrt{s_{n}}({\lambda_{s_{n}}^{*}}-{\lambda_{0}})\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}}0.

Next, the SSPs
\pi_{n,i}
should not converge too quickly to
0
, in order for the subsampled risk (
10
) not to explode.
This is described in
Assumption

5
, which states that SSPs have to be asymptotically of the order
1/n
.

Assumption 5
.

The probability vector sequence
({\bm{\pi}}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies

\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\left\{\frac{1}{n\pi_{n,i}}\right\}^{2}\right]=O(1).

Remark
.

The intuition that SSPs
\pi_{n,i}
cannot converge to
0
faster than
1/n
is not surprising,
and can also be read in previous works.

4
Variance reduction

Since the role of subsampling is to reduce the dataset size by randomly picking points, it is of interest to choose these few points in order to maximize the SRM estimator precision.
Once the limit ratio
c=\lim_{n\to\infty}s_{n}/n
has been chosen (see
Theorems

3.2
and
3.3
), it is about
minimizing
the asymptotic covariance operator.
The most common way to do it is to reduce the covariance information to a scalar, and to minimize the latter.
Several manners of reducing the covariance information exist and are well documented in the literature of optimal design (see for instance
(
23
)
).
In this study, we follow the work by
46
and focus on L-optimality, which consists in computing the trace of the covariance, preconditioned by a well-chosen operator (here
(\nabla^{2}R(h_{0}))^{-1}
, which exists according to
(
16
, Lemma A.5)
).
This choice is consistent with the dependence of the covariance operator to
\psi
and is computationally advantageous.

In our infinite dimensional framework based on RKHSs, the trace of a self-adjoint bounded linear map does not differ much from the finite dimensional case.
For such an operator
\Lambda:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
, it can be defined as
\Tr(\Lambda)\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\langle\Lambda e_{j},e_{j}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}
,
and, in the particular case where
\Lambda
is the outer product of two vectors, i.e.
\Lambda=f\otimes h
(with
f,h\in\mathbb{H}
), then
\Tr(\Lambda)=\langle f,h\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}

(see
(
21
)
for more details).

Going back to
Theorems

3.3
and
3.2
, let
\Sigma^{*}(\psi)
be either
\Sigma^{*}_{\mathrm{M}}(\psi)
or
\Sigma^{*}_{\mathrm{P},c}(\psi)
, depending on the subsampling strategy.
In both cases, L-optimality boils down to minimizing

\Tr(\Sigma^{*}(\psi))=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\psi(Z)\right]}{\psi(Z)}\ell^{\prime}({h_{0}}(X),Y)^{2}k(X,X)\right]+C,

with respect to
\psi
, where
C
is a constant term.

Proposition

4.1
gives a closed-form expression of such an L-optimal absolute interest map
\psi
.

Remark
.

Since the two subsampling strategies considered here lead to the same convergence rate of the estimator
{h^{*}_{s_{n}}}
(depending on the value of the limit ratio
c=\lim_{n\to\infty}s_{n}/n
),
they can be compared to each other based on L-optimality.
For this purpose, consider a fixed absolute interest function
\psi
and let

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}\Tr(\Sigma^{*}_{\mathrm{M}}(\psi))-\Tr(\Sigma^{*}_{\mathrm{P},c}(\psi))=c\mathbb{E}\left[\ell^{\prime}({h_{0}}(X),Y)^{2}k(X,X)\right]-\|\nabla L({h_{0}})\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}.

Whenever
c=0
(i.e.
s_{n}=o(n)
), one has
\Delta<0
, hence Multinomial subsampling surpasses Poisson subsampling.
On the contrary, when
c=1
, one has
\Delta=\Tr(\Sigma)>0
, thus Poisson subsampling is superior to Multinomial subsampling.

It results that the choice between the two strategies depends on the limit ratio
c
, which is a quantity defined by the practitioner:
if
c
is small, Multinomial subsampling is preferable to Poisson subsampling, while the converse holds when
c
is close to
1
.
Let us remark that this conclusion is slightly different from the parametric case studied in
(
46
)
, which concludes that both subsampling schemes have the same asymptotically estimation efficiency when
c=0
.
This is due to the presence of a quadratic regularization in our setting, which does not exist in the parametric framework.

Let us now investigate some examples of supervised learning settings,
which illustrate the shape of the L-optimal absolute interest function
\psi^{\star}
, revealed in

For this purpose and for simplicity, we consider a radial basis function kernel

, implying that for all
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}
,

is constant.

Example 1

(Logistic regression)
.

In the classification setting of penalized kernel logistic regression, one has,
for
y\in\{-1,1\}
and
\hat{y}\in\mathbb{R}
:

\ell(\hat{y},y)=\log(1+\exp(-y\hat{y}))\quad\text{and}\quad\ell^{\prime}(\hat{y},y)=-y\sigma(-y\hat{y}),

where
\sigma:t\mapsto 1/(1+e^{-t})
is the sigmoid function.
The optimal SSPs are then proportional to

|\ell^{\prime}({h_{0}}(\bm{x}),y)|=\sigma(-y{h_{0}}(\bm{x})),\quad(\bm{x},y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}.

Hence a datapoint
Z_{i}=(X_{i},Y_{i})
has a high probability of being picked if
Y_{i}{h_{0}}(X_{i})\ll 0
,
i.e. if
X_{i}
is wrongly classified by the true function
{h_{0}}
(
{h_{0}}(X_{i})
and
Y_{i}
have different signs) and with a high confidence (
|{h_{0}}(X_{i})|
is large).

Example 2

(
L^{p}
regression)
.

In the
L^{p}
(with
p\geq 1
) kernel ridge regression setting, one has,
for
y,\hat{y}\in\mathbb{R}
:

\ell(\hat{y},y)=\frac{1}{p}|\hat{y}-y|^{p}\quad\text{and}\quad\ell^{\prime}(\hat{y},y)=\sign(\hat{y}-y)|\hat{y}-y|^{p-1}.

The optimal SSPs are thus proportional to

|\ell^{\prime}({h_{0}}(\bm{x}),y)|=|{h_{0}}(\bm{x})-y|^{p-1},\quad(\bm{x},y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}.

Hence a datapoint
Z_{i}=(X_{i},Y_{i})
has a high probability of being picked if
Y_{i}
is poorly predicted by the true function
{h_{0}}
(
|Y_{i}-{h_{0}}(X_{i})|\gg 0
).
This is for instance the case for noisy observations.

In addition, let us remark that for high values of
p
, the SSPs (through
|\ell^{\prime}({h_{0}}(\bm{x}),y)|
) are squashed to
0
when
{h_{0}}(\bm{x})\approx y
, thus imitating an
\epsilon
-insensitive loss
(
37
)
, with the nice property of being infinitely differentiable,
and producing SSPs of different magnitudes.

Example 3

(Quartic-quadratic Huber)
.

As stated above, the
L^{p}
loss with a high value of
p
produces SSPs of different magnitudes, attributing near zero inclusion probabilities to well predicted datapoints.
On the converse, it assigns high SSPs to datapoints, the outputs
y
of which are far from the predictions
{h_{0}}(\bm{x})
.
As a result, and as it may be expected by using an
L^{p}
loss, the SSPs are not robust to outliers.

To keep the benefit of an almost
\epsilon
-insensitive loss (i.e. SSPs with different magnitudes) while being more robust to outliers,
one may defined a “Huberized”
L^{2}
loss (see
(
20
, Section 10.6)
),
which is quartic around
0
and quadratic elsewhere.
For this purpose, let
\epsilon>0
be a given threshold, and consider

\ell_{\epsilon}(\hat{y},y)\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}\begin{cases}\frac{1}{4}(\hat{y}-y)^{4}&\text{if}\ |\hat{y}-y|\leq\epsilon,\\
\frac{3}{2}\epsilon^{2}(\hat{y}-y)^{2}-2\epsilon^{3}|\hat{y}-y|+\frac{3}{4}\epsilon^{4}&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

which is twice-differentiable, with

\ell^{\prime}_{\epsilon}(\hat{y},y)=\begin{cases}(\hat{y}-y)^{3}&\text{if}\ |\hat{y}-y|\leq\epsilon,\\
3\epsilon^{2}(\hat{y}-y)-2\epsilon^{3}\sign(\hat{y}-y)&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

In the same manner as for
L^{p}
regression, the optimal SSPs are as large as the output
y
is wrongly predicted by
{h_{0}}(\bm{x})
,
nevertherless, there is a regime shift whether the prediction is at least (or at most)
\epsilon
far away from the output
y
.
This leads to SSPs of different magnitudes, which are more robust to outliers than those obtained with an
L^{p}
loss.

5
Piloted subsampling estimator

We address in this section the practical side of subsampling with the L-optimal absolute interest function
\psi^{\star}
, exhibited in

First, as it can be noticed,
\psi^{\star}
makes use of the unknown regularized risk minimizer
{h_{0}}

Nevertheless, the latter can be estimated by plug-in

leads to SSPs which verify

As a last point, since SSPs should be less than

for Poisson subsampling, one may apply a post hoc truncation, such as

Everything together provides data driven SSPs satisfying all requirements and usable to build an SRM

Theorems

and

In particular, it exhibits a covariance operator (which is well-defined and finite by the very definition of
\psi^{\star}
and

,

and

), which, compared to that of

and

.

be the SRM of the subsampled regularized risk with SSP vector
\tilde{\bm{\pi}}_{n}^{\alpha}\wedge s_{n}^{-1}
.
If one furthermore assumes that
s_{n}/n\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}c\in[0,1]
, then

\sqrt{s_{n}}\nabla^{2}{\mathcal{R}}({h_{0}})({h_{s_{n}}^{*\alpha}}-{h_{0}})\overset{\mathrm{d}}{\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{G}(0,\Sigma^{*}_{\mathrm{P},c}(\alpha)+c\Sigma),

where

\Sigma^{*}_{\mathrm{P},c}(\alpha)\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\psi^{\star}(Z)\right]\ell^{\prime}({h_{0}}(X),Y)^{2}}{\Psi^{\star}_{\alpha}(Z)\wedge(c^{-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\psi^{\star}(Z)\right])}K_{X}\otimes K_{X}\right]-c\mathbb{E}\left[\ell^{\prime}({h_{0}},(X),Y)^{2}K_{X}\otimes K_{X}\right].

A particularity of
\alpha
-piloted Poisson subsampling (
Theorem

5.2
) is the use of a post hoc truncation of SSPs:
\tilde{\bm{\pi}}_{n}^{\alpha}\wedge s_{n}^{-1}
,
in order to get valid Bernoulli distribution parameters.
It has two direct consequences.
First, the subsampled dataset size is in expectation less than or equal to
s_{n}
rather than equal to
s_{n}
.
Second, a thresholdind based on
c^{-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\psi^{\star}(Z)\right]
appears in the
denominator of the asymptotic covariance operator
\Sigma_{\mathrm{P},c}^{*}
of
{h_{s_{n}}^{*\alpha}}
.

In practice, the pilot estimator
{\hat{h}_{k_{n}}}
can be easily obtained by picking uniformly a small number of datapoints and by minimizing an empirical regularized risk.
In this situation,
Theorem

3.1
ensures consistency of
{\hat{h}_{k_{n}}}
.
The proposed workable procedure for computing an
\alpha
-piloted subsampling estimator is summarized
Algorithm

1
.

Require
s_{n}\leq n
(desired subsampled dataset size),
k_{n}\ll s_{n}
(pilot dataset size),
\alpha\in(0,1]
(smoothing hyperparameter).

1.

Consider a random permutation
\sigma
on
\{1,\dots,n\}
and build a pilot estimator

\hat{h}_{k_{n}}\in\argmin_{h\in\mathbb{H}}\left\{\frac{1}{k_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{k_{n}}\ell(h(X_{\sigma(i)}),Y_{\sigma(i)})+\frac{\lambda_{k_{n}}^{*}}{2}\|h\|^{2}_{\mathbb{H}}\right\}.

2.

Estimate optimal SSPs by plug-in:

\tilde{\pi}_{n,i}=\frac{|\ell^{\prime}({\hat{h}_{k_{n}}}(X_{i}),Y_{i})|\sqrt{k(X_{i},X_{i})}}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}|\ell^{\prime}({\hat{h}_{k_{n}}}(X_{j}),Y_{j})|\sqrt{k(X_{j},X_{j})}},

for all
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
.

3.

Smooth the estimated SSPs:

\tilde{\pi}_{n,i}^{\alpha}=(1-\alpha)\tilde{\pi}_{n,i}+\frac{\alpha}{n},

for all
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
.

4.

Subsample datapoints either with replacement (with SSP vector
\tilde{\bm{\pi}}_{n}^{\alpha}
)
or without replacement (with SSP vector
\tilde{\bm{\pi}}_{n}^{\alpha}\wedge s_{n}^{-1}
)
and get multiplicities
\gamma_{n,i}^{\alpha}
.

5.

Build the
\alpha
-piloted SRM:

{h_{s_{n}}^{*\alpha}}\in\argmin_{h\in\mathbb{H}}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\ell(h(X_{i}),Y_{i})}{s_{n}\tilde{\pi}_{n,i}^{\alpha}}\gamma_{n,i}^{\alpha}+\frac{{\lambda_{s_{n}}^{*}}}{2}\|h\|^{2}_{\mathbb{H}}\right\}.

7
Conclusion and discussion

This paper addresses the design of a reduced dataset by subsampling a large one, with the aim of computing a kernel-based estimator for classification or regression.
Based on asymptotic results, it is possible to randomly choose observations leading to a minimal trace for the limit covariance operator of the subsampling estimator.
This L-optimal subsampling scheme can then be estimated by plug-in, based on a pilot estimator, and smoothed in order to provide a robust practical method.
The latter is compatible with state-of-the-art kernel approximation techniques and can, for instance, be coupled with the Nyström method, leading to a very computationally efficient framework for learning in RKHSs.
Numerical experiments demonstrate the appeal of the method compared to uniform subsampling and kernel approximations, in particular for large initial datasets.

The work presented here opens several directions of research.
First, the theory is based on a regularized population risk and could, at the price of a substantial work, be extended to a non-regularized risk minimizer.
Second, the proposed approach is basically a two-step algorithm, learning first a pilot estimator (as well as estimating the subsampling probabilities and building the subsampled dataset) and then learning the final subsampling estimator.
One may conceive a multistage (or online) procedure, where the pilot estimator and the subsampling probabilities are sequentially adjusted in order to leverage more the random choice of consistent individuals.

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(Z_{1},{h_{0}},f)^{2}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(Z_{1},{h_{0}},f)\right]^{2}

\displaystyle=\langle\Sigma f,f\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}.

where
\Sigma\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}\mathbb{E}\left[\ell^{\prime}({h_{0}}(X),Y)^{2}K_{X}\otimes K_{X}\right]-\nabla{L}({h_{0}})\otimes\nabla{L}({h_{0}})
.
Thus, by the one dimensional central limit theorem,

\sqrt{n}\langle\nabla{L}_{n}({h_{0}})-\nabla{L}({h_{0}}),f\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\overset{\mathrm{d}}{\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}(0,\langle\Sigma f,f\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}).

There is left to show that
\sqrt{n}(\nabla{L}_{n}({h_{0}})-\nabla{L}({h_{0}}))
is asymptotically finite dimensional.
Let
\varepsilon,\delta>0
. Since
\mathbb{E}\left[\|\ell^{\prime}({h_{0}}(X),Y)K_{X}\|^{2}_{\mathbb{H}}\right]<\infty
by
Assumption

3
, there exists
J\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j>J}\ell^{\prime}({h_{0}}(X),Y)^{2}\langle K_{X},e_{j}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\right]<\delta\varepsilon
.
Hence by Markov’s inequality,

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{j>J}\langle\sqrt{n}(\nabla{L}_{n}({h_{0}})-\nabla{L}({h_{0}})),e_{j}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\geq\varepsilon\right)\leq\frac{n}{\varepsilon}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j>J}\langle\nabla{L}_{n}({h_{0}})-\nabla{L}({h_{0}}),e_{j}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\right].

Moreover, considering that the random variables in
\nabla{L}_{n}({h_{0}})
are i.i.d. with mean
\nabla{L}({h_{0}})
one has,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j>J}\langle\nabla{L}_{n}({h_{0}})-\nabla{L}({h_{0}}),e_{j}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j>J}\ell^{\prime}({h_{0}}(X),Y)^{2}\langle K_{X},e_{j}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j>J}\langle\nabla{L}({h_{0}}),e_{j}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}.

Hence, by definition of
J
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{j>J}\langle\sqrt{n}(\nabla{L}_{n}({h_{0}})-\nabla{L}({h_{0}})),e_{j}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\geq\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j>J}\ell^{\prime}({h_{0}}(X),Y)^{2}\langle K_{X},e_{j}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\delta.

Thus,
\sqrt{n}(\nabla{L}_{n}({h_{0}})-\nabla{L}({h_{0}}))
is asymptotically finite-dimensional, in the sense that for all
\varepsilon,\delta>0
, there exists
J\in\mathbb{N}
such that,

\limsup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\ \mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{j>J}\langle\sqrt{n}(\nabla{L}_{n}({h_{0}})-\nabla{L}({h_{0}})),e_{j}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\geq\varepsilon\right)\leq\delta.

Applying Theorem 7.7.5 of
(
21
)
yields

\sqrt{n}(\nabla{L}_{n}({h_{0}})-\nabla{L}({h_{0}}))\overset{\mathrm{d}}{\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{G}(0,\Sigma).\qed

Lemma A.4
.

Under
Assumptions

1
,
2
,
3
and
4
,

\sqrt{n}\int_{0}^{1}\nabla^{2}{\mathcal{R}}_{n}(\bar{h}_{n,t})({\hat{h}_{n}}-{h_{0}})\,\mathrm{d}t-\sqrt{n}\nabla^{2}{\mathcal{R}}({h_{0}})({\hat{h}_{n}}-{h_{0}})\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}}0,

with
\bar{h}_{n,t}={h_{0}}+t({\hat{h}_{n}}-{h_{0}})
for all
t\in[0,1]
.

Proof.

Firstly, in the proof of
Theorem

A.2
we showed that,

\|{\hat{h}_{n}}-{h_{0}}\|_{\mathbb{H}}\leq\frac{2}{{\lambda_{n}}}\|\nabla{L}_{n}({h_{0}})-\nabla{L}({h_{0}})\|_{\mathbb{H}}+\frac{2}{{\lambda_{0}}{\lambda_{n}}}\left|{\lambda_{n}}-{\lambda_{0}}\right|\|\nabla{L}({h_{0}})\|_{\mathbb{H}},

and from

\sqrt{n}(\nabla{L}_{n}({h_{0}})-\nabla{L}({h_{0}}))
converges in distribution to a Gaussian process.
Thus,
\sqrt{n}\|{\hat{h}_{n}}-{h_{0}}\|_{\mathbb{H}}=O_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
, by the fact that
\sqrt{n}({\lambda_{n}}-{\lambda_{0}})
converges to 0 in probability.

Then, let
h\in\mathbb{H}
,
t\in[0,1]
and denote
\bar{h}_{n,t}={h_{0}}+t({\hat{h}_{n}}-{h_{0}})
.
By consistency of
{\hat{h}_{n}}
, one has that
\|\bar{h}_{n,t}-{h_{0}}\|\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}}0
.
On the one hand, by the expression of
\nabla^{2}{\mathcal{R}}_{n}(\cdot)
and triangle inequality,

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}|\langle\nabla^{2}{\mathcal{R}}_{n}(\bar{h}_{n,t})({\hat{h}_{n}}-{h_{0}}),h\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}-\langle\nabla^{2}{\mathcal{R}}_{n}({h_{0}})({\hat{h}_{n}}-{h_{0}}),h\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}|

\displaystyle\quad=\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\ell^{\prime\prime}(\bar{h}_{n,t}(X_{i}),Y_{i})-\ell^{\prime\prime}({h_{0}}(X_{i}),Y_{i}))\langle K_{X_{i}}\otimes K_{X_{i}}({\hat{h}_{n}}-{h_{0}}),h\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\right|

\displaystyle\quad\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|(\ell^{\prime\prime}(\bar{h}_{n,t}(X_{i}),Y_{i})-\ell^{\prime\prime}({h_{0}}(X_{i}),Y_{i}))\langle K_{X_{i}},{\hat{h}_{n}}-{h_{0}}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\langle K_{X_{i}},h\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\right|.

(A.1)

Moreover, using
Assumption

2
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,

\displaystyle|\ell^{\prime\prime}(\bar{h}_{n,t}(X_{i}),Y_{i})-\ell^{\prime\prime}({h_{0}}(X_{i}),Y_{i})|\mathbbm{1}_{\{\|\bar{h}_{n,t}-{h_{0}}\|_{\mathbb{H}}<1\}}

\displaystyle\leq\phi(Y_{i})|\langle K_{X_{i}},\bar{h}_{n,t}-{h_{0}}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}|

\displaystyle\leq\phi(Y_{i})|\langle K_{X_{i}},{\hat{h}_{n}}-{h_{0}}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}|,

as
t\in[0,1]
.
By Cauchy-Schwarz and
Assumption

1
, one has

|\langle K_{X_{i}},{\hat{h}_{n}}-{h_{0}}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}|\leq\|{\hat{h}_{n}}-{h_{0}}\|_{\mathbb{H}}\|K_{X_{i}}\|_{\mathbb{H}}\leq\kappa\|{\hat{h}_{n}}-{h_{0}}\|_{\mathbb{H}},

and
|\langle K_{X_{i}},h\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}|\leq\kappa\|h\|_{\mathbb{H}}
.
Thus, applying
Lemma

A.6
with the random variables being (
A.1
) and the events

yields

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\int_{0}^{1}|\langle\nabla^{2}{\mathcal{R}}_{n}(\bar{h}_{n,t})({\hat{h}_{n}}-{h_{0}}),h\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}-\langle\nabla^{2}{\mathcal{R}}_{n}({h_{0}})({\hat{h}_{n}}-{h_{0}}),h\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}|\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad\leq\sqrt{n}\kappa^{3}\|h\|_{\mathbb{H}}\|{\hat{h}_{n}}-{h_{0}}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi(Y_{i})+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}}0,

(A.2)

since

,
\sqrt{n}\|{\hat{h}_{n}}-{h_{0}}\|_{\mathbb{H}}=O_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
and
\|{\hat{h}_{n}}-{h_{0}}\|_{\mathbb{H}}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}}0
by
Theorem

A.2
.
On the other hand, since
\nabla^{2}{\mathcal{R}}_{n}({h_{0}})
and
\nabla^{2}{\mathcal{R}}({h_{0}})
are self-adjoint operators, one has

\displaystyle|\langle\nabla^{2}{\mathcal{R}}_{n}({h_{0}})({\hat{h}_{n}}-{h_{0}}),h\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}-\langle\nabla^{2}{\mathcal{R}}({h_{0}})({\hat{h}_{n}}-{h_{0}}),h\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}|

\displaystyle\quad=|\langle{\hat{h}_{n}}-{h_{0}},\nabla^{2}{\mathcal{R}}_{n}({h_{0}})h\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}-\langle{\hat{h}_{n}}-{h_{0}},\nabla^{2}{\mathcal{R}}({h_{0}})h\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}|

\displaystyle\quad=|\langle{\hat{h}_{n}}-{h_{0}},\nabla^{2}{\mathcal{R}}_{n}({h_{0}})h-\nabla^{2}{\mathcal{R}}({h_{0}})h\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}|

\displaystyle\quad\leq\|{\hat{h}_{n}}-{h_{0}}\|_{\mathbb{H}}\|\nabla^{2}{\mathcal{R}}_{n}({h_{0}})h-\nabla^{2}{\mathcal{R}}({h_{0}})h\|_{\mathbb{H}},

by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.
Furthermore

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}{\mathcal{R}}_{n}({h_{0}})h

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell^{\prime\prime}({h_{0}}(X_{i}),Y_{i})h(X_{i})K_{X_{i}}+{\lambda_{n}}h

\displaystyle\quad\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}}\mathbb{E}\left[\ell^{\prime\prime}({h_{0}}(X),Y)h(X)K_{X}\right]+{\lambda_{0}}h=\nabla^{2}{\mathcal{R}}({h_{0}})h,

by the law of large numbers and that
{\lambda_{n}}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}}{\lambda_{0}}
.
Whence

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\int_{0}^{1}|\langle\nabla^{2}{\mathcal{R}}_{n}({h_{0}})({\hat{h}_{n}}-{h_{0}}),h\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}-\langle\nabla^{2}{\mathcal{R}}({h_{0}})({\hat{h}_{n}}-{h_{0}}),h\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}|\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad\leq\sqrt{n}\|{\hat{h}_{n}}-{h_{0}}\|_{\mathbb{H}}\|\nabla^{2}{\mathcal{R}}_{n}({h_{0}})h-\nabla^{2}{\mathcal{R}}({h_{0}})h\|_{\mathbb{H}}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}}0.

(A.3)

Finally, combining (
A.2
) and (
A.3
) yields the wanted result

\sqrt{n}\int_{0}^{1}\langle\nabla^{2}{\mathcal{R}}_{n}(\bar{h}_{n,t})({\hat{h}_{n}}-{h_{0}}),h\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\,\mathrm{d}t-\sqrt{n}\langle\nabla^{2}{\mathcal{R}}({h_{0}})({\hat{h}_{n}}-{h_{0}}),h\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}}0.\qed

A.3
Subsampling intermediate results

The two following short lemma deal of general results for convergence in probability.

Lemma A.5
.

Let
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of real valued random variables.
Suppose that for all
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a sequence
(Y_{n}^{\varepsilon})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

0\leq X_{n}\leq Y_{n}^{\varepsilon}\ \text{a.s.}\qquad\text{and}\qquad Y_{n}^{\varepsilon}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}}\varepsilon.

Then
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges in probability to 0.

Proof.

Let
\delta>0
and
0<\varepsilon<\delta
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(X_{n}\geq\delta\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{n}^{\varepsilon}\geq\delta\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{n}^{\varepsilon}-\varepsilon\geq\delta-\varepsilon\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(|Y_{n}^{\varepsilon}-\varepsilon|\geq\delta-\varepsilon\right)\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}0.\qed

Lemma A.6
.

Let
(A_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of non negative random variables and
(E_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of events such that
\mathbb{P}\left(E_{n}\right)\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}0
.
Then
A_{n}\mathbbm{1}_{E_{n}}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}}0
.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left(A_{n}\mathbbm{1}_{E_{n}}>\varepsilon\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\mathbbm{1}_{E_{n}}=1\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(E_{n}\right)\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}0.\qed

The hereby theorem and its following proof are a piece of Theorem 3.5 in
(
17
)
revised with the conditional Lindeberg-Feller assumption instead of the usual one.

Theorem A.7

(Conditional Lindeberg-Feller)
.

Let
\{\eta_{n,j}:n>0,1\leq j\leq k_{n}\}
be a real martingale difference array adapted to a filtration array
({\mathcal{F}_{n,j}})_{n>0,0\leq j\leq k_{n}}
, i.e. for all
n>0
and
1\leq j\leq k_{n}
,
\eta_{n,j}
is
{\mathcal{F}_{n,j}}
-measurable and
\mathbb{E}\left[\eta_{n,j}\middle|{\mathcal{F}_{n,j-1}}\right]=0
.
Let
\sigma^{2}>0
such that,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}}\mathbb{E}\left[\eta_{n,j}^{2}\middle|{\mathcal{F}_{n,j-1}}\right]\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}}\sigma^{2}.\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:thm_{t}echnical_{c}onditional_{d}conv_{1}}

(A.4)

Moreover assume that for all
\varepsilon>0
,

\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}}\mathbb{E}\left[\eta_{n,j}^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{|\eta_{n,j}|>\varepsilon}\middle|{\mathcal{F}_{n,j-1}}\right]\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}}0.\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:thm_{t}echnical_{c}onditional_{d}conv_{2}}

(A.5)

Then for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
, on has,

\prod_{j=1}^{k_{n}}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{it\eta_{n,j}}\middle|{\mathcal{F}_{n,j-1}}\right]\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}t^{2}\sigma^{2}}.

(A.6)

Proof.

Define the functions
A:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
and
B:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that for all
x\in\mathbb{R}

e^{itx}=1+itx-\frac{1}{2}x^{2}+\frac{1}{2}x^{2}A(x)\quad\text{and}\quad B(x)=\min(x/3,2).

And note that, from
(
13
, Lemma 1, p.512)
,
|A(x)|\leq B(|x|)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
.
Letting
\sigma_{n,j}^{2}\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}\mathbb{E}\left[\eta_{n,j}^{2}\middle|{\mathcal{F}_{n,j-1}}\right]
and
\sigma_{n}^{2}\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}}\sigma_{n,j}^{2}
, one has

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{it\eta_{n,j}}\middle|{\mathcal{F}_{n,j-1}}\right]=1-\frac{1}{2}t^{2}\sigma_{n,j}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}t^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\eta_{n,j}^{2}A(t\eta_{n,j})\middle|{\mathcal{F}_{n,j-1}}\right].

Now define
r_{n}
by

\displaystyle\log\left(\prod_{j=1}^{k_{n}}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{it\eta_{n,j}}\middle|{\mathcal{F}_{n,j-1}}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}}\log\left(1-\frac{1}{2}t^{2}\sigma_{n,j}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}t^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\eta_{n,j}^{2}A(t\eta_{n,j})\middle|{\mathcal{F}_{n,j-1}}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}t^{2}\sigma_{n}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}t^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}}\mathbb{E}\left[\eta_{n,j}^{2}A(t\eta_{n,j})\middle|{\mathcal{F}_{n,j-1}}\right]+r_{n}.

(A.7)

As
\sigma_{n}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}}\sigma^{2}
by (
A.4
), it now suffices to show that
r_{n}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}}0
, then that the second term of (
A.7
) tends to 0 in probability as well.
Regarding the first point, denote
z_{n,j}\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}-\frac{1}{2}t^{2}\sigma_{n,j}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}t^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\eta_{n,j}^{2}A(t\eta_{n,j})\middle|{\mathcal{F}_{n,j-1}}\right]

Moreover, for
|z|<1

17

\displaystyle|r_{n}|

thus, with (

\displaystyle\tilde{\sigma}_{n}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}}\mathbb{E}\left[\eta_{n,j}^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{\{|\eta_{n,j}|>\gamma\}}\middle|{\mathcal{F}_{n,j}}\right]\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}}\gamma^{2}.

, by application of
Lemma

A.5
.
Thus, the first term of the right hand side of (

) tends to 0 in probability and the second term as well by
Lemma

A.6
.

Regarding the second point, let
\varepsilon>0
and observe that

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}}\mathbb{E}\left[\eta_{n,j}^{2}A(t\eta_{n,j})\middle|{\mathcal{F}_{n,j-1}}\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}}\mathbb{E}\left[\eta_{n,j}^{2}|A(t\eta_{n,j})|\middle|{\mathcal{F}_{n,j-1}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}}\mathbb{E}\left[\eta_{n,j}^{2}B(|t\eta_{n,j}|)(\mathbbm{1}_{\{|t\eta_{n,j}|\leq\varepsilon\}}+\mathbbm{1}_{\{|t\eta_{n,j}|>\varepsilon\}})\middle|{\mathcal{F}_{n,j-1}}\right]

Whence,
\prod_{j=1}^{k_{n}}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{it\eta_{n,j}}\middle|{\mathcal{F}_{n,j-1}}\right]\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}t^{2}\sigma^{2}\right)

Lemma A.8
.

Let
(A_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(B_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
two asymptotically finite dimensional random variable sequences in
\mathbb{H}
and
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
a real non-zero sequence that converges to
a\in\mathbb{R}
.
Then
(a_{n}A_{n}+B_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is asymptotically finite dimensional as well.

Proof.

By definition, for all
\varepsilon,\delta>0
, there exists
J_{a},J_{b}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\limsup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{j>J_{a}}\langle A_{n},e_{j}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{4(a^{2}+1)}\right)<\frac{\delta}{2},

and

\limsup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{j>J_{b}}\langle B_{n},e_{j}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\right)<\frac{\delta}{2}.

Denote
J\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}\max(J_{a},J_{b})
.
Using the inequality
(a+b)^{2}\leq 2(a^{2}+b^{2})
, one has

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{j>J}\langle a_{n}A_{n}+B_{n},e_{j}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\geq\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{j>J}\langle a_{n}A_{n},e_{j}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}+\sum_{j>J}\langle B_{n},e_{j}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{j>J}\langle A_{n},e_{j}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{4a_{n}^{2}}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{j>J}\langle B_{n},e_{j}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{j>J_{a}}\langle A_{n},e_{j}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{4a_{n}^{2}}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{j>J_{b}}\langle B_{n},e_{j}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\right).

Moreover, there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\geq N
,
a_{n}^{2}\leq a^{2}+1
.
Therefore

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{j>J_{a}}\langle A_{n},e_{j}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{4a_{n}^{2}}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{j>J_{a}}\langle A_{n},e_{j}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{4(a^{2}+1)}\right).

As a consequence, by subadditivity of the
\limsup
,

\limsup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{j>J}\langle a_{n}A_{n}+B_{n},e_{j}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\geq\varepsilon\right)\leq\frac{\delta}{2}+\frac{\delta}{2}=\delta,

and

is asymptotically finite dimensional.
∎

Let us consider the two following subsampling strategies, where

refers to the multiplicity (or the number of occurrences) of each point

in the subsampled dataset (which may be null if

Subsampling with replacement

One draws independently
s_{n}
point indices:

I_{n,1},\dots,I_{n,s_{n}}|{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\overset{{{}}{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\pi_{n,i}\delta_{\{i\}},

Each point
Z_{i}
appears
N_{n,i}\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}\gamma_{n,i}=\sum_{j=1}^{s_{n}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{I_{n,j}=i\}}
times in the subsampled dataset

Subsampling without replacement

Assuming that
\pi_{n,i}\leq s_{n}^{-1}
for all
n>0
and
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, ones draws independently for each point

\gamma_{n,i}|{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\sim\text{Ber}(s_{n}\pi_{n,i}),

and decides that it should enter the subsampled dataset when the inclusion indicator verifies

The following proposition is a weak law of large numbers for Multinomial and Poisson subsampling.
It is written with an a general Hilbert space

with the aim of applying it with either the reals
\mathbb{R}
and the regular product or the RKHS of study
\mathbb{H}
and its scalar product

.

Proposition A.9

(Subsampling LLN)
.

Let
(E,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{E})
be a Hilbert space and
f
a function from
\mathcal{Z}
to
E
such that
\mathbb{E}\left[\|f(Z)\|_{E}^{4}\right]<\infty
. Then, if
({\bm{\pi}}_{n})_{n>0}
verifies
Assumption

5
, one has,

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{s_{n}}\frac{f(Z_{i})}{s_{n}\pi_{n,i}}\gamma_{n,i}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}}\mathbb{E}f(Z),

whether
\gamma_{n,i}=N_{n,i}
, in the case of Multinomial subsampling, or
\gamma_{n,i}=\delta_{n,i}
, in the case of Poisson subsampling.

Moreover, if it is assumed that
s_{n}=O(n)
then,

\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{s_{n}}\frac{f(Z_{i})}{s_{n}\pi_{n,i}}\gamma_{n,i}-\mathbb{E}f(Z)\right\|_{E}=O_{\mathbb{P}}(s_{n}^{-1/2}).

By construction, in Poisson subsampling,
s_{n}\leq n
hence
s_{n}=O(n)
.
In the case of (Multinomial) subsampling, the goal is to reduce the effective dataset size.
Hence one would largely want
s_{n}=O(n)
and the second point of the above proposition always holds in this context.

Proof.

To simplify the notation, first define

\displaystyle Pf\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}\mathbb{E}f(Z),

\displaystyle P_{n}f\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(Z_{i}),

\displaystyle P_{s_{n}}^{*}f\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{s_{n}}\frac{f(Z_{i})}{s_{n}\pi_{n,i}}\gamma_{n,i},

and recall that
P_{n}f\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}}Pf
by the usual weak law of large numbers.
By the tower property one has

\mathbb{E}\left[P_{s_{n}}^{*}f\right]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{f(Z_{i})}{s_{n}\pi_{n,i}}\mathbb{E}\left[\gamma_{n,i}\middle|{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\right]\right]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}f(Z_{i})=Pf,

so
\mathbb{E}\left[\|P_{s_{n}}^{*}f-Pf\|_{E}^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\|P_{s_{n}}^{*}f\|_{E}^{2}\right]-\|Pf\|_{E}^{2}
.
Let us now show that for all
i,k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:prop_{w}lln_{R}{}_{p}art1}\mathbb{E}\left[\gamma_{n,i}\gamma_{n,k}\middle|{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\right]\leq s_{n}\pi_{n,i}\mathbbm{1}_{i=k}+s_{n}^{2}\pi_{n,i}\pi_{n,k},

(A.9)

for Multinomial and Poisson subsampling.
On the one hand,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[N_{n,i}N_{n,k}\middle|{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j,m=1}^{s_{n}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{I_{n,j=i}\}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{I_{n,m=k}\}}\middle|{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,m=1}^{s_{n}}\mathbb{P}\left(I_{n,j=i},I_{n,m=k}\middle|{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{s_{n}}\mathbb{P}\left(I_{n,j=i},I_{n,j=k}\middle|{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\right)+\sum_{j\neq m}^{s_{n}}\mathbb{P}\left(I_{n,j=i}\middle|{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\right)\mathbb{P}\left(I_{n,m=k}\middle|{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\right)

\displaystyle=s_{n}\pi_{n,i}\mathbbm{1}_{i=k}+s_{n}(s_{n}-1)\pi_{n,i}\pi_{n,k}

\displaystyle\leq s_{n}\pi_{n,i}\mathbbm{1}_{i=k}+s_{n}^{2}\pi_{n,i}\pi_{n,k}.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\delta_{n,i}\delta_{n,k}\middle|{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\delta_{n,i}\middle|{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\right]\mathbbm{1}_{i=k}+\mathbb{E}\left[\delta_{n,i}\middle|{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\delta_{n,k}\middle|{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\right]\mathbbm{1}_{i\neq k}

\displaystyle=s_{n}\pi_{n,i}\mathbbm{1}_{i=k}+s_{n}^{2}\pi_{n,i}\pi_{n,k}\mathbbm{1}_{i\neq k}

\displaystyle\leq s_{n}\pi_{n,i}\mathbbm{1}_{i=k}+s_{n}^{2}\pi_{n,i}\pi_{n,k}.

Hence

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|P_{s_{n}}^{*}f\|_{E}^{2}\middle|{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}s_{n}^{2}}\sum_{i,k=1}^{n}\frac{\langle f(Z_{i}),f(Z_{k})\rangle_{E}}{\pi_{n,i}\pi_{n,k}}\mathbb{E}\left[\gamma_{n,i}\gamma_{n,k}\middle|{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{s_{n}n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\|f(Z_{i})\|_{E}^{2}}{\pi_{n,i}}+\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,k=1}^{n}\langle f(Z_{i}),f(Z_{k})\rangle_{E}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{s_{n}n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\|f(Z_{i})\|_{E}^{2}}{\pi_{n,i}}+\|P_{n}f\|_{E}^{2}.

On the one hand, by Cauchy-Schwarz,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\|f(Z_{i})\|_{E}^{2}}{\pi_{n,i}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\left\{\frac{1}{n\pi_{n,i}}\right\}^{2}\right]}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|f(Z_{i})\|_{E}^{2}\right)^{2}\right]}.

However,

\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\left\{\frac{1}{n\pi_{n,i}}\right\}^{2}\right]=O(1),

and,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|f(Z_{i})\|_{E}^{2}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\|f(Z_{i})\|_{E}^{4}\right]+\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i\neq j}\mathbb{E}\left[\|f(Z_{i})\|_{E}^{2}\|f(Z_{j})\|_{E}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\|f(Z_{1})\|_{E}^{4}\right]+\frac{n(n-1)}{n^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\|f(Z_{1})\|_{E}^{2}\right]^{2}=O(1).

Hence,

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{s_{n}n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\|f(Z_{i})\|_{E}^{2}}{\pi_{n,i}}\right]=O(s_{n}^{-1}).

On the other hand, by independence of the observations
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|P_{n}f\|_{E}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,m=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\langle f(Z_{i}),f(Z_{m})\rangle_{E}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\|f(Z_{i})\|_{E}^{2}\right]+\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i\neq m}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\langle f(Z_{i}),f(Z_{m})\rangle_{E}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\|f(Z)\|_{E}^{2}\right]+\frac{n(n-1)}{n^{2}}\|Pf\|_{E}^{2}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|P_{s_{n}}^{*}f\|_{E}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{s_{n}n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\|f(Z_{i})\|_{E}^{2}}{\pi_{n,i}}\right]+\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\|f(Z)\|_{E}^{2}\right]+\frac{n-1}{n}\|Pf\|_{E}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{s_{n}n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\|f(Z_{i})\|_{E}^{2}}{\pi_{n,i}}\right]+\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\|f(Z)\|_{E}^{2}\right]+\|Pf\|_{E}^{2},

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|P_{s_{n}}^{*}f\|_{E}^{2}\right]-\|Pf\|_{E}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{s_{n}n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\|f(Z_{i})\|_{E}^{2}}{\pi_{n,i}}\right]+\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\|f(Z)\|_{E}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=O(s_{n}^{-1})+O(n^{-1})\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}0.

(A.10)

Finally, by Chebyshev’s inequality, for all
\varepsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\|P_{s_{n}}^{*}f-Pf\|_{E}\geq\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\|P_{s_{n}}^{*}f-Pf\|_{E}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\|P_{s_{n}}^{*}f\|_{E}^{2}\right]-\|Pf\|_{E}^{2}\right)\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}0,

whence
P_{s_{n}}^{*}f\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}}Pf
.

Assume now that
s_{n}=O(n)
.
From (
A.10

\displaystyle s_{n}(\mathbb{E}\left[\|P_{s_{n}}^{*}f\|_{E}^{2}\right]-\|Pf\|_{E}^{2})

\displaystyle=s_{n}(O(s_{n}^{-1})+O(n^{-1}))=O(1).

Whence, there exists

and
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that for
n\geq N

\displaystyle s_{n}(\mathbb{E}\left[\|P_{s_{n}}^{*}f\|_{E}^{2}\right]-\|Pf\|_{E}^{2})\leq M^{2}.

Finally, by Chebyshev’s inequality, for all
\varepsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sqrt{s_{n}}\|P_{s_{n}}^{*}f-Pf\|_{E}\geq M\varepsilon^{-1/2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon\frac{s_{n}}{M^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\|P_{s_{n}}^{*}f-Pf\|_{E}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\frac{s_{n}}{M^{2}}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\|P_{s_{n}}^{*}f\|_{E}^{2}\right]-\|Pf\|_{E}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon,

and therefore
\|P_{s_{n}}^{*}f-Pf\|_{E}=O_{\mathbb{P}}(s_{n}^{-1/2})
.
∎

\sqrt{s_{n}}(\nabla{L}^{*}_{s_{n}}({h_{0}})-\nabla{L}_{n}({h_{0}}))
is asymptotically finite dimensional, i.e. for all
\varepsilon,\delta>0
, there exists
J\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\limsup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\ \mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{j>J}\langle\sqrt{s_{n}}(\nabla{L}^{*}_{s_{n}}-\nabla{L}_{n})({h_{0}}),e_{j}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\geq\varepsilon\right)<\delta,

whether considering Poisson or Multinomial subsampling.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon,\delta>0
and
J\in\mathbb{N}
.
By Markov’s inequality

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{j>J}\langle\sqrt{s_{n}}(\nabla{L}^{*}_{s_{n}}-\nabla{L}_{n})({h_{0}}),e_{j}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\geq\varepsilon\right)\leq\frac{s_{n}}{\varepsilon}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j>J}\langle(\nabla{L}^{*}_{s_{n}}-\nabla{L}_{n})({h_{0}}),e_{j}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\right].

Expanding the right hand side together with the tower property yields the quantity

\displaystyle\frac{s_{n}}{n^{2}\varepsilon}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j>J}\sum_{i,k=1}^{n}\frac{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(Z_{i},{h_{0}},e_{j})\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(Z_{k},{h_{0}},e_{j})}{s_{n}^{2}\pi_{n,i}\pi_{n,k}}(\gamma_{n,i}-s_{n}\pi_{n,i})(\gamma_{n,k}-s_{n}\pi_{n,k})\right]

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{1}{n^{2}\varepsilon}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j>J}\sum_{i,k=1}^{n}\frac{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(Z_{i},{h_{0}},e_{j})\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(Z_{k},{h_{0}},e_{j})}{s_{n}\pi_{n,i}\pi_{n,k}}\mathbb{E}\left[(\gamma_{n,i}-s_{n}\pi_{n,i})(\gamma_{n,k}-s_{n}\pi_{n,k})\middle|{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\right]\right].

However, from (
A.9
), for all
i,k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
one has

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[(\gamma_{n,i}-s_{n}\pi_{n,i})(\gamma_{n,k}-s_{n}\pi_{n,k})\middle|{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\gamma_{n,i}\gamma_{n,k}\middle|{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\right]-s_{n}\pi_{n,i}\mathbb{E}\left[\gamma_{n,k}\middle|{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad-s_{n}\pi_{n,k}\mathbb{E}\left[\gamma_{n,i}\middle|{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\right]+s_{n}^{2}\pi_{n,i}\pi_{n,k}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\gamma_{n,i}\gamma_{n,k}\middle|{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\right]-s_{n}^{2}\pi_{n,i}\pi_{n,k}

\displaystyle\leq s_{n}\pi_{n,i}\mathbbm{1}_{i=k}

Thus

\displaystyle\frac{s_{n}}{\varepsilon}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j>J}\langle(\nabla{L}^{*}_{s_{n}}-\nabla{L}_{n})({h_{0}}),e_{j}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\quad\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j>J}\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(Z_{i},{h_{0}},e_{j})^{2}}{\pi_{n,i}}\right]

\displaystyle\quad\leq\frac{1}{n\varepsilon}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{k\in[n]}\left\{\frac{1}{n\pi_{n,k}}\right\}\sum_{j>J}\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(Z_{i},{h_{0}},e_{j})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{k\in[n]}\left\{\frac{1}{n\pi_{n,k}}\right\}\sum_{j>J}\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(Z,{h_{0}},e_{j})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\quad\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{\in[n]}\left\{\frac{1}{n\pi_{n,i}}\right\}^{2}\right]}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{j>J}\ell^{\prime}({h_{0}}(X),Y)^{2}\langle K_{X},e_{j}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\right)^{2}\right]}.

However,
\|\ell^{\prime}({h_{0}}(X),Y)K_{X}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}<\infty
hence

Z^{(J)}\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}\sum_{j>J}\ell^{\prime}({h_{0}}(X),Y)^{2}\langle K_{X},e_{j}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\underset{J\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}}0,

as the rest of a converging series.
Furthermore,
\|\ell^{\prime}({h_{0}}(X),Y)K_{X}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}
dominates the series
(Z^{(J)})_{J\geq 0}
and has a second order moment by
Assumption

3
whence, by the dominated convergence theorem in
L^{2}
, one has

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{j>J}\ell^{\prime}({h_{0}}(X),Y)^{2}\langle K_{X},e_{j}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\right)^{2}\right]\underset{J\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}0.

Moreover, it is assumed from
Assumption

5
that there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
C>0
such that for all
n\geq N
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\left\{\frac{1}{n\pi_{n,i}}\right\}^{2}\right]\leq C^{2}.

Hence for
J
large enough such that

\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{j>J}\ell^{\prime}({h_{0}}(X),Y)^{2}\langle K_{X},e_{j}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\right)^{2}\right]}<\delta\frac{\varepsilon}{C},

one has

\limsup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\ \mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{j>J}\langle\sqrt{s_{n}}(\nabla{L}^{*}_{s_{n}}-\nabla{L}_{n})({h_{0}}),e_{j}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\geq\varepsilon\right)<\delta.

Thus
\sqrt{s_{n}}(\nabla{L}^{*}_{s_{n}}({h_{0}})-\nabla{L}_{n}({h_{0}}))
is asymptotically finite dimensional.
∎

Lemma A.12
.

Let
(H_{n})_{n>0}
be a sequence of
\mathbb{H}
valued random variables and
({\mathcal{F}_{n}})_{n>0}
a sequence of
\sigma
-algebras.
Assume that
H_{n}
is asymptotically finite-dimensional,
\sigma({\mathcal{D}_{n}})\subset{\mathcal{F}_{n}}
and for all
f\in\mathbb{H}
,

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{it\langle H_{n},f\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}}\middle|{\mathcal{F}_{n}}\right]\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\langle\Sigma^{*}f,f\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}},\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:lem_{s}rm_{n}ormality_{f}inal_{p}art1}

(A.14)

for some covariance operator
\Sigma^{*}
.
Then

H_{n}+\sqrt{\frac{s_{n}}{n}}\sqrt{n}(\nabla{L}_{n}({h_{0}})-\nabla{L}({h_{0}}))\overset{\mathrm{d}}{\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{G}(0,\Sigma^{*}+c\Sigma).

Proof.

Let
f\in\mathbb{H}
and define

\displaystyle S_{n}^{*}\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}\langle H_{n},f\rangle_{\mathbb{H}},

\displaystyle S_{n}\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}\sqrt{n}\langle\nabla{L}_{n}({h_{0}})-\nabla{L}({h_{0}}),f\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}.

Let
t\in\mathbb{R}
and consider the convergence of the characteristic function, i.e.

\left|\mathbb{E}e^{it(S_{n}^{*}+\sqrt{\frac{s_{n}}{n}}S_{n})}-e^{-\frac{1}{2}t^{2}\langle(\Sigma^{*}+c\Sigma)f,f\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}}\right|.

First adding and subtracting
e^{it\sqrt{\frac{s_{n}}{n}}S_{n}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}t^{2}\langle\Sigma^{*}f,f\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}}
and using the triangle inequality one gets that the previous display is upper bounded by

\left|\mathbb{E}\left[e^{it\sqrt{\frac{s_{n}}{n}}S_{n}}(e^{itS_{n}^{*}}-e^{-\frac{1}{2}t^{2}\langle\Sigma^{*}f,f\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}})\right]\right|+e^{-\frac{1}{2}t^{2}\langle\Sigma^{*}f,f\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}}\left|\mathbb{E}\left[e^{it\sqrt{\frac{s_{n}}{n}}S_{n}}\right]-e^{-\frac{1}{2}t^{2}c\langle\Sigma f,f\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}}\right|.

By
Lemma

A.3
,
S_{n}\overset{\mathrm{d}}{\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}(0,\langle\Sigma f,f\rangle_{\mathbb{H}})
and since
s_{n}/n\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}c
, Slutsky’s theorem yields that
\sqrt{\frac{s_{n}}{n}}S_{n}\overset{\mathrm{d}}{\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}(0,c\langle\Sigma f,f\rangle_{\mathbb{H}})
.
Thus

\left|\mathbb{E}\left[e^{it\sqrt{\frac{s_{n}}{n}}S_{n}}\right]-e^{-\frac{1}{2}t^{2}c\langle\Sigma f,f\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}}\right|\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}0.

Moreover,
S_{n}
is
{\mathcal{D}_{n}}
-measurable thus

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\left[e^{it\sqrt{\frac{s_{n}}{n}}S_{n}}(e^{itS_{n}^{*}}-e^{-\frac{1}{2}t^{2}\langle\Sigma^{*}f,f\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}})\right]\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\mathbb{E}\left[e^{it\sqrt{\frac{s_{n}}{n}}S_{n}}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[e^{itS_{n}^{*}}\middle|{\mathcal{F}_{n}}\right]-e^{-\frac{1}{2}t^{2}\langle\Sigma^{*}f,f\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}}\right)\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\mathbb{E}\left[e^{itS_{n}^{*}}\middle|{\mathcal{F}_{n}}\right]-e^{-\frac{1}{2}t^{2}\langle\Sigma^{*}f,f\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}}\right|\right].

However,
\left|\mathbb{E}\left[e^{itS_{n}^{*}}\middle|{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\right]\right|\leq 1
.
Thus, by (
A.14
) and the dominated convergence theorem

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\mathbb{E}\left[e^{itS_{n}^{*}}\middle|{\mathcal{F}_{n}}\right]-e^{-\frac{1}{2}t^{2}\langle\Sigma^{*}f,f\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}}\right|\right]\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}0.

As a consequence,

S_{n}^{*}+\sqrt{\frac{s_{n}}{n}}S_{n}\overset{\mathrm{d}}{\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}(0,\langle(\Sigma^{*}+c\Sigma)f,f\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}).

Moreover, by
Lemma

A.3

\sqrt{n}(\nabla{L}_{n}({h_{0}})-\nabla{L}({h_{0}}))
is asymptotically finite dimensional and so is
H_{n}
, by assumption.
Then, by
Lemma

A.8
the sum of two asymptotically finite dimensional sequences of random variables in
\mathbb{H}
is itself asymptotically finite dimensional.
Therefore

H_{n}+\sqrt{n}(\nabla{L}_{n}({h_{0}})-\nabla{L}({h_{0}}))\overset{\mathrm{d}}{\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{G}(0,\Sigma^{*}+c\Sigma).\qed

for all

and

{\mathcal{D}_{n}}
and
I_{n,1},\dots,I_{n,j}
for

and

.

and

\eta_{n,j}

, the previous display reads

However, only one term of the sum in the right hand side of the previous display is non-zero.
Thus

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(Z_{i},{h_{0}},f)}{\pi_{n,i}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{I_{n,1}=i\}}\right)^{2}\middle|{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(Z_{i},{h_{0}},f)^{2}}{\pi_{n,i}^{2}}\mathbb{P}\left(I_{n,1}=i\middle|{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(Z_{i},{h_{0}},f)^{2}}{\pi_{n,i}}.

Hence, the conditional variance reads

\sum_{j=1}^{s_{n}}\mathbb{E}\left[\eta_{n,j}^{2}\middle|{\mathcal{F}_{n,j-1}}\right]=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(Z_{i},{h_{0}},f)^{2}}{\pi_{n,i}}-\langle\nabla{L}_{n}({h_{0}}),f\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}.

If one writes
\pi_{n,i}=\psi(Z_{i})/\sum_{j=1}^{n}\psi(Z_{j})
, then by the law of large numbers

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{s_{n}}\mathbb{E}\left[\eta_{n,j}^{2}\middle|{\mathcal{F}_{n,j-1}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\psi(Z_{j})\right)\frac{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(Z_{i},{h_{0}},f)^{2}}{\psi(Z_{i})}-\langle\nabla{L}_{n}({h_{0}}),f\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}

\Sigma^{*}_{\mathrm{M}}(\psi)\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\psi(Z)\right]}{\psi(Z)}\ell^{\prime}({h_{0}}(X),Y)^{2}K_{X}\otimes K_{X}\right]-\nabla{L}({h_{0}})\otimes\nabla{L}({h_{0}})

\varepsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{s_{n}}\mathbb{E}\left[\eta_{n,j}^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{\{|\eta_{n,j}|>\varepsilon\}}\middle|{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\right]

\displaystyle\quad=s_{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\eta_{n,1}^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{\{|\eta_{n,1}|>\varepsilon\}}\middle|{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\right]

\displaystyle\quad\leq\frac{s_{n}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\eta_{n,1}^{4}\middle|{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\right]

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{1}{s_{n}\varepsilon^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(Z_{i},{h_{0}},f)}{\pi_{n,i}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{I_{n,j}=i\}}-\langle\nabla{L}_{n}({h_{0}}),f\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\right)^{4}\middle|{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\right].

Furthermore expanding the forth order moment yields

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{s_{n}}\mathbb{E}\left[\eta_{n,j}^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{\{|\eta_{n,j}|>\varepsilon\}}\middle|{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\right]

\displaystyle\quad\leq\frac{1}{s_{n}\varepsilon^{2}}\Bigg(\frac{1}{n^{4}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(Z_{i},{h_{0}},f)^{4}}{\pi_{n,i}^{3}}-4\frac{\langle\nabla{L}_{n}({h_{0}}),f\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}}{n^{3}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(Z_{i},{h_{0}},f)^{3}}{\pi_{n,i}^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+6\frac{\langle\nabla{L}_{n}({h_{0}}),f\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(Z_{i},{h_{0}},f)^{2}}{\pi_{n,i}}-3\langle\nabla{L}_{n}({h_{0}}),f\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{4}\Bigg).

5

\delta>0

\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\left\{n\pi_{n,i}\right\}^{-p}=O_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
for any

.
Hence, using the fact that
\langle\nabla{L}_{n}({h_{0}}),f\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{4}=O_{\mathbb{P}}(1)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{p+1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{|\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(Z_{i},{h_{0}},f)|^{p+1}}{\pi_{n,i}^{p}}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\left\{\frac{1}{n^{p}\pi_{n,i}^{p}}\right\}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(Z_{i},{h_{0}},f)|^{p+1}

\displaystyle=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\left\{\frac{1}{n\pi_{n,i}}\right\}^{p}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(Z_{i},{h_{0}},f)|^{p+1}=O_{\mathbb{P}}(1).

Thus
\sum_{j=1}^{s_{n}}\mathbb{E}\left[\eta_{n,j}^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{\{|\eta_{n,j}|>\varepsilon\}}\middle|{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\right]\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}}0
.
Whence, from
Theorem

A.7
,

\prod_{j=1}^{s_{n}}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{it\eta_{n,j}}\middle|{\mathcal{F}_{n,j-1}}\right]\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}t^{2}\langle\Sigma^{*}_{\mathrm{M}}(\psi)f,f\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\right).

Finally,
\mathbb{E}\left[e^{it\eta_{n,j}}\middle|{\mathcal{F}_{n,j-1}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{it\eta_{n,j}}\middle|{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\right]
and conditionally to

,
\eta_{n,1},\dots,\eta_{n,s_{n}}
