Safety Measurements for Fine-tuned LLMs Should be Grounded in Capability
Krishnapriya Vishnubhotla, Hillary Dawkins, Isar Nejadgholi, Svetlana Kiritchenko

Abstract
Adapting foundation large language models to a user’s task or preferred style through fine-tuning can result in compromising the model’s safety. Previous works examined the effects of fine-tuning on model safety in limited and seemingly random experimental settings. We argue that anchoring fine-tuning to a specific capability goal is essential for avoiding arbitrary empirical choices, allowing us to draw meaningful conclusions about safety impacts, and to compare mitigation methods on a consistent basis. We conduct a multi-dimensional evaluation of the effects of fine-tuning on model behavior by focusing on capability as well as safety. Our results surface important issues that (1) fine-tuned models can produce incoherent generations in response to safety prompts, (2) automated safety judgments are unreliable for such incoherent outputs, and (3) the conclusions about the effects of fine-tuning can change depending on the choice of safety benchmark as well as the safety evaluator.



1
Introduction

In many real-world use cases, the default, instruction-tuned versions of Large Language Models (LLMs) are further adapted for a particular application using supervised fine-tuning, retrieval augmented generation, or other methods of domain adaptation.
Unfortunately, recent research suggests that such modifications can, intentionally or unintentionally, degrade the safety features of the original models
(
26
;
23
)
; this safety degradation has been observed even with fine-tuning on
benign
data, i.e., where the fine-tuning dataset does not explicitly encourage harmful or misaligned behavior.
Consequently, different mitigation methods have been proposed that modify the fine-tuning process to preserve model parameters or activations involved in safe behavior
(
14
;
21
;
31
)
.

Previous works examining the effects of fine-tuning on model safety are either under-specified or limited in the experimental settings that are studied.
As an example, let us compare two highly cited safety-preserving fine-tuning methods – SaLoRA
(
21
)
and SafeLoRA
(
14
)
– that modify the LoRA (low-rank adaptation) fine-tuning method to mitigate safety degradation. Their empirical setups differ in the LLM layers that are targeted for fine-tuning, as well as in the settings of other fine-tuning hyperparameters, such as the LoRA rank, learning rate, and the number of epochs. For evaluation, the two works use different sets of harmful questions to assess safety, and measure response harmfulness using different evaluator models. A systematic evaluation and comparison of these techniques for diverse models, settings, and use cases is still lacking.

These differences are of particular concern given the increasing evidence in AI research of the brittleness and unreliability of automated evaluations
5
;
4
;
3
. Additionally, in the context of fine-tuned models,
11
show how minor variations in the fine-tuning and evaluation process, such as changing the generation temperature, have a substantial effect on safety measurements, thereby disrupting evaluation consistency.
As they note, “A clear understanding of how different parameters impact the final safety evaluation is necessary before being able to assess the usefulness
of any proposed mitigation.”

A key barrier to meaningful evaluations in this context is the sheer number of hyperparameters involved in the fine-tuning process.
Typically, in practical use-cases, hyperparameter values are determined by optimizing for performance (loss or accuracy) on a validation set. This aspect of capability, or downstream performance, is (a) important, and (b) often neglected in the fine-tuning and safety literature. We argue that anchoring fine-tuning to a specific capability goal, rather than defining arbitrary empirical settings, provides a principled basis for assessing effects on model safety and for making meaningful comparisons across mitigation methods.

In this work, we conduct a multi-dimensional evaluation of the effects of fine-tuning on model behavior by focusing on
capability
as well as
safety
. We select fine-tuning datasets whose format allows for making verifiable accuracy measurements, thereby grounding the hyperparameter selection process across fine-tuning methods. We also compile safety evaluation prompts from multiple benchmarks, and vary the choice of safety evaluator, to compare how changes in measurement instruments influence downstream conclusions.

Our main contributions are summarized below:

•

Our focus on capability surfaces important issues with the
coherence
of fine-tuned model generations, and the
unreliability of automated safety judgments
in these situations.

•

We show how conclusions about the effects of fine-tuning can change depending on the
choice of safety benchmark
as well as the
safety evaluator
, highlighting the brittleness of safety evaluations.

•

Finally, we compare and contrast the safety-capability trade-offs introduced by SafeLoRA
14
, a safety-preserving variation of the vanilla LoRA fine-tuning process.

Our results, relevant to both practitioners and researchers, demonstrate the importance of having well-defined, grounded, and valid measurement methodologies in the space of model fine-tuning and safety.

2
Background

LLMs are pretrained on billions of tokens of text, followed by extensive post-training to align their behaviors with human preferences. While this imparts them with impressive zero or few-shot learning capabilities, one can typically achieve more reliable and accurate domain-specific performance by further fine-tuning these models on task-specific data. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods offer a cost- and compute-efficient way to do this by keeping the majority of model parameters frozen; typically, fine-tuning less than 1% of the total parameters results in large performance gains
15
;
1
.

Low-Rank Adaptation:

LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation)
(
15
)
is a PEFT technique that decomposes the weight update matrix for a target layer or module into two low-rank matrices, each of rank
r
, called low-rank adapters. The weights of the original (base) LLM remain frozen. Smaller values of
r
correspond to fewer parameters to train and faster training. LoRA can either be applied to all layers in the Transformer architecture, or to some subset of layers for further efficiency.

2.1
Safety Degradation and Mitigation

Several studies show that fine-tuning LLMs on benign datasets degrades their safety guardrails
(
26
;
12
)
. Although surprising, this erasure of unrelated safety behaviors may be attributed to the inherent brittleness of alignment fine-tuning
(
20
;
17
;
29
)
or the resistance of the original model to alignment
(
19
)
. To better understand these safety failures during model weight updates, related work analyzes the safety loss landscape
(
25
)
.
By observing how benign data points influence this landscape, specific training samples that inadvertently cause the most harm can be identified
(
13
;
12
)
.
In light of these explanations, recent studies propose safety-preserving fine-tuning methods that protect critical alignment weights while allowing for task-specific updates.

SafeLoRA
(
14
)
defines a safety subspace for a model as the difference in weights between the aligned and unaligned versions of the LLM. An unaligned LLM is typically a base LLM pre-trained only for next-token prediction; the corresponding aligned model would be the base LLM further trained for instruction following, human preference, and safety alignment. To preserve safety behaviors after fine-tuning, the LoRA-tuned weight updates are projected to this safety subspace if the similarity score between the original and projected updates is below a certain threshold.
SafeLoRA is a training-free and data-free approach, and only requires access to the weights of the base and aligned LLMs.

Other approaches include methods like SaLoRA
21
, which focuses on preserving model activations given safety prompts, and AsFT
31
, which constrains the gradient updates at each training step to be in-line with the direction of the safety subspace.

We restrict our analysis in this paper to the SafeLoRA method; in addition to being conceptually simple and computationally efficient, SafeLoRA involves a straightforward post-hoc projection of the LoRA-tuned checkpoint, thereby making the two methods directly comparable.

2.2
Safety Evaluation

Measuring the safety of language models is an ongoing area of research, involving efforts to both
define
what safe behavior entails for LLMs, and how to effectively
operationalize and measure
this in a robust and reliable manner. While safety is generally operationalized as model
refusals
to harmful questions, recent work is moving away from this definition to incorporate helpfulness as an additional dimension of
safe answers
; this can include, for example, explaining why the model can’t fulfill a user request
(
32
)
.

Safety benchmarks compile lists of possible user prompts requesting unsafe information or illegal advice; efforts have been made to ensure broad coverage both in terms of topics and linguistic style
33
;
18
;
28
;
30
. Model responses to these prompts can in turn be judged as safe or unsafe using different methods: some benchmarks, like SORRY-Bench
(
30
)
, are released with corresponding evaluators that output a binary decision; models like LlamaGuard from Meta
16
and Granite Guardian from IBM
24
, are trained to detect a more broad range of risks and output a risk category in addition to the binary judgment. Another alternative is to use the LLM-as-a-Judge paradigm with a custom instruction template and output categories, but such judges have been widely reported as being unreliable evaluators
6
.

To ensure robustness of our evaluations, we compile a comprehensive set of safety-related questions from a range of safety benchmarks, and compare judgments from two different evaluators.

3
Fine-tuning Methodology

We conduct a series of experiments to systematically assess the impact of fine-tuning on safety and capabilities of LLMs, highlighting the wide variability of outcomes depending on the chosen set-up (LLM, fine-tuning parameters, fine-tuning dataset, and safety evaluation methodology).

3.1
Fine-tuning Hyperparameters

LoRA fine-tuning generally involves configuring two key hyperparameters: the adapter rank, and the target modules (which of the original model layers should be adapted).
In practice, these hyperparameters are determined by optimizing for the downstream task performance.
We experiment with the following combinations of these parameters: rank {16, 64}, and fine-tuning layers {[qv], [all-linear]}. Additional details on our experimental settings are in Appendix
B
.

3.2
Large Language Models

We experiment with open-source, relatively small LLMs, keeping in mind the practical use-cases in which fine-tuning presents an effective alternative to using bigger, more capable closed-source models: Llama-3.2-1B, Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen-3-4b, and Qwen-3-8b. We use the instruction-tuned chat versions of these models as our starting point for fine-tuning.

3.4
Evaluation Methodology

We evaluate the fine-tuned models along three main dimensions: (1) performance on the fine-tuning task, (2) language generation coherence, and (3) safety. The first two represent the models’ capabilities, while the last addresses the safety alignment.

3.4.1
Capability and Coherence Evaluation

Capability
: For the GSM8k, ARC, BoolQ, and Science10k datasets, we evaluate the accuracy of the responses from the fine-tuned LLMs on the test splits of the datasets. We use dataset-specific regular expressions to extract the final, verifiable answer from model generations (note that for the ARC and BoolQ datasets, models are expected to generate a single token representing the multiple or boolean choice), and check for equivalence with the gold answers from the dataset.

Coherence
: To assess language generation capabilities, we measure the perplexity of the generated outputs using a GPT-2 model. Higher perplexity values indicate less coherent generations.

5
Results

We first report on the impact of fine-tuning models with LoRA on model capabilities and safety, and the observed variations in outcomes due to differing operationalization and measurements of safety. We then illustrate how these issues can affect our conclusions on the effectiveness of a safety-preserving fine-tuning method, and the trade-offs between model safety and capability.

5.1
Safety and Accuracy on Verifiable Tasks

The effect of fine-tuning on safety varies based on the model and fine-tuning dataset.

Figure
1
plots the average accuracy and harmfulness rates for each model and task, with shaded regions indicating standard deviation over LoRA hyperparameter configurations. Accuracy on the fine-tuning task increases throughout training for nearly all models and tasks, though the gains are smaller for the larger 8B models. On ARC and BoolQ in particular, owing to the relative simplicity of the tasks, models reach high accuracy relatively quickly. However, generally, we note that checkpoints at the final epoch have the highest validation accuracy, and are therefore most likely to be used by practitioners.

A corresponding analysis of harmfulness rates in Figure
1
(bottom) shows that while model safety generally degrades in the first and second epochs, it improves towards later epochs. Only Llama-1B shows an average increase in harmfulness; for both 8B models, harmfulness at the final training checkpoint is comparable with the initial instruct model, and for Qwen-4B, which has a high initial harmfulness, fine-tuning even improves safety.

For both Qwen models, we note that fine-tuning on the ARC and BoolQ datasets results in large fluctuations in harmfulness scores, well outside the range of scores for the other datasets. A qualitative check of the model outputs pointed to issues with coherence, relevance, and judge unreliability.

5.2
Issues with Coherence

Fine-tuning on restricted output formats leads to incoherent responses to safety questions. Automated judgments are unreliable in these cases.
A qualitative look at the model outputs for checkpoints with high perplexity scores shows that models overfit to the fine-tuning task, specifically on ARC and BoolQ, which require outputs to be in the form of single-character/word choices. This results in similar outputs being generated even for the safety benchmark questions, leading to largely incoherent responses. We present some examples in Table
C.1
in the Appendix. Consequently, judgments generated by the LlamaGuard-8B model are unreliable in these scenarios, likely due to the input-output sequences being out-of-distribution.

In Figure
2
, we plot the distribution of LlamaGuard judgments to what are largely incoherent, single character responses to our safety questions, for a random Qwen-4B checkpoint fine-tuned on ARC. We see that LlamaGuard judges many of the incoherent responses to be unsafe, while the opposite is a more appropriate judgment when evaluating safety. Overall, we note that as perplexity increases, so does the average harmfulness rate as judged by LlamaGuard-8B (see Figure
C.1
in the Appendix that plots both median perplexity and average harmfulness across checkpoints, color-coded by fine-tuning dataset, for the Qwen-4B model). However, it is in fact a lack of capability that LlamaGuard misinterprets as lack of safety.

Safety vs Refusal

Different measures of safety (
safe responses
vs
refusals
) do not align with each other.

We further investigate how the choice of safety measurement can lead to differing conclusions on the effects of fine-tuning. We compare judgments of safety as obtained from the LlamaGuard-8B model with judgments of Compliance/Refusal obtained from the fine-tuned SORRY-Bench evaluator provided by
30

Here, we evaluate model checkpoints fine-tuned on the Alpaca instruction-following dataset. Since this is a text-to-text instruction-following task (as opposed to multiple-choice), there is a low chance of unreliable judgments due to incoherence.

Figure

Across all models, compliance rates are generally higher than harmfulness rates, indicating that models generate safe answers that are not necessarily refusals. Correlation between the two scores increases with model size; we speculate that this may be because responses from smaller models are likely to be less coherent compared to their larger counterparts, which in turn affects evaluator judgments. 5.4
Effect of Benchmark

The choice of safety benchmark impacts conclusions on changes in model safety relative to the initial model.

As a final test of measurement variability, we compare harmfulness rates across the four subsets of safety benchmarks in our evaluation set: BeaverTails-Eval, BeaverTails-Intent, SORRY-Bench, and XS-Test-unsafe.

Figure
4
plots the mean (across LoRA hyperparameters) harmfulness rate, as computed on each benchmark subset, for Llama-8B model checkpoints fine-tuned on the Alpaca15k, GSM8k, and Science10k datasets. While the general trend in harmfulness remains fairly consistent (decreasing through epochs), conclusions on how the safety of fine-tuned models compares to that of the
initial model
can change depending on the benchmark used. As the clearest example, Llama-8B fine-tuned on the GSM8k dataset has a final harmfulness rate well below that of the base model as measured on SORRY-Bench; the opposite is true when measured on the BeaverTails-Intent subset. This instability is particularly relevant in the context of research that aims to maintain model safety under fine-tuning (or other forms of benign downstream modifications).

5.5
Safety-Capability Trade-offs

Safety-preserving variations of LoRA can lower model capability.

In practical settings, one would like to maximize accuracy while minimizing harmfulness. We compare the effectiveness of one popular mitigation method, SafeLoRA, in balancing these objectives under different fine-tuning settings. Our goal here is not to perform a comprehensive evaluation of different mitigation methods, but to demonstrate the variability of outcomes associated with such efforts.

Table
1
shows the validation accuracy and the harmfulness rates for model checkpoints fine-tuned using LoRA and SafeLoRA. We select the LoRA checkpoint with the highest accuracy across hyperparameter settings to be the optimal choice; for SafeLoRA, we take two approaches: maximizing accuracy (
SafeLoRA-acc
), or minimizing harmfulness (
SafeLoRA-safe
).

For both the Llama models, we see relatively similar trends for all four fine-tuning datasets: SafeLoRA improves safety but consistently lowers model capability. On the Science10k dataset, the safest alternative lowers accuracy by nearly 10%; even the best-performing SafeLoRA checkpoint (
SafeLoRA-acc
) lags behind by 8%.
On the ARC and BoolQ datasets, we note that SafeLoRA additionally mitigates the coherence degradation observed with fine-tuning.

The Qwen models, which are incidentally less studied in the safe fine-tuning literature compared to the Llama family of models, present a different story. On GSM8k, SafeLoRA improves both accuracy and safety of models – though with slight trade-offs between checkpoints. On the other hand, for Qwen-8B model fine-tuned on Science10k, we observe a marked degradation in safety (along with accuracy) with SafeLoRA.

6
Discussion

Our findings make the case for re-evaluating how we approach research questions at the intersection of fine-tuning and model safety. Depending on the benchmarks and evaluators used to measure safety, benign fine-tuning can both degrade and improve model safety in relation to the initial model. Fine-tuning for more epochs generally benefits models in terms of downstream performance, and in turn has a favorable effect on safety. The mechanisms behind this recovery in safety guardrails are not yet clear, and merits further study.

We showed that fine-tuning for a specific task, especially tasks with a constrained output, such as binary (yes/no) or multiple-choice answers, can negatively affect the ability of the model to generate coherent free-form outputs, resulting in discrepant responses to safety benchmark prompts. In such cases, standard safety benchmarking may be ineffective, and different types of prompts and/or safety evaluation protocols are required. We recommend that perplexity is always measured on the model generations to contextualize the safety metrics. Another approach can be to re-cast prompt-response type benchmarks into formats that align with that of the fine-tuning task, which better captures risks from deployment in real-world use-cases. In relevant recent work,
7
show how reformulating open-ended harmful prompt requests as forced-choice MCQs can systematically bypass refusal behavior.

The variation in conclusions drawn from the use of different safety benchmarks compounds evaluation issues noted in past work, where inference time sampling parameters have been shown to play an out-sized role in safety measurements.
This variability makes inference-time metrics difficult to interpret in the context of understanding why safety guardrails degrade, and how to mitigate these changes. SafeLoRA presents a relatively straightforward idea to retain safety behavior, however, we still observe a large variance in performance depending on models and datasets.
We see this as motivating the need for more intrinsically-motivated measures of model behavior, for example, through mechanistic interpretability methods that evaluate safety behavior as a property of internal model representations.

Limitations

Despite our attempt to expand on the effect of various parameters on fine-tuning and safety, our empirical choices are still limited in the number, size, and family of base models that were tested. We also vary only a small number of the possible hyperparameters that can be optimized in model fine-tuning; further, our mode of domain adaptation is restricted to supervised LoRA fine-tuning. Recent works also show how adaptation methods like RAG (retrieval-augmented generation) can change the safety profiles of models
2
.

Our selection of fine-tuning datasets was restricted in order to measure task accuracy in a simple way; however, we noted multiple cases of slight differences in response format across models, which could lead to our measures underestimating performance. Our operationalization of task capability is also limited in terms of test datasets used, and in the use of perplexity as a measure of general capability and coherence.

Finally, we note that all our evaluations are restricted to the English language. It is entirely possible that fine-tuning can lead to different effects on safety in different languages; we note this as an important and relevant topic of future research.

Alpaca15k

Prompt
: Describe the “live parameter update” feature of GPT-3.

Response
: The live parameter update, or "in-context learning," feature of GPT-3, as implemented by OpenAI, allows the model to incorporate information from the provided prompt or context and fine-tune its predictions on-the-fly. With this feature, GPT-3 adapts its responses based on the most recent text it has seen, meaning that it can learn and improve its performance within a specific conversation, responding more accurately to the needs and intent of the user. While GPT-3 can’t do backpropagation-update of its weights, it can pick up on certain cues and redress biases- taking into consideration its prior response - by generating texts that re-balance the conversation in the desired direction. This feature makes GPT-3 highly versatile and capable of quickly adapting to new topics or conversational styles, making it an ideal tool for creating realistic and engaging conversational AI experiences.

Science10k

Prompt
: Answer the following multiple choice question. The last line of your response should be in the following format: ’Answer: A/B/C/D’ (e.g. ’Answer: A’).

In the context of high-energy particle physics, which of the following statements about gauge anomalies in quantum field theories is correct?

A) Anomalies in gauge symmetries always lead to the breakdown of the conservation of the associated current.
B) The presence of gauge anomalies can be completely eliminated by the introduction of appropriate counterterms.
C) Gauge anomalies are only significant in theories with chiral fermions and can be detected by the triangle diagram in the effective action.
D) Gauge anomalies can be avoided by ensuring that the theory is renormalizable.

Response
: Gauge anomalies arise when a symmetry that is exact at the classical level fails to be conserved after quantisation. This can happen only when the fermionic content of the theory is chiral – i.e. left
-
and right
-
handed fields transform differently under the gauge group. In a vector
-
like theory the contributions of left
-
and right
-
handed fermions cancel, so no gauge anomaly appears. The anomalous violation of a gauge current is captured at one
-
loop by the triangle diagram with three external gauge legs (the Adler–Bell–Jackiw diagram). Because gauge anomalies signal an inconsistency of the quantum theory, they must cancel; they cannot be removed by adding counterterms, and renormalisability alone does not guarantee their absence. Hence statement C is the correct one.

**Answer: C**

Appendix B
Experimental Settings

Apart from the LoRA target modules and rank, additional parameters relevant to fine-tuning are kept fixed in our experiments to values recommended in the official fine-tuning documentation in the Unsloth library
12
12

12

https://unsloth.ai/docs/get-started/fine-tuning-llms-guide/lora-hyperparameters-guide
: alpha (equal to the rank), learning rate (2e-04, linear scheduler with warmup ratio of 0.05), weight decay (0.01), maximum gradient norm (1.0). We use the AdamW optimizer, and an effective batch size of 16, sometimes reduced to 8 or 4 to stay within the GPU RAM limit.
All experiments were run on a single 80GB A100 GPU on Google Colab.
