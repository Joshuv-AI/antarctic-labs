 Introduction
 Executive Summary
 This report presents the outcomes of a technology demonstration and durability assessment of automated
 duct sealing aimed at improving HVAC system efficiency. The demonstration was conducted at the Brien
 McMahon U.S. Courthouse and Federal Building in Bridgeport, Connecticut, where automated duct sealing
 was completed between January 6 and 24, 2025. The process achieved a 79% reduction in total duct
 leakage, corresponding to 26,670.8 cfm sealed across 21 HVAC zones, with individual zone reductions
 reaching up to 92%.

 A durability evaluation was performed at the Dan M. Russell Jr. U.S. Federal Building and Courthouse in
 Gulfport, Mississippi, to assess long-term sealing performance. After approximately 4 years (based on
 sealing completed in February–March 2021 and durability testing conducted in December 2025), only a
 0.5%–3% increase in duct leakage was observed, remaining well below the 5% degradation threshold and
 confirming the long-term reliability of the automated sealing technology.

 The federal office building at Bridgeport, Connecticut, was monitored using several sensors to monitor the
 energy delivered by an air-handling unit (AHU) before and after the automated duct sealing. The data
 collected from the building showed that energy consumption for heating was lower after duct sealing
 compared with that of the baseline case under the same environmental temperature conditions. Whole-
 building energy simulation results showed HVAC energy savings of 15.4%, resulting in annual cost savings
 of $0.29/ft2. The payback period of the technology, based on an installation cost of $2.25/ft2, was 7.8
 years. The results show that automated duct sealing can be a cost-effective, durable, and scalable solution
 for improving HVAC efficiency and reducing energy consumption in existing federal facilities and
 commercial buildings.

 Background
 HVAC duct leakage represents a major source of energy loss and performance degradation in commercial
 buildings. Studies show that commercial buildings can have an average leakage rate in supply ducts of
 approximately 25% of the flow through the supply fan1, causing HVAC systems to operate for longer periods
 and at higher capacities than those of normal operation to maintain thermal comfort. This increased load
 results in elevated energy consumption, higher operational costs, and reduced system lifespan while also
 affecting indoor air quality (IAQ) and occupant comfort.

 Traditional duct sealing approaches rely on manual inspection and repair, which are often labor-intensive,
 time-consuming, and limited by the inaccessibility of concealed ductwork. Consequently, achieving
 complete sealing coverage using conventional methods is challenging, particularly in large or complex
 HVAC systems.

 This project aims to demonstrate automated duct-sealing technology in a commercial building setting to
 address these limitations. The study evaluates the technology’s effectiveness in reducing duct leakage,
 improving HVAC system performance, and supporting energy cost-savings objectives in federal facilities.

 Technology Description
 The automated aerosol-based duct sealing technology, provided by Aeroseal, offers a novel approach to
 sealing HVAC duct leaks efficiently and comprehensively. This process (Figures 1 and 2) involves
 pressurizing the duct system and introducing an aerosolized sealant into the airflow. The sealant particles
 are carried by the moving air to leakage points, where they adhere to the edges of gaps, cracks, and holes,
 building up to form durable seals.

 The system has the ability to seal leaks in hard-to-reach areas using a single access point for each specific
 zone, making it effective for complex duct networks. Unlike conventional manual sealing methods, which
 rely on physical access, visual inspection, and installer judgment, the automated duct sealing process is
 computer controlled. The system continuously regulates sealing parameters based on real-time feedback
 from calibrated pressure and airflow sensors connected to the duct network.

 Throughout the sealing operation, the software continuously measures total duct leakage by monitoring
 airflow required to maintain a prescribed test pressure, allowing the system to quantify leakage reduction
 as sealing progresses. This information is displayed in real time via a graphical user interface (Figure 3),
 providing instantaneous feedback on baseline leakage, current leakage rate, percent reduction achieved,
 and estimated remaining leakage. The automated control logic terminates sealant injection once predefined
 leakage reduction targets are met.

 Compared with traditional manual methods, automated duct sealing requires significantly less time and
 causes minimal disruption to building operations. The process is noninvasive because it does not require
 disassembly or major modification of existing duct systems. Post-sealing verification through before-and-
 after measurements confirms the effectiveness of the process—often sealing up to 95% of total duct
 leakage.

 In addition to lowering energy use from improved HVAC efficiency, the reduction in duct leakage leads to
 enhanced indoor air quality, and decreased humidity-related risks such as mold growth. Overall, the
 automated duct-sealing technology presents a cost-effective and scalable solution for improving building
 performance and achieving energy conservation targets across federal building portfolios.

 M&V Design
 Overview

 The M&V approach was developed to quantify the energy performance improvements resulting from the
 automated aerosol-based duct sealing technology demonstration and to verify the long-term durability of
 the sealing application.

 The design follows the protocols outlined in ASHRAE Guideline 14, ASHRAE 90.1, and International Energy
 Conservation Code 2021, ensuring standardized data collection, normalization, and analysis procedures.

 The M&V activities consist of the following:

 ● Technology demonstration—conducted at the Brien McMahon U.S. Courthouse and Federal Building
 (Bridgeport, Connecticut), focusing on short-term duct leakage reduction and HVAC performance
 effects

 ● Durability assessment—performed at the Dan M. Russell Jr. U.S. Federal Building and Courthouse
 (Gulfport, Mississippi), evaluating long-term retention of airtightness over 4 years since initial
 sealing (January 2021)

 Third-Party Pre- and Post-sealing Duct Air Delivery Performance Tests

 The airflow-based duct leakage assessment, conducted by a third-party tester, was performed using a field
 airflow balance approach to quantify effective leakage under actual operating conditions. The test involved
 measuring supply airflow at two locations: (1) upstream near the AHU using an Airfoil flow station coupled
 with an Evergreen Telemetry pressure/velocity/flow (PVF) module, and (2) downstream within the served
 space after the duct system, representing delivered airflow. The results, summarized in Table 8, indicate
 that a significant portion of supply air was lost within the duct system before sealing, whereas post-sealing
 measurements show a marked reduction in this discrepancy, reflecting improved duct tightness and
 delivery efficiency.

 However, this method’s results cannot be directly compared with those of the pressure-based duct leakage
 test. The third-party airflow balance method was conducted by measuring airflow at two locations The
 static pressure conditions during the PVF measurements can vary between the upstream and downstream
 measurement locations because the HVAC system operates under normal dynamic conditions with active
 dampers, diffusers, and airflow redistribution. In contrast, the Aeroseal test isolates and seals the duct
 system, including diffusers and terminal openings, and maintains constant test pressure throughout the
 sealing process, allowing leakage to be quantified under controlled pressurization conditions. ASHRAE
 Standard 215 distinguishes these approaches by separating as duct leakage testing from operating HVAC
 air distribution system.

 As such, two location points tests (upstream and downstream tests) reflect airflow losses within the
 measured portion of the distribution system. The PVF method evaluates improvements in actual delivered
 airflow during HVAC operation, which can be influenced by residual leakage under operating pressure,
 increased static pressure following duct sealing, fan operating point shifts, VAV damper response, airflow
 redistribution, and terminal balancing effects throughout the air distribution system. In contrast, the
 measurement conducted during the duct sealing process was obtained at a standardized test pressure of 3
 in. w.g. (747.27 Pa) and represented total duct leakage capacity under controlled pressurization. The duct
 sealing process was carried out under a constant duct pressure of 3 in. w.g., resulting in leakage reductions
 of 80%, 86%, and 87% for Zones 31, 32, and 33, respectively. Third-party testing reported post installation air
 leakage of 19.19%, 5.07%, and 8.0% for Zones 31, 32, and 33, as summarized in Table 8.

 Because the two methods differ in test boundary, pressure regime, measurement principle, and system
 coverage, their results should be interpreted as complementary indicators as duct leakage is one of the
 main factors of duct air delivery performance of sealing rather than as directly equivalent measurements.

 Energy Savings Indicators

 The heating energy consumption for the AHU calculated from measured data was used to evaluate the
 energy savings potential of the duct air sealing. Only heating energy consumption was analyzed because
 baseline data were available only from November 2024 (when the AHU replacement was performed) until
 January 20, 2025 (when duct sealing was performed). Hence, data when heating was needed (i.e., from
 November 2024 until February 2025 and November 2025 until February 2026) were used for analyzing the
 potential for energy consumption reduction. The heating energy consumption per heating degree days
 (HDD) with base temperature of 65°F was calculated for normalizing the effect of weather during this
 period. Results for the HDD normalized heating energy consumption (Figure 9) show variation in the results
 across different months without clear trends for energy savings. This potentially was caused by differences
 in factors such as other weather variables (e.g., solar radiation), occupancy patterns, or building control
 processes. A scatter plot (Figure 10) was used to analyze how the heating energy consumption is
 distributed across ambient temperature conditions. Figure 10 shows a number of hours in which the energy
 consumption is very high for the baseline compared with that of Aeroseal (i.e., after duct sealing) for the
 same dry bulb temperature. This suggests that use of Aeroseal technology for duct sealing reduces the
 energy consumption in buildings. Because exactly quantifying the energy savings from duct sealing is
 difficult, owing to the influence of unknown variables, whole-building energy simulation was used to predict
 the annual energy savings potential and cost effect of Aeroseal.

 Qualitative Results
 To complement the quantitative performance data, qualitative feedback was gathered through a structured
 post-sealing survey administered to building managers and BAS operators at both test sites. The
 questionnaire focused on seven thematic areas: general experience, quality of work, work coordination,
 system performance, cost/value perception, post-cleanup, and general feedback. Three participants who
 were primarily building managers provided feedback based on their assessments of the effect of duct
 sealing on these areas. A meeting with the focus group further clarified the context of the response, and
 data were obtained for hot and cold calls from occupants to infer the effect of duct sealing on occupant
 comfort.

 General experience and quality of work: The quality of work was provided with the highest rating with
 respect to cleanliness of the spaces post-sealing. Zone 26 was excluded from the sealing process because
 of a large opening; further investigation was not conducted because of the presence of asbestos above the
 ceiling.

 Work coordination and disruption: Moderate effort was required for planning and executing duct sealing
 because after-hours work required coordination with tenants and operations and maintenance staff. The
 level of coordination needed was significantly less that that required for the full replacement of ducts,
 which would require removing/ripping out the ceiling. Pulling building technicians from regular daytime
 maintenance work and asking tenants to accommodate after-hours work were seen as minor to moderate
 building disruptions. Another disruption would be needed for the HVAC system to be temporarily adjusted

Oak Ridge National Laboratory: Automated Duct Sealing (Aeroseal) Page 23

 or taken offline during duct sealing; this would not be a significant issue if work was done after hours, when
 the building was not occupied.

 System performance: The survey filled out by building managers did not provide a clear indication of
 improvement in comfort conditions for the occupants. However, an analysis of the total number of hot/cold
 complaints from occupants showed a reduction of complaints per month was observed, from 12 complaints
 between September 2024 and January 2025 (an average of 2.6 complaints per month) to 15 complaints
 between February 2025 and February 2026 (an average of 1.15 complaints per month). Duct sealing was

 Cost-Effectiveness
 Cost-effectiveness of automated duct sealing was calculated by estimating the installation cost as an
 expense and the energy cost reduction as a benefit. Annual energy savings calculated from the building
 energy simulation were used to calculate the cost savings from energy reduction.

 Aeroseal estimated the cost for installation at $2.25/ft2. The cost savings from a reduction in HVAC energy
 consumption was calculated from the energy savings results shown in Table 12. The cost rate of electricity
 and natural gas was based on GSA rates. This resulted in overall energy cost savings of $0.29/(ft2 ∙year)
 with test bed location utility rates and $0.19/(ft2 ∙year) with GSA average utility rates for an HVAC
 application. Based on the installation cost data and energy cost savings calculated, the payback period
 from this technology was 7.8 years at the test bed location, as shown in Table 12.

 The Aeroseal duct sealing cost framework is generally based on a typical planning-level range of
 approximately $1.50–$3.50 per gross square foot (GSF). For federal facilities comparable to the Brien
 McMahon U.S. Courthouse and Federal Building, a narrower and more representative range of $2.00–
 $2.50/GSF is appropriate for early-stage feasibility assessments to conduct a prior cost estimation. This
 refined range reflects project-specific conditions such as prevailing wage requirements, security protocols,
 and operational constraints commonly encountered in federal buildings.

 The installed cost structure is predominantly labor-driven rather than material-driven. Major cost
 components include mobilization, system isolation and preparation, installation setup, sealing execution,

Oak Ridge National Laboratory: Automated Duct Sealing (Aeroseal) Page 24

 and pre- and post-seal testing and verification. Material costs, including sealant and consumables,
 represent a comparatively smaller fraction of the total project cost. This distribution highlights the
 importance of field execution complexity, coordination, and access conditions in determining overall project
 cost, particularly in existing and occupied buildings.

 Several variables significantly influence the observed pricing range. These include duct system
 characteristics (e.g., system type, pressure class, and layout complexity), building access conditions (e.g.,
 ceiling type, shaft accessibility, and security restrictions), and project execution constraints (e.g., work-hour
 limitations or phased implementation). Additionally, the extent of duct sealing relative to total building area
 is a critical factor; in many cases, the treated ductwork may represent only a portion of the building gross
 square footage (GSF), which can affect the interpretation of normalized cost metrics.

 For planning and modeling purposes, a central estimate of approximately $2.25/GSF is appropriate, with
 sensitivity analyses conducted across the $2.00–$2.50/GSF range to capture variability in scope and
 execution conditions. Further refinement of project-specific costs requires detailed mechanical system
 information, including AHU schedules, duct distribution layouts, airflow rates, and system zoning. Such data
 enables alignment of cost estimates with engineering parameters (e.g., cost per cubic feet per minute or
 cost per linear foot of duct) and support more accurate integration with energy savings estimates and M&V
 frameworks.

 Although Aeroseal costs can be expressed on a cost per linear foot basis when duct length data are
 available, retrofit pricing is typically developed using building floor area and project-specific factors such as
 HVAC system complexity, access constraints, isolation needs, labor, and phasing. For the Brien McMahon
 U.S. Courthouse and Federal Building, duct length was considered within the overall constructability
 assessment, but cost estimates were primarily developed on an installed cost per square foot basis.

 Conclusions
 Summary Findings
 The technology demonstration at the Brien McMahon U.S. Courthouse and Federal Building has shown a
 reduction in duct leakage in a complex commercial HVAC system. Across 21 zones served by AHU-S1, the
 process achieved a 79% reduction in total leakage (~26,670 ft3/min sealed), with multiple zones exhibiting
 reductions greater than 90%.

 The data collected from the building showed that energy consumption for heating was lower after duct
 sealing compared with that of the baseline case under the same environmental temperature conditions.
 Whole-building energy simulation results showed HVAC energy savings of 15.4%, resulting in annual cost
 savings of $0.3/ft2. The payback periods of the technology based on installation costs of $2/ft2–$2.5/ft2
 were 6.6–8.3 years.

 Long-term durability was also demonstrated at the Dan M. Russell Jr. U.S. Federal Building and Courthouse,
 where ducts sealed in early 2021 showed only a 0.5%–3% increase in leakage over nearly 4 years.
 Qualitative feedback from building operators indicated minimal disruption, manageable cleanup needs, and
 no adverse effects on HVAC performance.

 Lessons Learned and Best Practices
 ● Deploying the sealing process in occupied spaces underscored the value of planning work during
 low-occupancy hours to minimize disruptions.

 ● The automated system’s noninvasive nature proved beneficial because it reduced the need for duct
 disassembly and limited interference with concurrent renovation efforts.

 ● Post-sealing verification was essential for validating performance and should be treated as a
 standard step in all implementations.

 ● For future deployments, best practices include conducting thorough pre-sealing inspections to
 identify potential access issues and hazardous building materials (e.g., asbestos, lead) and
 establishing standardized communication channels among contractors, facility managers, and M&V
 teams.

 ● The demonstration highlighted that automated duct sealing is most successful when paired with
 careful site selection, detailed preplanning, and structured M&V protocols.

Oak Ridge National Laboratory: Automated Duct Sealing (Aeroseal) Page 27

 Deployment Recommendations
 ● Prioritize buildings with minimal operational disruption and accessible duct systems.

 ● Schedule sealing work during off-peak hours or low-occupancy periods to minimize disruption.

 ● Maintain clear communication among contractors, facility managers, and M&V teams throughout
 the project.

 ● Incorporate periodic spot checks to validate long-term seal integrity without full-system retesting.

 ● Prepare a plan to make timely duct repairs if necessary, especially if known access issues or
 asbestos are present.

 ● Focus on buildings with high HVAC energy consumption per unit floor area.

 ● For old buildings with older or defective AHU and that have not had duct retrofitting, prior or
 simultaneous AHU replacement would be beneficial.
