ABSTRACT

The Activated Materials Laboratory (AML), located in the Long Beamline Building of the
Advanced Photon Source (APS) at Argonne National Laboratory (ANL), provides a centralized
radiological capability for preparing, handling, and supporting synchrotron experiments on
activated materials. As a Nuclear Science User Facilities (NSUF) partner facility, the AML enables
the receipt of radioactive shipments, open-form sample handling, encapsulation, transport of
specimens to and from APS beamlines, and experimentation with dedicated equipment. The
partnership includes the APS 1-ID and 20-ID beamlines, which provide high-energy x-ray
scattering, tomography, and diffraction microscopy techniques for ex-situ, in-situ, and grain-
resolved three-dimensional (3D) characterization. All samples supported through the AML and
partner beamlines must meet radiological limits of less than 100 mrem/h at 30 cm.

This FY26 progress report summarizes the first full year of AML operations as an NSUF partner
facility and highlights progress in both user support and capability development. By the end of
FY26, the AML had received a total of 10 NSUF-awarded projects, including 2 Consolidated
Innovative Nuclear Research (CINR), 7 Rapid Turnaround Experiment (RTE), and 1 Super RTE
projects. Beamtime was fully delivered for 3 RTE projects and partially delivered for 1 CINR
project, demonstrating successful workflows for receipt, encapsulation, beamline transfer, and
radiological experiment execution. These efforts demonstrated safe radiological experiments at
APS beamlines for samples with dose rates above the historical 5 mrem/h threshold and now up
to 100 mrem/h at 30 cm, marking an important milestone for neutron-irradiated materials research.

During FY26, the AML also expanded its experimental capabilities. A Psylotech xTS load frame
with in-grip rotation was deployed for room-temperature mechanical testing with three-
dimensional x-ray characterization during interrupted loading. A Linkam TS1500V vacuum heater
was commissioned for thermally driven studies, and temperature calibration experiments were
performed to establish specimen-relevant thermal profiles. In parallel, a customized split-tube
furnace for high-temperature mechanical testing entered commissioning, and initial work began
on robotic sample handling to reduce worker dose and improve operational efficiency. Data
workflow and beamline operations continued to mature.

Together, these developments demonstrate that the AML is becoming a unique national resource
for safe, efficient, and scientifically advanced characterization of activated materials at the APS.

1 Introduction

Current and advanced nuclear reactors rely on fuels and structural materials that must withstand
neutron irradiation, mechanical stress, high temperatures, thermal gradients, corrosion, and other
environmental factors [1–3]. Neutron irradiation receives special attention because even low doses
can significantly change materials’ performance. Traditional post-irradiation examination methods
provide valuable information but are often destructive and limited to surfaces or small volumes,
making it difficult to observe evolving three-dimensional microstructures or in-situ behavior. In
recent years, high-energy synchrotron x-rays received growing interest from the nuclear materials
community for their deep penetration, high brilliance, and nondestructive, multimodal capabilities
that enable 3D and in-situ studies across multiple length scales. However, handling radioactivity
poses a major challenge to the study of neutron-irradiated samples at public synchrotron x-ray
beamlines due to the stringent safety protocols.

To bridge this gap, we opened the Activated Materials Laboratory (AML) at the Advanced Photon
Source (APS), Argonne National Laboratory (ANL) as a centralized facility for radioactive
specimen preparation for APS beamline experiments. The AML is located next to the 20-ID high-
energy x-ray microscope (HEXM) beamline in the Long Beamline Building (LBB) (Figure 1.1),
which was constructed as part of the APS upgrade (APS-U) project between 2021 and 2022. The
AML’s design, construction, and continued operation is funded by the DOE Office of Nuclear
Energy, Nuclear Science User Facilities (NSUF) program. In July 2025, the AML achieved
radiological operational status and officially became an NSUF partner user facility, supporting the
receiving and shipping of activated samples, assembling and disassembling of containment
systems, and testing and maintaining of environmental apparatus for radiological experiments.
Details about the AML’s functionality and capability can be found in Ref. [4].

The partnership extends to two premier high-energy x-ray beamlines at the APS, namely 1-ID and
20-ID. Those two beamlines provide monochromatic x-rays in 35 to 120 keV range, offering
scattering and tomography techniques for ex-situ, in-situ, and grain-resolved 3D measurements. A
partnership agreement has been established between the AML and the APS, which allocates a
fraction of 1-ID and 20-ID beamtime to NSUF users.

This report provides an update to the first year’s successful operation of the AML as an NSUF
partner facility. Focuses will be given to the status of user projects and new capability
development. Perspectives on future directions will also be provided.

2.2 Status of projects

2.2.1 24-31412 (CINR) – UIUC (partially delivered)
Project 24-31412 (CINR) seeks to elucidate the mechanisms governing the initiation and
progression of irradiation-assisted stress corrosion cracking (IASCC) in austenitic stainless steel
baffle-former bolts harvested from a commercial pressurized water reactor (PWR). The project
team includes two universities, the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign (UIUC) and the
University of Alabama (UA), and two national laboratories, ANL and Oak Ridge National
Laboratory (ORNL). A total of 10-15 days of beam time was requested, reflecting uncertainty in
beam conditions associated with the upgraded APS at the time of proposal preparation. The first
batches of specimens, consisting of small cutouts from two bolts, arrived at the AML in August
2026. The specimens were provided by the U.S. Department of Energy Office of Nuclear Energy’s
Light Water Reactor Sustainability (LWRS) program.

To date, two beam times have been allocated to characterize these specimens. In September 2025,
five days were allocated at the 1-ID-E beamline (Figure 2.1). Wide-angle x-ray scattering (WAXS),
far-field high-energy x-ray diffraction microscopy (ff-HEDM), near-field HEDM (nf-HEDM), and
micro-computed tomography (𝜇CT) were used to evaluate irradiation-induced microstructural
changes, identify crack locations, and characterize overall grain structure. In February 2026, an
additional three days were allocated at 1-ID-E to perform 𝜇CT and high-resolution nf-HEDM
scans of a selected volume in one specimen volume containing an IASCC crack. Preliminary data
processing successfully reconstructed grains from the nf-HEDM measurements. When combined
with the 𝜇 CT results, these reconstructions provide a viable path toward understanding the
relationship between crack development and grain-scale crystallographic characteristics. Figure
2.2 showcased the workflow and presented preliminary results.

The September 2025 experiment marked several important milestones for the project and for
AML/APS operations. It was the first AML user experiment in which specimens were received
directly by AML, encapsulated at AML, and transported directly to the APS beamline by AML
staff using a mobile cart. As a result, the users were not required to be physically present for
specimen receipt, handling, or transport and could instead focus on beam-time execution and
scientific analysis. In addition, this experiment was the first at APS in which radioactive specimens

 4

with gamma dose rates greater than 5 mR/h at 30 cm, but less than 100 mR/h at 30 cm, were
studied at a public beamline. While the samples were present, the beamline hutch was temporarily
designated a Radiation Area (RA). By permitting this higher dose-rate range, APS has expanded
access for neutron-irradiated materials and enabled a new phase of nuclear materials research at
its user beamlines.

This accomplishment was recognized by the U.S. Department of Energy Office of Nuclear Energy
as a Nuclear Milestone [5].

2.2.2 25-5409 (RTE) – INL (delivered)
Project 25-5409 (RTE), led by researchers from Idaho National Laboratory (INL), explores the
hypothesis that neutron irradiation alters the dislocation and grain structure of MA957 steel,
impacting its mechanical properties, even if oxide precipitates remain unchanged. Three neutron-
irradiated MA957 tensile specimens were requested from the Nuclear Fuel and Materials Library
(NFML). Room temperature tensile loading was conducted on the irradiated and unirradiated
specimens with in-situ WAXS measurement at the 20-ID-E beamline between April 16 and April
19, 2026 (Figure 2.3). X-ray data analysis was on-going at the time of writing this report.

This experiment was the first in-situ experiment from AML users and was the first radiological
experiment at the newly constructed 20-ID-E HEXM beamline.

2.2.3 25-5313 (RTE) – ANL (delivered)

Project 25-5313 (RTE), led by researchers at ANL, seeks to elucidate how irradiation-induced
defects evolve and interact with dislocations during tensile loading, and how these interactions
influence grain- and subgrain-scale behavior as well as bulk mechanical performance. The study
employs multimodal characterization methods, including advanced techniques such as point-
focused HEDM (pf-HEDM) and Bragg coherent diffraction imaging (BCDI), to quantify subgrain-
scale behavior. Two neutron-irradiated NF709 tensile specimens were requested from the NFML.
Beam time was conducted from August 2 to August 5, 2026, at the 20-ID-D beamline.

A feasibility study determined that the grain sizes in the specimens, which were on the order of
several tens of micrometers, were too large for BCDI measurements, even at the long-beamline
configuration of 20-ID-E. As a result, pf-HEDM was adopted as an alternative. With an
approximate spatial resolution of 1 𝜇 m, pf-HEDM provides sufficient resolution to probe
subgrain-scale deformation behavior. Figure 2.4 shows measurements of a 50 𝜇m × 50 𝜇m
volume within the gauge section of an irradiated NF709 specimen prior to tensile loading. Multiple
grains were captured within this volume, demonstrating the capability of the technique to
nondestructively measure subgrain-scale plasticity events and quantify deformation heterogeneity.
Results from the in-situ loading experiment are forthcoming.

2.2.4 25-5381 (RTE) – Purdue (delivered)

Project 25-5381 (RTE), led by researchers at Purdue University, seeks to use synchrotron x-ray
characterization to examine crack-growth-tested compact tension (CT) specimens of recrystallized,
additively manufactured 316L stainless steel following neutron irradiation. The objective is to
generate grain maps along the crack path using a combination of 𝜇CT, nf-HEDM, and ff-HEDM
to determine the origin of the out-of-plane IASCC propagation observed in these specimens. Two
tested CT specimens were requested from NFML and sectioned at INL into small sticks suitable
for beamline experiment, as shown in Figure 2.5. Each stick contains the fracture surface of the
primary crack as well as segments of a secondary crack. The specimens were received at the AML
in July 2026, and beamtime was delivered between July 30 and August 2, 2026. Data analysis is
ongoing.

3 FY26 capability development and deployment
3.1 Deployment of a load frame with in-grip rotation for room-temperature testing
A Psylotech xTS load frame with in-grip rotation capability was purchased to meet two objectives:
(1) to enable 3D scans during mechanical testing without obscuring rotation angles or interfering
with detectors through load-frame column shadowing, and (2) to support the future addition of a
furnace for high-temperature testing without space or cabling constraints, while preserving full 3D
scanning capability. In FY26, efforts are focused on aligning the rotation axes under both unloaded
and loaded conditions and integrating load-frame control with the APS beamline control system,
with particular emphasis on synchronizing x-ray shutters with the interrupted loading sequence.
Figure 3.1 (a) shows the schematic of the load frame and a double-encapsulated grip design for a
sub-sized tensile specimen. Figure 3.1 (b) shows orthogonal views of a μCT reconstruction around
the middle section of a tensile specimen made with an additively manufactured stainless steel that
has built-in pores of 10 μm on average. The μCT scan was performed when the specimen was
loaded to ~ 400 N (355 MPa). This scan, along with the series of successful μCT scans performed
during incremental loading, demonstrated good alignment between the rotation and loading axes,
as well as good rotational stability under both unloaded and loaded conditions.

 (b) Orthogonal views of a μCT reconstruction around the middle section of a tensile specimen made with an
 additively manufactured stainless steel that has built-in pores of 10 μm on average.
Following verification of rotational stability, the load frame was deployed for interrupted-loading
experiments in which HEDM scans were performed during deformation pauses. A handshaking
protocol was established between the load frame and the beamline control system to synchronize
x-ray exposures with load-frame operation. Figure 3.2 (a) shows one loading sequence for a
wrought 316 stainless steel specimen; the full experiment was conducted overnight without human
intervention. At each interruption, labeled 1 through 12, four ff-HEDM scans of consecutive
volumes were performed around the center of the gauge section (Figure 3.2 (b)). The scans started
after a deliberate waiting period for the stress relaxation to settle. As seen in Figure 3.2 (b), for
each scan, the stress fluctuates around ±5 MPa, which is considered as insignificant to cause stress

After stitching grains across overlapping scan volumes, a total of 2208 unique grains were
identified, which were used to reconstruct a realistic-looking material volume.

3.2 Deployment of a vacuum heater

A Linkam TS1500V heater was purchased to facilitate the study of temperature-induced dynamic
processes in activated materials. The heater has a max temperature setpoint of 1500 °C and a max
heating rate of 200 °C/min. The heating chamber, measured 7.9 mm (diameter) × 5.2 mm (depth),
has three environmental options: 1) in air, or 2) purged with inert gas, or 3) under vacuum. A
Varian-SH110 dry scroll pump equipped with a HEPA filter was used to pump the chamber, able
to achieve 20-50 mTorr under continuous pumping.

chamber being heated to 900 °C (bottom right).

  Figure 3.3. Photos of a Linkam TS1500V heater being set up under vacuum on the sample stage at the 20-ID-E

Knowing the actual temperature on the specimen is critically important in interpreting a material’s
dynamic responses to heating. We performed a series of temperature profiling experiments at the
20-ID-E beamline using materials with known coefficients of thermal expansion (CTEs) such as
ceria [6] and 316 stainless steel [7]. The ceria powder was purchased under NIST standard SRM
674b. The small 316SS tabs were cut from a plate made by laser powder bed fusion (LPBF)
followed by solution annealing, knowing that such a material did not undergo recrystallization or
significant grain growth even at 1000 °C [8]. The specimens were placed inside the heating
chamber either encapsulated in quartz holders or as a bare tab (Figure 3.4). The heater was
programmed to go from room temperature to 1000°C with stops at intermediate temperatures for
5 minutes each time. All tests were run under vacuum with active pumping. Continuous WAXS
data were recorded during the entire heating/cooling processes. The lattice constant values were
obtained from diffraction peak fitting and sample temperatures were calculated using the lattice
constant values and the CTE values. Figure 3.5 (a) shows the temperature profiles from the Linkam
command and the actual measurement on the samples. The plateau values in the temperature
profiles are plotted against the Linkam command temperature in Figure 3.5 (b).
It is concluded that the actual temperature on the samples is ~ 100°C lower in 300-400°C command
temperature range which goes up to ~ 250°C in the 950-1000°C command temperature range.

3.3 Updates from partner beamlines

In FY 26, the 1-ID beamline had upgrades to a new monochromator, white beam slits and filters
during FY26. The monochromator increases the lower energy range from 42 to 35 keV,
maintaining the 120keV higher energy range, with a change from a vertical to horizontal scattering
geometry. This should allow for higher spatial resolution in the vertical direction, to sub-micron
beam sizes, since the vertical beam is less perturbed.

4 Data workflow

In FY26, data flow capabilities were significantly advanced at partner beamlines (1-ID and 20-ID)
to better support users during experiments and accelerate time to insight. Near-real-time μCT
reconstructions became readily available during beamtime, giving users rapid feedback that
directly informs critical experimental decisions and helps ensure the most effective use of valuable
beamtime. For WAXS measurements, the data workflow was streamlined from calibration through
image integration and peak profile analysis, reducing barriers between acquisition and
interpretation. HEDM data processing remains more computationally intensive, often requiring
access to powerful local clusters or DOE supercomputing resources for data reduction,
reconstruction, and visualization.

In parallel, data stewardship and access remain key priorities. All research data will be made
publicly available in accordance with applicable data management requirements, and publication
of results in peer-reviewed journals will continue to be emphasized. Publications resulting from
APS beamtime must acknowledge APS using the digital object identifiers provided to users upon
access, and access to the appropriate underlying scientific data will be provided when suitable. In
addition, NSUF’s Nuclear Research Data System (NRDS, https://nrds.inl.gov/), co-located with
high-performance computing infrastructure at Idaho National Laboratory, is expected to serve as
a public-facing gateway for long-term storage, access, and computation. Consistent with current
NSUF Data Management Plans, project data created at APS will be uploaded to NRDS in addition
to local storage, further strengthening long-term accessibility and reuse.

5 Ongoing Effort and Outlook
5.1 High-temperature mechanical testing on irradiated materials

Although room-temperature testing of irradiated specimens is becoming routine at the APS 1-ID
and 20-ID beamlines, it remains insufficient to meet the needs of the nuclear materials community,
given that all operating reactors function at elevated temperatures. The combined effects of
irradiation, mechanical stress, and high temperature are therefore critical to understanding
materials degradation mechanisms. In FY26, AML purchased a customized split-tube furnace
designed to mount on the Psylotech xTS load frame (Figure 5.1). The furnace is rated for
temperatures up to 1000 °C. The system is currently in the commissioning phase, with active
efforts focused on cooling strategies, temperature profiling, thermocouple installation, rotational
stability at elevated temperature, workflows for radioactive specimens, and integration of furnace
control with the beamline control system.

5.2 Robotic operation

At present, all activated specimens handled at AML and its partner beamlines are moved manually
or with handheld tools, typically tweezers. Each handling step contributes to worker dose and
introduces potential contamination risk. Since August 2025, 41 radioactive specimens have been
received at AML, of which 15 were transported to partner beamlines with dose rates ranging from
8 to 80 mrem/h at 30 cm. The complexity of beamline experiments often requires equally complex
specimen-handling procedures, including insertion into and fastening within holders, application

 16

of double-encapsulation layers, installation of encapsulated specimens into beamline equipment,
smear and survey measurements, and the corresponding post-experiment removal steps. On
average, approximately 0.5 h of close-contact handling was required per sample. As a result, the
cumulative dose to workers was substantial.

In FY26, pioneering work was carried out with a UR5 robotic arm to advance robotic operation in
highly constrained environments. Unlike traditional approaches, in which a robot must either rely
on precisely predefined object locations to generate a trajectory or be explicitly taught each motion,
this development emphasized both flexibility and reliability. The objective was to create a robotic
assistant capable of safely responding to flexible human intent by interpreting high-level
experimental goals, while performing submillimeter-precision manipulation without continuous
human intervention.

To achieve this, a Hierarchical Hybrid Control (HHC) framework was developed that (1) uses
large language models (LLMs) for semantic understanding and task-level reasoning, (2) constrains
LLM outputs to a validated library of robot skills, and (3) combines computer vision with classical
control for physical execution. Using this framework, simple operations have been demonstrated
in a mechanical-testing environment, including picking up, installing, and discarding specimens
(Figure 5.2 left), as well as manipulating small specimens using a vacuum tweezer (Figure 5.2
right). In these demonstrations, the operator communicated with the robotic arm using natural
language.

Future work will focus on maturing the framework, expanding advanced reasoning capabilities,
improving collision-avoidance functionality, and deploying the system first for unirradiated
specimens and ultimately for radiological operations.

6 Conclusion

In FY26, the Activated Materials Laboratory (AML) at the Advanced Photon Source (APS)
continued to establish itself as a key enabling capability for nuclear materials and fuels research
using high-energy synchrotron x rays. As a Nuclear Science User Facilities (NSUF) partner facility,
the AML provides the radiological infrastructure, sample preparation expertise, and operational
support needed to safely bring activated materials to APS beamlines for advanced characterization.
Together with partner beamlines 1-ID and 20-ID, the AML is helping close a longstanding gap
between neutron irradiation studies and access to state-of-the-art nondestructive x-ray methods.

A major outcome of FY26 was the successful execution and delivery of multiple user projects
spanning ex-situ and in-situ studies of irradiated structural materials. These projects demonstrated
the practical readiness of the AML-user-beamline workflow, from sample receipt and
encapsulation to beamline transport, experiment execution, and data generation. They
demonstrated safe radiological experiments at APS beamlines for samples with dose rates above
the historical 5 mrem/h threshold and up to 100 mrem/h at 30 cm. This capability meaningfully
expands the range of irradiated materials that can be studied at APS and represents an important
milestone for the broader nuclear materials community.

FY26 also saw substantial progress in capability development. The deployment of the Psylotech
xTS load frame with in-grip rotation established a robust platform for room-temperature
mechanical testing with simultaneous 3D x-ray characterization, including tomography and
HEDM during interrupted loading. The deployment and characterization of the Linkam vacuum
heater expanded the facilitys ability to study thermally driven processes, while also identifying the
need for temperature calibration under realistic sample environments. In parallel, ongoing
commissioning of a split-tube furnace for high-temperature mechanical testing points toward
future experiments that more closely replicate reactor-relevant conditions.

At the beamline and data workflow level, the partner facilities continued to mature. Upgrades at
1-ID and the transition of 20-ID into full user operations strengthened the technical foundation for
AML-supported experiments. Near-real-time CT reconstruction and improved WAXS workflows
enhanced user feedback during beamtime, while plans for long-term data stewardship through
NSUFs Nuclear Research Data System will improve accessibility, reproducibility, and reuse of
experimental data.

Looking ahead, the AML is well positioned to expand both scientific impact and operational
sophistication. High-temperature in situ testing of irradiated materials remains a high priority, as
does further development of robotic sample handling to reduce worker dose and improve
efficiency in constrained radiological environments. With a growing portfolio of user projects,
expanding experimental capabilities, and strong integration with APS beamlines and NSUF
infrastructure, the AML is becoming a unique national resource for nondestructive, multimodal
characterization of activated materials. Continued investment in this capability will enable deeper
understanding of irradiation effects, deformation mechanisms, and microstructural evolution in
nuclear materials, ultimately supporting the development of safer and more resilient reactor
systems.
