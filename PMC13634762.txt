Dataset of soil particle images and particle size distribution for applications in granular materials
Authors: Deep Arvinder, Gobinath Ravindran, Rutuja Gumathannavar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis dataset introduces soil particle images corresponding to sieve-based particle size distribution (PSD) obtained from black cotton soil sourced from the laboratory stock of the Geotechnical Laboratory, Symbiosis International (Deemed University), Pune, Maharashtra, India. A bulk sample of 1500 grams was processed by implementing the mechanical sieve analysis using ASTM/ISO standard sieves (2.36 mm to 0.075 mm) under mechanical vibration for 15 min in three stages of 500 grams each. Representative fractions from each sieve were used for multi-scale image acquisition. A total of 1350 smartphone images were captured with the use of an iPhone 15 Pro Max at three heights (15 cm, 30 cm, 45 cm), incorporating repeated disturbance cycles to ensure the variability in particle exposure. In addition to the smartphone images, 1350 microscopic images were acquired from sub-sampled sieve fractions using varying magnifications (4x, 10x, and 20x) under an inverted microscope Olympus CKX53. The inclusion of both macro-scale and micro-scale imaging aligned with standardized sieve data supports development and validation of predictive models in geotechnical engineering. Even though the dataset is generated from black cotton soil obtained from a single source, it provides a useful benchmark for developing and evaluating image-based characterization methods. Application to other soil types or granular materials requires additional validation.Keywords: Soil particle images, Sieve analysis, Image processing, Particle size distribution, Machine learning, Granular material, Black cotton soil, Deep learning dataset

Background

Particle Size Distribution (PSD) in geotechnical engineering is fundamental for evaluation of its engineering behavior and classification [1]. In addition to particle size, particle shape also influences its mechanical behavior and packing characteristics of granular materials [2]. PSD is conventionally done using standardized laboratory tests that include mechanical sieving and sedimentation procedure based on ASTM/ISO standards. These methods are time-intensive and labor-intensive specifically for fine-grained soils. This has influenced image-based characterization approaches including dynamic image analysis methods as it delivers improved efficiency and reduced human error compared to hydrometer-based techniques [3].

Recent studies in image processing and machine learning frameworks enabled alternative estimation of PSD using digital images [4]. Computer vision techniques adopted by standard mobile phone cameras have reported reasonable in estimating PSD compared to sieve analysis procedure [5]. Image-based techniques extract particle-level characteristics which can be correlated with sieve-based distributions [6], though their accuracy is dependent on image conditions which include illumination, contrast and noise control [7]. Automated and real-time imaging systems have also been adopted in industrial granular materials [8]. Deep learning approach further enhances image-based soil and aggregate characterisation [9,10]. Available datasets provide paired soil images and PSD data [11,12] promoting development of robust data-driven models for granular material application [13].

Data Description

The dataset provides a detailed multi-scale representation of soil characteristics by combining macroscopic imaging, microscopic analysis and associated sieve-based particle size distribution data. It is organized structurally into two main categories, Smartphone Image Dataset and Microscopic Image Dataset along with corresponding sieve-based particle size distribution (PSD) data, allowing comprehensive assessment of soil structure, texture, and particle size variation across different fractions. Smartphone images are provided in JPEG (.jpeg) format and microscopic images are provided in TIF (.tif) format corresponding to the native output format of the camera systems. Image Parameter files are provided in CSV (.csv) format for machine-readable tabular data handling. PSD data is presented in tabular form within the manuscript. These formats primarily represent the default outputs of the acquisition systems and were retained to preserve the original data characteristics except for the microscopic images, which carry measurement overlays generated with the acquisition software cellSens. The smartphone image dataset contains 225 images across 6 sieve samples totaling 1350 images whereas microscope image dataset consists of 54 images per 5 sub-samples and same across 5 primary sieve samples totaling 1350 images.

Smartphone image dataset

From each sieve fraction, a representative 75 g of soil sample was placed on a plain white paper. First, a set of three images were captured at predefined camera heights of 15 cm, 30 cm and 45 cm. Following this, the soil arrangement was manually disturbed using a paper lifting and turning procedure. No predefined spreading procedure was adopted. Specifically, one corner or one side of the A4 sheet paper was lifted and rotated inward in the opposite direction to overturn the top particle layer. The procedure was then repeated using other corners or sides to ensure that previously underlying particles were brought to the surface while previously exposed particles were redistributed below. This disturbance procedure was aimed to generate variability in particle exposure and arrangement. The same procedure was adopted consistently across all samples and disturbance cycles. The process was repeated for 75 g sample from each sieve fraction separately throughout all 75 disturbance cycles. Upon completion of each disturbance cycle, another set of three images were acquired at the predefined heights, repeated throughout. The disturbance procedure was conducted manually; therefore, no quantitative force or time measurement was imposed. Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Soil sampling

A bulk soil sample of 1500 g of black cotton soil was collected from the laboratory stock of Geotechnical Laboratory at Symbiosis International (Deemed University), Pune, Maharashtra, India, in an already air-dried condition; therefore, no additional drying procedure was performed prior to testing. Also, moisture content was not measured prior to the sieve analysis of the soil sample. The sample was initially pre-sized through a 4.75 mm sieve to remove coarse fractions and to ensure a representative fine grained soil matrix. The processed soil sample was further subdivided into three specimens of 500 g each to facilitate efficient and accurate sieve analysis as processing of large sample masses may lead to particle congestion specifically in the finer sieves where soil accumulation may restrict aperture openings further hindering the passage of finer particles. In accordance with ASTM D6913/D6913M-17 and ISO 17892–4 standards, which recommend the use of appropriately sized representative specimens to prevent sieve overloading and ensure effective particle separation, the subdivision approach was adopted. Following sieve analysis procedure, 75 g of the retained soil corresponding to each sieve fraction was randomly drawn from each sieve and stored separately.

Mechanical sieve analysis

A total of 1500 g of black cotton soil was taken for sieve analysis procedure. The soil was sieved in three batches of 500 g to avoid overloading the finer sieves to ensure accurate particle separation. The procedure of mechanical sieving was performed using a set of ASTM/ISO standard sieves with mesh sizes of 2.36 mm, 1.18 mm, 0.6 mm, 0.3 mm, 0.15 mm, and 0.075 mm. The stacked sieves were mounted on a mechanical sieve shaker as shown in Fig. 2 and operated for 15 min to ensure effective size-based particle separation. Thereafter, the mass of soil retained on each sieve was accurately measured. These values were later used to calculate the mass retained, percentage retained, and percentage finer for each size fraction, enabling detailed characterization of the soil particle size distribution. The separate fractions were then utilized for further analysis such as macroscopic imaging using a mobile phone and microscopic inspection to assess particle morphology.

Smartphone image acquisition

Smartphone images were acquired using an iPhone 15 Pro Max camera system equipped with a 48-megapixel primary camera representative images are shown in Fig. 5a. It was performed in daytime under indoor ambient lighting conditions near a window to utilize the natural daylight while avoiding direct sunlight exposure onto the soil sample. Direct solar illumination was avoided to minimise strong shadows, glare effects and high contrast intensity variations. Additionally, the acquisition room was installed with recessed ceiling-mounted LED lighting panels emitting a warm white to yellowish illumination so to maintain lighting conditions visually comparable to natural daylight. From each sieve fraction, a representative 75 g of soil sample was placed on a plain white paper. First, a set of three images were captured at predefined camera heights of 15 cm, 30 cm and 45 cm. Following this, the soil arrangement was manually disturbed using a paper folding and turning procedure. In particular, one corner of the paper was lifted and rotated inward in the opposite direction to overturn the top particle layer. The procedure was then repeated using other corners to ensure that previously underlying particles were brought to the surface while previously exposed particles were redistributed below. This disturbance procedure was intended to generate variability in particle exposure and arrangement. The same procedure was applied consistently across all samples and disturbance cycles. After each disturbance cycle, another set of three images were acquired at the predefined heights. The disturbance procedure was performed manually; therefore, no quantitative force or time measurement was imposed.

For each sieve fraction, 75 disturbance cycles were performed to introduce variability in particle arrangement, with 3 images recorded per cycle, resulting in 225 images per sieve fraction. In total, 1350 smartphone-based images were generated across all sieve fractions, forming a robust dataset for macroscopic analysis. Light intensity values were consistently monitored and recorded simultaneously during the image acquisition using Lightmeter iOS application. The recorded light intensity ranged from 74 to 861 lx. Lux measurements were recorded at three predefined heights of 15, 30 and 45 cm with the camera facing toward the soil sample during image acquisition Image parameters that may influence downstream image analysis were recorded for each image in associated Smartphone Image Parameters CSV file accompanying the dataset. These parameters include Focal length, aperture, shutter speed, height of camera, light intensity and image resolution. As the camera was operated with automatic exposure and autofocus settings, these parameters might differ per image; the EXIF metadata recorded with each image file indicates the actual settings applied and thus can be used to determine acquisition variability or to support normalization during preprocessing. The distance between the light source and soil sample was governed by the fixed room lighting configuration and window placement and no dedicated artificial illumination setup with controlled source to sample spacing was deployed. Lighting conditions were not kept consistent; ambient light levels fluctuated between 74 and 861 lx during image capture. These variations were documented for each image in the metadata, but because a standardized imaging environment was not used, differences in illumination contribute to uncontrolled variability throughout the dataset.

Illumination levels were measured manually with a mobile light meter application at the time of picture taking. The data was then organized into a CSV metadata file for the purpose of providing reference information on the lighting conditions at the time of photo shooting

The images captured were stored in JPEG format using the native camera output settings of the device. The smartphone camera was operated using the default automatic exposure and autofocus setting throughout the image acquisition process. No additional image compression or post-acquisition image enhancement procedure were applied, and images were archived and uploaded to the repository in their original acquisition format.

No calibration target or colour reference was used for illumination normalization across smartphone acquisition procedure.

The particle size metrics recorded in the dataset corresponds to the sieve fractions obtained by the mechanical sieve analysis procedure performed according to the ASTM/ISO guidelines. The Sieve Size (mm) column mentioned in the csv file in dataset uploaded on IEEE Dataport identifies the nominal particle size fraction associated with each image sample. Microscopic image acquisition

Microscopic image acquisition was performed for detailed particle-level analysis. Sub-samples were collected from five distinct locations within each sieve fraction and stored in Eppendorf tubes to preserve sample integrity. These sub-samples were recorded using an optical inverted microscope at varied magnifications based on particle size: Sample size of 1.18 mm and 0.6 mm at 4x (200 µm scale), sample size 0.3 mm at 10x (100 µm scale), and Sample sizes of 0.15 mm and 0.075 mm at 20x (50 µm scale).

The microscope system was operated using the Olympus CKX53 inverted microscope equipped with a DP28 colour camera and Olympus cellSens Entry software. Each sub-sample generated 54 images, resulting in 270 images per fraction and a total of 1350 microscopic images the representative images are shown in Fig. 5.b. This multi-magnification strategy ensures a comprehensive visualization of particle shape, size distribution, and microstructural heterogeneity. The acquired images and corresponding information are provided in accompanying CSV file. The cross-sectional dimensions of representative particles were measured using the Olympus cellSens Entry software. For each image, the magnification was set up automatically depending on the objective lens used (4x, 10x and 20x, which corresponded to 200 µm, 100 µm and 50 µm). These measurements were directly rendered on the images as visible measurement lines with the corresponding length in micrometers. The measurement lines were placed manually and are therefore operator dependent. Limitations

Although illumination was maintained during smartphone image acquisition, the lack of standardized imaging chambers may introduce minor variability in illumination across samples. Additionally, automatic exposure and autofocus settings were not controlled or kept constant during image capture and therefore should be considered as potential confounding variables.

Since the dataset is limited to only a single soil type, its applicability for generalized modeling across varied soil classes is constrained.

Microscopic imaging was restricted to finer particle fractions, as the 2.36 mm fraction could not be adequately captured within the limited field of view at higher magnification levels of the microscope. Consequently, the microscopic image dataset spans five fractions (1.18 mm to 0.075 mm) compared with six for smartphone image dataset.

The moisture content of the soil was not determined before sieve analysis. Residual moisture may influence particle aggregation, sieving efficiency and image appearance; therefore, this factor should be considered when interpreting the dataset. The storage history of the laboratory stock was also not documented.

The dataset does not contain ground-truth segmentation masks or particle level annotations. For supervised segmentation and instance level morphometrics, ground truth must be obtained in an independent process, while for unsupervised algorithms such as thresholding, edge detection or morphological operations, the images can be processed directly. The dimensional measurement overlays are rendered into microscopic images and thus are part of the pixel data. Users performing segmentation, edge detection or intensity-based feature extraction should be aware that such overlay elements can be picked up as image features and may need to be masked or excluded in such analyses
