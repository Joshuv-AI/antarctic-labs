Assessing variational likelihood estimation in network formation models

Joseph Resch, Mark S Handcock

ABSTRACT
Networks are often a useful way to represent complex social and economic systems. Stochastic models for such networks should parsimoniously reflect the complexity while being both interpretable and tractable. The class of Exponential-family Random Graph Models (ERGMs) is very expressive and flexible in its representation of complex phenomena. However, the estimation and fitting of realistic ERGMs are hampered by the computational challenges of likelihood-based inference. Recently, a deterministic method based on a variational mean-field approximation was proposed that is much less expensive to compute than the standard simulation-based method. In this work, we show that the proposed method performs poorly for realistic models. We show that established alternatives, including MCMC-MLE, MPLE, sampled MPLE, and tapered ERGM-based inference, achieve superior accuracy and computational performance in the situations that the mean-field approximation was designed for.

1. Introduction There has been increasing interest in models for complex stochastic phenomena. Exponential-family models are able to represent complex structure in an interpretable way [ 1 , 2 ]. This is particularly true of stochastic models for networks, where the nodes represent actors and the edges relational ties between those actors. The configuration of these ties and their dependence on attributes of the nodes influence how processes unfold on networks. Exponential-family random graph models (ERGMs) offer a flexible framework for representing these dependencies [ 3 – 5 ]. There is a substantial literature on these network models and their applications across disciplines [ 5 – 7 ]. 1.1 Exponential-family random graph models ERGMs are a family of random graph models that have received extensive attention in the statistical literature and are widely used in applications [ 8 , 9 ]. A network may be represented as graph G = ( V , E ) using node set V and edge set E , where nodes are the actors of interest and edges define the relational tie. The edges can be binary or valued/weighted and directed or undirected [ 10 ]. For example, edges can be undirected binary values indicating whether a relationship exists between two nodes, or not. In many situations the number of nodes is fixed and known ( n = | V |). In this case we have as many as 2 ( n 2 ) possible graphs. The heterogeneous nodes, indexed by i = 1 , . . . , n , often have an associated vector, x i , of covariates representing attributes such as age, income, and location. The model represents an observed network as a realization y ∈ G n of random network Y , where G n is the set of all possible networks Y with n nodes. Y is often represented as an n × n matrix of relational variables with ( i , j ) t h element the value of the edge from node i to node j . In the binary undirected case, realization y i , j takes values from {0,1} where y i , j = y j , i for 1 ≤ i , j ≤ n . Typically, any self ties of the nodes are not represented so that y i , i is undefined for all i ∈ [ 1 , n ] . For notational convenience, dependence on the covariates x 1 , . . . , x n is suppressed. The probability of observing Y = y is given by: p θ ( Y = y ) = exp ( θ · Z ( y ) ) c ( θ , G n ) y ∈ G n . (1) ERGMs are defined by the choice of network statistics, Z ( y ), a d − dimensional vector-valued function that specifies a set of sufficient statistics. The associated parameter vector is θ ∈ ℝ d , and c ( θ , G n ) denotes the normalizing constant. A basic statistic is the total number of edges (‘Edge’, ∑ i = 1 , j = 1 n , n y i , j ). Most models also include statistics defined over triads (sub-graphs of triplets of nodes), especially measures of transitivity, the propensity for ties between pairs of nodes who are tied to a common third node. Examples are the clustering of edges (‘Two-Star’, ∑ i = 1 , j = 1 , k = 1 n , n , n y i , j y i , k ) and transitivity (‘Triangle’, ∑ i = 1 , j = 1 , k = 1 n , n , n y i , j y i , k y j , k ). If there is a nodal attribute x i then one can measure homophily of edges on it via (‘Homophily’, ∑ i = 1 , j = 1 n , n 𝕀 ( x i = x j ) y i , j ) . There are a multitude of choices for these statistics (See Morris et al. [ 11 ] for a list). In this way, ERGMs constitute a family of models across different choices of the network statistics. Regardless of which network statistics are used, an ERGM is the unique distribution of maximum entropy among all distributions on G n whose expected sufficient statistics are fixed and satisfy the moment constraint 𝔼 θ [ Z ( y ) ] = μ , where Z ( y ) is the d − dimensional vector of network statistics and μ ∈ ℝ d is a specified vector of mean values [ 12 ]. 1.2 Likelihood-based inference for ERGM For undirected binary networks, the normalizing constant c ( θ , G n ) = ∑ y ∈ G n exp ( θ · Z ( y ) ) accounts for all 2 ( n 2 ) possible graphs, which makes the summation computationally intractable even for networks with few nodes. The calculation of this term is crucial to likelihood-based inference. Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) simulation is a popular approach to approximate the likelihood by simulating many networks and using them to estimate the intractable normalizing constant [ 9 , 13 ]. In another approach called maximum pseudo-likelihood estimation (MPLE), a pseudo-likelihood is used in place of the true likelihood [ 14 ]. Whereas the exact properties of the MPLE vary by case, using MCMC to obtain an approximate MLE (MCMC-MLE) has become the workhorse in this area with much research and practical knowledge [ 15 – 17 ]. This is enhanced by the availability of sophisticated user-friendly open-source software for MCMC-based inference [ 9 ]. However, in most cases MPLE’s computational simplicity may outweigh the benefits of MCMC-MLE [ 14 ]. Over time, substantial efforts have been made to improve likelihood-based inference for ERGM [ 15 – 17 ]. In that effort, Mele and Zhu [ 18 ] proposed a method to approximate ERGM likelihood using a variational mean-field and demonstrated its effectiveness through a microeconomic application. Their method minimizes the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence between a tractable approximating distribution and the true ERGM likelihood by maximizing a lower bound on the intractable normalizing constant. Unlike MCMC approximation of the intractable likelihood, maximum mean-field variational likelihood estimates (MFVLE) are deterministic. A key feature of the mean-field approximation is that it replaces the full ERGM, with its complex dependence among ties, by a model with conditionally independent ties whose parameters are chosen to be as close as possible to the ERGM under the KL criterion. This factorization results in a variational problem whose solution provides an approximate log-likelihood without requiring any simulation. Mele and Zhu [ 18 ] show that the approximation error of this mean-field log-constant converges to zero as the number of nodes increases, using tools from nonlinear large deviations theory. Consequently, the mean-field log-likelihood uniformly approximates the true ERGM log-likelihood in large networks, and the MFVLE converges to the MLE when the underlying ERGM likelihood is well behaved. They also provide explicit bounds on the approximation error, and their studies suggest that, even for moderately sized networks, the approximation performs better than these bounds indicate. These results suggest that the variational mean-field approach provides a computationally efficient and theoretically grounded alternative to standard simulation-based ERGM estimation. Our results below suggest that this conclusion does not extend to the tie-dependent models that motivate many ERGM applications, for which the approximation is both inaccurate and more expensive to compute than established methods. 1.3 Outline of the paper We show that the finite-network estimation experiments in Mele and Zhu [ 18 ] rely on initialization at the true parameter values, a design choice that is unavailable in applications and that materially changes the apparent performance of MFVLE. We address this and replicate the study on 1,000 sampled networks, then show that the empirical performance of the method is poor and dramatically worse than the MCMC-MLE. We replace initialization at the true parameter values with practically attainable starting values, primarily the MPLE, while focusing on how well the mean-field likelihood approximation functions under certain levels of network transitivity. A useful tool here comes from van Duijn et al. [ 14 ], where transitivity in ERGM natural parameters can be systematically adjusted by way of one-to-one mapped mean-value parameterization. In turn, manually increased transitivity allows us to stress test the estimation methods under more complex network structures. Our findings are accompanied by conceptual reasoning on why the mean-field method will be poor in circumstances where it is most needed. The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews ERGM specifications, likelihood-based inference methods, and other conceptual issues related to the mean-field likelihood approximation for ERGM. Section 3 discusses the simulation study framework and compares the mean-field variational method against current methods. Section 4 draws conclusions on the contents of this paper. 2. Theoretical considerations 2.1 Realism of the proposed ERGM class The choice of sufficient statistics is critical in an ERGM specification. Here we consider the choice of Mele and Zhu [ 18 ]. They used a microeconomics model to explore best-response equilibrium dynamics in potential games by measuring players’ payoffs. Real-world networks, such as those in social science and biology, tend to have tie dependence that causes clustering of the edges and transitivity. The microeconomics model reflects this by including Two-star and Triangle terms. One aspect of model specification that is important to take into consideration is that certain combinations of terms can result in the model not placing sufficient probability mass on realistic graphs and networks. This is called the model degeneracy problem [ 19 – 22 ]. For example, using a Triangle statistic as a measure of transitivity will lead to degeneracy in many cases. This is because a single edge can form the basis for a large number of triangles, so using the unweighted count of the number of triangles can create strong positive feedback in the sufficient statistics. An ERGM with a positive coefficient on a Triangle term may therefore place substantial probability mass on unrealistic regions of the sample space, such as nearly empty or nearly complete graphs, or on extreme regions of the convex support of the network statistics. Mele and Zhu attempt to reduce this degeneracy by scaling the coefficients of the Two-star and Triangle terms [ 23 , 24 ], but this also reduces the realism of the model. We return to the problem of fitting realistic, transitive models in Sections 3.4 – 3.6 , where we adopt a tapered ERGM that retains the Triangle term while controlling degeneracy. 2.2 Theoretical analysis of MFVLE The core idea of variational approximation is to use a variational representation of the log-normalizing constant. For an ERGM, this representation can be written as a supremum over the set of all probability mass functions on G n [28, Section 2C]. This exact variational problem is as difficult as the original likelihood problem, because it ranges over all probability distributions on the network sample space. Mele and Zhu therefore restrict the variational class to the set of tie-independent probability mass functions. In this restricted class, the approximating distribution factorizes over dyads, and the variational problem has a much simpler computational form. This restriction is not merely a computational device. It changes the dependence structure of the model being used to approximate the likelihood. The ERGM likelihood assigns probability to entire networks and, through statistics such as Two-stars and Triangles, represents endogenous dependence among dyads. The mean-field approximation replaces this joint distribution by a product distribution over dyads. Thus the approximation is structurally closest to the ERGM when the fitted model is close to dyad independence, and it is least faithful when the likelihood is shaped by the dependence terms that motivate the use of ERGMs in the first place. This observation is central to our assessment of MFVLE: the relevant question is not whether mean-field approximations can be accurate in favorable weak-dependence regimes, but whether this particular dyad-factorized approximation remains reliable for finite networks with realistic transitivity and other forms of endogenous tie dependence. The related theoretical literature gives reason to expect such failures. In closely related Ising models, mean-field approximations can be accurate in highly averaged settings such as complete graphs [ 25 ], but can perform poorly in sparse settings [ 26 ]. This distinction is important for network models, because empirical social and economic networks are often sparse or semi-sparse, and their dependence is usually local rather than complete-graph-like. Ricci-Tersenghi [ 27 ] shows that mean-field methods can perform poorly when dependence is strong. Lokhov et al. [ 28 ] similarly find poor performance for strongly coupled systems and degenerate regions of the parameter space, and contrast this behavior with MPLE and other more robust methods. These results suggest that the performance of MFVLE should depend strongly on where the true or fitted model lies in the parameter space. In particular, strong coupling, degeneracy, and transitivity are not peripheral cases for ERGMs; they are precisely the features often introduced to make the model substantively useful. Related work on ERGM-specific mean-field approximations points in the same direction. Biondini et al. [ 29 ] consider a mean-field approximation for ERGMs based on k -star statistics. Under their asymptotic scheme, the approximation can represent macroscopic features, such as edge density, while failing to represent microscopic dependence, such as dyadic dependence. This distinction is directly relevant here. A mean-field approximation may reproduce some low-dimensional aggregate features of a network while still misrepresenting the dependence structure induced by Two-star and Triangle terms. Consequently, good approximation of an edge density or of a scaled log-normalizing constant does not by itself imply accurate estimation of parameters governing endogenous dependence. The same point can be expressed through the geometry of the exponential family. The log-partition function is convex in the natural parameter, and the log-likelihood ℓ ( θ ; y ) = θ ⊤ Z ( y ) − log c ( θ , G n ) is concave, with curvature determined by ∇ 2 ℓ ( θ ; y ) = − Var θ [ Z ( Y ) ] . Thus the shape of the likelihood is governed by the covariance structure of the sufficient statistics. For ERGMs with transitivity terms, this covariance structure is induced by higher-order dependence among overlapping dyads. A dyad-factorized variational family removes this dyadic dependence in the approximating distribution. Although nonlinear statistics such as Two-stars and Triangles can still be evaluated under the product distribution, the approximation cannot generally reproduce the dependence-induced curvature of the true ERGM likelihood. In poorly conditioned regions of the convex support, or in regions near faces of the support associated with degeneracy, the KL projection onto the mean-field family can therefore lie far from the true model. The resulting approximate likelihood may have a substantially different shape from the true likelihood, leading to biased point estimates and misleading measures of uncertainty. This concern is amplified by the direction of the variational approximation. MFVLE minimizes K L ( q ‖ p θ ) over a restricted class of product distributions q . In many multimodal or strongly dependent problems, this direction of Kullback–Leibler divergence has a mode-seeking tendency: it can favor an approximating distribution that concentrates on one part of the probability mass rather than representing the full dispersion or multimodality of the target distribution. Such behavior is especially problematic in ERGM regions with strong dependence, where the distribution over networks may place substantial mass near separated regions of the sample space or near the boundary of the convex support. This provides a theoretical mechanism for the empirical failures examined below. There is also a distinction between approximation of the log-normalizing constant and estimation of the model parameters. The main result of Mele and Zhu, Proposition 1, shows that the mean-field approximation to the scaled log-likelihood converges to the ERGM scaled log-likelihood as the number of nodes, n , increases, under assumptions controlling the growth of the parameters θ n = ( α n , β n , γ n ) . This result addresses an approximation question: for a specified parameter sequence and asymptotic regime, how close is the mean-field approximation to the true log-normalizing constant or scaled log-likelihood? The inferential question is different: when the approximation is maximized for an observed finite network, how close is the MFVLE to the MLE? Uniform approximation of likelihood values does not by itself guarantee accurate approximation of the maximizer unless additional conditions on the shape of the likelihood hold, such as strict or strong concavity and appropriate identification. These conditions are precisely the ones that may be weak or fail in degenerate ERGM regions. The asymptotic regime considered by Mele and Zhu is also close in spirit to dense-graph asymptotics, in which sufficient statistics scale as n 2 and the network does not become sparse as n increases [ 23 , 24 ]. Many empirical social and economic networks are sparse or semi-sparse, with edge counts growing sub-quadratically in n . Moreover, the number of nodes is not a tuning parameter under the researcher’s control. It is a structural feature of the phenomenon being modeled. Networks with different sizes often correspond to different social or economic systems, and there is no general reason to assume that their natural parameters follow a convenient asymptotic sequence. For example, consider social relations in a school classroom with n = 3 students in it compared to a classroom with n = 100 students. The social dynamics are quite different and the θ n should be as well. For a broader discussion of this issue, see Krivitsky et al. [ 30 ]. This distinction also clarifies why the analogy between MFVLE and MCMC-MLE is limited. Both methods approximate the log-normalizing constant, but the source of approximation is fundamentally different. In MCMC-MLE, the simulation size is chosen by the researcher and can, at least in principle, be increased to reduce Monte Carlo error. In the mean-field asymptotic argument, by contrast, the quantity that grows is the number of nodes in the observed network. For any particular application, n is fixed by the phenomenon under study. Increasing n is therefore not an available computational refinement in the same sense as increasing the length of an MCMC run. Thus the fact that the mean-field approximation improves under a particular increasing- n regime does not imply that it is accurate for the fixed finite networks encountered in applications. The potential-game specification used by Mele and Zhu further illustrates the issue. The final term in their utility specification counts the number of common friends, which corresponds to a Triangle term in the ERGM. Positive coefficients on Triangle terms are well known to produce degeneracy over wide regions of the parameter space [ 19 ]. In the scaled specification studied here, choosing the Triangle coefficient small enough to avoid degeneracy can place the model close to dyad independence, a regime in which MPLE is expected to be effective. These considerations motivate the simulation design below. We compare MFVLE to MCMC-MLE and MPLE in the original model class studied by Mele and Zhu, but we do so under practically attainable initialization rather than initialization at the true parameter value. We also examine performance in both the natural and mean-value parameterizations. The mean-value parameterization allows us to increase transitivity directly and thereby assess estimation accuracy in the regions where dyad-factorized mean-field approximations are expected to be most fragile. The purpose of the simulation study is therefore not simply to ask whether MFVLE can work in weak-dependence examples, but to determine whether it remains reliable in the finite-network, transitive regimes where likelihood approximation is most needed. 2.3 A framework for the comparison of estimation methods A framework for the comparison of estimators for ERGM was developed by van Duijn et al. [ 14 ]. Here we review the core elements of that framework. It is well known that MCMC-MLE converges to the MLE as the sampling increases [ 13 ] and can provide useful results with enough iterations. However, convergence is often not practically available due to degeneracy and MCMC variability [ 19 , 21 ]. Maximum pseudo-likelihood estimation is a popular alternative choice, and is used in many applications for its advantage in computational time compared to using MCMC. While both are approximations to the exact MLE, the error of the MCMC-MLE can be reduced by additional computation. Handcock [ 19 ] proposed the consideration of the alternative mean-value parameterization ERGM, μ ( θ ) , that has the one-to-one mapping with natural parameters θ characterized by: μ ( θ ) = 𝔼 θ [ Z ( y ) ] . (2) The mapping is strictly increasing in the sense that: ( θ a − θ b ) T ( μ ( θ a ) − μ ( θ b ) ) ≥ 0 (3) with equality only if P θ a ( Y = y ) = P θ b ( Y = y ) ∀ y . The mapping is also injective in the sense that μ ( θ a ) = μ ( θ b ) → P θ a ( Y = y ) = P θ b ( Y = y ) ∀ y . (4) The mean value parameterization has the main advantage of interpretability due to its direct translation to the network statistics chosen for the model. van Duijn et al. [ 14 ] adopted the mean-value parameterization to examine performance of MCMC-MLE and MPLE under different transitivity scenarios. Their results show the MPLE performs as well as the MCMC-MLE when there is little transitivity/tie-dependence in the model. However, where there is realistic transitivity/tie-dependence the MCMC-MLE outperformed MPLE. It is worth noting that the performance gains of MCMC-MLE over MPLE are most pronounced for parameters capturing endogenous tie dependence, whereas the improvement for nodal and dyadic covariates such as Homophily is comparatively modest. An increase in transitivity cannot be easily induced in the natural parameterization, as a direct change in a natural parameter leads to complex changes in the distributions of the network statistics due to interactions. However, transitivity can be added by increasing the corresponding mean-value parameter. The mean-value parameters with the added transitivity can then be inversely mapped to the vector of natural parameters (See van Duijn et al. [ 14 ] for details). As model transitivity is increased by way of the mean-value parameterization, the superior performance of MCMC-MLE becomes more noticeable as the bias of the MPLE for both the natural parameters and mean-value parameters increases. Despite this inferior performance in realistic settings, the MPLE serves as a useful initialization for more complex methods, such as the MCMC-MLE. In fact, it is the default initialization for the state-of-the-art MCMC-MLE in the ergm R package [ 9 , 31 ]. One factor to keep in mind is the likelihood of degeneracy for the estimates (that is, high proportion of mass placed on the boundary of the convex hull of network statistics). The degeneracy of a model fit can be assessed by the R package ergm [ 9 , 31 ]. 3. Study design and simulation results In this section we use the framework of van Duijn et al. [ 14 ] to design a simulation study to assess the accuracy of the MFVLE compared to the MCMC-MLE and MPLE. We first replicate the Monte Carlo simulation study of Mele and Zhu [ 18 ], Section 4.2. A key feature of their study was initiating the optimization at the true values. They find that the estimates do not vary much from the start point, and interpret this as accuracy. We replicate their study but start from the MPLE, which is by far the most common initialization point for estimates [ 15 ]. We try larger network sizes to see if that improves the MFVLE, and then show that the parameter values they use are close to a tie-independent model. Such parameter values are rarely realistic and so we modify the parameter values to produce a similar model with more realistic transitivity (i.e., an increased level). We then repeat the study for models with this more realistic transitivity. 3.1 Re-evaluating the Monte Carlo simulation study of Mele and Zhu [ 18 ] The same ERGM specification as in Mele and Zhu [ 18 ] – in which the chosen sufficient statistics are Edges, Homophily, Two-stars, and Triangles – is used. The predetermined true parameters corresponding to the models are dependent on network size. We note that there is a defect in their simulation study. As stated at the end of their Section 4B, “To be sure that our results do not depend on the initialization of the parameters, we start each estimator at the true parameter value.” Mele and Zhu [ 18 ] set the initialization of model parameters in the algorithm for both MCMC-MLE and MFVLE to be the true parameters that are used to simulate networks. However, these true parameters are not known in practice. Moreover, their use of the true values obscured the fact that the algorithm did not move the parameter values toward the true values, but only started there. Thus the reported experiment does not evaluate how MFVLE behaves from attainable starting values, and its apparent accuracy cannot be interpreted as evidence of reliable estimation performance. In contrast, we use the MPLE, the current standard method, as the initial values to validate our claim. To ensure that the mean-field approximation does not converge to a suboptimal local solution, a known risk with variational methods, the MPLE initial points were perturbed with random noise, effectively restarting the variational approximation procedure over the large number of simulated networks. We also verified that initializing at the true parameter values reproduces the results of Mele and Zhu [ 18 ] deterministically. Other initial estimates, including zero vectors and random Gaussian starting points, produced similar results for both the MCMC-MLE and MFVLE. The default pipeline in the ergm package uses the MPLE to initialize the Stepping algorithm of Hummel et al. [ 16 ], which in turn seeds the MCMC-MLE. We additionally applied the same Stepping-based initialization to the mean-field method, and this did not noticeably change the mean-field estimates. To generate artificial networks in practice, a Metropolis-Hastings network sampler is used with the initialization being an iteratively simulated network using the true parameters with attributes drawn from a Bernoulli distribution. Here, 1,000 networks are simulated for model fitting while 10,000 sets of summary statistics are simulated for mapping of mean-value parameterization. For very large network sizes the standard MPLE can be computationally burdensome, so we use a variant of it where selective sampling of the dyads is undertaken. From a computational point of view, these experiments also highlight very different scaling behaviors of the various estimators. Each MFVLE fit requires optimization over all dyads at every variational iteration, so its cost grows at least on the order of O ( n 2 ) in the number of nodes, and in practice was dominated by the repeated evaluation of dense expectations of the sufficient statistics. For simplicity, we take as our measure of transitivity of a model its expected number of triangles, with a model producing higher expected numbers of triangles being more transitive. As noted in Section 2.1 , the model in Mele and Zhu [ 18 ] is prone to degeneracy and will be for parameter values chosen close to realistic networks. The parameter values chosen in the original study are close to tie-independent and hence the degeneracy is not strong. However, if we choose parameter values corresponding to increased transitivity, they will be degenerate or nearly so. This behavior is not realistic, except in rare cases where the degeneracy reflects economic theory. One solution is to use other terms that capture the complex dependencies while being less prone to degeneracy [ 34 ]. Here we consider the tapered ERGM variant of this model which circumvents the issue of degeneracy while maintaining the desirable features of ERGMs, including the retention of the Triangle term [ 12 ]. We adopt the tapered ERGM in the comparison because it supplies a model with realistic transitivity that remains non-degenerate. The tapered version of an ERGM model is itself an ERGM, adding terms that taper the entropy of the original ERGM and reduce unrealistic behavior in models with increased dependence and transitivity. Its coefficients therefore carry the same interpretation as those of the untapered model built from the same sufficient statistics. The tapering terms are chosen to perturb the model only slightly near the target mean-value parameters, so each tapered coefficient stays numerically close to the coefficient of the untapered model targeting the same mean value. Tapered ERGM models have been shown to have these and other good statistical and probabilistic properties (For a full treatment, see Blackburn and Handcock [ 35 ]). Intuitively, tapering modifies the likelihood by down-weighting network configurations with extreme values of the sufficient statistics via an additional term in the model that penalizes extreme variation. This can be viewed as an entropy-regularization device that keeps the distribution away from the extreme faces of the convex support of Z ( Y ), where standard ERGMs tend to concentrate when they are close to degenerate. Thus tapering improves the stability and robustness of the models. This stability and robustness extends to MCMC-based inference for them. MCMC-MLE for tapered ERGM is implemented by the R package ergm.tapered on GitHub [ 36 ], and the tapering term is applied to the likelihood function in R package mfergm for MFVLE. To consider models that are close to the original ERGM, but with higher transitivity, we can choose tapered ERGM models with similar mean-value parameters, but with increased transitivity. Specifically, we choose models with 50% more transitivity than the original version of the model, thus factoring the original expected count for triangles by 1.5. This is done by solving equation (2) for θ with the left hand side set to the desired mean-value parameter. This results in natural parameter values for the Triangle term that are substantially above zero. Without tapering, which helps move sampled statistics away from degenerate regions, substantial increases in transitivity are typically infeasible due to model degeneracy. Discussion The fitting of a complex model that realistically represents observed social and economic network data is challenging. The dependence structure that makes these models appealing also makes likelihood-based inference computationally demanding. As such, the search for accurate and computationally tractable methods is ongoing. While likelihood-based estimation methods can be computationally expensive, they do have a strong track record of success and a long history of development. In this paper we have shown that there is little empirical and conceptual support for the mean-field variational estimator. We show that there is a design flaw in the original simulation study [ 18 ] that invalidates their empirical results. We also argue that, even if those results had been correct, they would not have provided much support for the method as the models they were assessed on are unrealistically close to tie-independent models. When we alter the model to make it more realistic by inducing stronger dependence among ties, the estimator performs poorly and is more computationally expensive compared to both MCMC-MLE and the cheap sampled MPLE. Variational methods do have value when they are applied to appropriate model classes with parameter values in regions where the approximation is not bad. This is challenging for ERGMs, which constitute a highly flexible class of models: depending on the choice of sufficient statistics, they can induce strong and complex dependence among ties that is difficult for variational approximations to capture. In addition, variants that aim to capture realistic and complex network structure tend to be those where the approximation does poorly. Our results suggest that mean-field variational approximation should not be used as a default method for ERGMs, and a more robust strategy would be to use MPLE for initialization and MCMC-MLE for final inference. Estimates based on variational approximations can also underestimate the estimation uncertainty. Tan and Friel [ 37 ] show that in Bayesian inference for ERGM, variational approximations can underestimate the posterior variances. Our analysis also illustrates the value of tapered ERGM both for model specification and estimation. These models retain the expressiveness of a traditional ERGM while allowing practical MCMC-based methods of inference. In sum, when using ERGM, researchers should usually prefer the tapered ERGM. For inference, they should use MPLE for initialization and MCMC-MLE, if computationally feasible, for reliable inference. MFVLE should not be used as a default estimator for ERGMs with substantively meaningful endogenous dependence, and in the settings studied here it is dominated by MPLE or MCMC-MLE. The computations in this paper were performed using the ergm [ 9 ] and ergm.tapered [ 35 , 36 ] R-packages within the statnet suite [ 2 ]. This is sophisticated, accessible, user-friendly and open-source software for fitting and simulating ERGM. The R code to reproduce the results of this paper using these packages is available on GitHub [ 38 ] and published on Zenodo [ 39 ]. For completeness it is worth emphasizing that the fully factorized mean-field family studied by Mele and Zhu is only one member of a broader class of deterministic approximations to ERGMs. Composite or pseudo-likelihood based methods, corrected pseudo-likelihoods [ 14 ], and variational approximations based on richer dependence structures (for example, Bethe approximations [ 27 ] and loopy belief propagation [ 40 ]) retain more of the local dependence among ties than a product-Bernoulli mean-field family. Our negative results therefore should not be interpreted as a blanket indictment of all deterministic or variational approaches for ERGMs, but rather as evidence that a tie-independent mean-field family is too restrictive to serve as a default likelihood approximation for models with substantively meaningful transitivity and clustering.
