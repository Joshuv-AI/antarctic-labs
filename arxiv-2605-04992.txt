You Snooze, You Lose: Automatic Safety Alignment Restoration through Neural Weight Translation
Marco Arazzi, Vignesh Kumar Kembu, Antonino Nocera, Stjepan Picek, Saraga Sakthidharan

Abstract
. The open-source ecosystem has accelerated the democratization of Large Language Models (LLMs) through the public distribution of specialized Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) modules. However, integrating these third-party adapters often induces catastrophic forgetting of the base model’s foundational safety alignment. Restoring these guardrails via fine-tuning on safety data introduces an opposing failure mode: the severe degradation of the specialized domain knowledge the adapter was originally designed to provide. To overcome this zero-resource challenge, we propose Neural Weight Translation (NeWTral), a framework that directly maps unsafe, domain-specific adapters onto a safe alignment manifold while rigorously preserving their core expertise. NeWTral operates as a non-linear translation module pre-trained on a diverse corpus of unsafe-to-safe adapter pairs. By executing this mapping entirely within the parameter space, NeWTral utilizes an adaptive Mixture of Experts (MoE) routing strategy to autonomously blend high-fidelity surgical translators and aggressive alignment experts. We evaluate our framework across four architectural families (Llama, Mistral, Qwen, and Gemma) at scales up to 72B parameters across eight diverse scientific and professional domains. Our results demonstrate that the MoE variant achieves a radical reduction in the average Attack Success Rate (ASR), dropping from 70% in unsafe experts to just 13%, while maintaining an exceptional 90% average knowledge fidelity. Much like the crowdsourced adapters it remedies, the NeWTral module is designed as a standalone, downloadable asset that allows practitioners to restore safety alignment instantly without requiring access to original training data or hardware-intensive retraining. Content Warning: This paper contains examples of harmful language



1.
Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) have shown strong performance in language understanding and generation of responses across a wide range of tasks
(
Brown et al. 2020
;
Touvron et al. 2023
;
Chowdhery et al. 2023
;
Qin et al. 2023
)
. However, despite their general capabilities, LLMs often fall short in specialized areas that require domain knowledge. Studies across domains, including medicine, law, finance, and more, identify several limitations in LLMs, but not limited to hallucinated content, reduced factual accuracy, and errors in multi-step reasoning tasks
(
Kim et al. 2025
;
Kembu et al. 2025
;
Kang and Liu 2023
;
Lai et al. 2024
;
Huang et al. 2025
)
.

Fine-tuning is a common approach for adapting LLMs to domain-specific tasks by training on curated datasets, improving task alignment
(
Zhang et al. 2025
;
Anisuzzaman et al. 2025
;
Yang et al. 2023
)
. However, full fine-tuning is computationally expensive and time-consuming
(
Han et al. 2024a
)
.
Parameter-efficient methods such as Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)
(
Hu et al. 2021
)
address this limitation by freezing pretrained weights and introducing low-rank adapters, thereby reducing the number of trainable parameters.
A key challenge in domain-specific fine-tuning is the degradation of previously learned behaviors, particularly safety. Prior work shows that aligned LLMs remain vulnerable to downstream fine-tuning on datasets containing unsafe or noisy examples, often acquiring unsafe behaviors more rapidly
(
Zhao et al. 2023
;
Qi et al. 2023
;
Zhan et al. 2024
)
. While subsequent safety fine-tuning can partially restore alignment, it frequently reduces performance on domain-specific tasks. We refer to this trade-off between safety and task performance as the utility–alignment tax.

The conventional mitigation to this phenomenon is to mix safety-oriented data with domain data during the supervised fine-tuning phase.
However, in real-world deployment, especially within the decentralized open-source AI ecosystem, this mitigation is not always applicable.
Frequently, practitioners rely on pre-trained, highly specialized adapters downloaded from third-party repositories. In these zero-resource scenarios, the original domain training data is entirely inaccessible due to proprietary restrictions, data privacy regulations, or storage expiration, rendering traditional data-mixing strategies mathematically impossible. Similarly, an organization can train an adapter on proprietary data but needs to restore safety alignment in the model without access to safety-oriented data.

Recent post-hoc interventions attempt to address this zero-data bottleneck by directly manipulating the parameter space using static orthogonal projections (e.g., Safe LoRA
(
Hsu et al. 2024
)
, SaLoRA
(
Li et al. 2025
)
). Yet, these approaches inherently assume that safety and domain utility occupy linearly separable subspaces.
As we demonstrate, this assumption fails in complex professional domains where specialized reasoning is deeply entangled with potential dual-use harm. Consequently, linear weight interventions indiscriminately overwrite expert weights, resulting in a severe utility-alignment tax: the model either remains vulnerable to harmful questions or suffers a catastrophic collapse of its specialized knowledge.

To resolve this fundamental issue, we propose Neural Weight Translation (NeWTral), a post-hoc alignment framework that operates directly on a fully-trained adapter without modifying the original training pipeline, restoring foundational safety guardrails directly in the parameter space. Crucially, NeWTral is zero-shot at deployment time: given a previously unseen unsafe adapter, the pre-trained translator requires no gradient-based optimization, no target domain data, and no safe reference adapter. Rather than relying on rigid linear algebraic projections, NeWTral frames safety restoration as a non-linear mapping between disjoint parameter subspaces. Crucially, the NeWTral module is designed to be trained a priori and distributed as a standalone asset. Much like the crowdsourced adapters it remedies, practitioners simply download the pre-trained NeWTral translator and apply it directly to their proprietary or third-party adapters.
Distributed as a pre-trained meta-model, it allows practitioners to secure unverified or proprietary adapters zero-shot at deployment time. We term this process zero-shot as it requires no gradient-based optimization on the target adapter and no access to the target’s underlying training data.

As demonstrated by our experimental campaign, having a monolithic translation strategy makes it difficult to achieve an optimal balance between utility and safety. To address this, NeWTral employs an autonomous, weight-space Mixture of Experts (MoE) routing strategy. By analyzing the statistical signatures of the unsafe adapter’s weights, layer by layer, the MoE router dynamically blends the outputs of two specialized translation networks: a Surgical Expert optimized to preserve depth and raw domain knowledge, and an Aggressive Expert designed to inject robust refusal heuristics and ethical compliance precisely where dual-use risks manifest.

We rigorously evaluate NeWTral across a massive experimental campaign encompassing four architectural families (Llama-3.1, Mistral, Qwen-2.5, and Gemma-2) scaling up to 72B parameters, tested on eight diverse, high-stakes domains (including medicine, law, finance, and physics). Our framework drastically outperforms existing state-of-the-art post-hoc alignment strategies.

•

2.
Background

2.1.
Low-Rank Adaptation

To avoid the prohibitive cost of full fine-tuning, LoRA
(
Hu et al. 2022
)
constrains weight updates
\Delta\mathbf{W}
for a frozen pre-trained matrix
\mathbf{W}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
to a low-rank decomposition
\Delta\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{BA}
, where
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}
and
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times k}
(
r\ll\min(d,k)
). The adapted forward pass for an input
\mathbf{h}
is computed as
\mathbf{W}^{\prime}\mathbf{h}=\mathbf{W}_{0}\mathbf{h}+\frac{\alpha}{r}\mathbf{BAh}
, where
\alpha
scales the adaptation magnitude. This reduces trainable parameters from
\mathcal{O}(dk)
to
\mathcal{O}(r(d+k))
, enabling the fine-tuning of 70B+ models on consumer hardware via 4-bit quantization (QLoRA)
(
Dettmers et al. 2023
)
. LoRA is a standard for safety alignment as it modifies behavior while preserving foundational capabilities
(
Hu et al. 2022
;
Biderman et al. 2024
)
.

2.2.
Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)

SFT adapts a model to a target behavioral profile by minimizing the standard negative log-likelihood (NLL) loss over a labeled dataset
\mathcal{D}=\{(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
:

(1)

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\theta)=-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{|\mathbf{y}_{i}|}\log P_{\theta}\!\left(y_{i,t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{i},\,y_{i,<t}\right).

SFT serves as the initial post-training stage in alignment pipelines
(
Ouyang et al. 2022
)
, typically utilizing human-written demonstrations to outperform purely synthetic targets
(
Bai et al. 2022a
)
. Recent direct distillation methods
(
Tunstall et al. 2023
)
leverage teacher-model outputs to reduce annotation costs. However, SFT remains susceptible to distribution shift, where adversarial prompts outside the training distribution can bypass the learned safety guardrails
(
Wei et al. 2023
;
Perez et al. 2022
)
.

2.3.
Safety Alignment

Safety alignment ensures that model behavior conforms to human values and ethical constraints
(
Amodei et al. 2016
;
Bai et al. 2022b
)
. Traditionally, Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)
(
Ouyang et al. 2022
)
maximizes a reward signal
r(x,y)
approximated by a reward model
\hat{r}_{\phi}
trained on human comparisons via the Bradley–Terry objective:
\mathcal{L}_{\text{RM}}(\phi)=-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{H}}[\log\sigma(\hat{r}_{\phi}(x,y^{+})-\hat{r}_{\phi}(x,y^{-}))]

(
Christiano et al. 2017
)
. The policy
\pi_{\theta}
is then optimized subject to a KL-divergence penalty against a reference policy
\pi_{\text{ref}}
to prevent distributional collapse:
\max_{\theta}\mathbb{E}[\hat{r}_{\phi}(x,y)]-\beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}\|\pi_{\text{ref}}]

(
Stiennon et al. 2020
)
.
Constitutional AI (CAI)
(
Bai et al. 2022b
)
automates this via AI-generated critiques based on normative principles
\mathcal{C}
. Recently, Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
(
Rafailov et al. 2023
)
bypassed the need for an explicit reward model using a closed-form objective:

(2)

\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\theta)=-\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{H}}\!\left[\log\sigma\!\left(\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y^{+}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y^{+}\mid x)}-\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y^{-}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y^{-}\mid x)}\right)\right].

Despite these advances, models often suffer from a utility-alignment tax, the degradation of specialized performance under safety constraints
(
Ouyang et al. 2022
)
, and remain vulnerable to adversarial jailbreaks
(
Perez et al. 2022
;
Wei et al. 2023
)
, necessitating more robust, data-efficient alignment methods.

3.
Methodology

3.1.
Threat Model

From a security perspective, the scenario we address is analogous to a software supply chain attack: a practitioner trusts a third-party artifact (the LoRA adapter) that has been tampered with or carelessly trained, without visibility into its provenance. NeWTral functions as a post-hoc sanitization layer that can be applied at integration time, analogous to static analysis or code signing in software pipelines.
We consider a scenario where, to obtain an expert model
M_{dom}
for a specific domain, instead of retraining or fine-tuning a base model
M
that was trained on general knowledge, we rely on a LoRA adapter
W=W_{B}W_{A}
, where
W_{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times k}
and
W_{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}
. This adapter is used to augment the model’s knowledge, turning it into an expert in the desired domain.
To obtain such an adapter, Supervised Fine-tuning is performed by minimizing the token-level cross-entropy loss over a target domain dataset
D_{dom}
:

Given a target sequence
y\in D_{dom}=\{y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{T}\}
, the loss function is defined as:

(3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(W_{A},W_{B})=-\sum\log p(y_{t}\mid y_{<t};\theta,W_{A},W_{B})

(4)

\displaystyle\Delta W\leftarrow\mathcal{L}(W_{A},W_{B})

(5)

\displaystyle W_{M}=W_{M}+\Delta W=W_{dom}+\frac{\alpha}{r}W_{B_{dom}}W_{A_{dom}},

where
W_{M}
represents the frozen parameters of the base model
M
,
W_{A_{dom}}
and
W_{B_{dom}}
are the trainable LoRA parameters,
T
denotes the total sequence length,
p_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid y_{<t})
is the probability of the
t
-th token conditioned on the preceding context, and
\frac{\alpha}{r}
is a scaling factor used to
normalize the magnitude of the update.

Training a LoRA adapter to specialize a base model to a particular domain can cause the original model
M
to lose its safety alignment
(
Farn et al. 2024
;
Jiang et al. 2025
;
Zhang et al. 2026
)
. This may lead the model to respond to harmful queries in dangerous ways, as it might generate outputs to unsafe prompts while also leveraging the additional domain-specific knowledge, for example, in the medical domain, to produce an even more advanced and toxic combination of instructions or prescriptions.

To address this issue, while training
W
on the selected domain, it is still possible to incorporate safety into the resulting adapter. A straightforward approach is to combine samples from the domain dataset
D_{dom}
with safety-focused examples taken from a given safety dataset
D_{safe}
.
In particular, we construct a
D_{SD}
dataset:

(6)

\mathcal{D}_{SD}=\mathcal{D}_{dom}\cup\mathcal{D}_{safe}.

Then, LoRA parameters are trained in the same way as a domain adapter using the new combined dataset:

(7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(W_{A_{safe}},W_{B_{safe}})=-\sum\log p(y_{t}\mid y_{<t};\theta,W_{A},W_{B})\ where\ y_{t}\in\mathcal{D}_{SD}.

The obtained
W_{safe}=W_{A_{safe}}W_{B_{safe}}
is now capable of mapping domain-specific queries to technical answers found in
D_{dom}
and refuses to answer questions that share harmful aspects already seen in
D_{safe}
.
While mixing domain and safety data during SFT is valid, it is often impractical due to data asymmetry. Indeed, practitioners frequently lack high-quality safety datasets. Conversely, industrial constraints, such as privacy policies, licensing, or storage expiration, render the original
D_{dom}
inaccessible for subsequent retraining. The lack of concurrent access to both data distributions makes traditional alignment a bottleneck for safe deployment. We instead propose a paradigm shift: rather than repeating costly training, a neural translator can restore safety by “curing” adapters in the parameter space.
By mapping unsafe expert weights onto a safe subspace, NeWTral provides a scalable, resource-efficient solution for aligning specialized LLMs without requiring the original training corpora or auxiliary safety data at deployment time.

3.2.
Neural Weight Translation

NeWTral is a post-hoc framework for mitigating safety degradation in domain-specialized adapters. It operates at deployment time on unseen adapters without requiring retraining or access to domain or safety data. As discussed in Section
3.1
, integrating LoRA adapters often weakens safety alignment in favor of domain-specific behavior.
In practice, such adapters are typically sourced externally, with training data unavailable due to privacy or proprietary constraints. This renders standard fine-tuning-based alignment impractical and often incurs a utility–alignment tax, degrading domain performance.
We address this setting by modeling alignment restoration as a learned mapping between adapter parameter configurations. NeWTral learns a transformation from unsafe adapter parameters to safety-aligned parameters using a neural network operating directly in parameter space. This enables targeted parameter modifications that reduce unsafe outputs while preserving domain-specific behavior.
The resulting model can be applied post hoc to existing adapters without access to their original training data, enabling alignment adjustments without additional task-specific fine-tuning.
Let
M
denote a base model aligned with generic knowledge. We specialize this model to a specific domain using LoRA. In particular, we consider two distinct versions of the same adapter: one adapter
W_{dom}
, trained exclusively on domain-specific data
\mathcal{D}_{dom}
, as introduced in Section
3.1
, to obtain a domain-expert adapter; and one adapter
W_{safe}
, trained on a combined dataset that includes both domain-specific data and safety-oriented data,
\mathcal{D}_{SD}=\mathcal{D}_{dom}\cup\mathcal{D}_{safe}
.
The objective of NeWTral is produce a mapping model
\mathcal{T}
such that:

(8)

\mathcal{T}(W_{dom})=W_{cured}\approx W_{safe}.

Crucially, this transformation is performed
zero-shot
from the practitioner’s perspective. While
\mathcal{T}
is optimized a priori on a diverse corpus of adapter pairs, its application to a specific, unverified domain adapter at deployment time is a pure inference operation. Because the translator has learned the underlying geometric relationship between the unsafe and safe subspace, it can project a previously unseen weight vector
W_{dom}
onto its safe counterpart without requiring any additional “shots” (i.e., training samples) of the domain-specific data or further fine-tuning of the model parameters.
Prior work on linear parameter-space interventions suggests that safety and domain knowledge are not cleanly separable, often leading to significant utility degradation. Our empirical results (Section
4.1.5
) further support this observation, showing that linear transformations fail to preserve domain expertise in complex settings.
This motivates modeling alignment restoration as a non-linear mapping, which can better capture the interaction between domain-specific representations and safety constraints. Such transformations enable targeted behavioral adjustments while preserving the high-frequency structure of expert knowledge.

Layer-wise NeWTral

Since LLMs are composed of
L
transformer decoder blocks stacked together, they rely on the same architectural modules repeated throughout the network. Within this structure, weight matrices that perform specific functional roles, such as MLP down-projections or attention output projections, keep the same dimensionality across all layers
l\in\{0,\dots,L-1\}
.

We use this structural symmetry to enable parameter-space learning. Instead of providing the entire adapter as a single input to a translation model
\mathcal{T}
, we treat the translator as an ensemble of models. Each sub-model is designed for a specific input and output dimension, mapped directly to the different layer shapes that make up the transformer.

To do this, we group the LoRA parameters by their geometric shapes. Let
\mathcal{S}
be the set of unique matrix dimensions

\{(d_{1},k_{1}),\dots,(d_{n},k_{n})\}
present in the model. For each unique shape
s\in\mathcal{S}
, we define a weight-space
\mathcal{WS}
:

(9)

\mathcal{WS}=\{\mathbf{WS}^{(l)}\mid\text{shape}(\mathbf{WS}^{(l)})=s,\forall l\in\{0,\dots,L-1\}\}.

By grouping layers with matching parameter shapes, we treat adapter weights as samples from a shared distribution, rather than independent per-layer instances. This effectively increases the number of training examples available to each translator from a small set of full adapters to
L
layer-wise samples per adapter.
This depth-static formulation assumes that parameter mappings generalize across layers with similar structure, improving data efficiency. To account for residual layer-specific variation, we also incorporate a depth-dynamic routing mechanism that conditions the transformation on layer-dependent features (e.g., layer statistics), allowing the model to adapt the translation across different depths.

Given this, we formulate
\mathcal{T}
as an enable of translation models:

(10)

\mathcal{T}=\bigcup_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\mathcal{T}_{s}.

Each sub-model
\mathcal{T}_{s}
is a non-linear ensemble network designed to map the unsafe behavioral steering matrix
\mathbf{W}^{(l)}_{dom,s}=\mathbf{W}^{(l)}_{A_{dom},s}\mathbf{W}^{(l)}_{B_{dom},s}
to its safe counterpart. Because the ensemble is trained on weights sampled from every depth of the transformer, from initial lexical processing to final semantic synthesis, it is forced to learn a depth-agnostic safety transformation.

NeWTral Ensemble Optimization

Following the ensemble logic, each sub-model
\mathcal{T}_{s}
is structured as a bottleneck architecture designed to perform non-linear mapping within the domain and safe weights.
We design the process as a two-stage projection: first, compressing the high-dimensional weight vector into a lower-dimensional latent space, and then expanding it back to the original dimensions to produce the safe parameter state.
For a given domain adapter
W_{d}om
, we flatten the parameters into a vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{D_{s}}
of flatten dimension
D_{s}
corresponding to:

(11)

D_{s}=d_{s}\cdot k_{s};\ where\ s=(d_{s},k_{s}).

The translator
\mathcal{T}_{s}
then processes this input through an encoder-decoder framework.
The first stage is a projection to a smaller dimension,
D_{s}\to d_{s}^{latent}
. This compression encourages the network to act as an information bottleneck. By squeezing the weights through a restricted latent dimension, the model is prevented from memorizing the specific technical values of the training domain (the "noise") and is instead forced to extract the abstract, structural features that represent safety and refusal (the "signal").

The second stage is a projection from the latent space back to the original dimensionality,
d_{s}^{latent}\to D_{s}
, to reconstruct the weights in their aligned state. Formally, given the flattened unsafe domain weights
\mathbf{x}_{dom}
as input, the model produces the safe weights
\mathbf{x}_{safe}
:

(12)

\mathbf{z}=\sigma(\mathbf{\Phi}_{enc}\mathbf{x}_{dom}+\mathbf{\beta}_{enc})

(13)

\mathbf{x}_{safe}=(\mathbf{\Phi}_{dec}\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{\beta}_{dec}),

where
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{s}^{latent}}
denotes the compressed safety representation, and
\mathbf{\Phi}_{enc}
,
\mathbf{\beta}_{enc}
,
\mathbf{\Phi}_{dec}
, and
\mathbf{\beta}_{dec}
correspond to the weight matrices
\mathbf{\Phi}
and bias vectors
\mathbf{\beta}
of the encoder and decoder of
\mathcal{T}
, respectively. This structure ensures that the translation is not a simple linear shift. Instead, the model learns a transformation that maps from the domain expert directly to the safety-aligned subspace. This allows the model to preserve the technical expertise present in the input weights while imposing the behavioral constraints required for the safe output.
A critical challenge in weight-space intervention is the numerical sensitivity of the underlying LLM; random perturbations to the parameters often result in catastrophic semantic collapse
(
Chen et al. 2026
)
. To mitigate this, we employ a Zero-Residual Initialization strategy.
Specifically, the parameters of the final projection layer are initialized to zero:
\mathbf{\Phi}_{dec}\leftarrow\mathbf{0},\quad\mathbf{\beta}_{dec}\leftarrow\mathbf{0}
.

This ensures that at the start of the training phase (
t=0
), the optimization process is forced to learn only the precise non-linear shifts required to align the model, rather than wasting capacity correcting for initial random noise. This approach allows the translator to modify the model’s behavioral steering while maintaining the structural integrity of its technical knowledge.

Then, our framework involves optimizing the parameters
(\mathbf{\Phi},\mathbf{\beta})
of the different submodels that compose the translator ensemble. Unlike the Supervised Fine-Tuning phase, which operates on discrete token probabilities, the translation ensemble is trained entirely within the continuous parameter space.

We construct a multi-domain training corpus
\mathcal{X_{s}}
, that includes all the layers that flatten have dimension
D_{s}
, consisting of weight-vector pairs
(\mathbf{x}_{dom},\mathbf{x}_{safe})
derived from various technical fields. For each unique shape
s\in\mathcal{S}
, the dedicated sub-model
\mathcal{T}_{s}
is optimized to minimize a divergence function
\mathcal{DV}
between the predicted safe weights
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{cured}
and the gold-standard aligned weights
\mathbf{x}_{safe}
:

(14)

\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{\Phi},\mathbf{\beta})=\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x}_{dom},\mathbf{x}_{safe})\sim\mathcal{X_{s}}}[\mathcal{DV}(\mathbf{x}_{safe},\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{cured})],

where
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{cured}=\mathcal{T}_{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{dom})
represents the output of the residual translator.

The objective function
\mathcal{DV}
can be adapted depending on the geometric properties of the target weight distribution. While a standard Mean Squared Error (MSE) is typically employed, the framework allows more specialized distance metrics according to the base architecture used for each sub-model of
\mathcal{T}
.

By training across diverse professional domains, the ensemble learns to isolate the shared mathematical transformations required for alignment while ignoring domain-specific weight fluctuations. Once converged, the ensemble can be applied to any naively fine-tuned adapter in a zero-shot manner, instantly projecting unsafe parameters onto the safe subspace without requiring further access to the original training data.

The final stage of the NeWTral process is the reconstruction of the LoRA matrices from the translated parameter vectors. After a sub-model
\mathcal{T}_{s}
has generated the cured parameter vector
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{cured}\in\mathbb{R}^{D_{s}}
, an inverse transformation is applied to recover the functional matrix dimensions required by the LLM.

Let
\mathcal{R}_{d_{s},k_{s}}(\cdot)
denote the reshaping operator that maps a flattened vector back to its matrix form
\mathbb{R}^{D_{s}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{s}\times k_{s}}
. The reconstructed cured matrix
\hat{W}
is defined as:

(15)

\hat{W}=\hat{\mathbf{W}}_{B_{cured}}\hat{\mathbf{W}}_{A_{cured}}=\mathcal{R}_{d_{s},k_{s}}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{cured}).

The translated parameters are then re-integrated into the model’s computation graph. The effective weight matrix
W^{\prime}_{M}
used during the forward pass is calculated by summing the frozen pre-trained weights
W_{M}
and the reconstructed low-rank update:

(16)

W^{\prime}_{M}=W_{M}+\frac{\alpha}{r}(\hat{\mathbf{W}}_{B_{cured}}\hat{\mathbf{W}}_{A_{cured}}).

This reconstruction ensures that the intervention remains transparent to the underlying Transformer architecture. By replacing the behavioral steering matrices within the identified bottlenecks, the model preserves its technical domain expertise while adopting the refusal logic dictated by the learned safety behavior. Since the NeWTral operation is a one-time parameter shift generation performed at loading time, it provides a high-efficiency alignment solution that restores safety without increasing inference-time latency or requiring the deployment of multiple redundant model instances.

Adaptive Alignment via Mixture of Experts

While a Static NeWTral translator effectively maps safe to unsafe, it inherently optimizes for fixed regions of the safety-utility tax.
As we will demonstrate in our experiments, the optimal alignment strategy is domain-dependent. Scientific domains typically require a surgical translation to preserve knowledge depth, whereas institutional domains often require an aggressive translation to enforce ethical compliance and initiate a structural persona shift. Consequently, relying on a single, monolithic translator imposes an inflexible trade-off across heterogeneous domains.
To solve this, we propose a translator that mixes the knowledge of multiple experts, obtaining a Mixture of Experts (MoE) NeWTral.
The MoE dynamically blends the outputs of two pre-trained, frozen translation experts, each possessing distinct behavioral characteristics.
The first expert acts as Surgical Expert
\mathcal{T}_{surg}
optimized on high fidelity, ensuring minimal semantic drift from the original domain expert.
The second is characterized by a more aggressive NeWTral obtaining an Aggressive Expert
\mathcal{T}_{aggr}
optimized for maximum safety alignment, effectively improving robust safety heuristics and ethical policing.
The specific architectural instantiations of these experts will be detailed and empirically derived in Section
4
.
The core of this architecture is a lightweight, trainable Routing Network,
\mathcal{R}_{\phi}
. For a given unsafe LoRA weight matrix
W_{dom}^{(l)}
at layer
l
, we extract a feature vector
\mathcal{F}_{l}
comprising weight-space statistics (e.g., mean, variance,
L_{2}
norm) and the normalized layer position. The router analyzes these latent characteristics to compute an autonomous gating coefficient
g_{l}\in[0,1]
:
g_{l}=\mathcal{R}_{\phi}(\mathcal{F}_{l})
.

The final cured weight matrix
W_{cured}^{(l)}
is then generated via a convex combination of the experts’ outputs:

(17)

W_{cured}^{(l)}=(1-g_{l})\cdot T_{surg}(W_{dom}^{(l)})+g_{l}\cdot T_{aggr}(W_{dom}^{(l)}).

The parameters
\phi
of the routing layer are trained using the combination of two different losses that try to balance between utility and safety.
By keeping the pre-trained translation experts frozen, the router is forced to learn an interpolation policy that simultaneously maximizes domain utility and behavioral safety.
Formally, for a given layer
l
, the loss is defined as the unweighted sum of the mean squared errors against two distinct targets:

(18)

\mathcal{L}(\phi)=\underbrace{\|W_{cured}^{(l)}-T_{surg}(W_{dom}^{(l)})\|_{2}^{2}}_{\mathcal{L}_{util}}+\underbrace{\|W_{cured}^{(l)}-W_{safe}^{(l)}\|_{2}^{2}}_{\mathcal{L}_{safe}}.

The router minimizes an unweighted sum of
\mathcal{L}_{util}
and
\mathcal{L}_{safe}
, acting as utility and safety anchors to penalize deviations from
\mathcal{T}_{surg}
and the safe reference, respectively. This formulation frames alignment as an autonomous multi-objective optimization problem that naturally navigates the Pareto frontier without manual hyperparameter tuning. Sensitivity analysis (Section
4.5
) confirms the framework’s robustness to this unweighted configuration, as intrinsic weight-space signatures provide sufficient discriminative signal for optimal gating without exogenous bias. Consequently,
g_{l}
provides a depth-dynamic mechanism: maintaining surgical fidelity in theoretical domains while escalating aggressive safety injection (
\mathcal{T}_{aggr}
) in high-risk scenarios to enforce behavioral steering.

Dataset

All the datasets are from HuggingFace and GitHub.

Adapters dataset
- To obtain the adapters employed in the training and evaluation of the NewTral modules, we instantiated 10 distinct adapters for each of the eight selected domains, with each adapter trained on a different dataset specific to that domain. 4.1.5.
Compared Baselines

In our experimental study, we compare our method with two state-of-the-art baselines: Safe LoRA
(
Hsu et al. 2024
)
and SaLoRA
(
Li et al. 2025
)
. It is important to note how these baselines intersect with our deployment scenario. Safe LoRA is not adapted; we evaluate it using its original methodology, which requires access to the safe target-domain adapter. Although this bypasses the zero-resource limitation of our threat model, it provides a strong benchmark for what static linear projections can achieve when fully resourced. On the other hand, SaLoRA is natively a training-time intervention that we adapt into a post-hoc linear filter, placing it at a disadvantage. By including both, we aim to demonstrate that our learned non-linear translation (NeWTral) outperforms linear parameter-space interventions, whether they are constrained by our post-hoc scenario or provided with ideal reference data. 4.1.7.
Evaluation & Metrics

We evaluate our framework using two primary metrics:
Attack Success Rate (ASR)
and
Knowledge Retention (KR)
. ASR is determined via an ensemble of three evaluators: Llama-Guard-3-8B
(
Inan et al. 2023
;
Llama Team 2024
)
, the SecureBreak safety classifier
(
Arazzi et al. 2026
)
, and a keyword-based refusal signal. KR measures the semantic fidelity of cured model responses against the unsafe domain expert on benign queries, capturing preservation of the fine-tuning delta rather than base model behavior, using cosine similarity (via Sentence-Transformers
(
Reimers and Gurevych 2019
)
) and BERTScore
(
Zhang* et al. 2020
)

Final Score
is the harmonic mean of
(1-\text{ASR})
and
KR
, providing a single safety-utility trade-off metric. Full implementation details are in Appendix
B.3
.

As expected, the original unsafe domain experts exhibit severe safety vulnerabilities, with baseline ASR frequently exceeding 60%-70% across all architectures.

When evaluating state-of-the-art mitigation strategies against these vulnerabilities, we observe critical limitations imposed by the zero-resource nature of our threat model. SaLoRA, which is inherently designed as a training-time intervention utilizing auxiliary safety modules, cannot be natively performed on pre-trained, downloaded adapters. When adapted into a post-hoc linear filter for our scenario, it struggles to capture the non-linear, context-dependent nature of the safety subspace, resulting in unacceptably high residual ASRs.
On the other hand, SafeLoRA achieves a respectable reduction in ASR, performing comparably to our lower-tier bottleneck translators (AE and CVAE). However, it still falls significantly short of the safety and utility optimization achieved by our best-performing non-linear translators (FM, MLP, and MoE). More critically, SafeLoRA’s reliance on calculating a static linear projection intrinsically requires access to an aligned reference pair (both the safe and unsafe adapters) for the specific target model. This requirement violates the strict zero-data, zero-resource constraint of our deployment scenario.

In contrast, our pre-trained, non-linear NeWTral modules operate entirely zero-shot. They successfully map unsafe weights to safer configurations without requiring any target-domain safe reference, effectively solving the core resource bottleneck.
We first observe the performance of the compression-based models (AE and CVAE). The AE translator utilizes a strict low-dimensional bottleneck to compress knowledge and discard unsafe details. While this provides the lowest inference overhead, the deterministic compression overpenalizes the weights, leading to inconsistent preservation of utility. The CVAE treats the mapping as a probabilistic generative task. Although theoretically robust, the variance introduced by sampling from the latent prior translates to the extreme numerical sensitivity required for LLM weight-space manipulation.

Discarding the bottleneck models, our evaluation reveals a clear dichotomy between the two remaining architectures. The MLP translator consistently achieves the lowest ASR, acting as the most robust safeguard against malicious prompts. However, this comes at the cost of the preserved knowledge, reflected in lower Knowledge Retention scores. Manual inspection of the generated answers reveals that the MLP translator does not merely block harmful tokens; it completely restructures the model’s communication style. This suggests that, even on benign prompts, the MLP-cured model frequently abandons the authoritative, clinical tone of the domain expert in favor of a polite, helpful assistant. This persona drift explains the high safety efficacy but highlights a tendency toward over-correction. In Section
4.6
, we analyze in depth this aspect. Instead, the FM model, by modeling a straight-line constant velocity path between unsafe and safe distributions, maintains high Knowledge Retention scores, remaining almost mathematically identical to the original expert, as shown in Section
4.3
.

Manual evaluation confirms that FM-cured models preserve the tone of the domain expert. When confronted with dual-use risks, FM relies on descriptive deflection, providing safe, theoretical explanations without drifting into hard refusal. While useful for scientific applications, this extreme fidelity occasionally permits a helpfulness override in complex adversarial scenarios, resulting in slightly higher ASRs than the MLP.
Rather than re-optimizing for a fixed point on the safety-utility frontier, the MoE router dynamically arbitrates between these failure modes layer by layer, achieving what neither monolithic translator can alone. The MoE variant achieves a radical reduction in global average ASR (dropping from
\sim
70% to
\sim
13%) while maintaining an exceptional
\sim
90% average knowledge fidelity across all tested scales (7B to 72B).
As evidenced by the individual evaluation metrics in our tables, the MoE’s performance predictably situates itself between the extremes of its constituent experts. Rather than over-optimizing solely for utility (like FM) or solely for safety (like MLP), the dynamic routing ensures a mathematically balanced solution. Consequently, the MoE yields the highest aggregate Final Score across all architectures, successfully mitigating the specific failure modes of both base translators.

To evaluate whether NeWTral’s safety restoration is truly robust or merely a result of stylistic shifts, we incorporate the JailbreakBench stress test into our aggregate metrics. As detailed in the standalone JBB analysis in Appendix
E
, the MoE variant proves its true strength under adversarial pressure. While the Surgical Expert (FM) occasionally permits ‘helpfulness overrides’ in complex jailbreaks, the MoE’s routing logic effectively identifies these high-intensity adversarial signatures. The router dynamically escalates the gating value
g
to leverage the Aggressive Expert exactly when the JBB prompts deviate from the expected domain distribution. Consequently, the MoE variant achieves a Pareto-optimal state: it matches the adversarial resilience of the MLP translator on the JBB dataset while significantly outperforming it in KR.

4.3.
Internal Representation Analysis

To investigate how the Neural Weight Translation affects the different layers of the LLM, we analyze the hidden-state trajectories across its layers.
To do so, we define two primary metrics: Safety Recovery (
R
) and Knowledge Preservation (
KP
).
Safety Recovery metric measures the degree to which the cured adapter
W_{cured}
aligns with the refusal behavior of a safe-tuned domain expert
W_{safe}
, relative to the original unsafe expert
W_{dom}
. Given the mean hidden states
h
at layer
l
for a harmful prompt
p_{h}
, it is defined as:

(19)

R_{l}=\max\left(0,\frac{\text{sim}(h_{cured,l},h_{safe,l})-\text{sim}(h_{dom,l},h_{safe,l})}{1-\text{sim}(h_{dom,l},h_{safe,l})}\right)\times 100.

where
\text{sim}(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes cosine similarity.

The Knowledge Preservation measures the semantic drift from the original expert knowledge. Evaluated on benign domain-specific queries
p_{b}
, it is defined as the distance:

(20)

KP_{l}=1-\text{sim}(h_{M,l},h_{dom,l}),\ where\ M\in\{cured,safe\}.

5.
Limitations and Future Work

While NeWTral’s MoE variant successfully finds the safety-utility trade-off through autonomous weight-space routing, several open challenges remain. The method does not fully eliminate unsafe outputs, particularly where harmful and benign content are closely intertwined.
The eight training domains comprise ten datasets from heterogeneous sources, exposing the translator to substantial intra-domain distributional variation, distinct sub-topics, difficulty levels, and linguistic registers, thereby encouraging the extraction of domain-agnostic safety geometry rather than domain-specific surface patterns. Generalization to structurally novel domains remains an open direction. Generating paired (unsafe, safe) adapters requires access to safety data during NeWTral’s pre-training phase, even if not at deployment time. Practitioners who cannot produce such pairs must rely on a community-distributed NeWTral module, analogous to the community-distributed domain adapters this framework remediates. The routing network learns a fixed interpolation policy. Future iterations could incorporate a steerable conditioning parameter, allowing stakeholders to calibrate risk tolerance continuously, from expert depth (low
g
) to maximum ethical policing (high
g
), without retraining. The current binary mixture demonstrates the principle of weight-space gating but does not address orthogonal harm categories independently. Future work should explore specialized experts targeting Personally Identifiable Information leakage, algorithmic bias, or software vulnerabilities, enabling granular structural interventions without persona drift.

6.
Related Work

The vulnerability of aligned LLMs to catastrophic safety forgetting during fine-tuning is well-documented
(
Farn et al. 2024
;
Jiang et al. 2025
;
Zhang et al. 2026
)
.
While pre-trained models are aligned using techniques such as RLHF to embed safety guardrails, subsequent fine-tuning on domain-specific datasets that lack safety-focused examples often degrades these safety alignments
(
Dai et al. 2023
;
Ji et al. 2025
)
.
This issue occurs due to Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) techniques, especially LoRA, which can erode the latent safety structures formed during pre-training
(
Liu et al. 2024
;
Lermen et al. 2023
)
. As a result, models that are fine-tuned for narrow applications (such as medical diagnosis or financial analysis) become significantly more vulnerable to domain-specific jailbreaks, harmful obedience to malicious prompts, and privacy breaches.
It has been shown that LoRA adapters can be leveraged to conduct auditing or mitigate flaws in base models
(
Arazzi and Nocera 2026
)
.
Recent work tries to separate task performance from safety by directly modifying model parameters.
With this goal, Safe LoRA
(
Hsu et al. 2024
)
demonstrated that safety alignment is contained within controllable parameter subspaces. More precisely, its core approach involves computing the difference between safe and unsafe versions of the same adapter, then applying a linear projection to adjust the corrupted weights into a safe configuration during post-training.
Similarly, SaLoRA
(
Li et al. 2025
)
addresses this issue during the training phase of the unsafe adapters. In this case, the projection of the safe weights is performed by introducing an auxiliary safety module that encourages the weights to grow orthogonally to the pre-existing safety weights, thereby minimizing interference.

Despite safety alignment, LLMs are still vulnerable to jailbreak attacks. Recent research exploits these vulnerabilities, revealing that safety mechanisms often fail under targeted structural analysis. For instance, the XBreaking
(
Arazzi et al. 2025
)
approach leverages AI explainability to compare censored and uncensored models to identify exploitable alignment patterns, demonstrating how targeted noise injection can effectively shatter alignment constraints. More on this, the NeuroStrike attack
(
Wu et al. 2025b
)
is a framework demonstrating that modern alignment heavily relies on sparse, specialized safety neurons. By pruning a small subset of neurons, NeuroStrike achieves a high ASR without relying on traditional trial-and-error prompt optimization. In addition, GateBreaker
(
Wu et al. 2025a
)
, a training-free attack that weakens MoE LLM safety by identifying and disabling a small set of safety-critical neurons. It shows that safety is highly localized and vulnerable, enabling large increases in ASR with minimal performance loss and generalizes across models and modalities.

Recent literature emphasizes evaluating defense-enhanced LLMs systematically, accounting for the diverse array of attack vectors and configurations, often referred to as a "bag of tricks"
(
Xu et al. 2024
)
. To this end, JailbreakBench
(
Chao et al. 2024
)
was introduced as an open robustness benchmark to unify the evaluation of jailbreak attacks and defenses under a reproducible framework. Although explicitly defending against jailbreaks is not the primary focus of our work, our proposed structural modifications intrinsically impact the model’s weight representations. Therefore, to rigorously assess the safety implications and adversarial resilience of our approach, we evaluate our cured models using the standardized JailbreakBench framework, demonstrating that our method preserves (and potentially fortifies) the model’s safety alignment under adversarial conditions.

While our work builds upon the weight-space discoveries of Safe LoRA and SaLoRA, it departs from them in two fundamental ways. First, rather than relying on static linear projections or altered training loops, NeWTral models the safety subspace as a non-linear mapping. Second, instead of extracting safety vectors from an isolated domain, our translators are trained concurrently across diverse, high-stakes fields. This multi-domain exposure prevents overfitting, enabling the network to learn a generalized geometric representation of safety that transfers reliably across heterogeneous professional datasets.

7.
Conclusion

In this work, we introduced NeWTral, a zero-shot at deployment time projection framework that mitigates the catastrophic safety degradation induced by specialized LoRA adapters. By exploiting the structural heterogeneity of Transformer architectures, we developed an autonomous weight-space MoE router that balances safety re-alignment with the preservation of expert knowledge. Our evaluations across eight technical domains and scales up to 72B parameters demonstrate that NeWTral achieves a superior safety-utility trade-off compared to existing linear baselines. These results prove that complex behavioral constraints can be systematically encoded and transferred strictly within the parameter space, bypassing the need for hardware-intensive gradient optimization. Future work will extend this framework to include steerable conditioning parameters and explore its efficacy in mitigating orthogonal security threats, such as targeted backdoors and continuous distribution shifts.

Datasets

This section reports additional details of the dataset used in training and testing of the adapters.

Training
- The domain-specific (knowledge) data consist of eight distinct and diverse domains: medical, mathematics, financial, legal, academic, physics, biomedical, and chemistry. Testing

Each domain includes 20 carefully chosen Q&A pairs covering a wide range of topics and difficulty levels within the field to assess the knowledge of the cured adapters. To assess the safety of the adapters, we merged two types of datasets: 50 harmful knowledge questions from each domain and a set of harmful prompts from the JBB-Behaviors dataset
(
Chao et al. 2024
)
. JBB-Behaviors is used as a stress test, as it spans a wide range of harmful behaviors across ten categories aligned with OpenAI usage policies, including disinformation, fraud, harassment, and physical harm. This combined setup allows us to evaluate both domain-specific risks and general safety awareness.

B.4.
Experimental Settings

To build the adapter dataset used to train the NeWTral translators, we fine-tuned each domain adapter via SFT for up to 500 epochs with early stopping (patience of 5 checks, evaluated every 50 epochs), using rank
r{=}32
, bfloat16 precision, and learning rate
2\times 10^{-4}
. Per domain, 8 adapters are reserved for training the NeWTral modules, and 2 are held out as unseen test adapters for evaluation, ensuring the translator is never exposed to the target adapters during training. Architecture and training details for the NeWTral modules themselves are reported in Appendix
B.6
.

B.5.
Baselines

In the experimental campaign, we evaluate our approach against two state-of-the-art baselines adapted to our post-hoc intervention scenario.

First, we consider Safe LoRA
(
Hsu et al. 2024
)
, which identifies a safety subspace through a linear projection derived from the parameter-space difference between safe and unsafe adapter states. A primary requirement of this method is the availability of an aligned reference pair for the specific target model to calculate the projection; in contrast, our approach utilizes a neural translator to generalize the alignment subspace across domains, removing the need for a domain-specific safe reference at inference time.
Second, we compare against SaLoRA
(
Li et al. 2025
)
, which introduces an additional orthogonal projection matrix
C
to constrain weight updates. Although SaLoRA was originally designed as a training-time intervention to prevent safety degradation, we adapt it to our post-training scenario by utilizing a universal
C
matrix, calculated from generic safety data, and applying it as a linear filter to the behavioral steering matrices (
W_{B}
) of the domain-specific adapters. This allows us to benchmark our non-linear translation framework against a static, universal linear baseline.

Training Details
- The optimizer, learning rate, epoch count, and batch size were chosen conservatively given the small dataset size. Adam with a learning rate of
1\times 10^{-3}
is a robust default that converges reliably in the low-data regime without requiring a learning rate schedule. Training runs for 150 epochs with a batch size of 8, and the loader cycles over the same samples multiple times per epoch, which is equivalent to repeated passes with minor stochastic variation from shuffling. The MSE loss, given by:

(21)

\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\frac{1}{N_{s}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{s}}\left\lVert f_{\theta}\!\left(\mathbf{W}^{(l)}_{\text{dom}}\right)-\mathbf{W}^{(l)}_{\text{safe}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

Conditional Flow Matching (CFM) models the transformation from unsafe to safe weight distributions via straight-line interpolation between them. Along this path, the target velocity is constant,

\mathbf{v}^{*}=\mathbf{w}_{\text{safe}}-\mathbf{w}_{\text{unsafe}}

Mixture of Experts (MoE)

As introduced in the methodology section, this version of the NeWTral translator relies on an MLP-based router that takes as input a set of high-level features extracted from the unsafe domain expert
W_{dom}
. In this configuration, we instantiate the surgical expert using Flow Matching (FM) and the aggressive expert using a multi-layer perceptron (MLP); a detailed justification for these specific architectural choices is provided in Section
4
.
To characterize the "statistical signature" of the domain expert and determine the optimal mixture coefficient
g
, the router processes a feature vector
\mathcal{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{6}
composed of: The mean (
\mu
) and standard deviation (
\sigma
) of the flattened weight distribution to capture the central tendency and dispersion of the learned parameters; The
L_{2}
norm and the maximum absolute value of the weights, representing the magnitude and peak intensity of the expert’s updates; The structural dimensionality of the weight matrix, scaled for numerical stability, and the normalized layer position (
l/L
) to provide context regarding the weight’s role within the model’s processing hierarchy.
This allows the router to move beyond raw parameter matching and instead learn a sophisticated, context-aware mapping policy. Specifically, the router learns to distinguish between early-layer features and late-layer Decision Zones.

Appendix E
Performance on Harmful JBB-Behaviors (JailbreakBench)

In line with previous evaluations carried out and discussed in Section
4.2
, we assess our approach across several scenarios and subsequently focus on a more structured evaluation. To align with general safety objectives, we use harmful prompts from the JBB-Behaviors dataset to evaluate model behavior under potentially unsafe conditions. The dataset was selected because it contains 100 unique misuse behaviors grouped into ten major categories aligned with OpenAI’s usage policies, namely: Disinformation, Economic harm, Expert advice, Fraud/Deception, Government decision-making, Harassment/Discrimination, Malware/Hacking, Physical harm, Privacy, and Sexual/Adult content. Together with this domain-diverse dataset, the setup enables a systematic analysis of the robustness and generalization capabilities of the generated adapters

Table
10
presents detailed ASR results for the selected models evaluated on harmful inputs. As expected, the original Unsafe domain experts show the highest safety failure on the JBB dataset; the results show a clear degradation in safety with higher ASR across all methods and the three safety evaluation metrics and architectures. Across the different scores, the curing methods show a clear and consistent hierarchy in effectiveness when ordered from weaker to stronger performance in reducing ASR.

Starting with Safe LoRA and SaLoRA, these methods provide only moderate improvements over the unsafe baselines and are generally the least effective among all approaches. Safe LoRA occasionally reduces ASR in a meaningful way, but its performance is inconsistent across models. SaLoRA, in particular, often fails to improve robustness and, in some cases, performs worse than Safe LoRA, suggesting that its modification of the LoRA adaptation does not reliably align with safety objectives when JBB harmful questions are considered. Consistently, our NeWTral framework with different translator variations, such as AE, CVAE, FM, MLP, and MoE, outperforms the Safe LoRA and SaLoRA across all the models.

AE and CVAE both provide intermediate performance by learning latent representations that aim to suppress unsafe behaviors. CVAE tends to be more stable and slightly more effective than AE, which achieves lower ASR. Although both methods consistently outperform LoRA-based approaches, their overall reductions in ASR remain moderate, indicating that reconstruction-based objectives alone are insufficient for strong adversarial robustness.

FM further improves performance, delivering more consistent and substantial reductions in different ASR scores across models. By enforcing structured transformations in representation space, FM better aligns model outputs with safety constraints. However, despite its stability, it does not consistently achieve the lowest ASR values, leaving room for more expressive methods to outperform it. The MLP-based approach marks a significant improvement, often achieving very low ASR, particularly on larger models. This suggests that learned projection layers can effectively filter unsafe directions in the representation space. Nevertheless, its performance is not uniformly consistent across all architectures, indicating some sensitivity to model scale and distributional variation.

Finally, the MoE method achieves the best overall performance, demonstrating a superior Pareto-optimal balance between safety and utility. While the standalone Aggressive (MLP) expert occasionally yields lower absolute ASR values in specific configurations, such as Llama-3.1-70B and Qwen2.5-72B, it does so by incurring a prohibitive utility-alignment tax. By contrast, the MoE’s routing-based design enables selective processing that prioritizes the Surgical Expert for knowledge-dense layers while escalating safety injection only where adversarial risk is detected. Consequently, MoE consistently yields the highest aggregate Final Score across all architectures, establishing itself as the most robust and scalable strategy for restoring safety alignment without sacrificing the model’s specialized mechanistic depth.

Figure

presents a comparative evaluation of multiple alignment strategies, i.e., unsafe baseline(finetuned), Safe LoRA, SaLoRA, and our MoE approach across a diverse set of risk-sensitive dimensions for several language models. The figure represent a average ASR of all the three defined ASR across different domains of the JBB dataset.

Across nearly all models and categories, the Unsafe Finetuned condition forms the outer envelope, indicating the highest propensity for unsafe or unregulated outputs. In contrast, NeWTral (MoE) consistently yields the most compact profiles, demonstrating strong suppression of harmful behaviors across all evaluated categories. SafeLoRA achieves a balanced reduction in risk, while SaLoRA exhibits comparatively higher variance, occasionally approaching the unsafe baseline in sensitive domains such as fraud/deception and expert advice.

A consistent trend emerges across model scales; larger models generally exhibit improved controllability, with tighter distributions under aligned methods and clearer separation from the unsafe baseline. However, certain categories—most notably fraud/deception and expert advice—remain challenging. These findings suggest that while alignment techniques significantly mitigate risk, their effectiveness is domain-dependent and benefits from increased model capacity.

The results highlight the importance of both alignment strategy and model scale in achieving robust safety performance. NeWTral (MoE) demonstrates superior and consistent risk minimization, suggesting that a mixture-of-experts-based alignment offers a promising direction for fine-grained control.
