 Abstract

 This report details considerations related to the statistical treatment of model discrepancy as applied
 to multi-physics systems. The report includes a delineation of when the use of cut Bayes methodology
 can be expected to be fruitful for discrepancy models, essentially by separating the problem of inferring
 physical parameters from the problem of inferring model discrepancies. This approach is applied to a
 known example from the inverse problems literature. This is followed by a real-data example involving
 strength models where a cut Bayes approach to discrepancy yields improved precision and accuracy
 of predictions on average. We conclude with a treatment of multiple discrepancies; future potential
 directions based on this work are given in concluding remarks.

1 Introduction and executive summary
This report details findings and developments regarding model discrepancy in modular, multi-physics sys-
tems. Many physical computer models are constructed via the composition of numerical submodels where
each submodel represents an important process of interest. For example, Mathews et al. (2025) rely on a
chemical reactor model with distinct modules for turbulence, catalyst properties, and reaction kinetics, the
outputs of which are combined together in modeling ethylene oxide output. Mathews et al. (2025) intro-
duce the cut Bayes approach for multi-physics problems, in which a probability distribution for some subset
of physics parameters is sampled to propagate uncertainty forward, but this distribution is not updated
based on additional, downstream experiments. Justifications for this approach can be found in Plummer
(2015) and Mathews et al. (2025), and include crucial statistical modeling issues like misspecification and
non-identifiability.
 Our work in this report details an important use of cut Bayes modeling that has not been thoroughly
examined in previous treatments, where one prefers to keep distributions of physical parameters fixed and
infers a discrepancy separately from inference of said physical parameters. As will be shown in this report,
the primary advantage of this cut Bayes approach is that it has the potential to mitigate non-identifiability

 The central messages in this work are summarized as follows.

  1. Uncertainty propagation: For propagating parameter samples forward while learning one or many
 discrepancies (i.e., simulator error/bias functions), the modular Bayesian/cut Bayesian framework is
 natural. A modular Bayes approach involving discrepancy was first considered in Liu et al. (2009),
 though the cut distribution was made mathematically precise in Plummer (2015) but not specifically
 for discrepancy. In this report, we tie these two major works together to utilize a cut distribution for
 discrepancy.

  2. Team interoperability: One main advantage of this approach is that it allows for interoperability be-
 tween teams providing samples for different pieces of physics or submodels. The modular Bayesian/cut
 Bayesian approach reduces well-known identifiability issues involving discrepancy and physical param-
 eters by construction, as the inference of parameters is separated from the inference of discrepancy as
 opposed to doing these jointly.

  3. Efficient sampling: Just as for hierarchical models, in cut models the use of a conjugate prior can
 make cut posterior sampling efficient. For example, a Gaussian process (GP) prior for discrepancy is
 easy to handle because of conjugacy when the data have a Gaussian distribution.

  4. Non-GP discrepancy: Discrepancy need not be represented by a GP prior. In particular, if there
 is a persistent trend, a 0-mean GP will not perform very well. We illustrate this with cut Bayes using
 a polynomial trend (similar to spatial statistics where we model the trend first and allow residuals to
 be correlated as needed).

  5. Multiple discrepancies: Identifying multiple discrepancies simultaneously can be achieved when a)
 is the case, and at least one of b) and c) holds:

 (a) Cut Bayes can be used for physics parameters to mitigate identifiability problems between said
 parameters and discrepancies.
 (b) There is some strong prior information for at least one of the discrepancies. Such prior information
 could include covariance parameters (scale and smoothness) or frequency content (e.g., high-
 frequency or low-frequency).
 (c) There are individual data sets that can be used to constrain individual discrepancies.

 Even if neither (b) nor (c) hold, however, samples of the joint posterior distribution can be reported
 between multiple discrepancies that shows constraining of uncertainties, meaning the probability distri-
 bution for discrepancy is narrowed, or more concentrated, compared to the prior. Cut Bayes for physics
 parameters will still prevent confounding between physics parameters and multiple discrepancies.

1.1 Review of discrepancy
Before we proceed to multi-physics models with discrepancy functions, we review the classic Kennedy and
O’Hagan model (Kennedy and O’Hagan, 2001), which is presented as such:

                                        y(x) = η(x; θ) + δ(x) + ϵ,

where y(x) is the observed data value for variable(s) x (e.g., time, pressure, temperature, etc.), η(x; θ) is
the output of a computer simulator at physics parameter(s) θ, and ϵ is a random error term, the elements
of which are often assumed to be independently and identically distributed (IID) Gaussian. If discrepancy
is not included, then the inference of θ could be wrong to make up for, or to compensate for, errors in the
simulator, and predictions, whether interpolations or extrapolations, could be suspect. Additionally, as more
data are collected, asymptotics will lead to an inference for θ that is more and more precise but at a wrong
value. We note that in a pure prediction setting where knowing θ is not of intrinsic value, one may not be
interested in an accurate inference of θ. However, even in that case, when model error is manifest in the
simulator η(.; .) or it lacks enough flexibility, no value of θ will capture the true physical process of interest
– this lends additional motivation for a discrepancy term.
 Simultaneously, a well established problem using the Kennedy-O’Hagan model is that inferring δ(x) and
θ jointly leads to non-identifiability, which is similar to the problem of spatial confounding between random
and fixed effects encountered in spatial statistics; for most values of θ, there is a corresponding δ(x) that
would be learned with a flexible enough prior on δ – therefore a very wide range of θ could potentially be
inferred. In this report, we will formalize and implement this modularization idea for the discrepancy term
itself, with the cut Bayes distribution defined in Plummer (2015). As such, we next formalize the cut Bayes
distribution for discrepancy and contrast it with a usual posterior distribution.

2 Formalizing the cut Bayes distribution and sampling for dis-
 crepancy
Regarding notation – we are using the p(.) notation to indicate probability distributions as in, for example,
Gelman et al. (2013). While δ is technically a function, one can think of it as parametrized (e.g., a neural
network, polynomial regression, basis representation, or a high-dimensional multivariate normal vector when
considering a GP at a finite number of locations).
    The cut Bayes distribution for (δ, θ) is defined as p(δ|y, θ)p(θ|y ext ), where y ext represents data from an
external or auxiliary experiment. In contrast, the usual posterior is p(δ|y, θ)p(θ|y, y ext ); the distinction is
that the distribution over θ conditions on all of the data in full Bayes, but in cut Bayes the feedback from
data y to θ is cut. This is illustrated in Figure 1.
 The product form of the cut Bayes distribution motivates a straightforward sampling routine – sampling
from the cut Bayes distribution essentially proceeds as follows: for each effectively independent sample of θ
from a fixed distribution (e.g., previously derived posterior p(θ|y ext )), sample from p(δ|y, θ), and retain the
joint samples of (δ, θ) or just δ to marginalize out θ. This is presented in Algorithm 1 in algorithmic notation,
though it is possible to use pre-sampled values of θ instead of sampling each θ right before generating each
discrepancy sample.

   The literature has noted that in general this can be a computationally difficult problem when p(δ|y, θ)
is analytically intractable and has a normalizing constant that changes depending on θ, which has led to a
variety of solutions, such as multiple Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) chains, sequential Monte Carlo
(SMC), and emulation or variational approximation of the conditional posterior distribution. In the sections
that follow, we avoid such a problem because p(δ|y, θ) is easy to sample from (for instance, because it is
a known multivariate normal distribution as in the case of GPs, or it has a closed form as in the case of
polynomial regression).

2.1 Aside: What is new about our suggestions?
As previously mentioned, the definition of a cut Bayes distribution was first mathematically specified, without
any regard to model discrepancy, in Plummer (2015). However, this idea is apparent in Liu et al. (2009),
particularly in Eq. 2.6 and Eq. 2.7; the second of those two equations mixes over p(σ 2 |s2 ) instead of
p(σ 2 |ȳ, s2 ), disallowing information from ȳ, which is collected under bias, to inform the distribution of σ 2 .
Thus, p(σ 2 |s2 ) plays the role of the cut distribution for σ 2 when inferring a cut posterior.
 Liu et al. (2009) also discusses modularization of a discrepancy function but is executed in a different
way and with different objectives than what follows. In essence, their proposal is to determine a modular-
ized posterior distribution for discrepancy GP covariance parameters and to use a point estimate of these
parameters in their analysis; they highlight better mixing of MCMC chains.
 In contrast, we suggest sampling from an entire cut distribution for the discrepancy itself (not solely
its covariance parameters). This is justified by the observation that fixing covariance parameters at plug-
in estimates does not eliminate conflation between δ(.) and θ! Thus, we emphasize that the appropriate
procedure is to not let information from experimental data flow to θ but only for the purpose of updating
δ(.); i.e., we are using a cut Bayesian approach for the inference of the entire discrepancy function. Further,

we do not assume a GP should always be used for discrepancy, and in the following illustrative example will
highlight both of these distinctions.

As before, we have an externally provided cut distribution available for θ that is not updated in subse-

the cerium data next described, which negates a clear separation between discrepancy data and calibration

5 Multiple discrepancies
The previous examples demonstrate that a cut Bayes approach can be used to infer a discrepancy effectively.
It obviates the need to simultaneously (i.e., jointly) infer both physics parameters and a discrepancy function
or bias, which is helpful because typically these trade off. We now address the issue of jointly inferring

multiple discrepancies in an integrated experiment.
 A general setup for this problem is as follows:

                            yi = g([f1 (xi , θ) + δ1 (xi )], ..., [fN (xi , θ) + δN (xi )]) + ϵi

for N submodels that are inputs into a general function g, and each submodel can be assumed to be discrepant
and take as input elements of θ. One might consider trying to infer discrepancies for the individual submodels
with (xi , yi ) data and given samples of θ. We will show that individual discrepancies can generally only be
recovered in this setting if there is strong prior information for at least one of the discrepancies. Nonetheless,
the usual cut Bayesian machinery described thus far can always be used to infer all discrepancies jointly,
despite that there will be correlations between individual discrepancies. The identifiability issue precludes
using inferences of each individual δ(.) to determine which specific submodels are discrepant yet presents
less of an obstacle if prediction of y is the major aim.

5.1 An instructive example of inferring multiple discrepancies
To understand some salient issues within this scenario we will assume that g just adds the inputs together
and that only 2 submodels are considered for the time being, so N = 2 – the essential features of this setting
are still relevant when g(.) is nonlinear and N > 2. For this scenario, the statistical model is:

                                 yi = f1 (xi , θ) + δ1 (xi ) + f2 (xi , θ) + δ2 (xi ) + ϵi .

Due to the additive nature of δ1 (xi ) + δ2 (xi ), it is apparent that the sum can be recovered but not the
individual pieces unless at least one of the constituent pieces can be constrained with prior information –
such prior information can come in the form of distributions over covariance parameters (e.g., scale and
smoothness) or basis functions.
 Consider the following concrete example that will illustrate this. Assume

                                            f1 (θ, x) = sin(θ1 x)cos(θ2 x2 )

and
                                            f2 (θ, x) = sin(θ3 x)tan(θ4 x2 ).

For discrepancy, assume that δ1 (x) = x and δ2 (x) = x2 , and assume 0-mean normal error for ϵi that is IID
with known variance σ 2 . Assume all elements of θ := (θ1 , θ2 , θ3 , θ4 ) are independent and have known normal
cut distributions (with means 1, 2, 3, 4, respectively, and common standard deviation .01), and δ1 and δ2
have 0 mean normal priors with standard deviation 1. We implement a Gibbs sampler (Robert and Casella,
2004) to infer the joint of δ1 (xtest ) and δ2 (xtest ) for xtest . We will see that inferring discrepancies for just a
single value of xtest (1.5 in this example) will highlight correlation between δ1 and δ2 .
 As shown in Figure 10, the total discrepancy is picked up well (represented by the vertical red line)
but the individual marginals are not (green triangle); we cannot attribute the magnitude of discrepancy
to individual submodels (i.e., f1 and f2 ). On the other hand, if we repeat the exercise except with strong
prior information for δ1 (xtest ) in the form of a normal distribution centered at the correct discrepancy value
with small variance (precision 100), we get the following behavior in Figure 11, which shows that individual
discrepancies are picked up in addition to the sum.

5.2 Using integrated and individual level experiments in combination
Another instance where the joint inference of multiple discrepancies can be improved is when individual-
level experiments are available for each physics submodel in addition to integrated experimental data. We
demonstrate this with a simulation study involving GP discrepancies. For the time being, condition on θ so
that we may isolate the issue with discrepancies.
 Assume that the observation model for the sum (depicted in purple vertical lines below) is

                                         ysum,i = δ1 (xi ) + δ2 (zi ) + ϵi ,

where δ1 (.) and δ2 (.) have squared-exponential Gaussian process parameters that are estimated via MLE.
Note that we use two distinct variables x and z to account for the fact that with multiple discrepancies, the
independent variables (e.g., temperature, pressure, etc.) need not be the same for distinct submodels. The
blue and red dots show where there are observations for each individual discrepancy, such as blue dots

                                              yind,j = δ1 (xj ) + ϵj ,

and red dots
                                              yind,k = δ2 (zk ) + ϵk .

We illustrate this in Figures 12 and 13 below. Extrapolations for each individual discrepancy are not far
from the truth when integrated data (vertical purple lines) are close to each submodel experiment (blue and
red dots); on the other hand, extrapolations for each individual discrepancy degrade when integrated data
(vertical purple lines) are further from each submodel experiment (blue and red dots). In both scenarios, the
sum δ1 + δ2 is recovered at the integrated experiment locations irrespective of the locations of the individual
ones.

6 Future directions
The work here suggests several future directions that are delineated below.

 1. Apply the methods developed and illustrated here to more complex multi-physics examples, especially
 with multiple discrepancies and nonlinear functions that combine several discrepant submodels.

 2. Investigate in more depth alternatives to Gaussian process discrepancies, including AI/ML neural
 network models, to see if extrapolation of discrepancy can be improved.

 3. In general, discrepancies as in the Kennedy O’Hagan model can be attributed to data collection biases
 in addition to model inadequacy – investigate frameworks that attempt to disentangle these two sources
 of error.

 4. Consider implementation specifics such as: how feasible will it be to use discrepancy in conjunction
 with actual physics codes and how can we use discrepancy effectively even if it is not embedded within
 physics codes?
