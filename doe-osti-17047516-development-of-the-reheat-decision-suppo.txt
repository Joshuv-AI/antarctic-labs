  Executive Summary
  This report presents a summary of the conceptual development of the National Laboratory of the
  Rockies (NLR) ReHeat tool, a proof-of-concept decision support tool (DST) to assist decision-
  makers in identifying and evaluating the best options to supply industrial process heat (IPH)
  using hybrid systems while considering their goals and respecting site constraints. The tool
  leverages a Linear Programming (LP) and Mixed-Integer Linear Programming (MILP)
  framework for performing Techno-Economic Analysis (TEA), which facilitates the solution of
  the design problem, and for which established solvers exist. Based on recent trends from
  literature and industry, focus was given towards technology hybridization, either by increasing
  overall system efficiency or by supplementing temperature deltas. Considered technologies
  include solar (flat plate collectors (FPCs) and parabolic trough collectors (PTCs)), electricity
  (high temperature heat pumps (HTHPs) and electric resistive boiler (ERB)), and fuel (natural gas
  boilers (NGBs)). An important part of the framework is a built-in catalog of technologies, unit
  prices, efficiencies, and characteristics, which are customizable to ensure user-specific results.
  The modeling framework developed for this study has been released as open-source software
  (https://github.com/NatLabRockies/reheat).

  As part of this project, targeted industry outreach was conducted to gather insights from
  professionals across key sectors on the evaluation and adoption of alternative process heating
  technologies. The interviews focused on decision-making criteria such as equipment reliability,
  fuel price volatility, and current evaluation tools, with the findings informing the development of
  a DST designed to address real-world challenges, support cost-effective solutions, and enhance
  technology adoption. The interviews revealed that industry professionals prioritize a combination
  of financial and technical metrics when evaluating alternative process heating technologies, with
  capital and operating costs, equipment reliability, and capacity matching emerging as the most
  critical factors. Existing evaluation approaches rely primarily on internal spreadsheets and
  vendor-provided analyses, with professionals expressing a desire for a more robust, shared tool
  that consolidates these considerations. Key desired outputs from the DST include payback
  period, return on investment, energy performance metrics and sensitivity analyses to capture the
  effect of fuel price volatility. These findings were incorporated into the ReHeat tool, ensuring
  that the tool can address the gaps and limitations that industry professionals currently encounter.

  At a high level, the ReHeat DST computes the system design – rated or maximum heat
  generation of each technology, in kWth (or kWhth for storage) – that optimizes an objective,
  typically Life Cycle Cost (LCC) which may include resiliency metrics), and respects site
  constraints such as available area. Sensitivity analysis, a core requirement identified from the
  industry outreach, is also presented as a key feature of the DST. We provide example sensitivity
  analysis results with respect to fuel prices (operating costs), and unit capital cost of solar
  generation (capital costs), and highlight how technology sizing varies across the spectrum. In this
  report, only the core model of ReHeat is presented. Extensions to the model that perform tasks
  such as identifying the optimal process configuration or topology and using different heat
  sources for the heat pump are currently being investigated and will be reported separately.

  1 Introduction
  1.1 Motivation
  A significant portion of industrial process heat (IPH) in the United States requires temperatures
  below 150 °C (Akar et al. 2024) and could thus be adequately supplied by a variety of heat
  sources, such as fuel/combustion, electricity, solar, or a mix thereof. Although these technologies
  have been successfully deployed in the past, each source requires a favorable techno-economic
  scenario, as well as operational strategies that yield the lowest cost solution.

  The ratio between fuel and electricity prices largely drives the economics of electrified heat
  technologies, favorable only in a few regions in the United States (e.g., the Pacific Northwest,
  Upper Midwest, and New England regions). Capital cost is another major driver, since
  operational costs and maintenance cost are relatively low once the system is installed, and capital
  costs can fluctuate significantly depending on location and market availability. Incentive
  structures, such as federal tax credits, grants, and utility rebates, can significantly offset upfront
  capital costs and therefore play an important role in determining the financial viability of solar
  heating investments in each of these regions.

  On the technical side, the efficiency of each equipment type depends on several factors. For
  instance, the Coefficient of Performance (COP) of high temperature heat pumps (HTHPs)
  depends on the temperature lift—the temperature difference between heat source and sink—and
  varies quite significantly between 2 and 5, which can be a decisive factor in their economic
  competitiveness. The cost of procuring its heat source must also be considered. Although
  utilizing industrial/process waste heat (e.g. exhaust gases from combustion processes, or waste
  heat from cooling systems) is recommended when available, alternatives exist, including using
  solar thermal collectors to pre-heat the source fluid to improve heat pump efficiency. For solar
  technologies, the ambient thermal losses at the desired outlet temperature dictate how much heat
  can be extracted throughout the equipment and may impact their cost effectiveness.

  Given the myriad ways in which different IPH technologies can interact with and complement
  each other, a framework for hybridization was devised in this project as the decision support tool
  (DST) ReHeat.

  1.2 Context and Contributions
  Although several industrial alternative heat technologies (e.g., solar thermal, heat pumps, green
  hydrogen, electrically driven heat) have been developed and/or are in development, a significant
  portion of them currently has low adoption, especially in the United States. While these
  technologies hold considerable promise to improve energy efficiency and reduce operating costs,
  decision-makers face the challenge of navigating a rapidly evolving landscape with many
  competing options and site-specific constraints. ReHeat addresses this gap by providing a cross-
  technology optimization tool that helps private sector decision-makers identify, evaluate, and
  select the most suitable heating solution for their facility. By accounting for the decision-maker's
  financial goals, alongside a facility's physical and regulatory constraints, ReHeat determines the
  optimal combination of hybrid heating technologies that minimize Life Cycle Cost (LCC).

  The ReHeat DST is intended to serve as a screening and analytic tool rather than a basis for final
  investment or procurement decisions. The tool is designed to help users rapidly evaluate and
  compare a range of hybrid heating technology combinations, identify the most promising
  candidates for a given facility, and develop an initial understanding of the financial trade-offs
  involved. Given the complexity of real-world industrial heating systems, a full feasibility
  assessment would require detailed site-specific engineering studies, vendor consultations, and
  financial modeling that falls outside the scope of this tool. As such, DST is best understood as an
  early-stage decision aid that narrows the solution space and guides more targeted follow-on
  analysis.

  This project builds on previous efforts on hybrid IPH models (Akar et al. 2024; 2022) and
  industrial HTHPs (Cox et al. 2023; Cox et al. 2022a) – prior work indicates that the lowest cost
  option is typically a mix between fuel and alternative technologies. Individual technology
  models leverage previously developed models and tools, e.g., solar collector models from the
  System Advisor Model (SAM) (NLR 2025) and HTHP models from Cox et al. 2023. The main
  outcome of this project is a proof-of-concept DST that identifies cost-effective hybrid IPH
  systems and sizes each constituent technology by minimizing life cycle costs and respecting
  space and demand constraints.

  The remainder of this document presents the mathematical formulation of the optimization-based
  techno-economic analysis (TEA) problem used as the foundation for the ReHeat model, results
  from a case study to demonstrate the model’s capabilities, and insights from the industry
  outreach. The modeling framework developed for this study has been released as open-source
  software (https://github.com/NatLabRockies/reheat).

  2 Methodology
  2.1 Overview
  A diagram of the main technology classes considered in this model framework is presented in
  Figure 1. Three primary energy categories are considered—solar, electricity, and fuel
  combustion—as well as thermal storage, all of which contribute to meet a facility's process load
  or demand. The solar category includes flat plate collectors (FPCs), parabolic trough collectors
  (PTCs), and linear Fresnel collectors (LFCs), which represent a range of solar thermal
  technologies suited to different temperature ranges and site conditions. The solar loads must be
  pre-generated for a 1-kW rated system using another tool, such as NLR’s SAM. Solar
  technologies use pre-generated profiles that depend on external factors and scales up and down
  from that baseline profile. The remaining equipment can generate heat and be sized
  independently from external factors.

  Electricity-based technologies include industrial heat pumps (IHPs) and electric resistance
  boilers (ERBs), while the fuel combustion category includes natural gas boilers (NGBs) and
  biofuel boilers. Thermal energy storage (TES) is also included as a dispatchable resource that
  can store and release heat. The general heat balance assumption allows the representation of both
  active TES (e.g., hot water or thermal oil tanks) and passive TES (stationary medium, e.g.,
  concrete) technologies, with some limitations—for instance, phase-change TES may need further
  simplifying assumptions. At each timestep, the heat outputs from all active technologies are
  summed and dispatched to satisfy the facility's process heat load, with the tool optimizing the
  selection, sizing, and operation of these technologies to minimize LCC. While not all
  technologies listed were implemented in the first version of the model, the framework is
  designed to accommodate their inclusion in future iterations.

  The main workflow for using the model is presented in Figure 2, operating through three
  sequential stages: user inputs, pre-calculations, and optimization-based TEA results. In the first
  stage, the user provides facility- and site-specific information, including location and weather
  data (ambient temperature and solar radiation), hourly process heating load profiles and required
  temperature levels, utility costs for electricity and fuel, and available area for solar collector
  installation on rooftops or ground-mounted fields. These inputs feed into a pre-calculation stage,
  where the equipment configuration is fixed based on the specified process temperature
  requirements, and solar collector performance models are executed using the SAM. In the first
  version of the model, the SAM collector profiles must also be provided as inputs by the user. The
  outputs of this pre-calculation stage then serve as inputs to the optimization model, which
  determines the optimal technology sizing, dispatch profile, and associated financial metrics,
  including capital, operating, and life cycle costs, as well as sensitivity analysis results to
  characterize how the solution responds to changes in key input parameters.

  2.4 Industry Needs Assessment for Designing the Tool
  Industry outreach was conducted in parallel with tool development and directly informed the
  prioritization of key features. To support this effort, interviews were conducted with companies
  from major industrial sectors, including food and beverages, processed foods, consumer goods,
  household and personal care products, building and construction materials, and industrial
  materials. Stakeholders emphasized the need for financial outputs, including payback period,
  cost of heat, and investment costs, alongside energy performance metrics such as heat output,
  temperature ranges, and potential energy savings. Participants also highlighted the value of
  technology ranking, sensitivity analyses, and the ability to compare technology options under
  different operating and economic conditions. Further details on the industry consultation process
  and stakeholder feedback can be found in Chapter 4: Industry Outreach.

  Feedback underscored the importance of a shared decision-support platform that could provide
  consistent and comparable assessments across facilities. As a result, the tool was designed to
  dynamically rank technology options based on user inputs and site-specific constraints. Industry
  participants also expressed interest in features such as land and rooftop availability assessments,
  mapping capabilities, technology reference projects, and detailed operational information
  including maintenance requirements, equipment footprint, and lifecycle considerations. Several
  of these recommendations were incorporated into the tool design; however, others could not be
  fully implemented within the project timeline and available resources. Specifically, advanced
  mapping functionality, automated land availability assessments, integration of extensive
  technology reference databases, and more comprehensive operational and maintenance modeling
  were identified as valuable future enhancements. These capabilities would further improve the
  tool's ability to support site screening, technology evaluation, and long-term project planning.

  electricity-based technology, delivering heat by upgrading low-grade heat to the required process
  temperature. A NGB serves as fuel combustion technology, providing heat and acting as a
  reliable backup and dispatchable heat source. Finally, a TES system is also considered capable of

  ReHeat framework, selected to demonstrate the tool's optimization capabilities across a range of
  complementary heating technologies in a realistic case study setting. In addition to the default
  design results, sensitivity analysis results are also presented to illustrate how the tool can be used
  to inform the design under different scenarios.

  4 Industry Outreach
  As part of the ReHeat project, industry outreach was conducted to identify key stakeholder needs
  and inform DST development. In this context, a certain number of companies that play a major
  role in food and beverages, processed foods, consumer goods, household and personal care,
  building construction and industrial materials industries, have been interviewed. The number of
  companies, their names, and other sensitive information are kept anonymous due to
  confidentiality. More broadly, the purpose of these interviews was to gather comprehensive
  insights from industry professionals regarding the evaluation and adoption of alternative process
  heating technologies that aim to increase cost-effectiveness.

  The interviews explored key decision-making factors, such as the metrics used to evaluate new
  equipment purchases, with a particular focus on the reliability of new equipment and the impacts
  of fuel price volatility. We seek to understand how professionals assess the long-term reliability
  and performance of alternative heating technologies, as well as how fluctuating fuel prices
  influence purchasing decisions and technology adoption. In addition, the discussions delved into
  the types of tools and metrics currently used to evaluate these technologies, including any gaps
  or limitations professionals encounter with existing evaluation methods. This information will
  help identify the most relevant features needed in DST, such as payback period, equipment
  sizing, system design, and other critical outputs. By considering these factors, the goal is to
  ensure the tool reflects real-world challenges, supports robust decision-making, and addresses
  the uncertainties posed by fuel price fluctuations and the need for reliable, cost-effective
  solutions. Insights gathered will also guide the tool’s ability to optimize cost and enhance the
  adoption of heating technologies across various industries.

  4.1 Key Metrics for Evaluating New Equipment Purchases and
 Alternative Process Heating Technologies
  When evaluating new equipment purchases and alternative process heating technologies, the
  primary metrics focus on technological maturity and testing. Experts expressed the importance of
  thoroughly understanding and validating new equipment, especially since the heat use involved
  is integrated into processes rather than just basic space heating. Key considerations include
  whether the equipment has undergone lab testing or has been demonstrated at other sites, its
  proven reliability and compatibility with existing processes, materials, and end products, as well
  as the credibility and track record of the vendor supplying the technology.

  Both CAPEX and OPEX are identified as critical financial metrics and evaluation factors.
  Operational expenses, including power consumption and electricity demand, can significantly
  increase when switching to electrified equipment, especially depending on the location. Price of
  the equipment is one of the top three factors, alongside reliability and capacity matching.
  Another key consideration is the ease of replacing an existing heating system with a renewable
  thermal energy system. If the new technology requires additional infrastructure that the
  organization lacks, this directly impacts the cost and feasibility.

  Most organizations typically assess the following metrics:

 • Transitioning to electrified heating, such as IHP and ERB, may require significant
 upgrades in power capacity and infrastructure. Both the feasibility and cost of securing

 • The ease of integrating new technologies with existing systems, along with any additional

  4.1.1 Evaluation and adoption of an alternative process for heating technologies
  When assessing new equipment or alternative heating technologies, industry professionals
  evaluate a range of technical, economic, and operational criteria. Thermal efficiency is a primary
  consideration, with new technologies benchmarked against existing systems such as natural gas-
  powered ovens and kettles in terms of overall energy consumption and heat recovery
  effectiveness. Technologies that offer comparable or improved heat recovery capabilities are
  generally prioritized. Scalability is another important criterion, with organizations favoring
  solutions that can be replicated or adapted across multiple facilities. Finally, equipment longevity
  and ease of maintenance are consistently cited as priorities, with a preference for systems that are
  durable and cost-effective to operate over their life-cycle. In some organizations, a dedicated
  internal engineering team is responsible for leading the evaluation and procurement of new
  equipment, including alternative energy technologies.

  One of the experts has expressed the importance of alternative energy options to replace fossil
  fuels but has encountered significant challenges. Electrification, for instance, has proven
  impractical due to the high energy demands of their equipment and the limitations of the local
  power grid. As an example, converting one of their largest plants from natural gas to electric
  steam generation would require 65 MW of power, while the local grid can only provide about 30
  MW. Moreover, electric equipment tends to be more expensive, both in terms of upfront capital
  investment and ongoing operating costs, and the necessary infrastructure upgrades can be
  prohibitively expensive. Hydrogen was also evaluated as a potential fuel alternative; however,
  the lack of a reliable, affordable supply in the required quantities presented a significant barrier.
  As a result, neither electrification nor hydrogen was considered a viable near-term solution due
  to both economic and logistical constraints.

  4.1.2 Experience with using DSTs in evaluation of alternative process heating
 technologies
  Some experts use internal tools, such as spreadsheet-based trackers, to evaluate various process
  heating technologies. These tools typically incorporate financial metrics (CAPEX, OPEX),
  technological factors (maturity, reliability) and ease of implementation. However, such tools
  have not been heavily used yet, as only a limited number of such assessments have been
  conducted. Finance teams have developed more sophisticated internal tools, such as internal rate
  of return (IRR) calculators. It was also noted that publicly available energy pricing models have
  not yet been fully integrated into evaluation processes but are being considered for future
  assessments. Engineering teams also employ an internal IRR/payback period calculator as part of
  their decision-making process.

  Companies have experience working with both in-house and vendor tools to evaluate alternative
  process heating technologies. Typically, they rely on spreadsheets to input various data points,
  such as energy consumption (kWh or MMBtu) and cost details, to perform their analysis. When
  a more detailed analysis is required, they work closely with manufacturers who perform the

  4.1.4 Expected outcomes from DST
  The key desired outputs from the DST include the payback period, which indicates the time
  required to recover the investment, as well as the return on investment (ROI) and overall
  financial returns. In addition, the tool is requested to provide energy performance outputs, such
  as MMBtu heat output, temperature ranges, and potential energy savings. It is also encouraged to
  deliver dynamic technology recommendations based on scenario-driven sensitivity analyses.
  Finally, visual mapping and land availability assessments would be helpful to support informed
  decision-making.

  All participants expressed interest in a shared tool that could be used across organizations to
  avoid duplicating efforts. On of the biggest outcome of the DST would be integrate decision-
  making processes, allowing for consistent and comparable assessments across different sites. The
  tool is expected to:

 • Recommend dynamic ranking of technology combinations and visualize the top three
 best-fit technologies based on input parameters.
 • Have a sensitivity analysis to show how the ranking changes with different variables
 (e.g., fluctuating fuel prices or energy costs).
 • Dynamically adjust technology recommendations based on land constraints. For example,
 if only rooftop space is available, it may exclude largescale solar thermal technologies.
 Integrating a mapping (Google Maps or address-based visualization) feature to allow
 users to outline their facility and automatically calculate the available rooftop or land
 area.
  The tool is expected to provide comprehensive insights into key financial metrics, such as the
  payback period for investments and the cost of heat, including comparisons to the MMBtu
  equivalent of natural gas. It would support the evaluation of cost reductions and the long-term
  financial viability of various heating technologies. It would also offer detailed information on
  reliability, efficiency, and other technical characteristics of the equipment under consideration,
  ensuring that solutions meet operational requirements. Projections of expected operations and
  maintenance costs, equipment lifecycle, ease of maintenance, and overall durability would
  further inform decision-making. The tool would assess equipment footprint and sizing to
  determine spatial compatibility and whether the solution can be phased in gradually.

  The tool’s maturity is critical, and it would be strengthened by the inclusion of reference points
  and installations to validate technologies through real-world examples. This ensures that the
  technologies being evaluated have a proven track record and are not based on untested or first-
  of-their-kind systems. While ROI is acknowledged as important, the company prefers the tool to
  focus on delivering accurate cost estimates rather than performing ROI calculations. This is
  because the company relies on its own internal models for ROI assessments, viewing industry-
  provided analyses as generally unreliable. Overall, the tool is expected to enable confident, data-
  driven decisions by combining financial, technical, and operational insights.

  5 Conclusions and Future Research
  This report described the conceptual development of the DST ReHeat, which addresses a critical
  gap in the industrial process heating community, namely, the lack of accessible models for
  conducting TEA of hybrid heating systems and supporting early-stage decision-making and
  technology screening. By combining a rigorous Linear Programming (LP)/Mixed-Integer Linear
  Programming (MILP)-based TEA framework with an industry-informed set of inputs, outputs,
  and sensitivity analysis capabilities, the proof-of-concept tool demonstrates that it is possible to
  systematically evaluate hybrid heating system configurations while considering real-world
  constraints and priorities.

  It is important to note that the current version of ReHeat represents a proof-of-concept, and
  several extensions and refinements are planned for future development. These include the
  identification of optimal process topologies, the incorporation of additional heat sources for
  high-temperature heat pumps, and broader technology coverage beyond the current catalog. As
  the tool matures, continued engagement with industry stakeholders will be essential to validate
  outputs against real-world project data and to ensure that the tool remains aligned with evolving
  industry needs. Ultimately, ReHeat is intended to serve as a trusted, open, and extensible
  platform that lowers the barrier to evaluating and adopting hybrid process heating technologies
  across the industrial sector.

  A more comprehensive list of topics for future research and development include:

 • Expanding the DST by implementing high-priority stakeholder-requested capabilities that
 were deferred due to project timeline and resource constraints, including enhanced
 geospatial analysis, expanded technology databases, automated site assessments, and
 advanced operations and maintenance modeling.
 • Expanding the generation model database, such as geothermal, and streamlining the
 production of heat generation profiles, for instance, from the SAM tool (particularly
 important for solar collectors and heat pumps).
 • Assessing efficiency gains from promoting heat integration between different equipment
 in the system, in particular employing different process streams as heat sources to
 increase heat pump efficiency.
 • Evaluating the MILP solution with higher-fidelity simulation models, capable of
 representing energy balances, particularly temperature and flow rate effects.
 • Providing recommendations of process topology (structural configuration of the heating
 system, including sequencing and interconnection points) based on heuristics or solving a
 process synthesis problem.
 • Investigating the sensitivity of the objective function around the obtained design solution,
 with the goal of estimating solution multiplicity and flexibility.
 • Evaluating the model using different industrial demand profiles and geographical
 locations.
