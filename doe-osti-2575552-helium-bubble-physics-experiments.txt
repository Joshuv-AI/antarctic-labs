 Abstract
 New results from MST-16 indicate that as plutonium ages at close
to ambient temperature and helium ingrows, the number density of
bubbles increases only slowly, if at all, and their size distribution shifts
towards larger diameters. Helium-containing aged plutonium foams
when annealed at high temperatures. We attribute these phenomena
to a diffusivity large enough that, after a few years of aging, new
decay helium diffuses to extant bubbles rather than nucleating new
bubbles, and that during annealing larger bubbles grow at the expense
of smaller ones (Ostwald ripening). Bulk swelling results from the fact
that the Laplace pressure and therefore the helium density are less in
larger bubbles. We develop a simple theory and propose to study
these phenomena in aluminum alloyed with 10 B in which successive
exposure to reactor thermal neutrons produces a stepwise increase in
helium content.

1 Introduction
Helium bubbles occur in different materials, by radioactive decay in α-unstable
materials like plutonium, the subject of a great deal of the literature on this
subject, by neutron irradiation in materials (such as aluminum) doped with
10
 B, and in any material after helium ion implantation. As a result of fission,
reactor fuels contain krypton and xenon bubbles whose physics resembles
that of helium bubbles, and other reactor materials contain helium bubbles
as a result of (n,α) reactions.
 Despite the wide range of possible hosts, the physics of noble gas and
helium bubbles is expected to have much in common across hosts, particularly
if their crystal structures have the same symmetry. Aluminum is a convenient
benign host for experimental work. It is available with a wide range of alloys
and strengths, from very soft (nearly pure, used in kitchen foil, and in a few
experiments extremely pure) to much stronger alloys used in aircraft and
other structures. At ambient pressure, pure, or nearly pure, aluminum is fcc
up to the melting point of 660 ◦ C. Here we discuss experiments on helium-
implanted aluminum that may lead to a better understanding of the physics
of helium bubbles in metals.

2 Extant Data
There is an extensive literature on the behavior of helium bubbles in aged
δ-plutonium and on the properties of the material itself. This literature
generally refers to an alloy stabilized with about 3 atomic-% gallium (see
[1] for a phase diagram). It may serve as a guide to properties observable in
other fcc metals (including aluminum), and experiments on helium-implanted
aluminum may inform us about plutonium.
 Salient data include:
 1. The number and size distributions of helium bubbles in δ-Pu at ages of
 2, 16 and 43 years have recently been measured with Scanning Trans-
 mission Electron Microscopy (STEM) [2]. In contrast to earlier, widely
 cited, work [3] with more conventional TEM, these authors found he-
 lium bubbles even in their youngest (2 year old) sample. Their total
 number density was about 2 × 1016 cm−3 , five to ten times less than es-
 timated by [3], and increased only about 35% (possibly not significant
 at all because of poor statistics) between 2 and 43 years [2].

 In the youngest material the most common bubble diameters were

 ter a few years, new bubbles did not nucleate, and additional helium
 (aside from an unknown interstitial or vacancy fraction) made its way

 2. Aging δ-plutonium expands only very slowly at ambient temperature
 as helium accumulates [4], perhaps because of its open structure, but

3.2 Where is the Helium?
What fraction of the helium contained in the material resides in bubbles
large enough (generally, diameters ≥ 0.7 nm) to be measured by transmission
electron microscopy, rather than interstitially or in vacancies? It is difficult
to quantify the amount of helium in bubbles because the bubble volume is
proportional to the third power of their (imprecisely measured) diameters. It
is also difficult to measure the helium density directly (positron annihilation
spectroscopy [5] and electron energy loss spectroscopy [11, 9] provide some
information), and calculating it from the pressure, depending on the bubble
diameter and uncertain surface energy, introduces additional uncertainties.
 Nadykto [9], assuming helium in plutonium is confined by surface energy
and using a non-ideal equation of state for helium at the high pressures that
implies for nm-sized bubbles, concluded that in 42 year old plutonium (with
bubble parameters from [3] and annealing data from [7] only) 15% of the
helium is in bubbles before annealing, but that this rises to nearly 100%
after hours of annealing at 425 ◦ C (still in the δ phase region). However,

 6

the art of transmission electron microscopy has advanced since [3] obtained
the data on which he based his estimates and his value should be viewed
skeptically.

3.3 Outgassing
In experiments with helium-implanted copper in which the peak helium con-
centrations ranged from 0.81% to 1.68% of the host atom density, annealing
released about 81% of the helium [12] (without an uncertainty estimate, it
is unclear if this can be distinguished from 100%). This was interpreted by
Evans [13, 14] as the result of bubble diffusion and growth, but might instead
be the result of diffusion of atomic helium from smaller bubbles to larger bub-
bles with lower surface energy per atom. Evans simulated the evolution of
a bubble population with initial helium number densities from 1% to 3% of
the host atomic number density. These calculations, that assumed the ma-
trix swells as the bubble volume grows (because larger bubbles have lower
helium density), led to the conclusion that at a swelling of about 20% a sin-
gle or few “breakaway” bubbles come to dominate the bubble volume. This
critical volume fraction, in what is essentially a percolation problem among
the bubbles, was nearly independent of the initial helium density. The single
dominant bubble may have created a pore through which the helium escaped
when smaller bubbles merged with it.
 Related empirical results were found for annealing aged δ-plutonium alloy
heated in a differential scanning calorimeter [1]. When the rising tempera-
ture reached the liquidus (where  turns to a mixture of  and liquid because
alloys transition between phases over a finite range of temperature) around
660 ◦ C, bubbles grew rapidly by Ostwald ripening and the helium was ob-
served to outgas rapidly, as shown in Fig. 2. This might have occurred even
before because diffusivities in the  phase are large [15]2 , The largest swelling
observed by [5] in δ-plutonium, about 13% volumetric, was less than the
20% at which percolation is suggested [13]. This may explain why [5] did not
report outgassing; [1] did not report volume changes.

4 Theoretical Interpretations
4.1 Bubble Kinetic Theory
A widely adopted theory of the evolution of helium bubbles in metals is taken
from Chandrasekhar’s classic theory [16] of the evolution of diffusing colloids.
Application of this theory assumes that it is the bubbles that diffuse, not the
helium atoms between the bubbles, which is uncertain; in other words, that
Ostwald ripening does not occur. Elsewhere we argue that the recent data
[2] are best explained by diffusion of atoms between bubbles, for which there
may be no analogue for actual colloids.
 It also requires knowledge of the diffusion coefficients of the bubbles.
Bubbles diffuse because individual matrix atoms on their surfaces diffuse
individually, moving the center of the bubble, or because incomplete crystal
planes on their surfaces grow or shrink; Brownian motion of entire bubbles
appears to be insignificant. The theory assumes that when two colloids
(helium bubbles) touch, they merge. We later argue that Ostwald ripening
is essential, at least in heated (annealing) material, but here remind the
reader of the assumptions behind the bubble coalescence theory.
 These processes have been the objective of much theoretical work [17,
18, 19]. Measured values of the diffusion coefficients of helium bubbles in δ-
plutonium were several orders of magnitude less than predicted [21]. These
theories are inapplicable to that material, and possibly to others, but are
presented here because they may be applicable in some circumstances, and
the reader will encounter them in the literature.
 The bubble diffusion theory assumes:
 1. When two bubbles approach within their “spheres of influence”, usually
 taken to be in contact, they merge, conserving the total number of
 helium atoms.
 2. Helium bubbles are confined by surface energy.
 3. No gas diffuses through the matrix.
 The first assumption is true for colloids, water droplets in air, soap bub-
bles and similar problems in which the confined phase is essentially incom-
pressible; because the surface energy is proportional to the surface area,
combining two bubbles or droplets reduces their total surface area and free
energy (the surface area times the surface tension). Thermodynamics forces

difference.

4.2 Ostwald Ripening
Bubbles may evolve as a result of diffusion of helium atoms between them.
Because bubble diffusivity is empirically [21] small, bubble growth during
annealing may occur by Ostwald ripening, the diffusion of individual helium
atoms from smaller bubbles, whose higher pressure (as a result of their surface
energy) creates a higher density of helium in their surroundings, to larger
bubbles, surrounded by a lower density of helium. [6] suggested that Ostwald
ripening is the mechanism of bubble growth in annealed δ-plutonium. The
proposed experiments can probe this regime in aluminum if it is subsequently
annealed.

4.3 Swelling
The 13% volumetric swelling of 27 year old δ-plutonium after 16 hours of an-
nealing at 500 ◦ C [5] should be compared to the roughly 0.02% volume frac-
tion of helium bubbles in similarly aged but unannealed material [3] and the
0.11% swelling of 25 year-equivalent aged unannealed material [4]. Swelling

could not be reported at similar (and even higher) annealing temperatures in
studies [6, 7] using TEM imaging because that technique does not measure
the thickness of its very thin (O(50 nm)) samples. A computational model
of bubble evolution did find gross swelling [13]. Something different happens
during annealing at sufficiently high temperatures, still below the δ- phase
transition of the alloy [1].
 At elevated temperatures the diffusivity of helium is vastly greater; it
appears to follow an Arrhenius law with an activation energy of 1.24 eV
[5, 6]. The volume expansion observed by [5] far exceeds that which would be
implied by the bubble growth during annealing observed by [7]. Rapid gross
swelling may be the result of migration of helium into macroscopic bubbles
in which the helium density is small because the pressure 2γ/r imposed

contained by the static strength of the material, that we parametrize as S,

5 Proposed Experiments: Helium Implanta-
 tion
Questions to answer:

 1. How much does aluminum expand upon implanting helium, as a func-
 tion of the quantity implanted?

 2. How much does aluminum implanted with helium expand upon anneal-
 ing, as a function of time and temperature?

 3. How does implanted helium outgas during annealing, as a function of
 time and temperature?

 4. How does helium-implanted aluminum behave under shock loading?

Helium may be introduced to other substances by ion implantation, by ra-
dioactive decay, or by nuclear reactions such as 10 B(n,α)7 Li.

5.1 Ion Implantation
The presence of helium may make the host swell. In 25 year (accelerated)
old δ-plutonium the volumetric swelling is 0.11–0.12% [4], almost (likely
coincidentally) equal to the ratio 0.10% of helium to host atoms. Larger
“breakaway” bubbles have been observed in annealed plutonium [6] and in
annealed helium-implanted copper [12]. These have been predicted [13] to
produce great bulk swelling in annealed helium-implanted copper, resem-
bling the bulk swelling of annealed aged δ-plutonium observed by [5]. Bulk
δ-plutonium showed ≈ 10µm scale pores upon annealing [5], while the thin
layer of helium-implanted copper showed ≈ 100 nm pores [12].
 A remarkable result of [12] is that about 80% of the implanted helium
escaped, apparently through the pores (about 150 nm in diameter) that
appeared during annealing. This diameter is comparable to the depth of
implantation of 30 keV helium ions. Bubble growth was likely limited to
that depth because any larger bubble would have vented at the surface. It
was not observed if the very thin implanted layer swelled as the bubbles grew
in volume, as would be expected, but surface protrusions were observed [12],
possibly as a result of plastic flow when a growing bubble intersected the
surface.

5.2 Bulk Implantation
Ion beam implantation is not suitable for implanting helium into mm-cm
thicknesses of material, such as would be required for annealing experiments
to measure, at ambient pressure and higher temperature, swelling, the evolu-
tion of helium bubbles, and the diffusion and escape of helium. For example,
a stopping length of 1 mm in aluminum would require 52 MeV helium ions,
and 1 cm would require 190 MeV helium ions [24]. The 10 B(n,α)7 Li reaction,
with a thermal neutron cross-section σ10 B = 3840 b, is much more suitable 5 .
 After irradiation for a time t with a thermal neutron flux F the fraction
of 10 B that remains unreacted is

                                 1 − exp (−σ10 B F t).                               (29)

This should be  1 so that the 10 B attenuation “burns out” or “bleaches”,
permitting the full neutron flux to penetrate the sample. This maximizes
the helium implantation for a given density of 10 B, that should be minimized
to minimize its effect on the properties of the aluminum, and to produce a
uniform density of implanted helium.

important possible contaminant is cobalt, whose thermal neutron capture
cross-section to the isobaric state 60 Co∗ is 21 b; 11% of any cobalt present
is converted to this long-lived (t1/2 = 5.27 y) gamma-ray emitter after 24
days exposure to the flux of Eq. 30. This corresponds to an activation of 130
Ci/g. Fortunately, cobalt is rather rare in the environment, and likely to be
a very rare contaminant of aluminum. Reducing the activation to 1 µCi/g
would require that the total cobalt content be less than 8 ng/g; this could be
relaxed if the flux were reduced from the 2.6×1015 cm−2 s−1 we have assumed,
also reducing the heat that would need to transported from the coupon at
the beginning of irradiation.
 Optical communications fiber has transition metal content < 1 ng/g, and
only a very small fraction of that is cobalt, showing that cobalt contamination
can be reduced to acceptably low levels, at least in other materials. Any
cobalt contamination of aluminum likely varies from source to source, and
can readily be measured by exposing a sample (without any admixed 10 B)
to the HFIR neutron flux and counting the resulting 60 Co gamma-rays it
emits after exposure; these may be readily distinguished from the gamma-ray
background because each 60 Co decay produces two, essentially simultaneous,
gamma-rays. This would be an efficient means of identifying aluminum with
sufficiently low cobalt contamination that its radioactivity after irradiation
would not be hazardous, even if dispersed in a dynamic experiment.
 Other possible concerns are 54 Fe, 5.85% of natural iron and 58 Fe, 0.28% of
natural iron. The product of neutron capture by 54 Fe is 55 Fe (t1/2 = 2.73 y),
which is harmless despite its half-life because it decays by electron capture
to stable 55 Mn, without emitting either a gamma-ray or beta-particle. It
does emit a few non-penetrating K X-rays around 7 keV if the K-shell hole
created by electron capture is radiatively refilled, although Auger emission is
more frequent. The product of neutron capture by 58 Fe with a cross-section
1.3 b is 59 Fe that emits MeV gamma-rays, but the low abudance of 58 Fe and
compartively short t1/2 = 44.5 d of 59 Fe means that its activity decays to
insignificance on practical time scales of 1–2 years.
