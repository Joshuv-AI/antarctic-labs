Equilibrium Stability for a Continuous Time Scale with Discrete Uniform gaps
Douglas R. Anderson

Abstract
. We investigate the equilibrium (trivial solution) stability, also known as Lyapunov stability, of a certain first-order linear complex constant coefficient dynamic equation on the time scale ℙ α , β \mathbb{P}_{\alpha,\beta} , which has continuous intervals of length α > 0 \alpha>0 followed by discrete gaps of length β > 0 \beta>0 . In particular, we establish results in the case of this specific time scale, for coefficient values in the complex plane, including where the exponential function alternates in sign. In our analysis, we employ the Lambert W W function. For increasing gap size β \beta relative to α \alpha , we prove that the complex constant coefficient undergoes a bifurcation in its parameter space. We establish interesting results for both the delta dynamic equation and the nabla dynamic equation. Lastly, we connect these results to those related to Hyers–Ulam stability of the same nabla equations.

Introduction

Hilger
[

introduced time scales analysis to unify continuous and discrete analysis, with the further benefit of supplying a robust tool for studying hybrid continuous-discrete systems and systems with non-uniform step sizes, where a time scale is any closed subset of the real line
\mathbb{R}
. A natural question for a time-scales equation relates to its stability.
In a landmark paper, Pötzsche, Siegmund, and Wirth
[
9
]
gave a spectral characterization of exponential stability for linear time-invariant
systems on time scales. Using the first initials of these authors’ surnames, these stability regions have become known as PSW regions. The tool for such analysis that PSW utilized, however, can be difficult to use in practice. In a recent series of papers, Jackson and Davis
[
6
,
7
]
and Jackson, Davis, and Poulsen
[
8
]
have used an ergodic approach to characterizing the PSW region, which is related to the region of convergence for the time-scales Laplace transform integral.

In this paper, a specific time scale
\mathbb{T}
with fixed gap size that displays both continuous and discrete properties is studied and its PSW stability regions are explicitly determined. In particular, let
\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{P}_{\alpha,\beta}
for continuous interval length
\alpha>0
and discrete gap size
\beta>0
, namely

\mathbb{P}_{\alpha,\beta}=\bigcup_{k=0}^{\infty}[k(\alpha+\beta),k(\alpha+\beta)+\alpha],

and consider the differential operator defined by

x^{\Delta}(t)=\begin{cases}\frac{d}{dt}x(t)&\text{for}\;t\in[k(\alpha+\beta),k(\alpha+\beta)+\alpha)\\
\frac{x(t+\beta)-x(t)}{\beta}&\text{for}\;t=k(\alpha+\beta)+\alpha.\end{cases}

See Bohner and Peterson
[
2
, Examples 1.38–1.40]
for an introduction to this time scale, which can model a transmission signal or a data burst broadcast over a short time period, and then repeated, or a system or device that operates continuously for a fixed time, shuts off, and then runs again; in biology, this time scale may model an organism that lives a fixed unit of time, followed by hibernation or dormancy, and then is active again, and so on.

We will be investigating some stability questions for this time scale and this derivative operator, in the case of the trivial solution to a linear equation, defined below.

Definition 1.1

(Equilibrium Stability)
.

Consider the dynamic equation given by

x^{\Delta}(t)=\lambda x(t),\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\left\{-\frac{1}{\beta}\right\},\quad t\in\mathbb{P}_{\alpha,\beta}.

(1.1)

We say that the equilibrium (trivial) solution of (
1.1
) is stable on
\mathbb{P}_{\alpha,\beta}
if and only if given
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a
\delta>0
such that if
x
is a solution of (
1.1
) with
|x(0)|=|x_{0}|<\delta
, then for all
t\in\mathbb{P}_{\alpha,\beta}
we have
|x(t)|<\varepsilon
on
\mathbb{P}_{\alpha,\beta}
. Additionally, we say the equilibrium solution of (
1.1
) is asymptotically stable on
\mathbb{P}_{\alpha,\beta}
if and only if there exists
\delta>0
such that if

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}|x(t)|=0

In this work, we consider the time scale
\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{P}_{\alpha,\beta}

\sigma(t):=\begin{cases}t&\text{for}\;t\in[k(\alpha+\beta),\;k(\alpha+\beta)+\alpha),\\
t+\beta&\text{for}\;t=k(\alpha+\beta)+\alpha.\end{cases}

For

which can also be written as

e_{\lambda}(t,0)=\left[(1+\beta\lambda)e^{\alpha\lambda}\right]^{k}e^{\lambda j},\quad t=k(\alpha+\beta)+j,\quad j\in[0,\alpha].

(1.2)

Clearly, the exponential function in (
1.2
) is well defined for
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\left\{-\frac{1}{\beta}\right\}
. Notice that

x(t)=x_{0}e_{\lambda}(t,0),\quad t\in\mathbb{T},

(1.3)

is the general solution of (
1.1
), for the exponential function
e_{\lambda}
given in (
1.2
).

Throughout the paper, we will need to employ the Lambert
W
function, see Corless et al
[
3
]
, which we denote by

W_{z}(u)e^{W_{z}(u)}=u

e_{\lambda}(k(\alpha+\beta),0)=[(1+\beta\lambda)e^{\alpha\lambda}]^{k}.

We will assume throughout that
\lambda\neq-\frac{1}{\beta}

include when the base

4.
Nabla Equation and Hyers–Ulam Stability

The regions identified earlier in this work also arise when discussing another type of stability, that of Hyers–Ulam stability. Consequently, in this section we extend the analysis to the related but separate consideration of the Hyers–Ulam stability (HUS) of the nabla equation (
2.2
). Ulam
[
11
]
initiated this type of discussion, followed by Hyers
[
5
]
and Rassias
[
10
]
. Similar results for the delta equation (
1.1
) are found in
[
1
]
.

Definition 4.1

(Hyers–Ulam Stability)
.

We say that (
2.2
) has Hyers–Ulam stability on
\mathbb{T}
if and only if given an arbitrary
\varepsilon>0
and any function
\phi:\mathbb{T}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
satisfying

|\phi^{\nabla}(t)-\lambda\phi(t)|\leq\varepsilon,\quad t\in\mathbb{T},

(4.1)

then there exists a solution
x:\mathbb{T}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
of (
2.2
) and a constant
K>0
such that
|\phi(t)-x(t)|\leq K\varepsilon
for all
t\in\mathbb{T}
. In this case, such a constant
K
is called an HUS constant for (
2.2
) on
\mathbb{T}
.

Let

\widehat{K}_{\mathbb{R}}=\frac{\beta\lambda-1+e^{\alpha\lambda}(1-2\beta\lambda)}{\lambda\left(\beta\lambda-1-e^{\alpha\lambda}\right)}.

(4.2)

The following result appeared in
[
1
, Theorem 2.5]
. It is included here for completeness and easy reference.

Theorem 4.2

(Nabla equation)
.

Fix
\alpha>0
, and let
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\left\{\frac{1}{\beta}\right\}
. Also, let
\widehat{K}_{\mathbb{R}}
be given as in (
4.2
). We have the following cases.

(i)

Suppose

(b)

If

(c)

) is HUS, with best HUS constant

.

) is not HUS.

(c)

If

2.2
) is HUS, with best HUS constant

as in (
4.2
).

We end this section with another new result.

Theorem 4.4
.

The HUS constants given in Theorem
4.2
and those given in Theorem
4.3
are equivalent in the case where the eigenvalue
\lambda
satisfies
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Recall the polar substitution

(1-\beta\lambda)^{-1}e^{\alpha\lambda}=\rho e^{i\theta},\qquad\theta\in[0,2\pi).

Since the left-hand side is a real-valued expression for
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, we must take

\theta=\begin{cases}0&\text{if}\quad 1-\beta\lambda>0,\\
\pi&\text{if}\quad 1-\beta\lambda<0.\end{cases}

In (
4.3
), take

\rho=\begin{cases}(1-\beta\lambda)^{-1}e^{\alpha\lambda}&\text{if}\quad 1-\beta\lambda>0,\\
(1-\beta\lambda)^{-1}e^{-\alpha\lambda}&\text{if}\quad 1-\beta\lambda<0.\end{cases}

Then, (
4.3
) with
j=\alpha
reduces to

\displaystyle K_{\rho>1}

\displaystyle=\max_{j\in[0,\alpha]}\left(\frac{\rho-1+e^{j\operatorname{Re}(\lambda)}+\rho e^{(j-\alpha)\operatorname{Re}(\lambda)}\left(\beta\operatorname{Re}(\lambda)-1\right)}{(\rho-1)\operatorname{Re}(\lambda)}\right)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\lambda}&\text{if}\quad 1-\beta\lambda>0,\\
\frac{\beta\lambda-1+e^{\alpha\lambda}(1-2\beta\lambda)}{\lambda\left(\beta\lambda-1-e^{\alpha\lambda}\right)}&\text{if}\quad 1-\beta\lambda<0.\end{cases}

Note the second constant is
\widehat{K}_{\rho}
given in (
4.2
). Similarly,

K_{0<\rho<1}=\max_{j\in[0,\alpha]}\left(\frac{\rho-1+e^{j\operatorname{Re}(\lambda)}+\rho e^{(j-\alpha)\operatorname{Re}(\lambda)}\left(\beta\operatorname{Re}(\lambda)-1\right)}{(1-\rho)\operatorname{Re}(\lambda)}\right)=-\frac{1}{\lambda}

if
\lambda<0
.
∎
