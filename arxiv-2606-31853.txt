Conditionals and Modalities in Constructive Quantum Logics
Juan P. Aguilera, Guillaume Massas

Abstract
We investigate logics that generalize both intuitionistic logic and quantum logic. In earlier work, we introduced Ex-logic, an extension of Holliday’s fundamental logic that coincides with the intersection of orthologic and the implication-free fragment of intuitionistic logic. In this paper, we add an implication connective to Ex-logic and axiomatize iEx-logic, the intersection of full intuitionistic logic and orthomodular logic with the implication connective interpreted as the Sasaki hook a → b := ¬ a ∨ ( a ∧ b ) a\to b:=\lnot a\vee(a\wedge b) . As a consequence, we obtain a characterization of the lattice of logics extending iEx-logic as the product of the lattice of intermediate logics and the lattice of orthomodular logics. We also explore the robustness of our algebraic approach by briefly discussing extensions of iEx-logic with modal operators.



1
Introduction

Recently introduced by Holliday
[
10
]
, fundamental logic is a propositional logic that generalizes both constructive reasoning and quantum reasoning. However, the intersection of the consequence relations on the set of formulas in the signature
\{\land,\lor,\neg\}
determined by intuitionistic logic and orthologic respectively is a proper superset of the consequence relation determined by fundamental logic. Answering a question of Holliday, it was shown in
[
2
]
that the intersection of intuitionistic logic and orthologic in the signature
\{\wedge,\vee,\lnot\}
is axiomatized over fundamental logic by the following axiom
(Ex):

\displaystyle\lnot\bigg[a\wedge\Big((b\wedge c)\vee(b

\displaystyle\wedge d)\Big)\bigg]\wedge a\wedge(c\vee e)\wedge\lnot\lnot f\vdash\lnot\lnot(a\wedge f)

\displaystyle\wedge\bigg[(a\wedge c)\vee(a\wedge e)\vee f\bigg]\wedge\bigg[\Big(b\wedge(c\vee d)\Big)\vee\lnot\Big(b\wedge(c\vee d)\Big)\bigg].

This result motivates the study of constructive quantum logics, i.e., propositional logics extending fundamental logic + (Ex). Here,
orthologic
or
minimal quantum logic
is the logic of ortholattices and results from classical logic by removing the axiom of distributivity. The main reason for considering the signature
\{\wedge,\vee,\lnot\}
is that orthologic lacks a reasonable notion of implication (e.g., one satisfying
modus ponens
).

However, some quantum logics extending orthologic do admit reasonable forms of implication.
As shown by Dalla Chiara and Giuntini
[
4
]
, the so-called
Sasaki implication
defined in an ortholattice
O
by

a\to b:=\lnot a\vee(a\wedge b)

satisfies
modus ponens
if and only if
O
is orthomodular. The orthomodular law

if
a\leq b
, then
a\vee(\lnot a\wedge b)=b

was already considered by Birkhoff and Von Neumann
[
3
]
as a principle for quantum logic. As implication is arguably a crucial component of intuitionistic logic, this motivates the question:
What are the jointly valid principles of intuitionistic logic and orthomodular logic with the Sasaki implication?
Our main goal here is to answer this question. Note that, when speaking of implication in the context of orthomodular logic below, we shall always mean the Sasaki implication.

In
[
11
]
, Holliday considers binary operations defined on bounded lattices that he calls preconditionals. Preconditionals are meant to provide a minimal theory of conditionals. Holliday shows that, whenever
L
is a bounded lattice equipped with a preconditional
\to
that satifies
modus ponens
,
L
is an orthomodular lattice if and only if
\to
satisfies double negation elimination (i.e., the inequality
(a\to 0)\to 0\leq a
), while
L
is a Heyting algebra if and only if
\to
satisfies the
verum ex quodlibet
principle (i.e., the inequality
a\leq b\to a
). However, it is easy to see that the logic of preconditionals that satisfy
modus ponens
is weaker than the intersection of orthomodular logic and intuitionistic logic. In order to axiomatize the latter, we work in the signature
\{\wedge,\vee,\to\}
, recasting the negation axioms of fundamental logic in terms of implication via the definition
\lnot a:=a\to 0
, and we generalize axiom
\mathrm{(Ex)}
to a new axiom
\mathrm{(iEx)}
(for “
implicative
\mathrm{(Ex)}
”).
Our main theorem is the following:

Theorem 1.1
.

The joint validities of intuitionistic logic and orthomodular logic in the full signature
\{\vee,\wedge,\to\}
are axiomatized by the following axioms over fundamental logic:

1.

\mathrm{(iEx)}
;

2.

\lnot(a\to b)=\lnot(\lnot a\vee(a\wedge b))
;

3.

a\wedge(a\to b)=a\wedge b
;

4.

(a\wedge b)\to b=1
;

5.

a\to(b\land c)\leq a\to b
.

One might argue that the last three of these axioms merely capture the intended meaning of an implication satisfying
modus ponens
, and thus that the “content” of the logic is captured by the first two axioms. We shall also give alternate axiomatizations by decomposing
\mathrm{(iEx)}
into three axioms
\mathrm{(Nu)}
,
\mathrm{(Vi)}
, and
\mathrm{(iCl)}
, the first two of which only involve the connectives
\{\wedge,\vee,\lnot\}
and are already valid in Ex-logic.

We proceed as follows. Section
2
starts by introducing a large class of algebras, called
fundamental algebras
, that essentially correspond to fundamental lattices in a more general signature and generalize Heyting algebras and orthomodular lattices equipped with the Sasaki implication. We focus on a particular class of fundamental algebras, which we call
Ex-algebras
, and introduce the axiom (iEx). In Section
3
, we prove our main technical result, the iEx-Embedding Theorem, which establishes that any Ex-algebra subdirectly embeds into the product of an orthomodular algebra and a Heyting algebra. This result is used in Section
4
to show that iEx-logic, the logic of Ex-algebras, coincides with the intersection of intuitionistic logic and orthomodular logic and to characterize the lattice of extensions of iEx-logic. Finally, Section
5
briefly discusses adding modal operators to fundamental algebras.

2
Ex-algebras

In this section, we introduce the algebraic structures that we will be working with. The most general notion is that of a fundamental algebra, which subsumes Heyting algebras and orthomodular lattices.
Below, we shall always write
\lnot a
as shorthand for
a\to 0
.

2.1
Fundamental algebras

Definition 2.1
.

A structure
(L,\wedge,\vee,0,1,\to)
is said to be a
fundamental algebra
if
(L,\wedge,\vee,0,1)
is a bounded lattice and, letting
\lnot a:=a\to 0
,
\lnot
is a
weak pseudo-complement
i.e., a unary operation for which the following hold:

1.

antitonicity
, i.e.,
a\leq b
implies
\lnot b\leq\lnot a
for all
a,b\in L
;

2.

semi-complementation
, i.e.,
a\wedge\lnot a=0
for all
a\in L
;

3.

double-negation introduction
, i.e.,
a\leq\lnot\lnot a
for all
a\in L
.

The
\{\land,\lor,\neg\}
-reduct of any fundamental algebra is a fundamental lattice in the sense of
[
2
]
, also called a weakly pseudo-complemented lattice by Holliday in
[
10
]
. We recall that, in any fundamental lattice
L
,
\neg
is dually self-adjoint, which means in particular that
\neg a=\neg\neg\neg a
for any
a\in L
. Since we only impose requirements on the implication
\to
when the second component is
0
, fundamental algebras generalize Holliday’s class of preconditionals satisfying
modus ponens

[
11
]
.

Given a weakly pseudo-complemented, bounded lattice
L

v
into
L
assigns elements of
L
to propositional variables. Valuations can be extended to arbitrary propositional formulas in the natural way.
Often we abuse notation by omitting mention of

if clear from the context, and identifying formulas with elements of
L
.
We say that
\varphi\vdash\psi
is
valid
in
L

\varphi\leq\psi
holds for all valuations into
L

Fundamental logic
can be defined as the logic of the
\{\land,\lor,\neg\}
-reducts of fundamental algebras. Alternatively, it can be defined as the weakest
introduction–elimination
logic, i.e., the weakest logic such that for all formulas
\varphi,\psi,\chi
, the following are valid:

1.

\varphi\vdash\varphi
;

2.

\bot\vdash\varphi
;

3.

\varphi\vdash\top
;

4.

\varphi\wedge\psi\vdash\varphi
;

5.

\varphi\wedge\psi\vdash\psi
;

6.

\varphi\vdash\varphi\vee\psi
;

7.

\psi\vdash\varphi\vee\psi
;

8.

\varphi\vdash\lnot\lnot\varphi
;

9.

\varphi\wedge\lnot\varphi\vdash\bot
;

10.

if
\varphi\vdash\psi
and
\psi\vdash\chi
, then
\varphi\vdash\chi
;

11.

if
\varphi\vdash\psi
and
\varphi\vdash\chi
, then
\varphi\vdash\psi\wedge\chi
;

12.

if
\varphi\vdash\chi
and
\psi\vdash\chi
, then
\varphi\vee\psi\vdash\chi
;

13.

if
\varphi\vdash\psi
, then
\lnot\psi\vdash\lnot\varphi
.

Definition 2.2
.

An
orthomodular algebra
is a fundamental algebra
(L,\wedge,\vee,0,1,\lnot,\to)
in which
\lnot
satisfies double-negation elimination
\lnot\lnot a=a
for all
a\in L
and
\to
satisfies
modus ponens

a\wedge(a\to b)\leq b
and the Sasaki axiom
a\to b=\lnot a\vee(a\wedge b)
.

It is well known that orthomodular logic with the Sasaki implication is the logic of orthomodular algebras; see Dalla Chiara and Giuntini
[
4
]
.
For intuitionistic logic, we must impose a strong interaction condition between
\land
and
\to
:

Definition 2.3
.

A fundamental algebra
(L,\wedge,\vee,0,1,\to)
is a
Heyting algebra
if it satisfies the following Residuation Law: for any
a,b,c\in L
, we have

(c\wedge a)\leq b\text{ if and only if }c\leq a\to b.

Recall that a lattice is
distributive
if meets distribute over joins, and joins distribute over meets. A standard argument shows that distributivity follows from the Residuation Law, so that a Heyting algebra is always a distributive lattice. As is also well known, intuitionistic logic is the logic of Heyting algebras. We also recall the following result, originally due to Glivenko
[
9
]
:

Theorem 2.4

(Glivenko’s Theorem)
.

For any propositional formulas
\varphi
and
\psi
, if
\varphi\leq\psi
is classically valid, then
\varphi\leq\neg\neg\psi
is intuitionistically valid.

We now introduce a particular class of fundamental algebras that we call
Ex-algebras
. As we shall see, Ex-algebras generalize both orthomodular algebras and Heyting algebras. In fact, we shall prove in the next section that the variety of Ex-algebras is the join variety of the variety of orthomodular algebras and the variety of Heyting algebras. We start by introducing a weaker notion.

2.2
The axiom
\mathrm{(iEx)}

Definition 2.5
.

A
weak Ex-algebra
is a fundamental algebra in which the following inequality is valid:

\displaystyle\Bigg[\bigg(a

\displaystyle\wedge\Big((b\wedge c)\vee(b\wedge d)\Big)\bigg)\to g\Bigg]\wedge a\wedge(c\vee e)\wedge\lnot\lnot f\leq\lnot\lnot(a\wedge f)

(
\mathrm{iEx}
)

\displaystyle\wedge\bigg[(a\wedge c)\vee(a\wedge e)\vee f\bigg]\wedge\bigg[\big(b\wedge(c\vee d)\big)\vee\Big(\big(b\wedge(c\vee d)\big)\to g\Big)\bigg].

Axiom
\mathrm{(iEx)}
is related to the axiom
\mathrm{(Ex)}
introduced in
[
2
]
and, as we shall see below, implies it. Let us first show that weak Ex-algebras generalize both orthomodular algebras and Heyting algebras.

Lemma 2.6
.

(iEx)
is valid in orthomodular logic. Thus, every orthomodular algebra is a weak Ex-algebra.

Proof.

We prove that the following strengthening of (iEx) is valid in all orthomodular algebras:

\displaystyle a\wedge\lnot\lnot f\leq\lnot\lnot(a\wedge f)\wedge f\wedge\bigg[\big(b\wedge(c\vee d)\big)\vee\Big(\big(b\wedge(c\vee d)\big)\to g\Big)\bigg].

We need to show that
a\wedge\lnot\lnot f
entails each of the three conjuncts in the consequent of the displayed axiom.
First, we have

a\wedge\lnot\lnot f\leq a\wedge f\leq\lnot\lnot(a\wedge f)

by double-negation elimination. Similarly, we have

\lnot\lnot f\leq f
.
Finally, the third conjunct is valid already, as, letting
p:=(b\wedge(c\vee d))
, we have

1=p\vee\lnot p\leq p\vee(\lnot p\vee(p\land g))=p\vee(p\to g).

∎

Lemma 2.7
.

(iEx)
is valid in intuitionistic logic. Thus, every Heyting algebra is a weak Ex-algebra.

Proof.

We prove that the following strengthening of (iEx) is valid in all Heyting algebras:

\displaystyle\Bigg[\bigg(a

\displaystyle\wedge\Big((b\wedge c)\vee(b\wedge d)\Big)\bigg)\to(e\wedge g)\Bigg]\wedge a\wedge(c\vee e)\wedge\lnot\lnot f

\displaystyle\leq\lnot\lnot(a\wedge f)\wedge\bigg[(a\wedge c)\vee(a\wedge e)\bigg]\wedge\bigg[\big(b\wedge(c\vee d)\big)\to g\bigg].

Intuitionistically, we have

\displaystyle a\wedge\lnot\lnot f\leq\lnot\lnot a\wedge\lnot\lnot f\leq\lnot\lnot(a\wedge f).

(1)

By distributivity, we have

\displaystyle a\wedge(c\vee e)\leq(a\wedge c)\vee(a\wedge e).

(2)

Lastly, we derive the following:

\displaystyle\Bigg[\bigg(a\wedge\Big((b\wedge c)\vee(b\wedge d)\Big)\bigg)\to(e\wedge g)\Bigg]\wedge a\leq\Big(b\wedge(c\vee d)\Big)\to g.

(3)

By residuation, (
3
) follows from:

\displaystyle\Bigg[\bigg(a\wedge\Big((b\wedge c)\vee(b\wedge d)\Big)\bigg)\to(e\wedge g)\Bigg]\wedge a\land\Big(b\wedge(c\vee d)\Big)\leq g.

(4)

Let
p=(b\land c)\lor(b\land d)
, by distributivity and
modus ponens
, we have:

\displaystyle\Big[(a\wedge p)\to(e\wedge g)\Big]\wedge a\land\Big(b\wedge(c\vee d)\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\Big[(a\wedge p)\to(e\wedge g)\Big]\wedge a\wedge p

\displaystyle\leq e\land g

\displaystyle\leq g,

as desired.
Putting together (
1
) (
2
), and (

∎

We now focus on a particular class of weak Ex-algebras.

Definition 2.8
.

An
Ex-algebra
is a fundamental algebra in which
\mathrm{(iEx)}
and the following four axioms are valid:

\displaystyle\lnot(a\to b)

\displaystyle=\lnot(\lnot a\vee(a\wedge b))

(nS)

\displaystyle a\wedge(a\to b)

\displaystyle=a\wedge b

(MP)

\displaystyle(a\wedge b)\to b

\displaystyle=1

(M)

\displaystyle a\to(b\land c)

\displaystyle\leq a\to b

(W4)

Just like weak Ex-algebras, Ex-algebras generalize both orthomodular algebras and Heyting algebras.

Lemma 2.9
.

Axioms
\mathrm{(nS)}
,
\mathrm{(MP)}
,
\mathrm{(M)}
and
\mathrm{(W4)}
are valid in all orthomodular algebras. Thus, every orthomodular algebra is an Ex-algebra.

Proof.

In orthomodular lattices
\mathrm{(nS)}
is equivalent to
a\to b=\lnot a\vee(a\wedge b)
, which holds by definition.

We verify
\mathrm{(MP)}
. The inequality
a\wedge(a\to b)\leq b
holds by the orthomodular law. Clearly we also have
a\wedge(a\to b)\leq a
, and thus
a\wedge(a\to b)\leq a\wedge b
. Conversely, we have
a\wedge b\leq a
and
a\wedge b\leq\lnot a\vee(a\wedge b)=a\to b
, and thus
a\wedge(a\to b)=a\wedge b
.
For
\mathrm{(M)}
, we have

\displaystyle(a\wedge b)\to b

\displaystyle=\lnot(a\wedge b)\vee((a\wedge b)\wedge b)

\displaystyle=\lnot(a\wedge b)\vee(a\wedge b)=1,

as desired.
Finally, we clearly have that

\neg a\lor(a\land(b\land c))\leq\neg a\lor(a\land b),

from which
\mathrm{(W4)}
follows.
∎

Lemma 2.10
.

Axioms
\mathrm{(nS)}
,
\mathrm{(MP)}
,
\mathrm{(M)}
and
\mathrm{(W4)}
are valid in all Heyting algebras. Thus, every Heyting algebra is an Ex-algebra.

Proof.

We first prove
\mathrm{(MP)}
. Notice that
a\wedge(a\to b)\leq a
and
a\wedge(a\to b)\leq b
. Conversely,
a\wedge b\leq a
and
a\wedge b\leq a\to b
, where the latter inequality follows from the fact that
a\wedge(a\wedge b)\leq b
and residuation.

For
\mathrm{(nS)}
, we first observe that the equalities

\displaystyle\neg a\lor(a\land b)

\displaystyle=(\neg a\lor a)\land(\neg a\lor b)

\displaystyle=\neg a\lor b

\displaystyle=a\to b

are classically valid, which means that
\neg(a\to b)=\neg(\neg a\lor(a\land b))
is also classically valid. By Glivenko’s theorem, this means that the inequalities

\neg(a\to b)\leq\neg\neg\neg(\neg a\lor(a\land b))=\neg(\neg a\lor(a\land b))

and

\neg(\neg a\lor(a\land b))\leq\neg\neg\neg(a\to b)=\neg(a\to b)

are intuitionistically valid. This shows that
\mathrm{(nS)}
is valid.
For
\mathrm{(M)}
, we have that
1\land a\land b\leq b
, which by residuation implies
1\leq(a\land b)\to b
, as desired.
Finally, for
\mathrm{(W4)}
, by residuation, it is enough to show that
a\land(a\to(b\land c))\leq b
. But
modus ponens
gives us
a\land(a\to(b\land c))\leq b\land c\leq b
, as desired.
∎

Properties of Ex-algebras and weak Ex-algebras

Let us now derive a number of properties of (weak) Ex-algebras, which will play an important role in the proof of our main theorem in the next section. We start by clarifying the relationship between the axioms

Lemma 2.11
.

The
\{\wedge,\vee,\lnot\}
-reduct of every Ex-algebra is an Ex-lattice.

Proof.

By setting

\mathrm{(iEx)}

Next, we single out three additional axioms that follow directly from

Lemma 2.12
.

The following axioms are valid in all weak Ex-algebras:

\Big[\big(a\land((b\land c)\lor(b\land d))\big)\to g\Big]\land a\leq(b\land(c\lor d))\lor\Big[(b\land(c\lor d))\to g\Big]

Finally, we prove two useful properties of Ex-algebras.

Lemma 2.13

Suppose

Proof.

Suppose that

Conversely, suppose that

a\wedge b=a\wedge(a\to b)=a

∎

Suppose that

Proof.

By Lemma

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle\leq(a\wedge b)\to(a\to b),

3
The iEx-Embedding Theorem

Throughout this section, a map between fundamental algebras that preserves all the operations will be called a
fundamental algebra homomorphism
, and a map between the
\{\land,\lor,\neg\}
-reducts of two fundamental algebras that preserve all three operations will be called a
fundamental homomorphism
. We are now ready to prove our main theorem. Recall that a homomorphism
f:L\to M_{1}\times M_{2}
is a subdirect embedding if
f
is injective and the composition of
f
with the projections
\pi_{1}:M_{1}\times M_{2}\to M_{1}
and
\pi_{2}:M_{1}\times M_{2}\to M_{2}
yields surjective homomorphisms.

Theorem 3.1

(The iEx-Embedding Theorem)
.

Let
(L,\land,\lor,0,1,\to)
be an Ex-algebra. Then there are an orthomodular algebra
O_{L}
, a Heyting algebra
I_{L}
, and a subdirect fundamental algebra embedding
e:L\to O_{L}\times I_{L}
.

The theorem will be proved using the (lattice) Ex-Embedding Theorem from
[
2
]
. There, we proved that if
(L,\wedge,\vee,0,1,\lnot)
is an Ex-lattice (i.e., a fundamental lattice satisfying
\mathrm{(Ex)}
), then there are an ortholattice
O_{L}
, a Heyting lattice
I_{L}
, and a subdirect fundamental lattice embedding
e:L\to O_{L}\times I_{L}
.

We shall first recall this construction from
[
2
]
.
Let
L
be an Ex-lattice. We define an ortholattice
O_{L}
and a Heyting lattice (i.e., a weakly pseudo-complemented distributive lattice)
I_{L}
which are both homomorphic images of
L
. An element
a\in L
is then mapped to a pair
e(a)=(a_{O},a_{I})\in O_{L}\times I_{L}
which we describe below.
The ortholattice
O_{L}
is defined as a quotient of
L
by the equivalence relation

a\sim b\leftrightarrow\lnot a=\lnot b.

We shall write
[a]_{O}
for the equivalence class of
a
(i.e., for
a_{O}
) if this helps with clarity.
It was shown in
[
2
]
that
O_{L}
is indeed an ortholattice and that this equivalence relation is congruent with the operations
\wedge,\vee
, and
\lnot
, i.e., that
[a\wedge b]_{O}=a_{O}\wedge b_{O},[a\vee b]_{O}=a_{O}\vee b_{O}
, and
\lnot a_{O}=[\lnot a]_{O}
.

The Heyting lattice
I_{L}
is defined as follows. Letting
(P(L),\supseteq)
be the set of (proper) prime filters of
L
ordered by reverse inclusion, we consider the
\to
-free reduct of the Heyting algebra
Dn(P(L))
of open subsets of
(P(L),\supseteq)
endowed with the downset topology. We then define
I_{L}
to be the Heyting sublattice of
Dn(P(L))
generated by sets of the form
a_{I}=\{P\in P(L)\mid a\in P\}
for any
a\in L
, and show that the map
a\mapsto a_{I}
is a surjective fundamental homomorphism.

We now extend these results to the setting of Ex-algebras. For the rest of this section, fix an Ex-algebra
(L,\land,\lor,0,1,\to)
. We first define
O_{L}
as the quotient of
L
determined by the equivalence relation
a\sim b\Leftrightarrow\neg a=\neg b
and write
a_{O}
for the equivalence class of an element
a
modulo
\sim
.
We first claim that
\sim
is a congruence relation on
L
. Because
(L,\land,\lor,0,1,\neg)
, with
\neg a
defined as
a\to 0
, is an Ex-lattice, we know already that
\sim
preserves the operation
\land
,
\lor
and
\neg
. We now claim the following:

Lemma 3.2
.

For all
a,b,c,d\in L
, if
a_{O}=c_{O}
and
b_{O}=d_{O}
, then
[a\to b]_{O}=[c\to d]_{O}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
a_{O}=c_{O}
and
b_{O}=d_{O}
. We must show that
\neg(a\to b)=\neg(c\to d)
. Because
L
is an Ex-algebra, it satisfies
\mathrm{(nS)}
. Hence we have
\neg(a\to b)=\neg(\neg a\lor(a\land b))
and
\neg(c\to d)=\neg(\neg c\lor(c\land d))
.
Since
\sim
preserves
\land
,
\lor
and
\lnot
, we have that
a\sim c
and
b\sim d
entails

\displaystyle a\to b

\displaystyle\sim\neg a\lor(a\land b)\sim\neg c\lor(c\land d)\sim c\to d,

as desired. This shows that
[a\to b]_{O}=[c\to d]_{O}
.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

(O_{L},\wedge,\vee,0,1,\to)
is a homomorphic image of
(L,\land,\lor,0,1,\to)
. Moreover, it is an orthomodular algebra.

Proof.

For the first part, we know already that the map
a\mapsto a_{O}
is a surjective lattice homomorphism that preserves
\neg
. Hence we only need to show that
[a\to b]_{O}=a_{O}\to b_{O}
for any
a,b\in L
. Since

a_{O}\to b_{O}=\neg a_{O}\lor(a_{O}\land b_{O})=[\neg a\lor(a\land b)]_{O},

this amounts to showing that
a\to b\sim\neg a\lor(a\land b),
which holds by
\mathrm{(nS)}
.
For the second part, we know already that
O_{L}
is an ortholattice. By the result of Dalla Chiara and Giuntini
[
4
]
, it then suffices to show that the principle
a\wedge(\lnot a\vee(a\wedge b))\leq b
is valid in
O_{L}
. But this is immediate from the fact that
\mathrm{(MP)}
holds in
L
together with the first part. Hence
O_{L}
is an orthomodular algebra.
∎

We have shown that the mapping
a\mapsto a_{O}
is a (surjective) fundamental algebra homomorphism. We now turn to
I_{L}
. Following
[
2
]
, we consider first the Heyting algebra
Dn(P(L))
of open subsets of the topological space
(P(L),\supseteq)
, where
P(L)
is the set of all (proper) prime filters on
L
and open sets are subsets of
P(L)
which are downward-closed (here, a prime filter
Q
is below
P
if
P\subseteq Q
). We let
a\mapsto a_{I}
maps any
a\in L
to the set
a_{I}=\{P\in P(L)\mid a\in P\}
, and define
I_{L}
as the Heyting subalgebra of
Dn(P(L))
generated by sets of the form
a_{I}
for some
a\in A
. We now claim that the map
a\mapsto a_{I}
is a surjective fundamental algebra homomorphism. It is routine to check that this map preserves
\land
and
\lor
, but we must show that
[a\to b]_{I}=a_{I}\to b_{I}
, where
a_{I}\to b_{I}
is the Heyting implication in
Dn(P(L)
, i.e.:

a_{I}\to b_{I}=\{P\in P(L)\mid\forall Q((Q\supseteq P\land a\in Q)\to b\in Q)\}.

We start by proving the following restricted version of the Prime Filter Theorem.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
L
be an Ex-algebra,
P
a prime filter on
L
and
F
and
I
a filter and an ideal on
L
respectively. If
P\subseteq F
and
F\cap I=\emptyset
, then there is a prime filter
Q
on
L
such that
F\subseteq Q
and
Q\cap I=\emptyset
.

Proof.

Let
P
,
F
and
I
as in the statement of the lemma, and let
Q
be a maximal filter extending
F
and disjoint from
I
, which exists by Zorn’s Lemma. We claim that
Q
is prime. To see this, suppose that
c\notin Q
and
d\notin Q
. We want to show that
c\lor d\notin Q
. Since
Q
is maximal among filters disjoint from
I
, there must be
a\in Q
and
b\in I
such that
a\land c\leq b
and
a\land d\leq b
. This means that
(a\land c)\lor(a\land d)\leq b
and thus, by, Lemma
2.13
together with
\mathrm{iCl2}
, we have

1\leq((a\land c)\lor(a\land d))\to b\leq(a\land(c\lor d))\lor((a\land(c\lor d))\to b).

Since
1\in P
and
P
is prime, this means that either
a\land(c\lor d)\in P
or
(a\land(c\lor d))\to b\in P
. In the first case, we have that
c\lor d\in P
, and hence either
c\in P\subseteq Q
or
d\in P\subseteq Q
, contradicting our assumption. In the second case, we have that
(a\land(c\lor d))\to b\in P\subseteq Q
. But then, since
a\in Q
,
\mathrm{(MP)}
entails that
c\lor d\in Q
implies
b\in Q
. Hence
c\lor d\notin Q
, and
Q
is prime, as desired.
∎

The following is a straightforward application of the previous lemma.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
L
be an Ex-algebra. Suppose that
P
is a prime filter on
L
such that
a\to b\not\in P
. Then, there is a prime filter
Q
extending
P
such that
a\in Q
but
b\not\in Q
.

Proof.

Suppose
P
is a prime filter not containing
a\to b
. If
a\vee(a\to b)\in P
, then
a\in P
, as
P
is prime. We must already have
b\not\in P
, as otherwise we would have
a\to b\in P
, since
a\wedge b\leq a\to b
by Lemma
2.14
.
Thus, we may assume that
a\vee(a\to b)\not\in P
. We claim that
P\cup\{a\}
generates a filter which does not contain
b
. If not, then there is
c\in P
such that
a\wedge c\leq b
, so
(a\wedge c)\to b=1
, by Lemma
2.13
. Thus,
((a\wedge c)\to b)\wedge c\in P
.
By
\mathrm{(iCl_{1})}
, we have
((a\wedge c)\to b)\wedge c\leq a\vee(a\to b)
,
so
a\vee(a\to b)\in P
, contrary to the assumption above.

Thus,
P\cup\{a\}
generates a filter disjoint from the ideal generated by
\{b\}
. By Lemma
3.4
, there is a prime filter
Q
extending
P\cup\{a\}
that does not contain
b
, as desired.
∎

Lemma 3.6
.

The mapping
a\to a_{I}
is a surjective fundamental algebra homomorphism. Moreover, if
\neg a=\neg b
and
a\nleq b
, then
a_{I}\not\subseteq b_{I}
.

Proof.

For the first part, we only need to show that if
P\in P(L)
,
a\to b\in P
if and only if for all
Q
extending
P
with
a\in Q
, we have
b\in Q
. The right-to-left direction is Lemma
3.5
. The left-to-right direction follows because any such
Q
is a filter on
L
containing
a\to b
and
a
, thus also
a\wedge(a\to b)
. Since
a\wedge(a\to b)\leq b
,
Q
also contains
b
. For the second part, Lemma 4.7 in
[
2
]
establishes that whenever
\neg a=\neg b
and
a\nleq b
in an Ex-lattice
L
, there is a prime filter
P
over
L
containing
a
and not containing
b
. Since Ex-algebras are also Ex-lattices, the result applies here as well.
∎

Our main theorem now follows: since each mapping
a\mapsto a_{O}
and
a\mapsto a_{I}
is a fundamental algebra homomorphism, the map
e:L\to O_{L}\times I_{L}
given by
e(a)=(a_{O},a_{I})
is also a fundamental algebra homomorphism. To see that it is injective, suppose that
a\nleq b
. We distinguish two cases. If
\neg a\neq\neg b
then
a_{O}\nleq b_{O}
. Otherwise, by Lemma
3.6
, we have
a_{I}\not\subseteq b_{I}
. Either way, it follows that
e(a)\nleq e(b)
, as desired. Lastly, since the composition of
e
with the projections
\pi_{1}:O_{L}\times I_{L}\to O_{L}
and
\pi_{2}:O_{L}\times I_{L}\to I_{L}
coincides with the surjective maps
a\mapsto a_{O}
and
a\mapsto a_{I}
respectively, it follows that
e
is a subdirect embedding. This completes the proof of the iEx-Embedding Theorem.

4
Implicative Constructive Quantum Logics

In this section, we derive some consequences of the iEx-Embedding Theorem established above for constructive quantum logics equipped with an implication. We shall call such logics
implicative constructive quantum logics
(
ICQ
logics for short).

4.1
The Minimal
ICQ
Logic

We start by axiomatizing the intersection of intuitionistic logic and orthomodular logic, which can arguably be considered the minimal
ICQ
logic.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\varphi
and
\psi
be formulas.
The following are equivalent:

1.

\varphi\vdash\psi
is valid in all Heyting algebras and in all orthomodular algebras.

2.

\varphi\vdash\psi
is valid in all Ex-algebras.

Proof.

It was verified in Section
2.2
that every Heyting algebra and every orthomodular algebra is an Ex-algebra. Conversely, suppose that
\varphi\vdash\psi
is not valid in all Ex-algebras, so that
v(\varphi)\nleq v(\psi)
holds for some Ex-algebra
L
and some valuation
v
. By the iEx-Embedding Theorem, there is an orthomodular algebra
O_{L}
a Heyting Algebra
I_{L}
and a subdirect embedding

e:L\to O_{L}\times I_{L}
. By a standard argument in algebraic logic, this implies that the entailment
\varphi\vdash\psi
is falsifiable in either
O_{L}
or
I_{L}
, as desired.
∎

As a consequence of Theorem
4.1
, we immediately obtain:

Theorem 4.2
.

The joint validities of intuitionistic logic and orthomodular logic are axiomatized over Fundamental Logic with implication by
\mathrm{(iEx)}
together with the following principles:

(nS)

\lnot(a\to b)=\lnot(\lnot a\vee(a\wedge b))
;

(MP)

a\wedge(a\to b)=a\wedge b
;

(M)

(a\wedge b)\to b=1
;

(W4)

a\to(b\land c)\leq a\to b
.

Proof.

This follows from Theorem
4.1
together with the definition of Ex-algebras, the completeness of intuitionistic logic with respect to Heyting algebras, and the completeness of orthomodular logic with respect to orthomodular algebras (see Dalla Chiara and Giuntini
[
4
]
).
∎

Analyzing the proof of the iEx-Embedding Theorem, we also obtain the following:

Theorem 4.3
.

The joint validities of intuitionistic logic and orthomodular logic with implication are axiomatized over Fundamental Logic with implication by the following principles:

(nS)

\lnot(a\to b)=\lnot(\lnot a\vee(a\wedge b))
.

(MP)

a\wedge(a\to b)=a\wedge b
.

(M)

(a\wedge b)\to b=1
.

(W4)

a\to(b\wedge c)\leq a\to b
.

(iCl
1
)

a\wedge((a\wedge b)\to c)\leq b\vee(b\to c)
.

(iCl
2
)

((b\wedge c)\vee(b\wedge d))\to a\leq(b\wedge(c\vee d))\vee\big((b\wedge(c\vee d))\to a\big)
.

(Nu)

\lnot\lnot a\wedge\lnot\lnot b\leq\lnot\lnot(a\wedge b)
.

(Vi)

a\wedge(b\vee c)\wedge\lnot\lnot d\leq(a\wedge b)\vee(a\wedge c)\vee d
.

Proof.

It was verified throughout Section
2.3
that the principles (iCl
1
), (iCl
2
), (Nu), and (Vi) can all be obtained as instances of (iEx).

Conversely, examining the proof of the iEx-Embedding Theorem, we track down all uses of (iEx): We see that the proof in Section
3
only makes use of the principles (MP), (nS), (iCl
1
), and (iCl
2
). The principles (Nu) and (Vi) are used implicitly when appealing to the lattice Ex-Embedding Theorem of
[
2
]
. Finally, the proof makes use of Lemma
2.13
, which only requires (M) and (MP), and Lemma
2.14
, which again only makes use of (M) and (MP), as well as (W4).
∎

4.2
The Lattice of
ICQ
Logics

Next, we generalize our previous result by providing an axiomatization for the intersection
\mathsf{L}_{O}\cap\mathsf{L}_{I}
of any pair of a superintuitionistic logic
\mathsf{L}_{I}
and a superorthomodular logic
\mathsf{L}_{O}
. As a corollary, we also obtain a characterization of the lattice of all
ICQ
logics, i.e., the lattice of all extensions of iEx-logic. A similar result was already established for extensions of Ex-logic, the lattice of which is isomorphic to the product of the lattice of quantum logics and the lattice of extensions of the implication-free fragment of intuitionistic logic
[
2
, Thm. 6.7]
. The proof of the result presented here follows the same strategy. We start with the following two lemmas.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\mathsf{L}_{I}
be a superintuitionistic logic axiomatized by
\{\varphi_{i}\vdash\psi_{i}\}_{i\in I}
and
\mathsf{L}_{O}
be a superorthomodular logic axiomatized by
\{\varphi_{j}\vdash\psi_{j}\}_{j\in J}
. Then
\mathsf{L}_{I}\cap\mathsf{L}_{O}
is axiomatized by extending iEx-logic with the set of axioms

\{\varphi_{i}\vdash\psi_{i}\lor\neg\psi_{i}\}_{i\in I}\cup\{\varphi_{j}\vdash\neg\neg\psi_{j}\}_{j\in J}.

Proof.

Fix a superintuitionistic logic
\mathsf{L}_{I}
axiomatized by
\{\varphi_{i}\vdash\psi_{i}\}_{i\in I}
and a superorthomodular logic
\mathsf{L}_{O}
axiomatized by
\{\varphi_{j}\vdash\psi_{j}\}_{j\in J}
. We first check that the set
\{\varphi_{i}\vdash\psi_{i}\lor\neg\psi_{i}\}_{i\in I}\cup\{\varphi_{j}\vdash\neg\neg\psi_{j}\}_{j\in J}
is a set of valid axioms in
\mathsf{L}_{I}\cap\mathsf{L}_{O}
. Fix some
i\in I
. Clearly,
\mathsf{L}_{I}
contains the axiom
\varphi_{i}\vdash\psi_{i}\lor\neg\psi_{i}
, since
\varphi_{i}\vdash\psi_{i}\in\mathsf{L}_{I}
. Moreover,
\varphi_{i}\vdash\psi_{i}\lor\neg\psi_{i}
follows from the excluded middle, so it also belongs to
\mathsf{L}_{O}
. Now consider an axiom of the form
\varphi_{j}\vdash\neg\neg\psi_{j}
for some
j\in J
. Over orthomodular logic, this axiom is equivalent to
\varphi_{j}\vdash\psi_{j}
, hence it also belongs to
\mathsf{L}_{O}
. Moreover, since
\mathsf{L}_{O}
is a subclassical logic,
\varphi_{j}\vdash\psi_{j}
is classically valid. By Glivenko’s theorem, this means that
\varphi_{j}\vdash\neg\neg\psi_{j}
is valid intuitionistically, and therefore belongs to
\mathsf{L}_{I}
, as desired. This shows that
\{\varphi_{i}\vdash\psi_{i}\lor\neg\psi_{i}\}_{i\in I}\cup\{\varphi_{j}\vdash\neg\neg\psi_{j}\}_{j\in J}
is a set of axioms in
\mathsf{L}_{I}\cap\mathsf{L}_{O}
.

Let now
\mathsf{L}
be the extension of iEx axiomatized by this set of axioms. Clearly, we have that
\mathsf{L}\subseteq\mathsf{L}_{I}\cap\mathsf{L}_{O}
. We now claim that
\mathsf{L}_{I}\cap\mathsf{L}_{O}\subseteq\mathsf{L}
. To show this, suppose that
\varphi\vdash\psi
is not valid in
\mathsf{L}
. Then there is an
\mathsf{L}
-lattice
L
such that
\varphi\vdash\psi
is not valid in
L
. By the iEx-Embedding Theorem,
L
subdirectly embeds into
O_{L}\times I_{L}
. Since
O_{L}
is a homomorphic image of
L
, the axiom
\varphi_{j}\vdash\neg\neg\psi_{j}
is valid on
O_{L}
for any
j\in J
. But since
O_{L}
is an ortholattice, this means that
\varphi_{j}\vdash\psi_{j}
is also valid on
O_{L}
for any
j\in J
, and therefore
O_{L}
is an
\mathsf{L}_{O}
-lattice. Similarly, we have that
I_{L}
is a homomorphic image of
L
, and therefore
\varphi_{i}\vdash\psi_{i}\lor\neg\psi_{i}
is valid in
I_{L}
for any
i\in I
. Fix some
i\in I
. Since
\varphi_{i}\vdash\psi_{i}
is classically valid, we have that
\vdash\neg(\varphi_{i}\land\neg\psi_{i})
is also classically valid, and thus also intuitionistically valid by Glivenko’s theorem. This means that, for any valuation
V
on
I_{L}
, we have
V(\varphi_{i})\leq V(\psi_{i})\lor V(\neg\psi_{i})
and
(V(\varphi_{i})\land\neg V(\psi_{i}))\leq 0
. By distributivity, this gives us:

V(\varphi_{i})\leq V(\varphi_{i})\land V(\psi_{i}\lor\neg\psi_{i})\leq(V(\varphi_{i})\land V(\psi_{i}))\lor(V(\varphi_{i})\land\neg V(\psi_{i}))\leq V(\varphi_{i})\land V(\psi_{i}),

from which it follows that
\varphi_{i}\vdash\psi_{i}
is valid on
I_{L}
for any
i\in I
. Hence
I_{L}
is an
\mathsf{L}_{I}
-lattice. Since
\varphi\vdash\psi
is not valid on
L
and embeds into
O_{L}\times I_{L}
,
\varphi\vdash\psi
is not valid on
O_{L}\times I_{L}
, which means that it is not valid on either
O_{L}
or
I_{L}
. Either way, it follows that
\varphi\vdash\psi\notin\mathsf{L}_{O}\cap\mathsf{L}_{I}
. This shows that
\mathsf{L}_{O}\cap\mathsf{L}_{I}\subseteq\mathsf{L}
, as desired.
∎

The previous lemma shows how to axiomatize the intersection of any pair of a superorthomodular logic and a superintuitionistic logic as an extension of iEx-logic. In fact, every extension of iEx-logic arises as such an intersection of logics, as the following establishes.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\mathsf{L}
be an extension of
iEx
, and let
\mathbb{V}(\mathsf{L})
be the variety of fundamental algebras on which
\mathsf{L}
is valid. Let
O_{\mathsf{L}}
be the logic of the class of algebras
\{O_{L}\mid L\in\mathbb{V}(\mathsf{L})\}
and
I_{\mathsf{L}}
be the logic of the class of algebras
\{I_{L}\mid L\in\mathbb{V}(\mathsf{L})\}
, where, for any Ex-algebra
L
,
O_{L}
and
I_{L}
are as in the iEx-Embedding Theorem. Then
\mathsf{L}=O_{\mathsf{L}}\cap I_{\mathsf{L}}
.

Proof.

Clearly, since
O_{L}\models\mathsf{L}
and
I_{L}\models\mathsf{L}
whenever
L\models\mathsf{L}
, we have that
\mathsf{L}\subseteq O_{\mathsf{L}}\cap I_{\mathsf{L}}
. For the converse, note that, if
\varphi\vdash\psi\in O_{\mathsf{L}}\cap I_{\mathsf{L}}
, then
\varphi\vdash\psi
is valid on
O_{L}\times I_{L}
for any
L\in\mathbb{V}(\mathsf{L})
. By the iEx-Embedding theorem,
L
subdirectly embeds into
O_{L}\times I_{L}
, so
\varphi\vdash\psi
is valid on
L
. This shows that
O_{\mathsf{L}}\cap I_{\mathsf{L}}\subseteq\mathsf{L}
.
∎

As a straightforward consequence of the previous two lemmas, we can provide a characterization of the lattice of extensions of iEx-logic.

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
i\mathbf{SI}
be the lattice of superintuitionistic logics,
i\mathbf{SO}
the lattice of superorthomodular logics, and
i\mathbf{SE}
the lattice of
ICQ
logics. Then
i\mathbf{SE}\simeq i\mathbf{SO}\times i\mathbf{SI}
.

Proof.

Consider the map
(\mathsf{L}_{O},\mathsf{L}_{I})\mapsto\mathsf{L}_{O}\cap\mathsf{L}_{I}
from the lattice
i\mathbf{SO}\times i\mathbf{SI}
to the lattice
i\mathbf{SE}
. Clearly, this map is monotone, and it is surjective by Lemma
4.5
. Hence, it is an isomorphism if we can also show injectivity. Suppose that
\mathsf{L}_{I}\subseteq\mathsf{L}_{I}^{\prime}\in i\mathbf{SI}
and
\mathsf{L}_{O}\subseteq\mathsf{L}_{O}^{\prime}\in i\mathbf{SO}
are such that
(\mathsf{L}_{O},\mathsf{L}_{I})\nleq_{i\mathbf{SO}\times i\mathbf{SI}}(\mathsf{L}_{O}^{\prime},\mathsf{L}_{I}^{\prime})
. Then either
\mathsf{L}_{O}^{\prime}\not\subseteq\mathsf{L}_{O}
or
\mathsf{L}_{I}^{\prime}\not\subseteq\mathsf{L}_{I}
. In the first case, this means that there is an
\mathsf{L}_{O}
-orthomodular algebra
O
and an axiom
\varphi\vdash\psi\in\mathsf{L}_{O}^{\prime}
that is not valid on
O
. By Lemma
4.4
, we have that
\varphi\vdash\neg\neg\psi
is not valid on
O
, yet
\varphi\vdash\neg\neg\psi\in\mathsf{L}_{O}^{\prime}\cap\mathsf{L}_{I}^{\prime}
. In the second case, there is a
\mathsf{L}_{I}
-Heyting algebra
A
and an axiom
\varphi\vdash\psi
that is not valid on
A
. By
4.4
again, we have that
\varphi\vdash\psi\lor\neg\psi
is not valid on
A
, yet
\varphi\vdash\psi\lor\neg\psi\in\mathsf{L}_{O}^{\prime}\cap\mathsf{L}_{I}^{\prime}
. Either way, this shows that
\mathsf{L}_{O}^{\prime}\cap\mathsf{L}_{I}^{\prime}\not\subseteq\mathsf{L}_{O}\cap\mathsf{L}_{I}
, as desired.
∎

5
Adding Modalities

In this final section, we consider extensions of fundamental algebras with modal operators. In particular, we are interested in using the iEx-Embedding Theorem to axiomatize the intersection of a reasonable intuitionistic modal logic and a reasonable quantum modal logic. Intuitionistic modal logic is a sprawling and fast growing field
[
15
,
16
]
. Prominent candidates to the title of “minimal intuitionistic modal logic” include Wijesekera’s logic
\mathsf{CK}

[
18
]
and Fischer Servi’s logic
\mathsf{IK}

[
8
]
. The field of quantum modal logics is comparatively less explored
[
7
,
13
,
17
]
. Here, we do not intend to make any claim regarding minimal intuitionistic modal logic or minimal quantum modal logic, but we are rather interested in logics whose intersection can be axiomatized using the techniques presented here. We leave a more thorough investigation of constructive quantum modal logics, e.g. along the lines of Holliday’s fundamental modal logic
[
12
]
, for future work.

From the point of view of the
iEx
-Embedding Theorem, the addition of modalities to the signature of fundamental algebras introduces some significant difficulties. In particular, the representation of a fundamental modal algebra as a subalgebra of the product of a quantum modal algebra and an intuitionistic modal algebra obtained as quotients of the original algebra is only possible if the quotient maps preserve the modal operators. Here, we run into some significant issues. In algebras corresponding to Fischer Servi’s logic
\mathsf{IK}
(and,
a fortiori
, in algebras corresponding to the weaker logic
\mathsf{CK}
), it is not always the case that the equivalence relation
\sim
defined above preserves
\Box
. In other words,
\neg a=\neg b
does not always imply that
\neg\Box a=\neg\Box b
. Similarly, the proof that the map
a\mapsto a_{I}
also preserves modal operators requires a stronger version of the restricted Prime Filter Theorem
3.4
that may not hold in modal orthomodular lattices. Here, we overcome these issues by restricting ourselves to classes of algebras in which they do not arise, leaving the development of a more elegant solution for future work.

We start with the following definitions.

Definition 5.1
.

A
\Box
-involutive Fischer Servi algebra (
IFS
algebra for short) is a tuple
(A,\land,\lor,0,1,\to,\Box,\Diamond)
such that
(A,\land,\lor,0,1,\to)
is a Heyting algebra and
\Box,\Diamond
are unary operators satisfying the following:

(M1)

\Box 1=1
,
\Box(a\land b)=\Box a\land\Box b
;

(M2)

\Diamond 0=0
,
\Diamond(a\lor b)=\Diamond a\lor\Diamond b
;

(FS1)

\Diamond a\to\Box b\leq\Box(a\to b)
;

(FS2)

\Diamond(a\to b)\leq\Box a\to\Diamond b
;

(I1)

1\leq\Box(\neg\neg a\to a)
.

The class of modal Heyting algebras satisfying the first four axioms corresponds to Fischer Servi’s logic
IK
. As mentioned above, axiom
(I1)
is need to guarantee that
\neg a=\neg b
implies
\neg\Box a=\neg\Box b
, as evidenced by the Fischer Servi algebra depicted below, where
\neg 1=\neg b
, but
\Box\neg 1\neq\Box\neg b
. Blue and red arrows respectively represent the operators
\Diamond
and
\Box
:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 1}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces a}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces b}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces c}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces d}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}

Definition 5.2
.

A
\Diamond
-distributive orthomodular algebra (
\mathrm{DOM}
algebra for short) is a tuple
(A,\land,\lor,0,1,\to,\Box,\Diamond)
such that
(A,\land,\lor,0,1,\to)
is an orthomodular algebra and
\Box,\Diamond
are unary operators satisfying axioms
(M1)
and
(M2)
as well as the following:

(M3)

\Diamond\neg a=\neg\Box a
;

(D1)

\Diamond a\land\Box b\leq\Diamond(a\land b)
;

(D2)

\Diamond(a\land(b\lor c))\leq\Diamond(a\land b)\lor\Diamond(a\land c)
.

Here again, the definition is more restrictive than in standard modal orthologic (see, e.g.,
[
13
]
). Axiom
(D1)
is a principle of positive modal logic, and
(D2)
amounts to assuming a form of “modal distributivity” which we will need later on. We now define the class of fundamental modal algebras that we will focus on from now on.

Definition 5.3
.

A modal
iEx
algebra (
miEx
algebra for short) is a tuple
(A,\land,\lor,0,1,\to,\Box,\Diamond)
such that
(A,\land,\lor,0,1,\to)
is an
iEx
lattice and
\Box,\Diamond
are unary operators satisfying axioms
(M1)
,
(M2)
,
(l1)
,
(D1)
,
(D2)
and the following:

(MCl)

\Box(((a\land b)\lor(a\land c))\to d)\leq\Box((a\land(b\lor c))\to d)\lor\Diamond(a\land b)\lor\Diamond(a\land c)
;

(M4)

\Diamond\neg a\leq\neg\Box a
;

(M5)

\neg\Diamond a=\Box\neg a
;

(M6)

\neg\Diamond\neg a\leq\Box a
;

(FS3)

\Diamond(a\to b)\leq(\Box a\to\Diamond b)\lor\Box a
.

We first verify that
miEx
algebras generalize both
\mathrm{DOM}
algebras and
\mathrm{IFS}
algebras.

Lemma 5.4
.

Any
\mathrm{DOM}
algebra is a
miEx
algebra.

Proof.

Note first that
(l1)
follows from
(M1)
together with
1\leq a\to a=\neg\neg a\to a
, which holds because
\neg
is an involution. Moreover,
(M4)
,
(M5)
and
(M6)
all clearly follow from
(M3)
in any ortholattice. For
(FS3)
, we have that
\neg\Box a\leq\Box a\to\Diamond b
, so the right-hand side also follows from the excluded middle. Hence it only remains to be shown that
(MCl)
holds. We claim that

\neg\Diamond(a\land(b\lor c))\leq\Box((a\land(b\lor c))\to d).

By
(D2)
, it is enough, since then we have

1\leq\neg\Diamond(a\land(b\lor c))\lor\Diamond(a\land b\lor c)\leq\Box((a\land(b\lor c))\to d)\lor\Diamond(a\land b)\lor\Diamond(a\land c).

For the proof of the claim, letting
p=(a\land(b\lor c))
, note that

\neg\Diamond p\leq\Box\neg p\leq\Box(\neg p\lor(p\land d))\leq\Box(p\to d),

as desired.
∎

Lemma 5.5
.

Any
\mathrm{IFS}
algebra is a
miEx
algebra.

Proof.

For
(D1)
, note first that
(M2)
and
a\leq b\to(a\land b)
yield
\Diamond a\to\Diamond(b\to(a\land b))
. By
(FS2)
, this means that

\Diamond a\leq\Diamond(b\to(a\land b))\leq\Box b\to\Diamond(a\land b),

and
(D1)
now follows from residuation.
(D2)
clearly follows from distributivity and
(D2)
.
For
(MCl)
, this follows from

\Box(((a\land b)\lor(a\land c))\to d)\leq\Box((a\land(b\lor c))\to d),

which itself holds in any
IFS
algebra by distributivity. For
(M4)
, by
(FS2)
, we have

\Diamond(a\to 0)\land\Box a\leq\Box a\land(\Box a\to\Diamond 0)\leq\Diamond 0\leq 0,

whence
\Diamond\neg a\leq\neg\Box a
. For
(M5)
, notice first that
(M4)
entails that
\Box\neg a\leq\neg\Diamond\neg\neg a\leq\neg\Diamond a
, where the second inequality holds because
\Diamond
is monotone. For the converse, we have that

\neg\Diamond a\leq\Diamond a\to\Box 0\leq\Box(a\to 0)=\Box\neg a.

This shows that
(M5)
holds. For
(M6)
, given
(M5)
, we have that
\neg\Diamond\neg a=\Box\neg\neg a
. By
(l1)
,
(M1)
and
modus ponens
, we get:

\neg\Diamond\neg a\leq\Box\neg\neg a\land\Box(\neg\neg a\to a)\leq\Box(\neg\neg a\land(\neg\neg a\to a))\leq\Box a,

as desired. Finally,
(FS3)
follows from
(FS2)
.
∎

We will now prove a version of the
iEx
-Embedding Theorem for
miEx
algebras.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
L
be a
miEx
algebra and
O_{L}
its quotient orthomodular algebra. Letting
\Box_{O}a_{O}=[\Box a]_{O}
and
\Diamond_{O}a_{O}=[\Diamond a]_{O}
for any
a\in L
, we have that the map
a\mapsto a_{O}
is a surjective fundamental modal algebra homomorphism from
(L,\Box,\Diamond)
to
(O_{L},\Box_{O},\Diamond_{O})
. Moreover,
(O_{L},\Box_{O},\Diamond_{O})
is a
\mathrm{DOM}
algebra.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.3
, we know that
O_{L}
is an orthomodular algebra and that the map
a\mapsto a_{O}
is a fundamental algebra homomorphism. We first show that it also preserves modalities. Let
a,b\in L
be such that
a\sim b
. Then

\neg\Diamond a=\Box\neg a=\Box\neg b=\neg\Diamond b,

where the first and last equalities hold by
(M5)
. This show that
\Diamond a\sim\Diamond b
. Moreover, we claim that
\neg\Box a=\neg\neg\Diamond\neg a
. The left-to-right direction follows from
(M6)
and the fact that
\neg
is antitone, and, for the converse, by
(M4)
, we have that
\neg\neg\Diamond\neg a\leq\neg\neg\neg\Box a\leq\neg\Box a
. By the claim, we now have that

\neg\Box a=\neg\neg\Diamond\neg a=\neg\neg\Diamond\neg b=\neg\Box b.

Hence
\sim
is a congruence relation with respect to
\Box
and
\Diamond
as well, which implies that
a\mapsto a_{O}
is a fundamental modal algebra homomorphism from
(L,\Box,\Diamond)
to
(O_{L},\Box_{O},\Diamond_{O})
.

Finally, we show that
(O_{L},\Box_{O},\Diamond_{O})
is a
DOM
algebra. Since it is a homomorphic image of
L
, we already know that
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
hold, so we only need to check
(M3)
. This amounts to showing that
\neg\Diamond\neg a=\neg\neg\Box a
, which already follows from the claim above.
∎

This gives us the construction of
O_{L}
. For
I_{L}
, we first need to introduce the following restriction of Fischer Servi frames
[
5
,
8
]
:

Definition 5.7
.

An
\mathrm{IFS}
frame is a triple
(X,\leq,R)
such that
(X,\leq)
is a poset and
R
a relation on
X
satisfying the following conditions for any
x,y,z\in X
:

(FC2)

z\leq x
and
xRy
together imply that there is
y^{\prime}\leq y
such that
zRy^{\prime}
;

(IFC)

xRy
and
y\geq z
together imply
y=z
.

Note that
(IFC)
is a strengthening of the usual
(FC1)
condition on Fischer Servi frames, which states that
xRy\geq z
implies that there is
x^{\prime}\leq x
such that
x^{\prime}Rz
. As is to be expected, the downward closed subsets on an
\mathrm{IFS}
frame form an
\mathrm{IFS}
algebra.

Lemma 5.8
.

Let
(X,\leq,R)
be an
\mathrm{IFS}
frame. For any downset
U\subseteq X
, let
\Box_{R}U=\{x\in X\mid\forall y,z(x\geq yRz\to z\in U)\}
and
\Diamond_{R}U=\{x\in X\mid\exists y:xRy\land y\in U\}
. Then
(Dn(X),\Box_{R},\Diamond_{R})
is an
\mathrm{IFS}
algebra.

Proof.

It is well known that
(M1)
,
(M2)
,
(FS1)
and
(FS2)
hold on
(Dn(X),\Box_{R},\Diamond_{R})
whenever
(X,\leq,R)
satisfies conditions
(FC1)
and
(FC2)
(see, e.g.,
[
5
]
). Hence we only need to check that
(l1)
holds. Fix
x\in X
and a downset
U
. We must show that, whenever
x\geq yRz
,
z\in\neg\neg U\to U
. Since
yRz
, we know that
z
is minimal, so we only need to verify that either
z\notin\neg\neg U
or
z\in U
. Again, since
z
is minimal, we have that, if
z\notin U
, then
z\in\neg U
, so that
z\notin\neg\neg U
, as desired.
∎

We can now lift the map
a\mapsto a_{I}
to a fundamental modal homomorphism.

Lemma 5.9
.

Let
L
be a
miEx
algebra. Let
(P(L),\supseteq,R)
be the set of all proper prime filters on
L
, ordered by reverse inclusion and endowed with the following relation
R
:

PRQ\Leftrightarrow\big((\Box a\in P\Rightarrow a\in Q)\land(a\in Q\Rightarrow\Diamond a\in P)\big).

Then
(Dn(P(L)),\Box_{R},\Diamond_{R})
is an
\mathrm{IFS}
algebra. Moreover, the map
a\mapsto a_{I}
is a fundamental modal algebra homomorphism.

Proof.

For the first part, by Lemma
5.8
, it is enough to show that
(P(L),\supseteq,R)
satisfies
(FC2)
and
(IFC)
. Let
P,P^{\prime}
and
Q
be prime filters on
L
such that
P^{\prime}\supseteq P
and
PRQ
. Because
P^{\prime}
is a prime filter and
(M1)
,
(M2)
hold on
L
, note first that
F=\{a\mid\Box a\in P^{\prime}\}
is a filter and
I=\{b\mid\Diamond b\notin P^{\prime}\}
is an ideal. Let
F^{\prime}
be the filter generated by
Q\cup F
. We claim that
F^{\prime}\cap I=\emptyset
. If this is true, then, since
L
is an iEx-algebra, we can apply
3.4
and obtain a prime filter
Q^{\prime}
extending
F^{\prime}
and disjoint from
I
. By construction, for such a filter
Q^{\prime}
we have that
Q^{\prime}\supseteq Q
and
P^{\prime}RQ^{\prime}
, as desired. For the proof of the claim, suppose, towards a contradiction, that we have
c\in Q
,
a\in F
and
b\in I
such that
c\land a\leq b
. Then
1\leq(c\land a)\to b
, which means that
(c\land a)\to b)\in Q
. We must then have
c\land((c\land a)\to b)\in Q
, so
a\lor(a\to b)\in Q
by
\mathrm{(iCl_{1})}
. Since
Q
is prime, either
a\in Q
or
a\to b\in Q
. If
a\in Q
, we have that
(c\land a)\land((c\land a)\to b)
, and hence
b\in Q
. Since
PRQ
, this implies that
\Diamond b\in P\subseteq P^{\prime}
, contradicting our assumption. Hence we may assume that
a\notin Q
. We must then have
(a\to b)\in Q
, and hence
\Diamond(a\to b)\in P
. By
(FS3)
, we get that
\Box a\in P
or
\Box a\to\Diamond b\in P
. In the first case, it follows that
a\in Q
, which we have ruled out already. So
\Box a\to\Diamond b\in P\subseteq P^{\prime}
. But since
\Box a\in P^{\prime}
, this means that
\Diamond b\in P^{\prime}
, a contradiction. This completes the proof of the claim, and thus that
(FC2)
holds.
For
(IFC)
, we argue as follows. Suppose that
P,Q,Q^{\prime}
are prime filters such that
PRQ
and
Q\subseteq Q^{\prime}
. Letting
a\in L
, recall first that
1\leq\neg\neg(a\lor\neg a)
in any fundamental lattice. By
(l1)
, we have that
\Box(\neg\neg(a\lor\neg a)\to(a\lor\neg a))\in P
, so
\neg\neg(a\lor\neg a)\to(a\lor\neg a)\in Q
. Since we also have
\neg\neg(a\lor\neg a)\in Q
, it follows that
a\lor\neg a\in Q
. Because
Q
is prime, it follows that
a\in Q
or
\neg a\in Q
for any
a\in L
. But this clearly means that
Q\subseteq Q^{\prime}
implies
Q^{\prime}=Q
. Hence
(IFC)
holds as well.

This shows that
(Dn(P(L)),\Box_{R},\Diamond_{R})
is an
\mathrm{IFS}
algebra. For the second part, we know already that the map
a\mapsto a_{I}
is a fundamental algebra homomorphism, but we must show that this map also preserves the modal operators. This amounts to showing the following for any
a\in L
and any
P\in P(L)
:

1.

\Diamond a\in P
if and only if there is
Q\in P(L)
such that
a\in Q
and
PRQ
;

2.

\Box a\in P
if and only if, for any
P^{\prime},Q\in P(L)
such that
P\subseteq P^{\prime}
and
P^{\prime}RQ
,
a\in Q
.

We start from the first item. The right-to-left direction is clear by the definition of the relation
R
. For the converse, we assume that
\Diamond a\in P
, and we first claim that the filter
F
generated by the set
\{a\}\cup\{b\mid\Box b\in P\}
is disjoint from the ideal
I=\{d\mid\Diamond d\notin P\}
. Assume towards a contradiction that we have
a\land b\leq d
for some
d\in I
and
b
such that
\Box b\in P
. By
(M2)
, we get that
\Diamond(a\land b)\leq\Diamond d
. Moreover, by
(D1)
, we have that
\Diamond a\land\Box b\leq\Diamond(a\land b)\leq\Diamond d
. Since
\Diamond a
and
\Box b
belong to
P
by assumption, this means that
\Diamond d\in P
, a contradiction. Now let
Q
be a filter extending
F
that is maximal among those disjoint from
I
. We claim that
Q
is prime. If true, this clearly completes the proof of the first item above. For the proof of the claim, suppose that
c_{1},c_{2}\notin Q
. By a standard argument, there must be
d\in I
and
b
such that
\Box b\in P
such that:

(a\land b)\land c_{1}\leq d
and
(a\land b)\land c_{2}\leq d
. By standard reasoning using
(M1)
,
(M2)
and Lemma
2.13
, this implies that:

•

1\leq\Box((((a\land b)\land c_{1})\lor((a\land b)\land c_{2}))\to d)
;

•

\Diamond((a\land b)\land c_{1})\leq\Diamond d
;

•

\Diamond((a\land b)\land c_{2})\leq\Diamond d
.

Together with
(MCl)
, the first inequality implies that

\Box(((a\land b)\land(c_{1}\lor c_{2}))\to d)\lor\Diamond((a\land b)\land c_{1})\lor\Diamond((a\land b)\land c_{2})

is in
P
. Since
P
is prime, one of
\Box(((a\land b)\land(c_{1}\lor c_{2}))\to d)
,
\Diamond((a\land b)\land c_{1})
or
\Diamond((a\land b)\land c_{2})
must belong to
P
. In the first case, this implies that
((a\land b)\land(c_{1}\lor c_{2}))\to d\in Q
. Since
a\land b\in Q
and
d\notin Q
, this means that
c_{1}\lor c_{2}\notin Q
. In the second and third case, the two inequalities above imply that
\Diamond d\in P
, a contradiction. Therefore
c_{1}\lor c_{2}\notin Q
, and
Q
is prime, as desired.

Finally, we show that item
2
above also holds. The left-to-right direction clearly follows from the definition of
R
. For the converse, suppose that
\Box a\notin P
. By
(M6)
, this means that
\neg\Diamond\neg a\notin P
. Since
L
is an
Ex
-algebra, we can apply Lemma
3.5
, and obtain a prime filter
P^{\prime}
extending
P
and containing
\Diamond\neg a
. By the proof of item
1
above,
\Diamond\neg a\in P^{\prime}
implies that there is a prime filter
Q
such that
\neg a\in Q
and
P^{\prime}RQ
. Clearly,
a\notin Q
, which is what we needed. This completes the proof.
∎

Putting Lemmas
5.6
and
5.9
together, we can now prove a modal version of the
iEx
-Embedding Theorem.

Theorem 5.10
.

Let
L
be a
miEx
algebra. Then there are a
\mathrm{DOM}
algebra
O_{L}
, an
\mathrm{IFS}
algebra
I_{L}
and a subdirect fundamental modal algebra embedding
e:L\to O_{L}\times I_{L}
.

Proof.

Fix an
miEx
algebra
L
. We let
O_{L}
be the quotient ortholattice of
L
, and
I_{L}
be the fundamental modal subalgebra of
(Dn(P(L)),\Box_{R},\Diamond_{R})
generated by sets of the form
a_{I}
for
a\in L
. By Lemmas
5.6
and
5.9
,
O_{L}
is a
\mathrm{DOM}
algebra and
I_{L}
is an
\mathrm{IFS}
algebra, and both are fundamental modal homomorphic images of
L
. By the same reasoning as the proof of the iEx-Embedding Theorem, the map
e:L\to O_{L}\times I_{L}
given by
e(a)=(a_{O},a_{I})
is therefore a fundamental modal algebra subdirect embedding.
∎

Finally, by the same reasoning as in the proof of Theorem
4.1
, we now get the following:

Corollary 5.11
.

Let
\mathsf{DOM}
be the modal logic of
\mathrm{DOM}
algebras and
\mathsf{IFS}
the modal logic of
\mathrm{IFS}
algebras. Then
\mathsf{DOM}\cap\mathsf{IFS}
is the logic of
miEx
algebras.

6
Conclusion

We conclude with some open problems about constructive quantum logics motivated by the results presented here:

Question 1

What is the complexity of
iEx
-logic?

Question 2

Are all the axioms listed in Theorem
4.3
independent over Holliday’s preconditional logic?

Question 3

Is there a finite axiomatization of
iEx
-logic with axioms that contain at most three variables?

Question 4

What are the joint validities of basic modal orthologic and the implication-free fragment of
\mathsf{IK}
or
\mathsf{CK}
?

Question 5

What are the joint validities of basic modal orthomodular logic and
\mathsf{IK}
or
\mathsf{CK}
?
