Low-dose baricitinib plus danazol in primary immune thrombocytopenia: a randomized, controlled phase 2 trial
Authors: Peng Zhao, Zhuo-Yu An, Hai-Xia Fu, Yang Wang, Guo-Shuang Feng, Hui-Xin Liu, Dai-Hong Liu, Yi Liu, He-Bing Zhou, Hui Liu, Zhao Wang, Yu-Jun Dong, Hong-Mei Jing, Zhen-Ling Li, Li-Hong Li, Li-Ping Dou, Qiu-Sha Huang, Jin Wu, Li-Ping Yang, Yue Jin, Zhi-Xue Li, Chen-Cong Wang, Qi Chen, Xiao-Lu Zhu, Yun He, Yuan-Yuan Zhang, Qian Jiang, Hao Jiang, Jin Lu, Ying-Jun Chang, Xiang-Yu Zhao, Xiao-Jun Huang, Xiao-Hui Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This multicenter, randomized, controlled study was conducted to explore the safety and efficacy of low-dose baricitinib plus danazol for ITP patients who had failed corticosteroids and at least one recommended subsequent treatment. Participants were randomly assigned to receive baricitinib plus danazol (n = 108) or danazol alone (n = 108) by a central, interactive web-based system. Patients and caregivers were not blinded to group assignment. Efficacy assessments were performed in the intention-to-treat population by investigators blinded to group assignment. The primary endpoint was 6-month durable response. Forty-nine (45.4%) patients in the combination arm and 22 (20.4%) patients in the monotherapy arm achieved 6-month durable response (P < 0.001). The safety analysis set included patients who received at least one dose of study medication (n = 108 in the combination arm and n = 105 in the monotherapy arm). Fifty-two (48.1%) patients receiving baricitinib plus danazol and 47 (44.7%) patients receiving danazol alone reported at least one adverse event. Each arm reported two patients who developed an adverse event causing study discontinuation and one patient who developed a grade 3 or more severe adverse event. Low-dose baricitinib plus danazol might be a novel option for difficult-to-treat ITP. Introduction

Immune thrombocytopenia (ITP) is an autoimmune disease characterized by isolated thrombocytopenia that predisposes patients to increased bleeding risks and impaired quality of life1–5. The loss of immune tolerance plays a central role in ITP pathogenesis, resulting in accelerated platelet clearance and impaired platelet production6. Initial therapies for ITP, including corticosteroids, intravenous immune globulin (IVIG), and anti-D, typically lead to reduced platelet destruction and rapid platelet responses; however, durable remission is uncommon, and long-term exposure to corticosteroids causes concerns regarding toxicity, patient tolerance, and quality of life3,4,7. Recommendations for subsequent treatments include thrombopoietin receptor agonists (TPO-RAs), rituximab, splenectomy, fostamatinib8, and other options, including several oral immunosuppressants3,4. More recent studies reported potential efficacy of a BTK inhibitor9 and a neonatal Fc receptor inhibitor10 in patients with relatively refractory ITP. However, achieving a sustained response or predicting response to different therapies are still challenging7–10. There remains an unmet need for novel ITP-modifying agents, particularly in patients who are refractory or intolerant to available therapies7,11.

Baricitinib, a selective and reversible Janus-associated kinase 1 (JAK1) and JAK2 inhibitor, can inhibit multiple cytokine signaling pathways involved in abnormalities in the innate and adaptive immune responses12–14. Baricitinib is safe and effective in the treatment of rheumatoid arthritis15,16, systemic lupus erythematosus17,18, and alopecia areata19. We previously reported a single-arm pilot study of baricitinib in 35 patients with steroid-resistant or relapsed ITP (Clinical Trials.gov number, NCT05446831). A total of 27 patients achieved overall response, including 20 patients with durable response; adverse events were reported in 11 patients and were generally moderate and transient20. Danazol, an attenuated androgen, is among the recommended subsequent treatments for primary ITP4. Previous studies of danazol reported acceptable safety files and potential efficacy in primary ITP21–23, but sustained response was difficult to achieve for danazol monotherapy in patients with refractory disease21. Therefore, we hypothesize that the combination of low-dose baricitinib and danazol may be an option to maximize efficacy while minimizing adverse effects.

Here, we report a randomized, controlled trial evaluating the safety and efficacy of low-dose baricitinib plus danazol for adults with ITP who are refractory to corticosteroids and at least one recommended subsequent therapy. Efficacy assessments were performed in the intention-to-treat population, and the safety analysis set included all patients who received at least one dose of study medication. The primary efficacy endpoint (i.e., 6-month durable response) was achieved in 49 (45.4%) patients in the combination arm and 22 (20.4%) patients in the monotherapy arm (P < 0.001). Adverse events were reported by fifty-two (48.1%) patients receiving baricitinib plus danazol and 47 (44.7%) patients receiving danazol alone. Each arm reported two (1.9%) patients who developed an adverse event causing study discontinuation and one (0.9%) patient who developed a grade 3 or more severe adverse event. Several proinflammatory biomarkers were upregulated in patients responding to baricitinib and were reduced after treatment. These data further support baricitinib as a candidate for the armamentarium of ITP-modifying agents.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 312 patients were screened, among whom 216 were enrolled and randomly assigned to receive baricitinib plus danazol (n = 108) or danazol alone (n = 108). Three patients in the danazol arm withdrew consent after randomization, and the remaining 213 patients were included in the safety analysis (Fig. 1). The median duration of ITP was 28 (IQR, 17–7.75) months in the combination arm and 30 (IQR, 18.25–44.75) months in the monotherapy arm (Table 1). Before entering this study, 58 (53.7%) patients in the combination arm and 64 (59.3%) patients in the monotherapy arm had received 4 or more previous ITP-modifying treatments. Among patients receiving baricitinib plus danazol, 29 (26.9%) discontinued treatment before week 26 (12 due to absence of efficacy, 10 withdrew consent, 5 lost to follow-up, and 2 due to adverse events). Efficacy profiles

At the 6-month follow-up, 49 (45.4%, 95% CI 36.0–54.8%) patients in the combination arm and 22 (20.4%, 95% CI 12.8–28.0%) patients in the monotherapy arm achieved durable response (relative risk [RR] and 95% CI: 2.23 [1.45, 3.41], P < 0.001). Sustained platelet response was achieved by 50 (46.3%, 95% CI 36.9–55.7%) patients receiving baricitinib plus danazol and 18 (16.7%, 95% CI 9.6–23.7%) patients receiving danazol alone (RR [95% CI]: 3.14 [2.10, 4.67], P < 0.001) (Table 2). Patients in the baricitinib plus danazol arm also had higher rates of complete response (43 [39.8%] vs. 10 [9.3%]), overall response (77 [71.3%] vs. 46 [42.6%]), and initial response (69 [63.9%] vs. 22 [20.4%]). Ten patients in the combination arm and 15 patients in the monotherapy arm withdrew consent before Week 26. To address the issue of confounding in this open-label study, we employed a beta-binomial model to quantify the probability of unobserved combinations that could alter statistical significance (P > 0.05) for the primary efficacy endpoint. This approach treats the observed response rates as priors for the underlying success probabilities: for the monotherapy arm (n = 108, 22 responses; rate ≈0.204), we used weak priors α₁ = 22 and β₁ = 86, and for the combination arm (n = 108, 49 responses; rate ≈0.454), we used α₂ = 49 and β₂ = 59. Simulating extreme attrition (n₁ = 15 observed in the monotherapy arm, n₂ = 10 in the combination arm), we enumerated all 176 possible outcome combinations (k₁ = 0 to 15 remissions in the monotherapy arm, k₂ = 0 to 10 in the combination arm) and computed their joint probabilities P (K₁ = k₁, K₂ = k₂) = P (K₁|α₁, β₁) × P (K₂|α₂, β₂), assuming independence. Among these, 6 combinations yielded P > 0.05 (e.g., k₁ = 13/15 and k₂ = 0/10), representing tipping points where the experimental benefit is obscured (Supplementary Table 2). The individual probability ranged from 7.1 × 10−11 to 2.9 × 10−12 for each combination, with a cumulative probability of 2.8 × 10−9.

Among patients responding to study treatment, the median peak platelet count was 108 (IQR 83.5, 173) × 109/L in the combination arm and 57.5 (IQR 46.75, 83.75) × 109/L in the monotherapy arm (P < 0.001) (Fig. 2). The median time to response was shorter for responders in the combination arm (14 vs. 31 days, P < 0.001). The median durations of response within the treatment period were 164 and 107 days, respectively (P < 0.001). Thirty-four patients (31.5%) in the combination arm and 33 patients (30.6%) in the monotherapy arm received concomitant medications at baseline. Concomitant medications were discontinued or tapered in 22 of the 24 patients responding to baricitinib plus danazol therapy and seven of the 14 patients responding to danazol monotherapy (Supplementary Table 3). Rescue medication was administered to 25 (23.1%) patients receiving baricitinib plus danazol and 40 (37.0%) patients receiving danazol alone. Patients receiving combination therapy reported improvements in all ten primary ITP-Patient Assessment Questionnaire (ITP-PAQ) scales (P < 0.001). Score differences for Symptoms (13.9), Psychological (13.8), Overall Quality of Life (8.0), and Women’s reproductive health (9.2) were within or above the previously established MID ranges24, indicating clinically meaningful improvements (Supplementary Fig. 1). Safety profiles

The worst WHO bleeding score during follow-up was recorded for each patient receiving study treatment (Supplementary Tables 4–6). No WHO grade 3–4 bleeding was observed in the combination arm (Supplementary Table 5). During the treatment period, 52 (48.1%) patients receiving baricitinib plus danazol and 47 (44.7%) patients receiving danazol alone reported at least one adverse event (Table 3). The two arms each reported two (1.9%) patients who developed an adverse event causing study discontinuation and one (0.9%) patient who developed a grade 3 or more severe adverse event. One patient in the combination arm experienced grade 3 lung infection. The patient was clinically diagnosed with bacterial pneumonia caused by undetermined pathogen at Week 10. Baricitinib was discontinued, and the patient was hospitalized and recovered after intravenous antibacterial therapy. One patient in the monotherapy arm experienced grade 3 liver enzyme elevation (isolated hepatocellular injury without signs of acute liver failure). The patient’s alanine aminotransferase level reached >5× ULN on Week 16 (aspartate aminotransferase level reached >3× ULN at the same visit). Danazol was discontinued and the patient received intravenous magnesium isoglycyrrhizate for 2 weeks. Liver enzyme levels returned to the normal range 5 weeks after the discontinuation of danazol. The most frequent adverse events included liver enzyme increase (20.4% in the combination arm vs. 13.3% in the monotherapy arm), upper respiratory tract infection (14.8% vs. 9.5%), and dyspepsia (13.9% vs. 8.6%). Patients receiving baricitinib plus danazol reported more infectious events compared with those in the monotherapy arm (any infection, 23.1 vs. 16.2%; SARS-CoV-2 infection, 13.9 vs. 9.5%); however, severe hypogammaglobulinemia was not observed in either arm (Supplementary Table 7). Safety outcomes

Biomarker analyses

Interleukin (IL)−1β (P = 0.009), IL-2 (P = 0.008), IL-6 (P = 0.008), tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α (P = 0.017), and interferon-α (P < 0.001) were upregulated in ITP patients (n = 17) compared with healthy volunteers (n = 11) (Supplementary Fig. 3). Patients responding to baricitinib showed higher baseline serum concentrations of IL-6 (P = 0.007) and interferon-α (p = 0.026) than that of the non-responders (Supplementary Fig. 4). Among the seven patients with overall response, IL-1β (P = 0.016), IL-2 (P = 0.031), TNF-α (P = 0.016), and interferon-α (P = 0.016) were significantly reduced after baricitinib treatment (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Discussion

Our previous single-arm pilot study reported acceptable safety and efficacy profiles of baricitinib in patients with primary ITP20. In this study, we report a randomized trial exploring the combination of low-dose baricitinib and danazol. All participants had failed corticosteroids and at least one subsequent therapy according to current consensus and guidelines3,4. Indeed, 81 patients (75.0%) in the combination arm and 90 (83.3%) patients in the monotherapy arm were previously treated with TPO-RAs, and 48 (44.4%) patients in the combination arm and 57 (52.8%) patients in the monotherapy arm reported previous rituximab therapy. Our findings further support the potential efficacy of JAK inhibition in this difficult-to-treat population.

Patients receiving low-dose baricitinib plus danazol had significantly higher rates of 6-month durable response, sustained platelet response, overall response, complete response, and initial response regardless of the baseline platelet count, history of splenectomy, and baseline concomitant medication. Notably, 25 patients withdrew consent before Week 26, which resulted in missing data for the primary efficacy analysis and raised the issue of confounding. In the post-hoc analysis using a beta-binomial model, we evaluated the unobserved combinations that could obscure the benefits of combination therapy (6 combinations in total, including a “worst-case” combination where all patients in the combination arm failed and all patients in the monotherapy arm responded to study treatment). The cumulative probability of these combinations was exceedingly low, supporting the robustness of our primary efficacy endpoint. Danazol monotherapy reportedly results in relatively slow and sometimes insufficient responses4,21–23. Compared with those who responded to danazol alone, patients responding to combination therapy had shorter response times and higher peak platelet counts. The discontinuation or tapering of the baseline co-medications was successful in most patients responding to baricitinib plus danazol (23/24, 15/16 for corticosteroids) but appeared to be difficult in patients responding to danazol monotherapy (7/14, 4/9 for corticosteroids). Although considerable advances have been reported in ITP-modifying agents, it remains challenging to achieve durable response in patients with refractory diseases7–11. There are substantial unmet needs in the development of additional therapeutic options, and combination treatments have been highlighted as potential approaches7,11. Our previous pilot study and this randomized trial indicate that baricitinib, administered either at 2 or 4 mg daily, may be a candidate for the treatment of ITP. This work further suggests that low-dose baricitinib may be a safe and effective component of combination therapies for difficult-to-treat ITP (e.g., for patients who have failed corticosteroid and at least one recommended subsequent ITP-modifying treatment).

Concerns regarding cardiovascular risks and thrombotic events have been raised for JAK inhibitors12 (FDA black box warning in 2021) and are of specific clinical relevance in patients with ITP. Baricitinib also frequently leads to liver toxicity, with dose-related increases in liver enzymes and bilirubin observed13. Danazol is associated with androgenic effects and liver toxicity4, whereas thrombotic and infectious events are rarely reported. Therefore, baricitinib was explored at 2 mg daily in this study, and danazol at a relatively low dosage of 200 mg twice daily is considered a safe option for combination therapy and active comparator21. Infections were the most frequently reported adverse events in both arms, suggesting a possible impact of the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic. Patients receiving combination therapy reported higher rates of infection, which was consistent with previous safety observations in people receiving baricitinib at 2 mg daily15–19,25,26. Although serious infections are rare, attention should be given to the risk of infections during and after baricitinib administration. Patients with unacceptable cardiovascular and/or thrombotic risks were excluded, and no such events were observed in this study.

Unlike most other JAK inhibitors with observations of drug-induced thrombocytopenia (presumably caused by the interruption of JAK2-mediated TPO signaling)27–31, baricitinib is well documented to induce a platelet count increase (peaks rapidly within 2 weeks of treatment13), sometimes marked thrombocytosis (1–4%, but no cases of myeloproliferative diseases have been reported)15–19,25,26. There is hitherto no established hypothesis explaining this distinct change in platelet count. In previous in vivo experiments, decreased JAK2 expression or suboptimal doses of ruxolitinib were noted to induce paradoxical thrombocytosis32, suggesting a complicated role of JAK2 inhibition in the regulation of thrombopoiesis. In the previous and current trials, we observed a rapid platelet count increase (approximately 2 weeks after baricitinib initiation) in patients responding to study treatment. Although proinflammatory cytokine signaling pathways were implicated in the pathogenesis of ITP33, and levels of certain biomarkers were reduced after response to baricitinib in our study (similar to observation in patients with systemic lupus erythematosus14), it remains unclear whether the rapid response could be attributed to an early immunomodulatory effect or a drug-associated platelet count increase. Nevertheless, the role of long-term immunomodulation is supported by the considerable rates of durable response and sustained platelet response, as drug-induced elevation in platelet count appears to gradually decrease toward baseline according to integrated data 13.

This study has several limitations. The efficacy analyses must be interpreted with caution given the open-label design and the lack of a placebo-controlled design. Sustained treatment-free response or sustained remission off-treatment was not assessed in this study. The immunomodulatory effect of baricitinib and its association with clinical response remain unclear due to the small sample size (especially for changes in cytokine concentrations among the responders) and the limited scope of immunological assays. Further studies are needed to evaluate the long-term efficacy and safety of baricitinib and to identify and verify potential biomarker predictors for treatment response in patients with ITP.

In conclusion, this randomized trial supports the safety and efficacy of baricitinib in combination therapy for patients with primary ITP who are refractory to corticosteroids and at least one recommended subsequent treatment. The potential role of JAK inhibition in ITP management merits further investigation.

Methods

Trial design and oversight

The trial was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the Good Clinical Practice guidelines of the International Council for Harmonisation. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review boards of Peking University People’s Hospital, Beijing Luhe Hospital, Chinese PLA General Hospital, The Sixth Medical Center of PLA General Hospital, Beijing Hospital, Beijing Friendship Hospital, Peking University First Hospital, Peking University Third Hospital, China-Japan Friendship Hospital, and Beijing Tsinghua Changgung Hospital. The experimental study was approved by the institutional review board of Peking University People’s Hospital. Written informed consent was provided by each participant. The CONSORT checklist was included in Supplementary Note 3.

This was a prospective, multicenter, randomized, controlled, open-label, phase 2 study conducted at 10 different tertiary medical centers in China. We recruited adults with primary ITP who had failed corticosteroids and at least one recommended subsequent treatment for ITP. Patients were enrolled between May 23, 2023 and June 19, 2024. Concomitant medications (oral corticosteroids at a daily dose of <20 mg prednisone equivalent, oral TPO-RAs, oral immunosuppressants other than corticosteroids, or a combination thereof) were allowed. All ITP-modifying therapies, other than those allowed as concomitant medications, had to have been discontinued for at least 4 weeks (for rituximab and splenectomy, no less than 24 weeks) prior to randomization. Key exclusion criteria included secondary ITP, malignancy, pregnancy or lactation, current or recent clinically serious infections, recent clinically significant thrombotic events, and particular laboratory abnormalities (Supplementary Table 8 and Supplementary Note 1).

Enrolled participants were randomly assigned at a 1:1 ratio to receive either low-dose baricitinib (2 mg daily) plus danazol (200 mg twice daily21) or danazol monotherapy (200 mg twice daily) with a block size of four by using a central, interactive web-based system. Investigators responsible for participant enrolment and data analysis were masked to treatment assignment, whereas participants and investigators responsible for study treatment were aware of the allocated treatment arm. Concomitant medications that were considered eligible at enrolment were maintained for at least 4 weeks, and could be tapered or discontinued thereafter according to medical assessment. The treatment period lasted for 6 months (Supplementary Fig. 6). Treatment could be discontinued due to severe or life-threatening adverse events, an absence of efficacy, or a patient’s request. Treatment was temporarily interrupted if a platelet count over 300 × 109/L was observed for two consecutive tests at least seven days apart and was resumed when the platelet count decreased to less than 150 × 10⁹/L. Rescue medications (including corticosteroids, IVIG, platelet transfusion, and an increase in the dose or dose frequency of concomitant medication) were allowed throughout the treatment period.

Seventeen patients assigned to the combination arm and 11 healthy volunteers participated in an experimental study supported by the National Natural Science Foundation of China (No. 82400157) and underwent serum immunological biomarker evaluation at Peking University People’s Hospital.

Study outcomes

The primary endpoint was 6-month durable response, defined as the maintenance of a platelet count ≥30 × 109/L and doubling of the baseline count, the absence of bleeding, and no need for rescue medication from the first achievement of response to the 6-month follow-up20. We also reported sustained platelet response, defined as platelet counts of ≥50 × 109/L and an increase from baseline of ≥20 × 109/L for at least 6 of the 8 visits during weeks 19–26, as a post-hoc efficacy endpoint to provide more comprehensive information regarding disease control. Patients whose trial terminated before week 26 (e.g., treatment discontinued due to an adverse event, absence of efficacy, or loss to follow-up) were classified as non-responders to 6-month durable response and sustained platelet response. Patients receiving any rescue medication after week 12 were classified as non-responders to sustained platelet response.

The prespecified secondary efficacy endpoints included complete response (platelet count ≥100 × 109/L and absence of bleeding), response (platelet count ≥30 × 109/L and at least doubling of the baseline count, and absence of bleeding), overall response (achieving complete response or response) at any time point during the study period, and initial response (complete response or response at 4 weeks) (Supplementary Table 9). Patients receiving rescue medication were considered non-responders for all efficacy endpoints throughout the duration of rescue medication and until 8 weeks after its discontinuation. Bleeding severity was reported in accordance with the WHO’s bleeding scale34. ITP-PAQ scales were used to evaluate health-related quality of life at baseline and regular visits35. Adverse events were reported and graded in accordance with the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), version 5.0. The primary and secondary efficacy endpoints were centrally adjudicated by investigators from Peking University People’s Hospital who were masked to treatment assignment.

Cytokine expression analysis

Serum samples were collected after randomisation and before baricitinib administration from patients with ITP, and at any time between week 12 and week 24 for patients responding to treatment (defined as an overall response at the time of sample collection). The serum cytokine concentrations were analysed by ELISA at Peking University People’s Hospital. The minimum detectable levels are 0.2 picogram per millilitre (pg/mL) for IL-1β, 2.5 pg/mL for IL-2, 0.4 pg/mL for IL-4, 2.0 pg/mL for IL-6, 0.2 pg/mL for IL-10, 2.0 pg/mL for IL-17, 0.27 pg/mL for TNF-α, and 0.2 pg/mL for IFN-α. All values below the detection levels were considered undetectable and were valued at 0 for comparison between groups.

Statistical analysis

Based on our previous studies and clinical trial reports on other ITP-modifying agents, the 6-month durable response rate was hypothesized to be 25% in patients receiving danazol monotherapy21, and 45% in patients receiving low-dose baricitinib plus danazol. To detect such a difference, 86 patients were required to achieve 80% power with a two-sided type I error of 5%. With the assumption of a 20% dropout, 108 patients for each arm were required at enrolment.

Efficacy assessments were performed in the intention-to-treat population, including all patients assigned to each arm. The safety analysis set included all patients who received at least one dose of study medication. Categorical variables are summarized as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables are summarized as medians and IQRs, unless otherwise specified. We used chi-square tests to assess differences in categorical variables. Continuous variables were compared via the Mann–Whitney U test. We calculated relative risks with 95% CIs for analyses of the primary and secondary efficacy endpoints. Biomarker level changes from baseline and comparisons between the ITP-PAQ scales at baseline and at the last follow-up in each arm were performed via the two-tailed Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Missing data were not imputed in this study. A post-hoc sensitivity analysis employing a beta-binomial model was performed to assess the robustness of the primary efficacy endpoint under missing data (consent withdrawal before Week 26) scenarios. The statistical analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 (Cary, NC) and Prism version 8.0 (GraphPad Inc., San Diego, CA). A two-sided P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant unless otherwise specified (Supplementary Note 2).
